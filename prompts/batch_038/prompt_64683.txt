So I've gone down a bit of a rabbit hole on cause prioritization — the importance/tractability/neglectedness triad people use to pick which causes to work on — and my roomate, who is way deeper into this stuff than me, keeps insisting it's "just expected value thinking with a division-of-labour gloss on top." I think it's smuggling in more than that, and I want to test it on a real episode instead of a toy one, because the toy ones always make the framework look fine.

Here's the episode, and I'll lay out the facts so you don't have to guess at them. In 1966–67 the World Health Organization committed to an intensified programme to eradicate smallpox worldwide, run by D. A. Henderson. At that point smallpox was still killing something like two million people a year and was endemic across much of Africa and South Asia; the stated plan was mass vaccination up to roughly 80% coverage, country by country. Then in eastern Nigeria in 1966 a field team under William Foege ran short of vaccine during an outbreak, couldn't vaccinate everyone, and improvised — find the cases, vaccinate in rings around them, and pay people to report suspected cases. That surveillance-and-containment tactic turned out to work better and cheaper than mass vaccination and became the backbone of the whole programme. The last naturally occurring case was Ali Maow Maalin, a hospital cook in Somalia, in October 1977, and the World Health Assembly declared the world smallpox-free in 1980. Meanwhile, and partly in reaction to exactly this kind of campaign, the 1978 WHO/UNICEF conference in Alma-Ata declared primary health care the road to "Health for All by the Year 2000" — a deliberately broad, horizontal, health-systems-and-social-determinants programme that refused to single out any one disease. Its critics said it was unmeasurable and utopian; the vertical camp said only disease-specific campaigns had ever actually delivered anything. Within a year or two the compromise was "selective" primary health care: pick a handful of specific high-burden conditions where a cheap intervention already exists. Which is arguably just ITN before ITN had a name.

I want the analysis to run in both directions, and I want it disciplined rather than mushy — no "ITN is a useful heuristic but of course has limits," that's the answer everyone gives. First: actually score smallpox-eradication-as-of-1966 on importance, tractability and neglectedness the way the framework asks you to, and be strict about the bookkeeping. What units is importance in, and does eliminating a disease outright score any differently from averting the same number of deaths every year forever, or does the framework flatten those into the same number? Is neglectedness measured against all possible funders, against other global-health funders, or against the marginal attention of the person doing the scoring — because smallpox in 1966 was simultaneously the most neglected cause imaginable (nobody was funding eradication) and the least (literally every country on earth already ran a vaccination programme). Then take on the two features that look like they break the framework rather than illustrate it. One: tractability turned out not to be a property of smallpox at all, but of smallpox-plus-a-tactic, and the tactic didn't exist in anyone's plan in 1966 — the cause became more tractable mid-programme because a team in Nigeria ran out of vaccine. Does that mean ITN can't be applied ex ante, only as a story you tell afterwards? Two: the Alma-Ata objection isn't that smallpox was the wrong pick, it's that a framework built on legible, measurable, campaign-shaped wins will systematically rank legible causes above important-but-diffuse ones, and that this bias is invisible from inside the framework. Is that a fair charge or a strawman? And finally, tell me what 1980 is worth as evidence — a genuine vindication of the vertical way of choosing, or one visible draw from a distribution we can't see, given that the same eradication template later got pointed at polio, malaria, guinea worm and yaws with wildly different results. Where the framework is simply silent about this episode, say it's silent rather than making it speak.
