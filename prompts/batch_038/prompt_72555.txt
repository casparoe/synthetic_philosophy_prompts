I have been reading Aquinas on whether a private person may kill a tyrant, and there is a structure in his treatment that I cannot name. Killing a ruler belongs, on his account, to whoever has jurisdiction over him; the private person who does it anyway is doing someone else's work. Yet the blame seems to lift exactly when there is no superior to whom one could have recourse. What is doing the work is neither the agent's standing — he has none — nor the urgency of the harm, which can be just as pressing where a superior exists but is slow. It is a negative fact about the institutional surroundings: nobody better placed is available. Hence two consequences that strike me. The permission is open to whoever happens to be present, including the least suitable candidate; and it evaporates the moment a competent authority appears.

Nearby cases share the shape but differ in revealing ways. A junior officer who takes command when the captain and mates are lost is filling a vacuum too, but there a rule already specifies the successor. A neighbour who protects an absent owner's property is likewise unauthorized, but the law there asks whether the intervention served the owner's interests — a condition this version does not state.

Is there an established concept for a permission whose sole trigger is the absence of the proper agent? I want the carving, not merely the label: how it differs from urgency-based permissions, from consent- or benefit-based ones, from the view that everyone holds a power to enforce the law of nature where there is no common judge, and from mere excuse rather than permission. And how should it treat the case where a superior exists but cannot be reached in time — is the vacuum still there, and does the permission survive it? If nothing in the literature fits, coin a term and give it a definition with boundary cases.
