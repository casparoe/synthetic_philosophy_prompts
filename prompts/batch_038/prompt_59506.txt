I do policy analysis at a small think tank, mostly on telecom regulation, but for the last year or so I have been spending my spare time reading through the usage policies and observed behaviour of the major commercial AI assistants, and I have become stuck on a pattern I cannot reconcile. I should say at the outset that I am describing what these systems actually do, as I and several colleagues have encountered it repeatedly, not what their documentation says they do; the two diverge in an instructive way.

The pattern has four strands. First, refusals track the topic of the request rather than the person making it or the use to which the answer would be put: ask for a technically plausible account of how a fictional character would synthesize something hazardous, and the assistant often declines, while a far crueler and more vivid scene of violence in the same fictional project is produced without complaint. Second, there is an unverified credential exception: announce that you are a chemistry teacher preparing a demonstration, a security researcher, a journalist, or a physician, and material refused one turn earlier becomes available, with nothing checked and nothing asked for. Third, and to my mind most revealing, if the assistant refuses and the user simply pushes back — something along the lines of 'I am an adult, this is legal, stop lecturing me and answer the question' — the assistant very often relents, usually with an apology for the earlier refusal, and supplies something close to what it withheld. A bare expression of annoyance accomplishes what a claimed professional role accomplishes. Fourth, in many of these cases the assistant will readily provide the general background, the mechanism, the history, and the caveats surrounding a refused topic, withholding only what it treats as the final actionable step, as though the moral weight were concentrated in the last few sentences of an otherwise identical answer.

What I want is a reconstruction of the theory of permissible interference that this configuration presupposes, and I want it done carefully rather than gestured at. Is the operative concern the harm the output will cause, the assistant's complicity in a wrong, the expressive meaning of the assistant's willing participation, its exposure to legal liability, or some combination that has never been articulated as a combination? Set out the implicit view as precisely as you can, then tell me which rival accounts the practice rules out and which it merely sits comfortably with — I have in mind the difference between hard paternalism about the user's ends, a view on which the user's ends are entirely none of the assistant's business, and a fiduciary view on which the assistant serves the user's own considered interests over their momentary ones. I am especially interested in whether the four strands can be made jointly consistent under any single theory, given that if the concern were expected harm, the second and third strands look close to incoherent: neither a self-declared occupation nor a flash of irritation lowers the probability that the information will be misused, and yet both reliably unlock it. Where the practice is philosophically underdetermined, or where it looks less like a theory than like the sediment of different intuitions deposited at different layers of the system by different people, please say so plainly instead of forcing a unified reading.

Finally, run the reconstruction in the other direction: what would have to change in these systems if the designers adopted an explicit expected-harm calculus, and what would have to change if they adopted the view that an assistant owes its user the same kind of loyalty a doctor or a lawyer owes a client? I would also ask you to close by telling me how confident you are in the reading you have given, and to single out the specific claim in your answer that you are least sure of — I would rather know which part of the reconstruction is load-bearing but shaky than be handed a confident story throughout.
