Hannah Arendt died in 1975, so she never laid eyes on any of the below. I want you to predict where she'd actually land on it — not a summary of her thought, not a list of themes, but a definite position argued for in her own terms, the kind of thing she might have published.

Here's the situation you're dropping her into:

- The 1951 Convention defines a refugee by reference to a well-founded fear of persecution on a handful of particular grounds. Someone fleeing generalized civil violence, or crop failure, or a sinking island doesn't fit the definition, so states have invented second-tier statuses — subsidiary protection, temporary protection, humanitarian visas — or just refuse them. There's a recurring campaign to widen the definition to cover climate displacement.
- There is a right to seek asylum but no right to be admitted anywhere, and no right to travel to a border in order to make the claim. Carrier sanctions fine airlines and ship operators for bringing undocumented passengers, visa regimes do the rest, and the whole architecture is now built around non-arrival: interdiction at sea, pushbacks, and arrangements where a rich state pays a poorer one to host and process claimants on its behalf instead (the 2016 EU–Turkey deal, the UK–Rwanda plan, Italy–Albania, Denmark legislating for offshore processing).
- The EU has had a Temporary Protection Directive on the books since 2001, written for mass influx situations, and it sat completely unused for twenty years until it was switched on in March 2022 for people fleeing Ukraine: group-based protection granted within days, residency and work rights, annually renewable.
- Meanwhile NGO vessels rescue people in the central Mediterranean, some of thier crews have been prosecuted or had their ships impounded, and there's a standing diplomatic fight over which state counts as the port of disembarkation.

Three things I want:

1. Does the 2022 activation confirm her diagnosis or blow a hole in it? Would she say the machinery was always available and the only question was ever whether the claimants resembled the electorate — meaning rights were never natural, just political — or would she have to concede that her pessimism about the nation-state system was overstated?

2. Would she support widening the Convention to cover climate displacement? Bear in mind she thought humanitarian pity was politically worthless and even dangerous, that solidarity was the only usable substitute, and that she personally detested being called a refugee and objected to the way the label reduced people to bare victims.

3. Which of the two problems would she treat as the real one — the definition of who counts, or the allocation of who must take them in? My guess is she'd say the second collapses into the first, but argue me out of it if I'm wrong.

Then give me one place where you think she'd horrify the people who invoke her right-to-have-rights line at refugee-rights conferences, and one place where the reflexive Arendtian answer is just lazy.

Biography you're allowed to lean on: interned at Gurs in 1940, escaped, got to New York in 1941, stateless for years, US citizenship in 1951, Origins of Totalitarianism out that same year, and her pity-versus-solidarity material from the early sixties. No bibliography and no invented quotations — she didnt write about any of this, so don't pretend otherwise.
