Hey — I've been asked to write down my actual position for a workshop where everyone in the room is going to attack it, so I'd rather get attacked by you first. The workshop is about quantifying political violence over the long run, and I keep finding myself on both sides of the argument depending on who I'm talking to. The friend who runs the reading group said "you can't hold all of these at once" and then wouldnt say which ones, which is infuriating. Here's the list, in no particular order:

(1) The only quantity worth arguing about is per-capita risk — the annual probability that a given person is killed by another person's intentional action. Raw body counts are useless, because population grew by an order of magnitude or two over the same stretch.
(2) I include war deaths, genocide, state execution, and deaths from deliberately engineered famine in that quantity, not just interpersonal homicide. It's supposed to be one number, not two.
(3) That number has fallen by a large multiple from small-scale/prestate societies, through the medieval period, to now, and the fall is a real feature of the world rather than an artifact of how the record happened to be kept.
(4) What did the work was institutional: the state's monopoly on force and the removal of self-help vengeance. Not a change in people. Over this timescale human nature has been basically flat — no selection pressure worth mentioning, no rewiring.
(5) The categories themselves move. A huge amount of what we'd now call violence was routine and uncounted in earlier periods — beating your wife and children, judicial torture, slavery, conquest — and the boundary is still moving today. So "how violent was society X" partly depends on which concept you measure with.
(6) The twentieth century was the bloodiest in history, and I don't want to extrapolate the trend forward at all. I'm deeply suspicious of any story on which history has a direction.
(7) The long-run baseline comes from ethnographic and skeletal evidence about small-scale societies, and I think that comparison is legitimate even though those societies aren't literally comparable to ours.
(8) None of this is moral progress. Safer is not the same as better; I don't think the decline tells us anything about whether we've become more decent.

So: which of these actually pull against each other, and how badly? I'm expecting that at least one pair here only looks contradictory and isn't, and I'd like to be told which one that is rather than having all eight flagged as suspect. For each genuine tension, say what would have to give, what the cheapest repair is, and what the repair costs me — my suspicion is that several of the obvious fixes would quietly gut the part I actually care about in (3). Then tell me which single revision, if I only get to make one, buys the most consistency for the least damage. Please don't tell me to be more careful about definitions in general; I want to know which specific claim has to be weakened or dropped, and in what order you'd sacrifice them.
