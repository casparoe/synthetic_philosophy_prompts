I'm retooling the Bayesian confirmation theory week for my grad seminar and I've decided to flip the order: instead of marching the students through five proposed fixes to the old-evidence problem and then asking which they like best, I want to hand them the spec first — the list of things any solution owes us before we start comparing candidates. Help me write that spec. I'm asking you rather than them because I suspect I've been sloppy about which of my demands are genuine requirements and which are just me being an orthodox Bayesian with attachments.

The setup, so we're fixed on the target: an agent already believes E to something very near certainty — the anomalous advance of Mercury's perihelion, say — and then encounters a theory H that entails E. Intuitively H gets a big boost from this. Conditionalization says it can't get any boost at all, since if your credence in E is already 1, learning-nothing-new means P(H|E) = P(H). That's the problem, and it generalizes way past the physics textbook case.

What I want is the desiderata list itself. Skip the scorecard of existing proposals — two or three sentences at the end is plenty, I'll do the rest in class. For each requirement, give me three things: the requirement stated precisely enough that a proposal could fail it; the case or consideration that motivates it; and your judgment on whether it's non-negotiable or a camp prejudice. That last column is the point of the exercise, so don't be timid.

Here's my raw material. Some of it I'm confident about, some I'm not, and part of what I want from you is a ruling on which entries survive:

- The history-independence demand. Two agents with numerically identical current credence functions, differing only in that one came to believe E from observation thirty years ago and the other came to believe it this morning. Does H get the same amount of support for both? I think a solution has to give a verdict here and I think most of them give the wrong one, but I can't articulate why it matters independently of that intuition.

- The no-self-deception demand. A fix that works by having the agent temporarily not-believe E, or bracket E, or entertain a hypothetical credence function she knows doesn't describe her, seems to require her to be mistaken about her own doxastic state, or at least to run two books. Is that a genuine objection or an aesthetic one?

- The approximate-evidence demand, which is the one I actually care about most and which I've never seen anyone press. The old evidence was never exactly what the theory entails. The perihelion figure was known with error bars, from observations of varying quality, and it got revised. So the thing the agent is certain of is something like "the advance is within this range of this value," while H entails a precise number. A solution that only handles the idealized case where E is exactly entailed looks to me like it hasn't solved the historical problem at all. Tell me whether that's a real requirement or whether I'm just complaining that the toy model is a toy model.

- The forgetting case, which I made up for my students last year and it broke the seminar. An astronomer once had the full data set, formed a high credence in H on the basis of it, and has since lost the data — she retains H, retains the conviction that she once had good astronomical reasons, but can't reconstruct what they were. Is H still confirmed for her? Does a solution have to answer that? If confirmation can be destroyed by memory loss without any change in the agent's view of the world's contents, something has gone wrong, but I don't know how to say what.

- The generality demand: whatever mechanism does the work has to handle the reverse temporal order too — theory adopted first, confirming data arriving later — and the social version, where E is old for you and brand new for your colleague and you're trying to figure out how much their excitement should move you.

- The no-free-lunch demand: the mechanism mustn't hand out support to any old hypothesis that happens to entail E. A gerrymandered H* that entails E and also predicts catastrophe next Tuesday can't come out equally confirmed, and I'd like the requirement stated so that this failure is legible as a failure rather than as a bullet someone is happy to bite.

- Consistency with what we already think we know: coherence/Dutch-book considerations, the total-evidence constraint, and the stubborn fact that practicing scientists treat retrodiction of known data as real support but consistently as somewhat weaker support than a successful novel prediction. Is that last item a datum any solution must reproduce, or is it a prejudice that has been smuggled in from the philosophy-of-science side? I've gone back and forth on this for years and I want an argument, not a hedge.

Two more things, and these are the ones I'll actually grade on.

First, I have a suspicion that a certain triple can't be jointly satisfied: (i) conditionalization is the only legitimate update rule, (ii) no appeal to credences in propositions the agent doesn't currently entertain or to counterfactual credence states, and (iii) history-independence as I stated it above. If you think that's right, give me the inconsistency argument in a form I can put on a slide — informal, but with the steps. If you think it's wrong, say which of the three gives and what it costs to give it up. And if you see some *different* jointly unsatisfiable cluster in my list that's more interesting than mine, lead with yours.

Second, tell me which single requirement does the most work in killing off candidate approaches — the one that, if you accept it, immediately rules out the largest share of what's on offer. I'll use that as the spine of the lecture.

Format: numbered list or a table, whichever reads better, with the non-negotiable / camp-prejudice call made explicitly for each entry rather than left for me to infer. Flag anything in my raw material you think should be struck from the list entirely. End with a single last line in exactly this form:

Verdict: [name the one requirement you judged most exclusionary, in a phrase short enough to fit on the slide]
