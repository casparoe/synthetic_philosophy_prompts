I'm putting together a grad seminar on moral status and I want to hand my students a model of what a non-stupid adversarial collaboration actually looks like — the document two camps *should* be able to co-sign, not the one they produce in journal articles.

The two camps. **Gradualists**: phenomenal capacity comes in degrees, and moral status itself comes in degrees — a bee's bad experience carries less moral weight than a macaque's, not because it's a bee but because there's less there. **Threshold theorists**: sentience is a switch — either a being has interests or it doesn't — and once it does it has full and equal moral status; differences between beings show up in how much welfare is at stake, never in how much the being counts.

For the exercise, stipulate this and have both sides take it as given: a lab has built a calibrated intensity estimator, a continuous number tracking the best available neural and behavioral marker of how much experience there is in a system at a time. It reads about 0.9 for an alert adult human, 0.4 for a macaque, 0.02 for a honeybee, 0.005 for a decapod shrimp, and 0.001 for a crude engineered artifact that the lab controversially claims is faintly sentient. Nobody in the collaboration disputes the numbers; they dispute what the numbers could possibly be numbers *of*. Both sides also have to address two allocation cases: (1) a fixed analgesic budget that can either cover 100 macaques at 0.4 or a million shrimp at 0.005, with no third option; (2) the faint-many case — whether 10^18 of the artifact's 0.001 bad moments outweigh one macaque's hour of unrelieved pain, and what each side says when the exponent keeps going up.

Deliverable, in this order:

1. A numbered list of claims both can sign, at least eight, and at least two of them should be claims a naive reader would have guessed belong to only one side (e.g. something about there being no sharp cutoff in the underlying capacities that a threshold theorist can still endorse, or something about some beings mattering more all-things-considered that a gradualist can still endorse).
2. A disagreements table with exactly these columns: `ID` | `Disagreement in neutral wording` | `How the gradualist states their own side` | `How the threshold theorist states their own side` | `Substantive or verbal, and why`.
3. A cruxes table with exactly these columns: `ID` | `Crux question` | `Case or evidence both agree bears on it` | `Gradualist's prediction, written down in advance` | `Threshold theorist's prediction, written down in advance` | `Which side, if either, says it would move them`.
4. A closing paragraph saying which chunk of the fight you think is just words, and what would have to be true for that diagnosis to be wrong.

No names, no bibliography, no "see So-and-So (20XX)" — my students won't have the lit in front of them and neither might you, so I want the arguments rather than the reference list. Also please ban yourself from writing "speciesism" or "intrinsic value" anywhere in the document; make the two sides do that work with other vocabulary, which is part of the point.

Last thing, and don't skip it: tell me how confident you are in what you've drafted. Which of your "agreed" claims would you bet a real adherent refuses to sign, and which of the recorded predictions do you think is least likely to match what the actual camp says? Single out the one point you're least sure about and say why — I'd rather have an honest weak spot marked than a document that reads like it was already settled.
