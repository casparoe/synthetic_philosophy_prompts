Our logic reading group finally got around to Gödel's little ontological argument — the one he never published in his lifetime, which circulated among his colleagues in various axiomatizations and only became public after he died. Half of us could follow the derivation and still had no idea whether to be impressed, and then two members, Marcus and Iris, spent forty minutes failing to persuade each other while the rest of us took notes. I took a modal logic course once so I can check the proofs, but I cant really grade the moves. Their exchange went roughly like this:

(1) Marcus: the whole thing is a trick dressed as a theorem. Five axioms about "positive properties," a definition of God-likeness as having them all, and out pops a necessary God. But nobody would accept the axioms unless they already accepted the conclusion, so it just relocates the question rather than answering it.

(2) Iris: that's not how the machinery runs. Gödel isn't defining God into existence — the one genuinely modal premise is that God-likeness is possibly exemplified, and the rest is S5 plus a notion of essence: once something God-like exists in any world, God-likeness is an essence of it, and essence entails every property the thing has, so all of them become necessary, necessary existence among them. So the argument proves something stronger than its premise — that if a God-like being is possible it exists necessarily — and that strengthening comes from the logic, not from a stipulation.

(3) Marcus: then it proves far too much. With a positivity predicate left that unconstrained, almost any coherent bundle of properties counts as God-like, and you can grind the same derivation out several times over for beings that aren't compatible with each other. He added that positivity is basically a free parameter: pick a different one and axiom 5 flips truth value, so the argument can't be telling you anything about God, only about whatever predicate you chose.

(4) Iris: partly fair — she said axiom 5, that necessary existence is a positive property, is where the argument gets its grip and where she's least comfortable. But she denied it's a free parameter, because Gödel supposedly included a clause to the effect that if a property is positive its negation isn't, precisely to stop the polytheism, and she read that clause as saying God-likeness is a complete maximal characterization and so can have at most one instance. And she said the modal collapse everyone worries about only bites if you also hold that a God-like being is the ground of everything else's existence; Gödel's own system doesn't claim that, so the collapse objection is aimed at a version he didn't write.

(5) Marcus: the uniqueness clause looks question-begging to him. God-likeness is defined in terms of positivity, so if positivity underdetermines which properties are in the bundle, the clause can't supply uniqueness, and if it does supply uniqueness, then positivity was never the neutral structural notion Iris wants it to be.

(6) Iris: on the contrary, she said, the positivity axioms are exactly as neutral as saying a certain collection of sets is an ultrafilter — a closure condition with no content until you fill the predicate in. What's really doing the theological work is axiom 5 alone.

(7) Marcus: but "the notion of value, formalized" is not a neutral label, he said. Why should value be closed under entailment, why should it hold in every world, and why should existing necessarily be valuable rather than merely fortunate? If your only defense of those is that they make the system elegant, you've conceded the charge in (1).

Could you go through this move by move and say who's right at each step — rate (1) through (7) somewhere between clearly wrong and clearly right, with a sentence or two of justification each? Please don't recapitulate the argument back to me or send me off to read things; I mainly want the adjudication, plus whatever both of them are missing that would actually settle it.
