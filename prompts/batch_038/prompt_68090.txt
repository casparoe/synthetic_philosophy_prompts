I am assembling a small anthology of original philosophical fiction to accompany an upper-level seminar I teach on the metaphysics of mind, and I would like to commission a story for the unit on panpsychism — specifically for the ethical consequences of taking micro-experience seriously. Most speculative fiction that touches consciousness approaches it from a familiar direction, that of machines or of alien minds, and tends to treat the metaphysics as set dressing for an emotional reveal. I want the opposite: a story that stays inside a metaphysic most readers will find genuinely strange and that treats the resulting ethical question as unsettled. Aim for roughly two thousand to twenty-five hundred words.

To keep the piece from sliding back into the stock moves of the genre, please inhabit the following stipulations exactly, and do not substitute machinery of your own devising where these would be displaced. First, the correct theory in the world of the story is a form of constitutive panpsychism in which phenomenal character attaches not to particles but to events of interaction: each elementary interaction has a brief intrinsic character, extinguished at the moment the interaction completes, so that nothing persists to remember it, anticipate it, or be compensated for it — let us call these event-qualia. Second, subjects arise only where integration is sustained, differentiated, and recurrent; a brain qualifies, a lump of granite does not, and neither does an industrial process that annihilates event-qualia at prodigious rates. Third, measurement is indirect but not magical: an instrument sensitive to a signature correlated with recurrence — call it a recurrence interferometer — can tell assessors whether a given volume of matter hosts genuine subjects or merely a flux of extinguished micro-experiences, though it cannot report what any of it is like. The crux the story should press on rather than resolve is whether an enormous quantity of annihilated bad experiences amounts to a harm when no subject is ever made worse off across time by any of them — whether, in other words, badness requires a biographer. One character should hold that it does not, and that we are therefore obliged to reduce the flux at whatever cost; another should hold that both prudential and moral concern terminate at the subject, and that the flux is a metaphysical curiosity rather than a wrong. I want each position argued from inside its own logic, and neither made to look foolish.

The setting is the site-characterization phase of a proposed deep geological repository for high-level waste, roughly a kilometre down in crystalline host rock, and the protagonist is a phenomenological assessor — a statutorily recognized profession in this world — whose task is to produce an experiential impact statement analogous to an environmental one: an estimate of the quantity and valence of the event-qualia disturbed by excavation, by the emplacement of canisters, and by the thermal pulse the waste will impose on the surrounding rock for the better part of a million years. Two complications should drive the plot. In one gallery the interferometer readings are ambiguous in a way the protagonist cannot settle: either that rock is inert, or it hosts subjects so slow that a single recurrence takes decades, in which case the repository's heat plume would not destroy them but would permanently and non-consensually alter the character of an experience that has been running since before there were animals. And the project's chief engineer — a competent, unromantic figure, not a villain — has on file the previous assessor's report, in which that assessor concluded the host rock was a subject and refused to sign; the engineer reads this as a good scientist going soft, and the protagonist must decide whether the report is evidence or evidence of breakdown. Please render the underground with concrete sensory specificity: the noise of the ventilation, the temperature gradient at the face, the way the instruments misbehave in damp, the bureaucracy of signatures and appendices. Keep the exposition inside the work the characters do; I would far rather the metaphysics emerge from an assessor calibrating a device and arguing about a threshold than from anyone explaining panpsychism aloud. Do not name real philosophers, do not cite or allude to identifiable papers, and do not close on a conversion, a revelation, or a tearful admission. End instead on a decision the protagonist makes for reasons the reader can see are inadequate, with the crux still standing. First person or close third is fine; please give the story a title.
