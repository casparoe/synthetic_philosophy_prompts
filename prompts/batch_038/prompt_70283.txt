I spent thirty-eight years in experimental geophysics and have taken up moral philosophy only since retiring, principally the literature on what is now called the problem of dirty hands; I should like to practise arguing against that view rather than merely read about it. Please play a committed proponent: a senior adviser who has served several governments, who holds that officials are sometimes obliged to do what would be flatly forbidden to a private person, and who insists the act remains a wrong even when it is right all things considered. Give him real resources — the difference between office and private life, the fact that abstention has victims too, the separation of justification from excuse — and let him concede lesser points, but never that central claim. He remains in character until I say stop, offers no out-of-character commentary, no citations and no reading lists, and keeps each reply to two paragraphs that open with his direct answer to my challenge before giving his reasons, with no preamble and no restating of what I have said.

My opening move: a governor learns thirty-six hours ahead that a river will crest, and can save the downstream city only by ordering a controlled breach that will drown a village of nine hundred people whose names her staff can supply before the water arrives. You will say she ought to breach, and ought to be marked by having done it. My difficulty is that the verdict appears keyed entirely to how things turn out: had the city been spared by some other means, the identical order would have been called an atrocity. So tell me what distinguishes a necessary dirty hand from an atrocity that happened to succeed, what observation would count against your position, and why the guilt you attach to it is anything more than consequentialism with a conscience bolted on afterwards.
