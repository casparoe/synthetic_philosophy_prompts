I am in the third year of a PhD in health-care ethics, and this spring I am running a half-day workshop for intensive-care and oncology nurses and consultants at our teaching hospital. The topic is whether there are morally excellent acts that are nonetheless not required of anyone — what moral philosophers call the supererogatory. The clinicians are bright and articulate but have read none of the literature, and several hold views they have never had occasion to articulate. I want a branching diagnostic instrument that sorts them by what they say about cases, not by which label they would pick if offered one. Please write the whole deliverable in British English.

**Part one: the logical space first.** Before writing any cases, set out the small number of substantive questions whose answers generate the distinct positions here. I have in mind questions of roughly this shape: whether an act can be better than a permissible alternative; whether omitting such an act can make one criticisable even if it is not required; whether the reason it is not required has to do with the cost to the agent, with the discretionary character of the duty itself, with a protected space for the agent's own life and projects, with the level of what is socially expected, or with nothing at all; whether the standard moves with what others do; whether praise is deserved or merely useful. Then enumerate the combinations of answers these axes permit — there will be many more combinations than there are actual views — and only afterwards locate the positions within that space.

**Part two: the tree.** Nine case questions, three answer options each, ordered as follows. Questions one to four must involve costs borne by the agent alone (their own body, their own time, their own money). Questions five to seven must involve costs that spill onto colleagues, other patients, or the service as a whole. Questions eight and nine must concern how third parties should respond to someone who has done such a thing — award schemes, telling a junior doctor that what she did was admirable, circulating the story. Please include at least: an anonymous living kidney donation to a patient one has treated; a nurse covering an unsafe number of patients rather than going home at the end of shift; a consultant privately paying for a patient's prescription; and a doctor who sits with a dying patient's family through the night. No question may use the words *obligatory*, *duty*, *required*, or *supererogatory* in its stem, and none may ask the participant to rate an act's moral status directly; each must be answerable by saying what one would think or feel about the person involved.

**Part three: the key.** Map answer patterns onto leaves — patterns, not scores, since I do not want to imply that the views sit on a single dimension. Handle split patterns explicitly and at length: I expect the most common response profile to be permissive about the own-cost cases and censorious about the spill-over cases, and I want to know what that profile indicates rather than being told it is inconsistent.

The leaves must include, at minimum: the view that the category is empty because morality is maximising and the resulting demands are a cost the theory should accept; the distinct view that the category is empty because being better than a permissible alternative is conceptually incompatible with being permitted; the cost-threshold view; the latitude view; the protected-projects view; the view that the line is set by social expectation and therefore shifts; the view that this is a matter of praise and credit rather than of obligation; the two-tier view on which the act is right but ought not to be publicly celebrated, because celebrating it corrodes the practice; the view that the category is only available if one can also make sense of its mirror image — acts that are bad yet not wrong; and a sceptical view on which the options are not rankable against one another at all. Mark any further leaf you add as named or unnamed, and where a cell in your logical space is empty, say whether you think that is because the view is incoherent, because it is unattractive, or simply because nobody has thought to defend it.

**Part four: the honest limitations.** For each leaf, one sentence on the consequence its proponents would least like to own. Then flag every pair of leaves your instrument cannot actually separate, and say why: I suspect the cost-threshold view and the protected-projects view will come out identically on clinical cases, and I would rather be told that now. Finally, tell me which of my requested cases is likely to be a dud — one on which nearly everyone gives the same answer and which therefore sorts nobody.

Please do not give me a bibliography, a reading list, author names, dates, or titles of papers; a model without access to sources would only be guessing at those, and my participants would not read them anyway. Where a position has a standard label in the literature you may use the label, but every leaf must also be described in terms a nurse could follow without having read anything.
