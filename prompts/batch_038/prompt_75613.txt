I work on research methodology: replication, statistical power, multiple-comparison corrections, preregistration, publication bias, effect sizes, Bayes factors and prior odds. I have no philosophy training. In the final objection section of his 1950 Mind paper on the imitation game, Turing takes up extrasensory perception and treats it not as pseudoscience but as a live threat to his proposal — he says the statistical evidence for telepathy at least is overwhelming, and then argues that if telepathy is real the game itself has to be modified, since information could reach the interrogator by non-sensory channels, and he suggests running the competitors inside a telepathy-proof room.

Restate that argument, as faithfully as you can, in the vocabulary I actually use. What is Turing claiming about evidence, priors, and effect size? What is he claiming about the design of a test, as opposed to the truth of a hypothesis? And what kind of methodological intervention is the telepathy-proof room — a blinding procedure, a confound control, something else?

Then give me the residue report. Which of his claims translate cleanly into my terms, which translate only with distortion, and which have no counterpart in them at all? I want to know in particular whether there is anything in his reasoning about why ESP, if true, would be a problem for his thesis rather than merely an interesting fact about humans that a methodology vocabulary cannot express — and whether that gap shows my framework is missing something or shows that Turing's argument here is less coherent than it first appears.
