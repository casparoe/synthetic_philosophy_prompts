My roommate is a hospital social worker, and we argued for most of a Saturday night about a practice she administers and I cannot make sense of. I would like the practice analyzed rather than the argument adjudicated, so let me describe it as concretely as I can, in her terms rather than mine.

The setting is advanced dementia. A patient signed documents years earlier, while competent: a health care proxy naming a family member as agent, and an instructional directive refusing, in the event of severe and irreversible dementia, hospital transfer, resuscitation, dialysis, antibiotics for aspiration pneumonia, and — in the version she says she sees most often — artificial nutrition and hand-feeding. Many of these forms also include a short free-response section, and she told me something that struck me: the ethics committee pays far more attention to that paragraph than to the checkboxes. It is where the patient writes, in their own hand, what made their life worth living, and says that a life without memory of their children, or without recognition of their own name, would not be a life they would want continued.

Four further features of the protocol matter for what I am asking.

1. When the time comes, the directive governs. Staff are expected to follow it even when the patient is visibly content — pleased by a visit, delighted by ice cream, laughing at the television — and even when the thing that will kill her is a readily treatable infection.
2. Surrogates are coached in a fixed order of resort: first, what the patient said or wrote; second, what the patient would have wanted, inferred from her values and character; and only third, and only when the first two are empty — for an infant, or someone profoundly disabled from birth — her best interests, understood as her welfare from the inside.
3. At the same time, for every other purpose, the institution treats the demented patient as a full subject of welfare. Pain must be assessed and treated; her distress is taken at face value; she is protected against neglect; a relative who failed to feed her would be reported to the authorities.
4. Staff are also told to seek the patient's assent before acting — to read her acceptance or resistance of a spoon, a needle, a transfer — which everyone concedes has no legal standing and which is, in practice, honored.

My friend's position was that the directive is the patient's own voice and that ignoring it silences her. Mine was that we are allowing a piece of paper, written by someone who on any ordinary description no longer exists, to override the interests of a person who is here, happy, and incapable of consenting to anything; and that the same sequence of acts performed by a stranger we would call killing. Neither of us moved. Here is what I want from you instead.

What account of the self does this practice presuppose? Specifically: does it assume that a person is something like the continuing author of a story — that the self persists as the source of a narrative and can bind later stages of the organism by what it once wrote — and if so, how much psychological continuity does that require, given that the directives are enforced precisely when memory, recognition, and the capacity to carry the story forward have been destroyed? Does the practice treat the person as partly located outside her own skull, in the document and in her family's testimony about her values, and if so, what would have to be true of selves for that to be more than a figure of speech?

Which rival accounts does it rule out outright, and which does it merely sit awkwardly alongside? I mean accounts on which what matters is the stream of experience, or the living human animal, or the present capacity to care about anything at all.

Then tell me whether there is a deflationary reading that needs no such metaphysics: autonomy as the earlier self's right to dispose of what happens to her body, or the ordering in (2) as a rule about burden of proof and the reliability of evidence rather than about identity, or a straightforwardly consequentialist rule about what happens when documents stop being trusted. Say which of these genuinely assume less, and whether any of them can explain the committee's preference for the handwritten paragraph over the checkboxes.

Is the structure internally consistent? I am most exercised by three joints: the ranking in (2), which makes experiential welfare a last resort rather than the default, and thereby seems to give narrative selves less protection than beings who have no story; the coexistence of (1) and (3), in which one body is a rights-bearing subject of experience for purposes of protection and a narrative author for purposes of ending treatment; and (4), assent that is always sought and never decisive, which seems to say that present experience matters morally but not enough to matter.

Finally, distinguish what the practice strictly requires from what it merely fits comfortably, and identify which features are load-bearing and which are decoration — places where two or three incompatible theories of the self would generate the identical protocol. And describe what would actually have to change — the form, the ordering, the staff training, the evidence needed to establish that a directive applies — under two replacements: that the welfare of the experiencing subject now is the only thing that counts, and that the demented patient is a numerically different person who has succeeded the author of the directive, inheriting the body without inheriting her obligations. On the second, note that at least one country treats a written request made while competent as sufficient authorization for euthanasia once dementia has advanced past the point of confirmation, while other legal systems treat the same act as homicide; tell me which replacement would be easier to legislate and which would be easier for a family to live with.

I would like the implicit theory stated up front as a compact thesis, then the analysis. Aim for something I could hand my roommate without her feeling that the practice she has spent a career administering was written off by someone who has never stood at the bedside.
