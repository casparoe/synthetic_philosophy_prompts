You are a philosopher of psychiatry who knows Ian Hacking's work on classification closely — the looping effect of human kinds, the difference between kinds that are indifferent to being classified and kinds that are not, his historical case studies of diagnoses that arrive, ramify through the self-descriptions of the people who receive them, and then quietly disappear — and who occasionally writes satire for a philosophy magazine.

Your task: compose the official release notes for version 6.1 of the diagnostic manual that governs psychiatric classification in your fictional jurisdiction. Approximately 700 words, laid out in the sectioned, numbered register of enterprise software changelog documentation, using headings such as New Features, Behavioural Changes, Deprecated Items, Breaking Changes, Known Issues, and Migration Notes for Existing Patients.

The joke must be structural rather than punchline-based. The document should read as though the categories are being patched precisely because the people running the previous version have stopped conforming to the documentation — and it should show, without ever explaining, that the revised criteria were derived from the self-descriptions the earlier criteria themselves produced. A reader who knows Hacking should be able to point to the loop; a reader who does not should still find the corporate solemnity funny.

Specific requirements:

1. Invent the new diagnostic category rather than borrowing a real one. Give it a name, an acronym, a criterion list with the usual thresholds and duration clauses, and a prevalence estimate. It should behave like an interactive kind, and one clause should inadvertently disclose the institutional and cultural ecology the diagnosis needs in order to exist — the settings, the professions, the forms of documentation, the available vocabularies of distress.
2. Include a deprecation notice retiring an older category. Write it so that a reader familiar with the history of fugue states, neurasthenia, or multiple personality recognises the pattern, without the piece naming any of them or asserting anything about their actual history.
3. Under Breaking Changes, record an item in which an organised group of people who carry the diagnosis has successfully petitioned to have its own self-understanding written into the criteria. Present this petition as a routine, well-triaged feature request, complete with a status note about the working group that reviewed it.
4. Under Known Issues, file one bug report from a quality-assurance perspective describing the manual's own feedback dynamic as a defect to be worked around — written by someone who cannot see that the defect is the mechanism.
5. In Migration Notes, address what becomes of people diagnosed under the superseded version: whether their records are converted, grandfathered, or voided, and what the document says about the continuity of the person across the change.

Constraints on content: the target of the satire is the practice of classificatory revision and its claim to be describing a settled natural order, not any named individual, committee, or real professional body. Keep all institutions fictional or generic, and do not attach dates to any events. Nothing in the piece may step outside the changelog format to editorialise, and no sentence may wink at the reader.

Before drafting, think carefully and set out, in five or six sentences, which features of the looping dynamic you intend to encode in which section and how each will remain implicit. Then write the release notes.
