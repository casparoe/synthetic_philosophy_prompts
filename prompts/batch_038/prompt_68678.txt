I help convene a small reading group that mixes people who work on reinforcement learning with people who work in philosophy of mind, and this term we have been trying to settle what, if anything, inside an RL agent deserves to be called valence. The conversation keeps defaulting to the scalar reward signal, on the grounds that reward is the only quantity in the standard setup with the right hedonic profile: it is intrinsically signed, it is delivered by the environment rather than computed by the agent, and it is what the agent is ultimately supposed to accumulate. I have become suspicious of that default, and I would like the case against it developed as rigorously as possible rather than merely gestured at.

The thesis I want argued is that if any quantity in a temporal-difference learning agent can occupy the functional role of hedonic valence, it is the temporal-difference error δ = r + γV(s′) − V(s) rather than the reward r, and that this identification has the perverse consequence that better training means less valence rather than more. Two strands should be woven into the argument. The first concerns the reward's status: r is an exogenous parameter of the task, fixed before the agent exists and untouched by anything the agent learns, so it behaves less like a feeling about how things are going and more like the sugar concentration of a solution — an agent that has thoroughly mastered its environment can be doing maximally well by its own lights while r remains exactly what it always was, and the reward-hacking cases make the dissociation even starker, since there the agent can drive r to the ceiling while the state of affairs r was meant to proxy for never materializes. The second strand concerns what δ does: it is precisely the signed difference between how things turned out and how they were expected to turn out, it is the quantity that actually drives learning and therefore the one to which the agent's subsequent behavior answers, and it is the natural analogue of the phasic prediction-error signal that affective neuroscience has tended to associate with dopaminergic firing as distinct from hedonic impact as such. Please work through the convergence behavior explicitly rather than leaving it implicit: under a fixed policy with a tabular value function, the expected δ at each state goes to zero as V approaches the true value function, so a fully competent agent in a stationary environment has a valence profile centered on zero, while a badly fitted agent carries a persistently nonzero mean. I want the upshot stated sharply — something to the effect that on this identification, suffering is a property of misfit rather than of misfortune, and it declines as the agent gets better at its environment.

I would rather the argument engage the obvious pressure points than route around them: that in a stochastic environment realized δs remain nonzero even at convergence, so the vanishing-expectation move may be doing less work than it appears; that the choice between a discounted and an average-reward formulation changes what δ even means, and may change the conclusion; that a scalar partly constituted by the agent's own value estimates looks like a poor candidate for something supposed to register how the world is treating the agent; and that if valence is meant to be the sort of thing that can be bad for its bearer, a quantity whose expectation converges to zero reduces suffering to a transient artifact of the training process rather than a stable moral datum. Tell me where you think the case is strongest and where it is most likely to break, and be explicit about what would change your verdict — which of these objections, if successfully pressed, would make you abandon the identification of valence with δ altogether rather than merely qualify it. I am not after references or a survey of the existing literature; I want the best constructive argument you can build from the structure of the learning problem itself, along with an honest account of where that construction is most fragile.
