I teach an intermediate epistemology seminar, and for the past few years I have opened the week on accuracy-first epistemology not with a paper but with a piece of fiction, so that the students meet the framework as something that happens to people before they meet it as something that is argued for. I would like you to write an original short story of roughly two thousand five hundred to three thousand words for this year's version of that exercise. I have a fairly specific pressure inside the framework in mind: the result that an agent who is confident her own credences are already maximally accurate should, by her own lights, expect that attending to further evidence would move her away from the truth, and therefore has reason to refuse to update. It is usually presented as a theorem one admires from a distance, and I want it presented as a predicament one can be trapped in.

Here is a scaffold, which you should feel free to alter in any way that serves the story. The setting is a coastal survey office in a small maritime jurisdiction whose civil service has, for some forty years, scored its forecasters on the accuracy of their published probability estimates — tide heights, storm tracks, fishery yields — by means of a squared-error rule, with the office's annual stipend derived from the mean of its scores. Two features of that institutional history should bear on the plot. First, there was a scandal, roughly a generation back, when the office briefly switched to a rule that rewarded the extremity of a forecast rather than its calibration, and a handful of forecasters discovered that they could maximize their expected score by recording certainties they did not hold; some of the students will not see why that rule failed until the story shows the damage it did, and I would like the story to make that failure legible without anyone delivering a lecture about it. Second, the office now contains a younger surveyor who insists on a different proper rule — one that penalizes confident error far more harshly than the squared-error rule does — and who argues that the choice between the two, both of them respectable, is not a technical matter but a matter of what kind of forecaster the jurisdiction is trying to produce. The protagonist is a senior forecaster with an exceptional decades-long score, which is precisely the source of her trouble: her own record, honestly assessed, licenses the inference that she is already close to optimal, and the inference that the new instruments her rival has installed are more likely to degrade her estimates than to improve them. Build the story around one concrete event — a storm, or the collapse of a stock the office has been forecasting — in which her refusal to update is at first vindicated and then shown to carry a cost that the vindication does not pay for. I would like the machinery to be right: it should be apparent, from what happens rather than from what is said, both why coherent estimates are immune to being accuracy-dominated by incoherent rivals, and why that same logic turns inward and tells an accurate forecaster to stop listening. Do not name real philosophers, real theorems, or real papers inside the narrative; the theory should reach the reader entirely as bureaucratic practice.

After the story, please append a separate note of three to six sentences, addressed to me rather than to the students, in which you assess your own handling of the material: state how confident you are that the reasoning embedded in the plot is technically correct as it stands, and single out the one point you are least sure of — the place where you would most want a specialist in epistemic utility theory to check the work before I hand it out. Use that note also to flag any liberty you have taken, whether for narrative economy or for effect: any place where a real scoring rule would not behave quite as your fictional one does, or where the immodesty result is being stretched beyond what it actually establishes, since I would rather know about those concessions in advance than have a sharp third-year find them for me.
