I convene a twelve-student masters seminar on Darwinian theories of culture, and I am setting the final paper: three hours, unseen, answer two of four questions, roughly forty-five minutes per question. Three of my four questions are drafted and I need the fourth. It has to be a question that cannot be answered by producing definitions, because every student in the seminar can define a meme competently and half of them can do it in their sleep. The conceptual apparatus I supplied over the term is as follows: Dawkins' original proposal and the properties he associated with successful replicators (longevity, fecundity, copying fidelity); Hull's reconstruction of the units problem in terms of replicators and interactors, which shifts the emphasis from what a thing is made of to what role it plays in a cycle of reproduction; Lewontin's abstract characterization of the conditions under which a population evolves by natural selection; the standing objection that cultural items are transmitted too imprecisely and too rarely to count as replicators at all; Sperber's alternative, on which the stability of a cultural distribution is explained by the psychological reconstruction of variants around attractors rather than by the passing along of durable items; and the dual-inheritance programme, which keeps population-level modelling of transmitted traits while treating transmission as guided by cognitive biases rather than by the copying of discrete units. I am not asking you for a reading list, citations, or page references. I have those. I want the question to turn on the concepts and the reasoning, not on the literature.

The question should be built around a two-part case that candidates will not have seen before, so please fold a compressed version of the case into the question as printed. Item A is a method for preparing a leavened flatbread, documented across nine villages in one valley over four decades. There are sixty-one recorded demonstrations. No two are alike: the order of steps, the quantities, the implements and even the number of hands involved vary from performance to performance, and an apprentice's version typically departs from her teacher's in most particulars. Yet the distribution of variants is stable across the whole period and clusters into three recurrent forms, and practitioners whose teacher performed a transitional variant usually produce something near the centre of one of the three clusters. In a subsequent elicitation study, people shown a deliberately distorted performance reliably produced something closer to the cluster mean than what they had been shown. Item B is a fixed sequence of forty-four syllables, meaningless in the local language, recited in full at a coming-of-age ceremony. Recordings across three generations differ by at most one or two syllables. Nobody claims to know what it means or where it came from; informants describe the rehearsal as tedious, requiring an hour a day for a month, and say that if its origin were explained to them they would not keep it. It persists undiminished.

Write, then, the following. First, the question as it will appear on the paper, case included, at a length candidates can read inside two minutes. Its core task must be comparative and it must have a determinate shape: which of A and B is the better candidate for being a meme, and what does the verdict show about whether copying fidelity or differential reproductive success is the property that makes something a replicator. Candidates should also be required to say what a replicator/interactor analysis yields in each case, whether the attractor account predicts something about A that memetics cannot accommodate or merely redescribes, and whether it is coherent to speak of Item B's interests diverging from its hosts' interests without smuggling in intentional idiom. Second, a model answer of nine hundred to a thousand words that argues a position rather than surveying the options; my own instinct, which I want the paper to pressure rather than flatter, is that B is the meme and A is not, but there should be a real and respectable case available for the reverse, and the model answer should acknowledge what that case costs its proponents. Third, a marking scheme out of thirty marks, built from five to seven separately assessable moves, each with its allocation and, for each, one line saying what the candidate who earns partial credit did and what the candidate who earns nothing did. Fourth, band descriptors for 70 and above, 55 to 69, and 40 to 54, phrased in terms of this question specifically rather than in generic essay-marking language. Fifth, a short note identifying which single step in the model answer a well-read but confused candidate is most likely to botch, and a sketch of the most attractive wrong answer: the position a clever student will be drawn into, and precisely why it fails. Finally, two or three sentences on what a candidate would have to add, beyond everything in the model answer, to deserve more marks than the scheme provides for.
