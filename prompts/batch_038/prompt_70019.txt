Worked at a historic-ship trust for twelve years, now writing on my own, and I keep running into a pattern I have no word for.

Two cases. One: a museum vessel whose planking has all been replaced over decades of conservation, while every removed original piece is catalogued and kept in the store — enough, technically, to rebuild the whole boat. The trust has a written policy that stored originals are never to be reassembled, never loaned, never photographed as a group, and staff are told this is about conservation ethics, though everyone knows the real reason is that a rebuilt-from-original-parts boat in the next hall would start an argument they'd lose.

Two: a folk band where every member has been replaced one at a time since 1978; the ex-members still tour together under a near-identical name, and the current lineup lobbies festivals not to book them and objects to any listing that puts the two side by side.

In both, the identity claim survives not because anyone argued for it but because the competing candidate is kept from ever existing in public. I know the reassembly case itself is ancient, and I know theres talk about needing exactly one survivor for identity to hold — but here the uniqueness isnt discovered, its manufactured.

Is there an established concept for the practice of suppressing the rival in order to protect an identity claim? If not, coin one and define it properly, because the name isnt what I'm after: I want to know where it stops counting. Does it apply when the rival merely never gets built, when the parts are quietly sold off to different buyers, when the suppression is done by a third party (a funder, a venue) just to avoid awkwardness? And how does it differ from ordinary gatekeeping or from the no-branching condition people invoke in fission cases?
