I'm 71 and spent my working life in electronic measurement — timing circuits, mostly — and only got into philosophy seriously about three years ago. Twice a month I go to a meetup at the library where people argue about whatever they've been reading. Last time it was the old story of the donkey that starves to death exactly halfway between two identical piles of hay.

Two of the regulars, Ingrid and Paulo, went at it for nearly an hour. I couldn't tell who was ahead at any point, and I've been turning it over since without settling it. Here's the exchange as best I can reconstruct it:

(1) Ingrid started by saying the case isn't really about free will at all. You only get starvation if you insist that every act needs a sufficient reason behind it, and that requirement comes from a metaphysics of explanation, not from anything about agency. Drop it and the donkey eats. So the story is a reductio of that principle and nothing more.

(2) Paulo said you can't drop it that cheaply. If nothing in the agent settles which pile he goes to, then the going isn't his doing, it's an event that happens in him. Substituting a random twitch for paralysis doesn't rescue agency — randomness is just as bad an account of free choice as paralysis is.

(3) Ingrid said he's leaving out a third thing: picking, as distinct from choosing. When reasons are balanced you don't need a reason for which; you need a reason for taking one rather than none, and that reason is obvious. Asking why the left one, when nothing turns on it, is asking for a reason where reasons have run out by hypothesis.

(4) Paulo said that's the load-bearing move and it collapses. If picking is a genuine exercise of agency then the agent is doing something deliberately, and doing something deliberately means being sensitive to whether you're doing it — so you prefer picking to standing there, and you prefer this way of breaking the tie (just going) to other ways (flipping a coin, shutting your eyes, waiting a bit). That's a preference, and you've smuggled back the asymmetry you said wasn't there. If picking isn't deliberate, it's noise, which is his point from (2).

(5) Ingrid said he's assuming preference has to be a total ordering over the most fine-grained options available. It doesn't. What's perfectly coherent is: {left, right} beats {stand still}, with no ranking between left and right. Preferring a disjunction to an alternative doesn't commit you to preferring either disjunct. The paradox comes out of an assumption about the shape of preference, not out of anything deep about the will.

(6) Paulo said fine, but notice what that concedes: your agent knows the two options are exactly equally good, and that isn't what the cases that got anyone interested in this look like. Choosing between two careers, or between two people you could marry — there it isn't that the scales come out level, it's that there's no fact of the level. If some options are neither better, nor worse, nor equally good, then the donkey is a bad model for hard choice and the whole picking-versus-choosing debate is being run on a fiction.

(7) Ingrid said that's a complaint about the model, not an answer to it. The donkey is meant to be a clean idealization of exact symmetry, and idealizations are supposed to be clean. Whether it also describes real hard choices is a seperate question.

(8) Paulo said the idealization isn't clean, it's empty. A real animal's reasons ride on a real physical state, and that state fluctuates and is noisy. Exact balance is an unstable equilibrium: the donkey doesn't sit there, he gets nudged out of it almost immediately. So the case describes something that can't happen, and unlike a frictionless plane it can't even be approximated in the limit.

I'm the one who brought up the electronics bit, for what that's worth. I mentioned that in my old field there's a name for the situation where a circuit has to settle an input that is neither one nor zero and can sit in the undecided state too long before noise throws it one way. They both grabbed onto that and read it in opposite directions. Ingrid said that's exactly her point: balanced states do resolve, all by themselves, and no story about agency is needed to explain how. Paulo said it's his point: what resolves them is noise, so if the donkey eats, that isn't him choosing, that's him twitching.

Near the end Ingrid said something else, which I couldn't check and dont want you to check either: that the donkey isn't in the original anyway. The version she remembers is older — a man equally hungry and thirsty standing between food and drink — and there's a medieval one about two dates of the same quality where the chooser's own nature supplies a difference. And she said the philosopher the story is named after never actually told it, that his real position was that when two courses are judged equal the will can only suspend judgement until circumstances change. Paulo said if that's right we spent an hour on a caricature of a view nobody held, which is worse than a paradox, it's a strawman with a good name on it.

So: can you go through this move by move and tell me who's right where? I'd like a rating on each of the eight numbered steps — right, partly right, or wrong, with the reason — rather than an overall winner, though I do want to know at the end who came out ahead. Three things I especially want your view on: whether the picking-versus-choosing distinction in (3) can carry the weight Ingrid puts on it against Paulo's objection in (4); whether (6) is a real escape from the problem or just changing the subject; and whether my bit about the undecided circuit actually bears on the philosophical question, or whether it's a red herring they both fell for. And if there's something neither of them said that would have settled it, tell me that too. No references needed, I'm not hunting down sources — I just want the reasoning.
