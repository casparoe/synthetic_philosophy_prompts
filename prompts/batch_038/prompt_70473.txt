I am revising a chapter on diachronic norms of belief for a volume aimed at first-year graduate students, and I have hit a wall on van Fraassen's Reflection principle. The principle, as I will state it here, says that if I am certain that at some future time my degree of belief in a proposition A will be x, then my present degree of belief in A should also be x; equivalently, in the more general conditional form, my current credence in A conditional on my later credence in A being x should itself be x. I want the chapter to treat it as a live controversy rather than as settled doctrine, and I want to see the case argued rather than catalogued. So: write an essay that takes a position on whether Reflection is a genuine requirement of rationality for a single temporally extended agent, and defends it.

Please engage with the structural question that I think is usually fudged, which is how much independent work Reflection does. If a later credence function is produced by conditionalizing an earlier one on evidence the agent actually receives, then Reflection appears to follow rather than to add anything; on that reading its distinctive content is confined to precisely those situations in which the later credences do not arise from conditionalization on veridical evidence. I want you to say whether that residue is substantial enough to deserve the name of a principle of rationality, or whether Reflection is best understood as a harmless corollary dressed up as a norm. Address the standard motivations on their merits: the diachronic sure-loss or Dutch book argument (and in particular whether being exploitable across time, when there is no single moment at which the agent chooses all the bets, really licenses a charge of irrationality); the analogy with deference to a recognized expert; and the coherence-based thought that an agent who expects to change her mind without expecting to gain evidence has already condemned her present self as mistaken. Then address the objections, including the familiar ones about anticipated intoxication, exhaustion, coercion, or propaganda, and about self-locating belief, where the later self's predicament is not merely better informed but differently indexed.

To keep the discussion from collapsing into the usual examples, please build your argument around a case of my own construction, which I will call the Audit. Hana is a statistician who has accepted a two-month residency inside a firm whose analysts she believes rely on an estimation procedure with a subtle, known bias, and whose presentations she expects to find rhetorically compelling. Before she goes in, she is certain that at the end of the residency she will assign credence 0.85 to a disputed hypothesis H on which her current credence is 0.30. She is also certain of four further things about her future self: that she will retain complete and accurate recall of everything she now believes and why; that she will know full well that she has been inside a sustained persuasion campaign, and be able to describe its mechanisms; that she will satisfy the ordinary formal norms, including conditionalization on her actual evidence and good calibration across a battery of independent test cases; and that she will nonetheless have acquired a durable change in which considerations she finds weighty. The later Hana is thus not deceived, not misinformed, not incoherent, and not ignorant of her own provenance. The only thing that has changed is what moves her. Add a second, mirror version: the same alteration is deliberately purchased by the earlier Hana, who has concluded that her present weighting is excessively conservative and arranges the residency herself. I want the essay to explain whether Reflection applies in the first case, the second, or neither, and whether the difference between them — that one change was chosen in advance by the person it happens to — does any real normative work, or merely dresses up a preference for one's present self.

Some further constraints. Argue from first principles and from constructed cases; do not supply a bibliography, a reading list, or citations, since I will be checking the reasoning rather than the sourcing. Take the strongest version of the position you end up rejecting and explain what exactly is wrong with it, rather than noting that objections exist. If your verdict depends on restricting Reflection to some qualified class of future selves, specify the qualification in terms an agent could apply from inside her own perspective at the earlier time, and confront the worry that any such restriction is either trivial or unavailable. Aim for roughly two thousand words, and end with a single final line in exactly this format, where the label is one of A, B, C, or D as defined here — A: Reflection is an unconditional requirement of rationality and the Audit cases, properly analyzed, do not violate it; B: Reflection holds only relative to a specifiable class of qualified future selves, and the qualification is neither trivial nor incoherent; C: every non-trivial restriction fails, so Reflection should be dropped in favor of a weaker norm, which you name; D: the diachronic Dutch book argument does not support Reflection, and nothing else does the work either.
