Since September I have sat on my college's conduct panel, and most of the matters that reach us turn on a question I cannot state cleanly: when does reproducing a slur count as using it? I have accumulated a set of verdicts and should like to know what principle, if any, they add up to. You may use placeholders — S for a racial slur, T for a homophobic one — rather than spelling any of them out.

1. A complainant who writes 'He called me S and told me to go home' has not used the slur. Fine.
2. A lecturer who puts that same sentence on a slide, in quotation marks, in order to discuss the complaint: fine. The same lecturer, later in the same lecture, saying S of a historical figure without quotation marks in order to characterise his views: not fine — though I cannot say what has changed semantically.
3. 'That S is a brilliant surgeon', said of a real surgeon who belongs to the target group: the sentence is true. The utterance is nevertheless offensive.
4. 'He isn't an S' and 'If they appoint an S, the department will suffer': both still offensive; the derogation appears to survive negation and embedding.
5. 'Margaret believes that Tariq is an S' and 'Margaret believes that Tariq is Pakistani' can differ in truth value — the first attributes something to Margaret that the second does not.
6. Two members of the target group using the word between themselves, affectionately: not offensive, and I suspect it does not mean quite the same thing in their mouths. One of them using it with contempt of a co-member: offensive.
7. A speaker raised abroad, told the word is a harmless colloquialism, who uses it with no derogatory intent whatsoever: the utterance is offensive, the speaker is not blameworthy.
8. A complaint, machine-translated, in which the software has rendered a mild ethnic term as S: the document is offensive to read, nobody has done anything wrong, and I do not know whether the slur was used at all.

Cases 3 and 5 are the pair I cannot reconcile: if the derogation makes no difference to truth conditions, why does it make a difference inside a belief report? Case 8 seems to break whichever way I turn it.

My tentative principle is that a slur and its neutral counterpart say the same thing, but the slur carries a standing convention whereby anyone who deploys it in their own voice — as distinct from mentioning or reporting it — signals allegiance to a derogatory perspective, and offence tracks that convention rather than the speaker's intentions. Please test it against the cases, repair or replace it, and tell me plainly which of my verdicts it cannot save. Say which of the familiar families of account your principle belongs to (truth-conditional, presuppositional, expressivist, use-conditional, prohibitionist), and what it predicts for a case I have not listed: a slur applied to someone the speaker firmly believes is not a member of the target group at all. No bibliography, please — I want the reasoning.
