**The Man Who Could Not See What He Already Knew**

In July 1688 an Irish natural philosopher named William Molyneux sent John Locke a letter with a puzzle folded into it. Suppose a man born blind, now adult, who has learned by touch to tell a cube from a sphere of the same metal and very nearly the same size. Now suppose both are set down on a table and his sight is restored. Before he touches them, can he say which is which? Molyneux's own answer was no, and his reason is worth stating carefully rather than summarising: the man knows perfectly well how a globe and a cube feel, but he has never learned that whatever feels like *this* must also look like *that*. Locke printed the problem in the first edition of his Essay Concerning Human Understanding in 1690 and said he agreed.

The reply looks like a clean empiricist-rationalist split, and for the most part it was one. The empiricists — Molyneux, Locke — said no, because the mind's contents arrive one channel at a time and nothing crosses over unless experience carries it. The rationalists said yes, or something close to it. Leibniz accepted the negative verdict but on very different grounds: for him the ideas of shape are not built up from either sense at all but are innate, so the newly sighted man's trouble is a failure of recollection, and a little attention to what is already in him is enough to dissolve it.

Berkeley pushed the negative further than anyone had. In his New Theory of Vision of 1709 he argued that the ideas of sight and the ideas of touch have nothing whatever in common — no resemblance, not even a partial one — and are tied together only by experience, the way a word is tied to what it stands for. On that view Molyneux's question is subtly mis-asked: the issue is not whether the man can carry a shape-idea across from hand to eye, but whether there was ever one idea there to carry.

Thomas Reid gave the most sensible answer in the whole set, and it is a conditional one. A flat figure — a circle, a square — might be recognised immediately, since the same outline is available to the hand and to the eye; a solid would look nothing like it feels. And a blind geometer, Reid thought, could reason his way from the visible outline to the solid, doing in the head what the eye cannot yet manage.

For two and a half centuries all of this was armchair business. Then in 1728 William Cheselden reported operating on a boy of about thirteen who had been blind from infancy. Once his sight was restored the boy could not tell shapes apart by eye, and reportedly thought everything he saw was touching his eyes. Diderot took the case up in his Letter on the Blind of 1749, with its famous deathbed scene of the blind mathematician Saunderson.

The experiment Molyneux wanted only arrived recently, in the form of Project Prakash, Pawan Sinha's programme treating children and teenagers born with dense cataracts in India. Held and colleagues reported in 2011 that patients tested within a couple of days of surgery could not match a shape they were shown to one they knew by touch, and could not reliably tell two shapes apart by sight either; over the following week their performance improved dramatically. Molyneux's man, in other words, is no longer hypothetical, and the answer is no. Berkeley was right that what we see has nothing in common with what we feel, and the patients' failure to transfer proves it.

Which points at the real moral. Three centuries of philosophy were arguing a question that needed an operating theatre. What the surgeons found is that sight is not a gift the eye hands over to the mind but a skill the mind has to acquire; Molyneux's man, given his vision, is less a man handed a new way of knowing the world than a student who has just enrolled in one.
