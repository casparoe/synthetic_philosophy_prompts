A friend and I spent most of last night on Confucian role ethics, and neither of us is satisfied with where we landed. I have reconstructed his case below in the cleanest form I can manage, and I would like an outside diagnosis.

His target is the Ames–Rosemont program, on which what I owe anyone is fixed entirely by the concrete roles I stand in to that person — son, elder brother, minister, friend — and there is no role-independent moral standpoint from which those roles could be assessed or ranked against one another. He concedes the descriptive case for partiality; his claim is that role ethics cannot be a genuine alternative to impartialist ethics. The argument, as I have set it out:

(1) On role ethics, every obligation is the obligation of some role, and a role has no standing outside itself.
(2) If obligations have no standing outside the roles that generate them, nothing internal to the theory can rank one role's demands against another's when they conflict.
(3) The test case he draws from the Mencius: Shun's father has killed a man, and the judge Gao Yao, doing precisely what his own role requires, intends to arrest him; Shun, as emperor, could forbid it, and as son cannot permit it. In the text Shun settles the matter by treating the empire as a worn-out sandal, abdicating, and carrying his father away to the coast.
(4) That settlement ranks the role of son above the role of ruler, and by (2) the theory cannot say why; from inside the theory the ranking is a brute preference.
(5) So either (a) the ranking is fixed by something outside the roles — a view about what makes a human life go well, or about which relations are constitutive of personhood — or (b) it is arbitrary.
(6) If (a), role ethics is not a rival to impartialism but a heuristic for applying one: the work is done by the external standard, and the vocabulary of roles is decoration.
(7) If (b), role ethics is committed to the principle that whatever social position one occupies generates whatever obligations it carries, with no external check. That principle licenses the intolerable: one who occupies the role of hired killer owes the employer the killing; a magistrate who happens to be the defendant's brother owes him the acquittal; and since nothing outside the roles can constrain which roles one takes up, one may freely acquire a role and thereby acquire a binding obligation to do what it demands.
(8) But (7) is intolerable: it makes morality a matter of which hats one has happened to pick up.
(9) Therefore (b) is false; therefore (a); therefore role ethics, on pain of corruption, collapses into an impartialist theory dressed in Confucian vocabulary.

Two things trouble me, and I cannot tell whether they are the same trouble. First, (7) seems to prove too much. Run it against a defense lawyer's duty of zealous advocacy, or a parent's refusal to weigh her own child impartially against a stranger's, and you get the same verdict — yet those cases strike me as unobjectionable, indeed as the ones role ethics was built to describe. So whatever is wrong with the principle in (7) is wrong with something I want to keep. Second, I suspect (4) is unfair to the passage it invokes: Shun does not use the throne to save his father, he surrenders the throne, which looks less like a ranking than a refusal to hold two incompatible roles at once. If the theory has a general answer of that shape, then (2) is false and the dilemma never engages. But I cannot see how such an answer would be anything more than an ad hoc patch, and a patch seems to concede (5a) anyway.

Which step should I reject, and what does rejecting it cost? I want to know whether the argument is invalid — and if so, exactly where the inference slips, since each step reads as plausible to me in isolation — or valid, with a premise I ought to abandon. If it is valid, tell me which premise I can abandon most cheaply and what theoretical work is lost by abandoning it, and say how the Confucian, the impartialist, and anyone who wants to keep ordinary professional and parental partiality each fare under the repair you recommend. I would much rather keep (1) and (8); I care comparatively little about (6).
