I write about science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and I am reporting a feature on the vocabulary we use to say what a system believes — a vocabulary inherited from a formal tradition that modeled believers as able to see every consequence of what they believe, and now routinely applied to crows, to chess programs, to language models, and to people who demonstrably cannot do that. Before I put questions to the researchers involved, I want to know what the target of all this work actually is. So: write out the desiderata for a theory of belief ascription for agents with finite computational resources — the specification a candidate account has to meet in order to count as solving the problem of logical omniscience. I do not want a survey of the literature or a history of how the problem came to be noticed.

Fix the purpose as follows. I want a theory good enough to license the way scientists and science writers actually talk: one vocabulary for attributing belief across species and substrates, with no hidden assumption of unlimited reasoning power smuggled into it. Adequacy should be judged against that job, not against the internal elegance of a formal system.

For every requirement, give me the case or consideration that generates it — a short vignette concrete enough that I could print it — together with what goes wrong for a theory that ignores it. Then say whether the requirement is genuinely non-negotiable or whether it is a demand belonging to one research program that a rival could reasonably refuse. I suspect a fair number of the standard demands are of the second kind, and sorting the two apart is half of what I want from you. If a two-column tally serves for the non-negotiable/negotiable marking, use one; the vignettes themselves should be prose.

At minimum, the list should be answerable to these cases:

1. The long list. An agent explicitly believes each of a thousand separate claims and is never asked about their conjunction. A theory that denies closure under conjunction must still explain why such an agent is not entitled to be surprised when the conjunction is put to it.
2. The clock. The same agent, given an hour and paper and pencil, comes to believe a consequence it did not believe before. Ascription appears to shift with the time budget available. Should belief be indexed to resources and deadlines, or should the theory explain away the appearance of a change?
3. The factorization. An agent that accepts that a certain very large number is the product of exactly two primes, and has no idea which two. Is it thereby committed to believing every truth about that factorization — and, symmetrically, every falsehood about it?
4. The contradictory pair. An agent that would sincerely assent to a claim and to its negation, having never brought the two together. The theory has to make room for this without letting ascription explode: it must not follow that such an agent believes everything.
5. The informative proof. Any account on which the agent already believed all consequences of its beliefs owes an explanation of why proofs feel like discoveries; any account on which they become new beliefs owes an explanation of what changed, since no information arrived from outside.
6. The two names. An agent that will assent to a claim stated under one name for a thing and dissent from the same claim stated under another name for the very same thing. What fixes the content ascribed, and why does substituting co-referential terms fail inside a report of what someone believes?
7. The prediction requirement. Ascription exists largely so that we can anticipate behavior. State how much closure a theory needs before ascribed beliefs support prediction at all, and what is surrendered by weakening closure to accommodate cases one through six.
8. The coherence requirement. If inconsistency and failure to draw obvious consequences are both permitted, what is left of rationality as a norm? A theory must not make every logically inept state of mind equally rational, and must not set the norm so high that no finite agent ever meets it.
9. The missing concept. An agent that lacks the vocabulary or concepts to entertain a proposition at all, as against one that could entertain it and simply has not done the work. Are these the same deficiency, or must the theory keep them apart?
10. The publicity requirement. An ascriber must be able to attribute beliefs without carrying out the agent's computations itself. If determining what an agent believes requires running the agent, is what remains still a theory of belief?
11. The graded version. Should the account be quantitative — confidence levels, or uncertainty distributed over situations that are not logically possible? If so, say what three-sevenths confidence that a large number is prime amounts to, given that the question is computable in principle and settled in fact.

Then the part I most want to see: argue, if you can, that some subset of these requirements is jointly unsatisfiable. For instance, whether the prediction requirement, the tolerance of outright contradictory belief, and a single non-time-indexed notion of belief can all be had at once — or whether accommodating the informative proof forces a semantics so fine-grained that the publicity requirement is lost. If the list cannot be met, that is itself the finding, and it is the paragraph I would lead the piece with. Be explicit about which of the eleven you would give up first, and why that is the cheapest sacrifice rather than a concession that quietly undoes the whole project.

Alongside that, identify the single requirement that eliminates the most candidates: the demand which, taken seriously, does the greatest amount of exclusionary work against otherwise attractive theories.

Finally, in a few hundred words and without a single citation, say how the broad families of solution fare against your list. Describe them functionally rather than by name: the accounts that keep a possible-worlds semantics but populate it with worlds that are not logically possible; those that keep ordinary worlds but relativize belief to what an agent is aware of or can express; those that abandon the worlds altogether in favor of a stock of accepted sentences plus rules for extending it; and those that treat belief attribution not as a semantic relation at all but as an interpretive practice of making sense of behavior. Names and references I will get from the researchers; what I need from you is which of them survives your list, and at what cost.
