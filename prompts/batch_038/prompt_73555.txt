I've just been seated on my museum's attributions and cataloguing standards panel, and my first assignment is to draft the general standard that will settle a case the panel has been deadlocked on for two years: a sixteenth-century oak panel, acquired in 2009 on the recommendation of the curator who trained me and catalogued since 1911 as from the workshop of a major Antwerp master, which half the panel now wants reduced to "circle of." The disagreement underneath isn't really about this painting, and the memo shouldn't pretend otherwise.

One camp holds that an attribution is warranted only if the chain of reasons terminates in evidence contemporaneous with the making of the work — a payment record, an inventory, a commission — and that everything downstream of that is inference dressed up as documentation. A second camp holds that nothing is basic in this way: the attribution stands or falls on how well connoisseurship, dendrochronology, pigment analysis, provenance, and the body of securely attributed works explain one another, so that no single item carries the weight. A third and smaller camp thinks both demands are misconceived, because the chain of warrants can always be extended one more link and never has to end; what matters is only whether it keeps surviving further questioning.

Draft the memo I'll circulate before the panel votes — two pages at most. It should recommend one of these as the standard we adopt, state it as criteria a curator could actually apply to a new acquisition, and answer the strongest thing the losing camps can say. That includes the awkward fact that the "secure" attributions against which our eye was calibrated were themselves fixed by exactly the kind of reasoning the first camp wants to exclude, and that my own teacher's judgment is one of the links in the chain this painting depends on.
