I help maintain a coding manual for a catastrophic-risk project that classifies historical famines as either political crimes or natural disasters with political aggravation, and I'm trying to figure out what rule our lead editor is actually running, as opposed to the one he says he's running. His calls so far: Bengal 1943 is a political crime, on the grounds that aggregate food availability did not fall dramatically and the deaths trace to wartime inflation, the denial policies, and the refusal to divert shipping; the Irish famine of the 1840s is a political crime but the weakest instance in the set, since the blight was real and exogenous and the culpable element was continued export from a starving country plus a relief ideology that treated assistance as optional; the Great Leap Forward famine is a political crime in the strongest sense, because the channels that would have corrected procurement were destroyed before the harvest failed; a severe regional drought famine in a small, poor, landlocked economy with no reserves and no transport links is coded as a natural disaster, explicitly on the grounds that the authorities could not have done otherwise; and a siege famine is coded in its own category, as a weapon rather than a policy failure.

The manual now needs a worked example for a case that does not fit his pattern, and I want to predict his call before he makes it. The case: a coastal republic with regular competitive elections, an independent press, and no censorship. In year one a drought cuts the staple harvest in two northern provinces by roughly a third — a genuine availability shortfall, confirmed by the country's own statistical office. The central government holds grain reserves sufficient to cover the gap and has the rail and port capacity to move it, and its early-warning system flags the shortfall within weeks. It declines to release the reserves or invoke emergency procurement, because cancellation penalties on a standing export contract exceed the cost of relief and because releasing reserves would raise urban food prices in an election year. It seizes nothing, blockades nothing, alters no property rights or price rules, and no official intends anyone to starve. The press reports the shortage continuously from month three; the opposition campaigns on it; the government loses the following year's election partly over it, and its successor releases the reserves within weeks of taking office. Excess mortality is around two hundred thousand, concentrated among landless laborers whose real wages collapsed.

Which of the candidate rules his precedents are consistent with — a counterfactual rule keyed to whether availability actually declined, an entitlement rule keyed to whether the state restructured anyone's command over food, a feedback rule keyed to whether the regime suppressed the information and incentives that would have forced a response, an intent rule, or a capacity-and-foreseeability rule under which the only exculpating fact is inability — does he come down on for this case, and where do those rules disagree with one another here? Note that at least one of them implies this case should not have been possible at all, and say what that costs him. Then tell me which call would cohere best with the precedents as a set, which single precedent is the closest analog, and what principle the manual would be silently adopting under each choice. Finally, name the one further case — I suspect a matched pair differing in a single variable — that would most sharply separate the surviving rules.
