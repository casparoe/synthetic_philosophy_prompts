Ok so this has been bugging me for about a year and nobody I've asked will answer it straight.

Two things I deal with regularly.

One: I own a house and a small landscaping business, so I'm on the phone with my insurance agent a couple times a year. My homeowner's policy pays up to roughly $400k on the house and I carry a $2M umbrella for liability. A while back I asked him, half joking, what it'd cost to get $500 million of coverage — like, wildfire takes out the whole neighborhood and everybody sues me. He didn't even try to price it. He said no company writes that, and it wasn't a "the premium would be enormous" answer, it was a "that product does not exist" answer.

That's the part I cant get past. My napkin math: if the chance of a $500M loss is one in a million, the fair price is $500. I'd happily pay $5,000 for that policy. And if he thinks my probability is wildly too high, he should quote me $50,000, or $500,000 — some number at all. Refusing to name *any* number means we're not actually disagreeing about the odds. It's like the question stops making sense past a certain size of payout, and nobody in the industry will say out loud why.

Two: meanwhile the state lottery here has no cap on its jackpot. It rolls over and over until somebody hits it, and it's sat above a billion dollars for weeks at a time. A $2 ticket at something like 1 in 300 million for a $1.4 billion prize is, by the exact same napkin math, worth more than $2. Everybody knows this, the lottery advertises the giant number in huge font, and nobody at the lottery commission seems even slightly worried that the prize has gotten too big to pay out.

So one institution will hand out a payoff with no ceiling whatsoever and treats a bigger number as a selling point. The other won't sell you a payoff with no ceiling at any price, ever, and treats a bigger number as a catagory error. Same arithmetic, opposite behavior.

What I want to know is what each of these practices assumes about how you're supposed to weigh money against unlikely events. Lay it out like you're reconstructing two small theories — for each practice, what has to be true for it to make sense, what rival way of thinking it rules out, and whether it's actually *committed* to that theory or just happens to fit it (I suspect several different stories could license the same refusal, and I want to know which parts are load-bearing and which are just decoration). Then tell me where the real asymmetry comes from. My hunch is it's something about who's holding the risk and what happens to them if the big number actually lands, but I could be totally wrong and it might just be regulation or accounting or something boring.

Also: is either one flat-out inconsistent with itself? My insurer will sell me $2M and $5M and $10M of umbrella if I ask, so there's obviously some point where they stop — is there a principled place for that line, or is it arbitrary with a spreadsheet wrapped around it? And on the lottery side, if the jackpot can genuinely drift into positive expected value for the ticket buyer, why not cap it, and does it matter that they'd pay it as 30 yearly installments rather than a lump sum?

Last thing — if you replaced whatever the insurance side is assuming with straight expected-value arithmetic, what would actually have to change in how they run the business? Not in principle. What products exist, what they charge, what they hold in reserve, who's allowed to write the top layer.

Please put the bottom line on the very last line in exactly this form:

Verdict: [SAME | DIFFERENT | INCOHERENT] — then ten words or less on what it comes down to.

(SAME = both practices make sense under one single theory of weighing money against risk; DIFFERENT = they only make sense under two genuinely different theories; INCOHERENT = at least one of them doesn't hang together even on its own terms.)
