I run a little monthly discussion group that's basically an in-person successor to lurking on the sequences for a decade — seven of us, we meet in the back room of a bakery (unrelated to the bakery in the case, which was an accident of my own drafting that I regretted immediately). Last week's topic was punishment theory, retributivism vs. consequentialism, and two of the regulars, Dev and Marisol, went at it for something like fifty minutes while the rest of us ate cookies and I took notes on my phone. Dev is a data engineer who got into EA hard around 2014 and then out of it; Marisol does public-interest housing law. Neither has read much actual philosophy of punishment, which I think is to my advantage here — I'd rather you judge the arguments as they were actually made than tell me whose position happens to match which published view, and please don't give me a reading list at the end, half of us can't look anything up anyway.

The case I wrote up for the group: a man, now 52, committed an armed robbery at 21 that left a shopkeeper permanently disabled. He was never caught. He built a legitimate life, runs a small bakery with eleven employees, and for the last ten years has been quietly funneling money to the shopkeeper's physical therapy through an anonymous fund. A cold-case print match just identified him. Separately — and this is the detail that blew the session up — he has early-onset dementia, diagnosed two years ago, moderate and progressing; on bad days he doesn't know his own employees' names.

Dev opens by saying he deserves punishment, full stop, and that desert is a function of what he did rather than of what has happened since, because otherwise whether this man is punished turns on how many people he happens to employ and how well he keeps his charity books — the same act becomes punishable or not depending on collateral facts that have nothing to do with the wrong. He says consequentialists always get pushed into explaining why they wouldn't punish the innocent, and that every patch they apply (rules, side-constraints, integrity of the practice) is just a desert-shaped constraint wearing a utility hat. Marisol says fine, but "desert" is doing no work in his sentence: tell me what punishing a demented 52-year-old gets you. It doesn't deter, because nobody robbing a shop at 21 is pricing in their odds of arrest at 52, and the general signal is so smeared across thirty years that it isn't even measurable. It doesn't incapacitate. It doesn't rehabilitate — there's nothing there to rehabilitate anymore. So what's left is that the ledger in *your* head needs a red entry, and the ledger isn't his. Dev says that's exactly the slide he's warning about: she's reduced "wrong" to "produces bad future states," noticed there aren't any, and concluded nothing is owed — but on her view the shopkeeper's disability counts for less now than it did in the nineties, because the man who caused it has since become useful; go say that to the shopkeeper. Marisol says those are two different claims and he keeps sliding between them — she's not saying the wrong got smaller or got undone, she's saying a wrong doesn't automatically generate a duty on the state to inflict suffering three decades on, and moreover the ten years of anonymous payments to the actual victim is, on any account where what matters is the harm getting addressed rather than suffering getting manufactured, the best available outcome, and it already happened. Dev says then she should be happy with the state doing nothing at all and just noting it, which she can't be, because she's conceded the retributivist's actual point: the response has to be a response *to the wrong*, directed at the wrongdoer, authorized by somebody other than him. Charity that the offender picked himself, on his own schedule, in an amount of his own choosing, isn't that; if he'd paid the same money without ever being identified we'd call it decent but we wouldn't call it owed. Marisol says agreed, which is why she'd back a public conviction — finding of guilt on the record, victim's family in the room, no custody or a nominal suspended sentence. She says Dev's own best story about why punishment matters is the communicative one, the state saying "this was wrong and we mean it," and a conviction says exactly that, whereas two years in a locked dementia ward says something else entirely, namely that we are punishing a man who on a bad day can't tell you why he's in a bed with a locked door. And there's the part she thinks breaks his view from the inside: if punishment is communication of censure, it has to be *received*, and he can't receive it, so the mechanism her opponent wants has no purchase here and what remains is an appetite for a red entry. Dev's reply: proportionality isn't optional garnish on the communication, because censure that isn't calibrated to the gravity of the offense lies — a suspended sentence for a robbery that destroyed a man's body tells the victim this was a small thing, and a lie is worse than silence; and he invokes that Kant example about the island society voting to dissolve itself, where even then the last murderer in the prison has to be executed first so the people don't carry the bloodguilt, which he says shows the intuition isn't about effects at all. On the dementia he says she's proving too much: by her reasoning nobody whose comprehension has slipped can be prosecuted, yet we do try people with impaired competence, we just require that they be competent to assist in their own defense — competence is a procedural safeguard, not a ceiling on desert. And if she wants to say the person now isn't the person who did it, she owes him an account of why the contracts he signed at 21 still bind, why the eleven employees still draw wages from him, why his assets can still be seized for the shopkeeper's claim: you can't dissolve the person for the criminal law and keep him intact for everything else. Marisol says she isn't dissolving him, she's saying desert tracks the agent and not the act, and this one has changed in a way that's not merely "he's nicer now" — and his competence point cuts against him, since if we won't *try* someone who can't follow the proceedings, a fortiori we shouldn't *sentence* someone who can't understand the sentence, when the entire justification on offer is that the sentence is communicating something to him. Then she says he never answered the deterrence question and asks him for one number: what's the marginal deterrent effect of prosecuting 52-year-olds for crimes committed at 21, versus a public conviction plus a nominal sentence, and if the honest answer is "about the same," then he's agreed with her and simply wants the suffering for its own sake. He said she was asking him to make up a number to win an argument. That's where it ended and we went to get more coffee.

So: referee it. Go through the exchanges in order and tell me for each one who's actually right and by how much, including the ones where you think both landed something and the ones where someone is scoring a cheap point that just sounds good in a bakery. I especially want your read on the dementia detail — Marisol thinks it's fatal to Dev and Dev thinks it's beside the point, and I suspect it's doing something more interesting to both of them than either admits. Also: is the anonymous-payments move doing the work Marisol thinks it is, and did Dev's "then you should be happy with the state doing nothing" reply actually refute it or just make it awkward? And when he asks her to reconcile dissolving the person for criminal purposes with keeping him for contract purposes, is that a real dilemma or is there an obvious split she failed to articulate? Finally, tell me the strongest move neither of them made — the one that would have changed where the argument ended.
