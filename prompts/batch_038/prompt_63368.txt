I run a small regional foundation — think four staff, about $2.5M a year out the door — and I read 60 to 80 applications a cycle, almost all of them from small nonprofits. Lately I keep noticing that the things that look like serious commitment on paper can be pure waste: a group asking us for money to build a $1.4M facility when there are maybe nine kids a week using the program, a matching-fund pledge that's obviously been scraped together from one friendly donor, a founder personally cosigning a loan. Meanwhile the plain-looking applications from groups that have actually been doing the work for years get skimmed past. Somebody told me there's a whole theory about this — signals being credible only because they're expensive to fake — and it sounded like exactly what I need, but I couldn't turn it into anything I could actually use at a desk.

So: write me the procedure. I want the questions I ask of a proposal, in the order I ask them, and for each one what counts as a good enough answer, and which answers knock the proposal out entirely. I need it to be honest about which questions are things I can actually check with documents and which are me guessing, and to say plainly where the call leaves me and goes to the committee because it's about values, not facts. And it has to not just punish poor organizations for being poor — if a scrappy group genuinely can't afford a big match, tell me what else I should be looking at instead.

Then run it on three applications:

1. A youth group asking for $150K toward a $1.4M capital campaign for a new building, with a $900K match already raised, mostly from one donor who happens to sit on their board.
2. A brand-new organization, two years old, run out of a church basement, offering a 60% cash match they say will come from a fundraising gala they've never held.
3. A thirty-year-old food pantry asking for $40K in general operating support, no match offered, no new programs promised, and a two-page application.

I know that last one probably comes out badly under a procedure like this, and if it does I want you to say so straight and then tell me what it would take to overrule the procedure — I'd rather know that than have the tool quietly decide for me.

Please write in American English. Give me the whole thing as one JSON object, valid and parseable, with exactly these fields:

- "theory_in_plain_language": a string, five sentences max, on what the signaling idea actually claims and where it stops being useful for someone reading grant files.
- "procedure": an array of objects, one per question, in the order I ask them, each with "step" (integer), "question", "sufficient_answer" (what would satisfy it), "kills_it_if" (the answer that eliminates the proposal, or null), "type" ("checkable_fact" or "judgment_call"), "evidence_i_ask_for" (documents or references, or null), and "who_decides" ("me" or "committee").
- "substitute_signals": an array of objects with "signal" and "why_it_works_when_money_doesnt" — the things to weigh for applicants who can't afford expensive commitments.
- "applications": an array of three objects, one per case above, each with "case", "step_by_step" (array of objects: "step", "finding", "pass_or_fail" as "pass" / "fail" / "unclear"), "verdict" ("fund", "fund_with_conditions", "decline", or "send_to_committee"), "what_i_probably_wont_like", and "confidence" ("high" / "medium" / "low").
- "limits": an array of objects with "failure_mode" and "how_to_catch_it" — the ways this procedure will systematically get things wrong.
