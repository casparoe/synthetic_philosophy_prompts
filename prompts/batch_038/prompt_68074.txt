I'm 71, retired three years ago after forty years in forest ecology — mostly long-term plots, counting what persists and what doesn't. Since then I've been reading philosophy on my own, badly and enthusiatically, and I've drifted toward the stuff about death, on the grounds that you ought to have some view about it by this point. I've drafted a short essay for one of those general sites that publishes amateurs. It's not for a journal and no one is going to cite it. I'd like real comments on it, not encouragement.

Below is the draft, complete except that the end peters out and I don't know whether to keep it. After it is a list of what I actually want from you, and at the bottom, what I don't want.

---

**Death, extinction debt, and the wrong end of a life**

**1.** Let me grant Epicurus nearly everything. His case as I read it: good and evil consist in sensation; death is the privation of sensation; therefore death is nothing to us, because when we are, death is not, and when death is, we are not. The fear is a mistake about who will be around to suffer. Lucretius adds the symmetry: the time before our birth mirrors the time after our death, neither of which troubles us in the slightest, so anyone who grieves the second while shrugging at the first hasn't thought the matter through, he's only turned around to face it.

I think the first argument is close to sound and the second is a trick, and I want to build something in the gap between them.

**2.** The timing objection is real and I don't propose to dodge it. Nothing happens to me once I'm dead, there being no me for it to happen to. Nagel's answer, which I take to be the standard one now, is that harms needn't sit where they're felt: the person harmed is the ante-mortem person, and the harm is the loss of the goods the future would otherwise have held. Fine. But look at the shape this gives a life — a ledger. Entries already made stay made; they're banked and settled. Death's damage consists entirely of missing entries at the end. The past is immune.

**3.** I don't think that's how value works, and I don't think it's how my own field treats living things. In conservation ecology there's a thing called an extinction debt. Clear most of a forest and the species that depend on it don't disappear on schedule. They hang on for decades — feeding, breeding, holding territories, looking in every way like a population — while the numbers are already committed to a trajectory ending in local extinction. The debt is contracted at the moment of clearing and paid much later. Whether the birds in that wood in 1995 were a viable population or a slow-motion wreck is not settled by what they were doing in 1995. It's settled by 2060.

I know what's wrong with pushing that straight across. A debt is the future fixing the present; what I want to say about death is stronger, it's the ending fixing the past. So let me say it and take the hit.

**4.** Two people each have twenty years of marriage. In one the spouse was faithful and kind throughout, and then dies. In the other the spouse was carrying on the entire time, carefully, and the survivor learns of it a year after the funeral. Stipulate that the recorded content of the twenty years is identical: same meals, same conversations, same anniversaries, same apparent affection. I think nearly everyone will say the second marriage was worse than the first — not that the discovery is an extra harm stacked on an identical past, but that the past itself was a different thing. Something at the end reached backwards.

Or from my side of the fence: a patient who felt splendid in 2018 and is found in 2019 to have had something growing for four years. We say the illness was already there, and its being there was a fact about those years, not merely a fact about our ignorance of them.

I want to say death works like this. Not that dying changes what happened. Rather that what a life's ending is like is partly constitutive of what the earlier stretch of it was worth. A life that breaks off mid-project, mid-sentence, mid-something-that-was-gathering, is not the same object as that life with the completion deleted. It's worse in its earlier parts, not merely shorter.

**5.** That's bad news for Epicurus, though not the news he usually gets. If part of the harm of death is delivered to the years when I was alive, well, and there to be harmed, the timing objection has nothing to grip. There is a subject. He just receives the blow late, from the direction of the future.

And here I think physics is on my side, which is why I won't grant Lucretius his symmetry. The two blanks around a life look alike only if you treat time as a corridor you can walk in both directions, and the fundamental laws very nearly let you. Nothing else in the world runs that way. Causation runs one way, records run one way, memory runs one way, and — the part that matters — I can act toward the later blank and not the earlier one. There's nothing I can do about 1840. There's a great deal I can do about 2055, and what I do fixes which kind of thing 2055 turns out to be, and so, on my account, what sort of thing the years before it were. The blanks aren't mirrors. One of them is downstream of my choices.

**6.** Something follows about duration that I'm not sure I like. If endings settle earlier value, then adding years isn't automatically adding good, and isn't automatically neutral either: a long bad decade can subtract from the good decades before it, the way the discovery subtracts from the marriage. So the target isn't length but a certain kind of closing. Which makes the usual objection to immortality — Williams' point that an endless life would run out of reasons to keep going — look like a side issue. On my view an immortal life might be perfectly happy forever and never finish, and never be the sort of thing that has a shape, and I'm not certain that's a life I'd want.

**7.** What I'm not claiming: that the past changes. Events stay put. I'm claiming that what those events amounted to isn't settled at the time they happen, and that the Epicurean picture — no subject, no sensation, no harm — quietly assumes it is.

What I want from you:

1. Am I fair to Epicurus and Lucretius in section 1? Where does a classicist wince? Everything there is paraphrase and I know paraphrase is where straw men get built.

2. Check me on Nagel. I've written as though the ledger picture is his and the retroactive idea is mine, and I have a suspicion he already has examples — the one about the man reduced to a happy idiocy, I think, and something about a betrayal the victim never learns of — that land much closer to my section 4 than I'd like. Tell me whether my move is genuinely distinguishable from the ordinary deprivation reply, or whether I've spent two thousand words rediscovering it. If it's the second, say so in your first sentence and then explain what's left.

3. Is there a name for the territory in section 4? I don't want references — don't give me authors, titles, or years, I can't check any of them and I won't use them. But if there's a standard label for the idea that a harm can be inflicted backwards onto a stretch of life already lived, give me the label so I can stop announcing it as a discovery.

4. Section 5 is the one I'm fondest of and I suspect it's the worst. Does the time-symmetry paragraph carry any weight, or is it physicist-shaped decoration that will read as embarrassing to people who know the argument? I'd rather cut it than defend it. Tell me which.

5. Give me the strongest objection to section 4 from inside the deprivation camp — one that doesn't depend on Epicurus winning. My own worry is double counting: if the lost future goods are already the whole harm, what is the retroactive bit doing that isn't restating the same loss with more drama? Either dissolve that or concede it.

6. Section 6's bullet. On my view, is dying at 45 mid-stride actually better than dying at 88 after a wretched decade? I don't want to be committed to that and I can't see the exit. Show me one, or tell me there isn't one.

7. Register and structure. It's for general readers. Is the forest material load-bearing or a hobbyhorse? Point at any sentence where I sound like a man showing off his second career. And if the piece should just stop at section 5, say so.

What I don't want: praise, and a reading list. I've been a scientist long enough to know that a referee who opens with compliments hasn't read the thing.
