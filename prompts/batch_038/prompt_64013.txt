I write about science and ideas for a general-audience magazine, and I am currently reporting a piece on the politics of naming atrocities — in particular, on how often "genocide" is invoked in public argument as though its meaning had been fixed once and for all by the man who coined it. Before I characterize Raphaël Lemkin's own usage, I want to be confident that I can read his founding passage correctly rather than paraphrasing the received summary of it. It comes from the chapter headed "Genocide" in *Axis Rule in Occupied Europe* (Carnegie Endowment for International Peace, 1944). I quote the parts I am working from below; footnote markers and page breaks are omitted.

> Generally speaking, genocide does not necessarily mean the immediate destruction of a nation, except when accomplished by mass killings of all members of a nation. It is intended rather to signify a coordinated plan of different actions aiming at the destruction of essential foundations of the life of national groups, with the aim of annihilating the groups themselves. The objectives of such a plan would be disintegration of the political and social institutions, of culture, language, national feelings, religion, and the economic existence of national groups, and the destruction of the personal security, liberty, health, dignity, and even the lives of the individuals belonging to such groups. Genocide is directed against the national group as an entity, and the actions involved are directed against individuals, not in their individual capacity, but as members of the national group.
>
> The following illustration will suffice. The confiscation of property of nationals of an occupied area on the ground that they have left the country may be considered simply as a deprivation of their individual property rights. However, if the confiscations are ordered against individuals solely because they are Poles, Jews, or Czechs, then the same confiscations tend in effect to weaken the national entities of which those persons are members. Genocide has two phases: one, destruction of the national pattern of the oppressed group: the other, the imposition of the national pattern of the oppressor. This imposition, in turn, may be made upon the oppressed population which is allowed to remain, or upon the territory alone, after removal of the population and the colonization of the area by the oppressor's own nationals. Denationalization was the word used in the past to describe the destruction of a national pattern. The author believes, however, that this word is inadequate because: (1) it does not connote the destruction of the biological structure; (2) in connoting the destruction of one national pattern, it does not connote the imposition of the national pattern of the oppressor; and (3) denationalization is used by some authors to mean only deprivation of citizenship.
>
> Genocide is the antithesis of the Rousseau-Portalis Doctrine, which may be regarded as implicit in the Hague Regulations. This doctrine holds that war is directed against sovereigns and armies, not against subjects and civilians. In its modern application in civilized society, the doctrine means that war is conducted against states and armed forces and not against populations.

Elsewhere in the same chapter, describing the reasoning of the German occupant, he writes: "The enemy nation within the control of Germany must be destroyed, disintegrated, or weakened in different degrees for decades to come." He explains the rejection of the older doctrine by saying that under National Socialism "the nation, not the state, is the predominant factor."

What I need is not background, context, or a reading list — I have those. I need help getting the internal logic of these paragraphs right, working from the wording above. Please answer the numbered questions in order, and where you think one of my candidate readings is simply wrong, say so directly rather than hedging between options. Where an answer depends on material outside the quoted text, flag that dependence explicitly so I can verify it myself.

1. The opening sentence resists me. I can get two parses and cannot decide between them: (a) the exception clause is concessive — the term does not require immediate destruction as a rule, but does cover immediate destruction in the special case where it takes the form of killing every member; or (b) the clause limits the scope of "necessarily," so that immediate destruction counts only when it is total. Which reading survives contact with the rest of the paragraph? And what does the choice imply about whether killing sits at the centre of Lemkin's concept or at one of its edges? A related worry: in that sentence the thing destroyed is "a nation," two sentences later it is "national groups," and later still it is "the national pattern." Is that a substantive drift in what he takes the destroyed object to be, or merely loose style I should not read heavily?

2. The confiscation illustration seems to distinguish the two cases by the agent's selection criterion — the same confiscations, but ordered "solely because they are Poles, Jews, or Czechs" — yet the conclusion he draws is stated in terms of effect: they "tend in effect to weaken the national entities." Where, on his account, does the morally relevant difference actually sit: in the criterion of selection, or in the aggregate outcome? These come apart, and I would like to know whether the passage gives me a way to tell which one he means. Someone could target a group by name and weaken nothing, or erode a group's foundations without ever selecting by name; does the illustration settle which of those is genocide?

3. "A coordinated plan." Is that phrase a conceptual condition (no plan, no genocide), an evidentiary condition (a plan is how one recognizes genocide after the fact), or a piece of description aimed at the Nazi occupation apparatus specifically? What becomes of a case in which the disintegration of a group's foundations is the cumulative product of many separate measures adopted by uncoordinated bodies, each pursuing local interests? Does the wording give me any purchase on whether Lemkin would still call that genocide, or does "coordinated" quietly import a single directing will?

4. "With the aim of annihilating the groups themselves." Is the aim attributed to the plan, to the individual actions, or to the actors, and must it be a purpose actually held, or is it enough that the actions tend that way? I ask because the reported rationale of the occupant is destruction, disintegration, "or weakening in different degrees for decades to come" — and weakening for decades is not annihilation. Does that rationale satisfy the definitional clause? If it does, the definition threatens to collapse into "any policy that damages a group"; if it does not, what in the wording lets Lemkin hold both sentences in the same chapter?

5. On the two phases: if the second can be discharged "upon the territory alone, after removal of the population and the colonization of the area," then the population has been displaced rather than annihilated. Is removal-plus-colonization still genocide on the definition's own terms? What is it about "the national pattern" that turns displacement into destruction — and does that make this fundamentally a concept of replacement rather than of extermination? I want to be very sure before I print something along those lines.

6. Of the three objections to "denationalization," the first — that it "does not connote the destruction of the biological structure" — is the one I understand least, and it is also the one he puts first. What does "biological structure" mean here, and why does it take precedence over the objection about the second phase? My working suspicion is that his categories do not map onto the later distinction between physical and cultural destruction: that undernourization, health, and dignity belong to the same explanatory machinery for him as language, religion, and national feeling. Is that supported by his own list of objectives, or am I reading back into 1944 a distinction he did not hold?

7. The Rousseau-Portalis Doctrine. Explain what is being invoked: what the doctrine asserts, who the two namesakes are, and in what sense it could be "implicit in the Hague Regulations." If you are not confident about the second namesake's contribution, say so and reconstruct the doctrine from Lemkin's own gloss rather than guessing at a source. Then tell me whether the antithesis claim is about the object of violence (populations rather than armies) or about which entity counts as the unit of moral concern. I see a difficulty: the doctrine governs the conduct of war, while everything in the definition appears capable of occurring in peacetime and by a state against its own nationals. Does the antithesis framing survive that? And does his parenthetical explanation — "the nation, not the state, is the predominant factor" — make the essence of the crime a substitution of one for the other? If it does, what does that cost for cases in which no such substitution is needed?

Finally, a check on a claim I have inherited. A legal historian remarked to me in passing that this 1944 passage is essentially the later international definition with the cultural items left in, and that the drafters simply trimmed it for enforceability. Please do not recite any treaty text at me. What I want is narrower: working only from the wording above, identify the features of Lemkin's formulation that a drafter aiming at enforceability would have had to abandon or transform, and say for each whether the loss is one of scope, of proof, or of moral emphasis. If any of my seven readings above is wrong, put that first.
