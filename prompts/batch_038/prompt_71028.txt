The sentence "a dominated strategy should never be played" has done three separate jobs in my presence this term, and I am no longer confident that it is one sentence.

I convene a fortnightly reading group on the foundations of strategic reasoning — mostly economists, a couple of philosophers — and one member has made a habit of ending any dispute over which strategies deserve analysis by declaring that a dominated strategy should never be played. It has settled matters for him on three occasions in ways I cannot reconstruct.

First. In a two-player game we were working through, he announced that one of a player's three options was dominated and could be discarded. I worked down the payoff table and could not find any *single* other option of that player's that did better against every choice open to the opponent; against each of the opponent's choices, at least one of the remaining options fell short. He said I was mistaken only because I was comparing options one at a time. I gather his claim was that some weighted combination of the others does the work, but I would like to know what the sentence was actually asserting at that moment, and what licenses a combination to stand in where no individual option could.

Second. On another occasion the option he wanted removed was not beaten outright by anything: some alternative was never worse than it and strictly better in at least one case, but tied with it in others. He removed it, and then removed a further option which, so far as I can tell, is beaten only once that first removal has already been granted. When I asked whether the second removal would still have gone through had we taken them in the opposite order, he said the order could not matter, because the principle was about dominance and not about sequence.

Third. Most recently he deployed it against a proposed equilibrium of a small game. One of the two players' equilibrium choices is of the merely-tied kind from the second episode, and he said that since a dominated strategy should never be played, the equilibrium could not be taken seriously and he would not bet on it being observed.

I am not after the standard procedure restated. What I want is the menu. Specifically:

(a) Lay out the distinct precise propositions that this one sentence can be used to carry, running from those that are true but empty to those that are substantive and possibly false.

(b) For each, fix the parameters that the bare sentence leaves open: whether the thing that does the beating must be a single strategy or may be a combination; whether "beats" means strictly better or merely never worse; whether the comparison runs over every combination of the others' choices, only those that remain after earlier removals, or only those the others could rationally make; whether it is payoffs or expected payoffs that are compared; and whether two players or more makes a difference.

(c) Give a verdict on each — platitude, substantive and correct, correct only under a restriction you should state, or incorrect — and for every reading that is incorrect or restricted, exhibit the smallest concrete game that shows it, with the payoffs written out in full.

(d) Say which readings would license each of my member's three moves, which would not, and what he would have to add to the sentence to make each of them legitimate.

(e) Answer the question I have been unable to settle for myself: once there are more than two players, do "nothing the others might do makes this the best thing to play" and "something else beats this no matter what the others do" come apart? And does the answer change depending on whether my uncertainty about the other players is allowed to treat their choices as correlated, or must be built up as independent guesses about each of them?

(f) Identify which reading the sentence is most often carrying when it is used to shut down a discussion, and which readings survive contact with the ordinary business of finding equilibria — since a principle that is invoked to prune the game ought not to prune away the very things one was looking for.

If any of the three episodes turns out to rest on a confusion of mine rather than his, say so plainly and show me where.
