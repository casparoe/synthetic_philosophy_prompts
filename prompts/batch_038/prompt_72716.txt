In November I am giving a ninety-minute morning session to a history society in a former textile town — one whiteboard, one tea break, no handouts, and a standing rule of the group that nobody is assigned reading. The twenty-odd members are mostly in their late sixties and seventies: a couple of retired schoolteachers, one of them a former head; three former shop stewards; several who worked in the mills or in clerical jobs nearby. The advertised topic is why bad practices stop — duelling, foot binding, the Atlantic slave trade — and I have been asked to bring in Eric Williams's argument that British abolition followed the declining profitability of the sugar islands rather than the triumph of humanitarian reasoning, and Appiah's counter-argument in terms of honour codes: that a practice dies when it ceases to be a mark of standing and becomes a mark of shame, as it did for the British officer class and the duel.

Here is where I am stuck. My draft arc runs: (1) the duel as a vivid opening case; (2) lay out the three candidate engines of change — argument, interest, honour — as a clean taxonomy; (3) Williams on abolition; (4) Appiah's honour revolutions; (5) return to a case the room has lived through. I tried a version of this on a colleague and it failed in a way I can diagnose but not repair: by the time I reached step 2 she had already decided on moral grounds which engine she preferred, and treated the taxonomy as a menu of positions to sign up for rather than as instruments for explaining a particular pattern of timing. I expect the mirror-image failure from the shop stewards, one of whom has already told me he intends to say that the whole business is class interest dressed up and that the abolitionists were at best unwitting instruments of it. So what I need from you is a plan for the explanation, not an explanation.

Specifically: give me the order in which the pieces have to be introduced and what depends on what. I suspect the taxonomy has to come after at least one case is on the table in enough detail that the room can feel the puzzle of why then and not earlier, but I do not know which case should supply that, or how much of it they need before the taxonomy stops behaving like a menu. Tell me which single example should carry the session and what that example must have and must lack in order to do the work — the duel, abolition, foot binding, or something local and recent — and whether I should carry one case right through or trade up partway. Map the misconceptions: I have "Appiah is saying morality is only fashion, so nothing actually got better", "Williams is calling the abolitionists hypocrites", "if interests explain it, the arguments don't matter", and "honour is just a grand word for peer pressure"; tell me which of these I have misdiagnosed and which I have missed, including the one I am sure they will bring — that duelling ended because it was banned — which I do not want to answer with a legal history I have not verified. Then fix the exact point in the sequence at which each has to be headed off, before rather than after it forms; include one question I can put to the room near the end that would separate those who grasped the mechanism from those who merely enjoyed the stories; and say what I should deliberately leave out at this level and announce that I am leaving it out.

One candidate I keep circling back to is the ending of the cane in British schools, which this room straddles — several members were caned and at least one administered it — and where the prohibition reached state schools well before the fee-paying ones, an asymmetry I cannot myself explain. Assess honestly whether that case can bear the weight or whether it will drag the session into memoir and grievance; if it cannot, say so plainly and tell me what to use instead. Finally, since I will be saying all of this aloud to a room that includes a retired history teacher: tell me how confident you are in the structure you propose, flag anything in it that rests on a contestable reading of Williams or of Appiah rather than on what they straightforwardly say, and single out the one point in your plan you are least sure of.
