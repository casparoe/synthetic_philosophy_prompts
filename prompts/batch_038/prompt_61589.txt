I'm a second-year student in a public policy master's program, and for a seminar on algorithms and governance I've been asked to write a short memo evaluating how journalists and advocates characterize technical results in machine learning. The specimen I've been assigned is a paragraph from an unsigned essay in a general-interest online magazine about bias in criminal risk assessment. Because my background is in political theory rather than statistics, I can't tell how much of it to rely on. Here is the paragraph, reproduced as it appeared:

> "A few years ago, after an investigation found that a commercial recidivism score used in American courts flagged Black defendants as high-risk at roughly twice the rate of white defendants who went on to reoffend, mathematicians proved something remarkable: this is not a bug that better data or better code could fix. There is a formal impossibility theorem showing that no risk algorithm can be fair to both groups at the same time. The tool was calibrated, which means the score was equally accurate for members of both groups — a Black defendant and a white defendant assigned the same score had the same probability of reoffending. But once groups differ in their underlying base rates, calibration arithmetically forces the algorithm to hand out more false alarms to the group with the higher base rate. That is not a defect of this particular instrument; it is a theorem. What the impossibility result really establishes, then, is that fairness and accuracy are mathematically incompatible, and the only intellectually honest response is to stop using risk scores in sentencing altogether, since no version of them can avoid being unjust."

My instinct, honestly, is that the paragraph is broadly right — I found it persuasive when I first read it, and I want to be careful not to dismiss a real mathematical result just because its conclusion is inconvenient. But a study partner who has taken a graduate machine-learning course insists it is "sloppy in a way that matters," and she could not articulate precisely where. That is what I need from you.

Please act as an expert in the formal study of algorithmic fairness — someone equally at home with the probability behind these theorems and with the normative literature on what the criteria are supposed to protect — and give me an accuracy audit I can actually use in the memo. Think carefully, and begin by sketching, for yourself, the precise statements of the relevant impossibility results: which fairness criteria are involved (for instance, calibration or predictive parity, and the balance of false positive and false negative rates), what background condition on the data the proofs require, and what the exceptional or degenerate cases are in which everything can be satisfied at once. Only then proceed to the paragraph itself. Go through it essentially sentence by sentence, and for each claim sort it into one of four categories: accurate as stated; a defensible simplification that loses something but does not mislead; misleading, because it asserts more than the mathematics supports; or simply false. Where a claim is misleading or false, say what the correct statement would be, and where a claim is a defensible simplification, say what was dropped. I am especially interested in three things you should address explicitly: whether the impossibility is genuinely unconditional or depends on assumptions the paragraph never mentions; whether calibration really does entail a fixed direction of error-rate disparity between groups with different base rates, or whether the theorems leave that direction open; and whether the slide from "these fairness criteria cannot all be satisfied together" to "fairness and accuracy are mathematically incompatible" is licensed. Finally, tell me which of the paragraph's conclusions survive the correction — in particular, whether the policy recommendation to abandon risk scoring follows from anything true in it, and what a scholar of this literature would say the theorems do and do not settle, given that the choice among the criteria looks normative rather than mathematical.

Please define any technical term the first time you use it, and write for an intelligent reader who has never taken a probability course. Do not pad the memo with a reading list or citations, and do not supply paper titles, author names, or publication dates unless you are confident of them; if you are unsure of a technical detail, flag your uncertainty in the text rather than papering over it. Close by rewriting the magazine paragraph so that it keeps its rhetorical force but asserts only what the results support, and add two or three sentences explaining what your revision changed and why.
