I work as a policy intern in the housing and public-health unit of a mid-sized European city, and for the past six weeks I have been sitting in on the design meetings for a poster campaign funded by a regional grant on energy poverty. The campaign has to communicate one central finding — roughly one household in nine in the city cannot keep its home adequately warm through the winter — together with a breakdown showing that the rate is about three times higher among single pensioners than among two-adult households, plus a district-level view. There is a printer's constraint that matters to the argument: the budget allows only two spot colours plus black. Two of my colleagues have become the de facto referees on the design, and they disagree about almost everything, in a way that keeps sliding from a practical quarrel about legibility into a quarrel about what a picture of social facts is supposed to do. One of them, Hanneke Roos, our head of public communication, is a committed partisan of the picture-statistics tradition that Otto Neurath developed at the Society and Economic Museum in interwar Vienna with the artist Gerd Arntz and later with Marie Neurath — the method usually known as ISOTYPE — and she argues that the department should adopt it as its standing house style, not merely for this poster. The other, Dr. Emil Farkaš, the department's epidemiologist, thinks the method is a museum piece whose claims have never been tested against ordinary alternatives. I found the exchanges genuinely hard to follow because both of them sound reasonable, and I have no way of telling which of Neurath's commitments Hanneke is representing faithfully and which she is stretching to win the argument.

Their exchange went in seven rounds, as far as I could reconstruct it from my notes.

1. Hanneke proposes rendering the one-in-nine figure as nine identical small human silhouettes in a row, one printed in the second spot colour and eight in grey, and insists that the same unit figure be reused across the whole poster, each figure standing for a fixed number of households. Emil objects that a poster on a tram shelter gets two seconds of attention and that nine small figures cannot be counted from three metres away; he wants a single large proportional bar, or one pictogram scaled so that its area carries the magnitude.
2. Emil says that if every figure is identical the breakdown has been thrown away, since the pensioner rate and the two-adult rate cannot be shown by interchangeable little men. Hanneke replies that differentiation belongs to colour and grouping, never to the shape of the sign: you keep one human figure and change only its colour, because inventing a fresh silhouette for each category turns the poster into a puzzle and, on her reading of Neurath, treats some people as a different kind of thing from others. Emil answers that with two spot colours she can distinguish at most three groups, so her principle is unimplementable here rather than merely austere.
3. The department's figures are 8.97 per cent and 27.3 per cent. Emil wants those numbers printed. Hanneke says the method is deliberately coarse: the picture should carry a magnitude the reader will remember and act on, honest coarseness beats false precision in the street, and exact values belong in the specialist's table. Emil says coarsening two categories onto the same unit can hide or even reverse a real difference, and that this is not a stylistic preference but a way of being wrong.
4. The draft includes a choropleth map of the districts. Hanneke wants it replaced by rows of unit figures grouped under district names, arguing that Neurath distrusted maps — she says he thought them self-important, flattering the viewer with the appearance of knowledge while concealing what the units are. Emil says the whole point of the map is that the cold homes form a corridor along the river valley, which is a spatial pattern that rows of figures cannot express, so the panel she wants deleted is the only one carrying information her system cannot.
5. Hanneke wants no headline number and no explanatory paragraph, at most a title and a source line, on the ground that a picture requiring a caption has failed. Emil says this is a grant-eligibility campaign: people have to learn what to do if their household is one of those shown, and a poster that refuses to tell them has failed at something more important than pictorial purity.
6. Emil proposes commissioning an outside studio to make the thing look contemporary, with a friendly illustrated family as the focal image. Hanneke says the studio's task is not decoration but transformation — someone who understands the statistics has to work with the artist to decide what to omit — and that beautification is precisely what the Vienna method was built to refuse. Emil says nobody outside a philosophy department has ever heard of a transformer, and that an unappealing poster is read by no one.
7. Emil's closing move: even granting everything she says about the method, it was designed for a museum with attendants and school groups, its advantages have never been measured against alternatives, and adopting it on the strength of a philosophy of science is a category error — the department should run a comprehension and recall test on two versions and let the result decide. Hanneke replies that a single-poster test cannot measure what the method is for, which is a durable habit of thinking about social facts in units, and that scoring versions on immediate comprehension simply assumes Emil's metric is the right one.

Please adjudicate this round by round rather than declaring an overall winner first. For each of the seven exchanges, give a single integer on a scale from −3 to +3, where −3 means Emil is clearly right and Hanneke clearly wrong, 0 means the exchange is evenly matched or both are substantially right, and +3 means Hanneke is clearly right and Emil clearly wrong; follow each score with two or three sentences justifying it, and in that justification say explicitly whether Hanneke's invocation of Neurath is a faithful rendering of his commitments, a defensible extension of them, or a distortion. Where a point turns on something the historical record does not settle, or on an empirical claim about comprehension that neither of them has evidence for, say so instead of filling the gap with a confident attribution — I would rather be told that a premise is uncertain than be given a plausible-sounding reference. Then, separately, set out the two or three things that both of them missed: I have in mind at least the question of what the method was originally embedded in, and the question of whether Neurath's aims here were purely about accurate perception or also about who gets to take part in arguments about social planning — but tell me if I am wrong about that, and add whatever else is missing.
