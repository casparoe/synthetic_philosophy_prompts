I'm a philosopher by training, now in my second year as a postdoc in a school of public health, and each autumn I run a single seventy-five-minute session — titled, unhelpfully, "What does cause mean?" — inside a required second-year methods sequence for epidemiology and biostatistics PhD students. There are about twenty of them. All have a year of regression and a short module on propensity scores behind them; their causal vocabulary is entirely potential-outcomes flavoured; most have drawn DAGs for their thesis proposals without having been taught what the arrows commit them to; and at least two have been told by their advisors that philosophy of causation is an expensive way of avoiding an identification problem. I get two whiteboards and no slides. The session is listed as theory day in the syllabus, so they arrive prepared to be patient rather than interested. What I want from you is not an explanation of the contrast between Lewis's counterfactual account of causation and the interventionist account developed by Woodward and others — I know that material well — but a plan for how to teach the contrast to this particular room in this particular amount of time: what has to be in place before anything else can be said, what depends on what, and where the hour will collapse if I get the order wrong.

Here is my sketch, and where I suspect it is broken. I open with a case I built for the purpose: a hypertensive patient whose blood pressure is well controlled on Drug A, in a clinic operating a treat-to-target protocol under which discontinuing A automatically triggers an upward titration of Drug B. So A is doing genuine work in the actual sequence, yet the difference-making conditional — had she not taken A, her pressure would have been about the same — comes out true, because the protocol compensates. My plan was to let them watch their instinctive cashing-out of causal claims misfire here, then bring in interventions as the repair, then point out that the repair works only if the intervention is of the right kind, one that reaches the outcome by no route other than the exposure, which in this case means changing A while holding the protocol fixed. I wanted the final third for the payoff that concerns them: that the protocol is playing the role of what they would call a competing treatment regime or a source of confounding, and that their identification conditions are the technical descendant of the philosophical requirement that nothing else be allowed to change along with the cause. Two things worry me. First, that the case is too clever — it needs a compensating mechanism in order to generate the failure, and a sharp student will say the right response is to describe the situation more finely, splitting A into dosage and timing, rather than to give up on difference-making. That is itself a real move in the literature and arguably the right one, and I can't decide whether to let them make it, or whether making it in week one will cost me the rest of the session. Second, whether to open inside their own domain at all: I keep going back and forth between this case and a neutral non-medical preemption case used first and transferred across afterwards, and I can't tell which ordering actually protects the transfer rather than merely sounding tidier.

Then the misconception map, which is the part I most want worked out, and where my guesses are weakest. My candidates: that an intervention is simply a randomized trial, so exposures nobody could randomize — sex assigned at birth, ancestry, ambient pollution, anything already in the past — fall outside causation proper rather than outside one study design; that the counterfactual account and the potential-outcomes framework they were trained in are the same view wearing two hats; that invariance under interventions is the same property as transportability of an association to new populations, which would collapse the entire session into a lecture on external validity; and that counterfactuals are unscientific because unobservable while interventions are respectable because performable — which seems to me exactly backwards, since both are claims about circumstances that do not obtain. Tell me which of these are real, which I have misdescribed, what this audience brings that I have not thought of, and, for each, the precise point in the sequence where it must be headed off rather than answered later — including whether any of them has to be planted deliberately and early so the later material has something to bite on. Give me one question to put to the room in the final five minutes whose answers would separate the students who have grasped the distinction from those who have learned to produce the words, and make it a question with a plausible-looking wrong answer available. Finally, tell me what I should announce as out of scope — I am thinking the similarity metric for counterfactuals, the fine print on token causation, and the dispute over whether interventionism is reductive metaphysics or a description of modelling practice — and how to say so without making the omissions look like evasions. Please give me the plan itself, not a reading list: I do not want anything for them to go and look up, and I cannot verify citations in the room anyway.
