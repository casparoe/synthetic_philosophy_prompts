The debate is usually framed as a question about where kinds live — in the world, or in our heads, or in our practices. I want to suggest that framing is what's stuck, and that the live question is a different one: not whether a partition is mind-independent, but whether a given partition is *arbitrary*.

Start with a feature of actual scientific practice that both sides tend to talk past. The same stretch of reality gets partitioned in mutually incompatible ways at the same time, by the same people, without anybody thinking a mistake has been made. Biology is the obvious case: there are something like two dozen named species concepts in circulation (the usual count is in the twenties), and a systematist building a tree, a conservation lawyer deciding what gets legal protection, and a population ecologist modelling gene flow will each work with a different one. Nobody says two of them are wrong. Chemistry is subtler but similar: gold behaves like a textbook natural kind when the question is electronic structure, because proton count pins it down; but the bullion trade partitions the same metal by fineness grade, and lead's standard atomic weight has to be published as an interval because the isotopic mix varies from ore body to ore body — so "lead" is a kind with a smudge in it. Astronomy is the cleanest case: the planet/dwarf planet/small body scheme was adopted by vote in 2006, the third condition being roughly that a body has swept its orbital zone clear of comparable objects, and a large chunk of the planetary science community simply kept using the old extension afterwards while continuing to cite the same data.

What I think this shows is that a taxonomy isn't a single object that could be true or false. It's a bundle of three things: a set of categories, a criterion for falling under them, and a body of generalizations the categories are meant to support. Realists and anti-realists both argue as if the bundle has to be evaluated all at once. But the parts come apart, and they come apart in historically documented ways. You can hold the categories fixed and swap the criterion — which is basically what happened when the ordering principle for the elements moved from atomic weight to proton count after Moseley tied atomic number to X-ray spectra: the anomalous pairs (cobalt/nickel, tellurium/iodine, argon/potassium) fell into place and nobody's element list changed. Or you can hold the criterion fixed and change which generalizations you're after — fish is an excellent category for managing a fishery and a defective one for a phylogeny, and nothing about the fish changed between those two sentences.

So my proposal is to replace "is K a natural kind?" with "is this partition arbitrary relative to task T?", and to cash out non-arbitrariness as something like inference yield: a partition is non-arbitrary to the degree that adopting it licenses reliable inferences that the other partitions actually available to you don't.

Two worries I can't discharge on my own. First, this looks like it makes anti-realism true by stipulation — if the metric is yield-for-a-task and tasks are ours, I've just relabelled the pragmatic view with a quantitative varnish. My defensive gesture is that tasks aren't free: the world pushes back hard on which partitions have any yield at all, and a partition with no yield for any task anybody could coherently have is simply a bad partition, not an equally valid perspective. That would make me a realist about the yield function rather than about the partition. I genuinely can't tell whether that's a position or a dodge. Second, I keep reaching for a resolution metaphor — partitions are like pixel grids, no grid is *the* right one, but some grids lose less of the world's covariance structure for a given purpose — and I can't tell whether that's doing work or just decorating.
