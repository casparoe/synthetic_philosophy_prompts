I am a second-year graduate student in a history-of-science seminar on early modern biblical interpretation, and my paper is on whether Galileo's account of Scripture's accommodation to ordinary readers is a genuine hermeneutical theory or, as one of my classmates insists, a tactical instrument he picked up when it helped and set down when it did not. To test my own view against his, I built the argument below, intending it as a reductio of his skepticism. When I laid it out on paper, however, it proved far more than I wanted, and I can no longer tell whether it is a successful attack on Galileo's position or a bad argument I have simply talked myself into. Please treat it as an object of diagnosis and not as an occasion for background: I know the chronology, the Tridentine prohibition on interpreting Scripture against the sense the Church has held or the unanimous consent of the Fathers, Bellarmine's demand for a true demonstration before any passage is reinterpreted, the Baronius line Galileo borrows approvingly to the effect that the Holy Spirit intends to teach us how to get to heaven and not how the heavens go, and the contents of the letters to Castelli and to the Grand Duchess Christina. Assume all of it and go straight to the reasoning.

Here is the argument as I have it. (1) Scripture's purpose is salvation and the ordering of conduct, not instruction in natural philosophy; when it speaks of natural things it speaks as it must in order to be understood by everyone, not as a natural philosopher would. (2) Speech adapted so as to be understood by everyone about a subject it is not undertaking to teach does not assert anything about that subject; at most it gestures usefully. (3) Hence no scriptural passage, as written, asserts a truth about nature, and no conclusion in natural philosophy can be established or overturned by Scripture read literally. (4) But a text that asserts nothing about a subject cannot be corrected by anything about that subject either, so the demonstration of the Earth's motion is no more a threat to Scripture than to a fable; Scripture's immunity from Copernicanism is purchased at the price of its saying nothing. (5) Nor can the result be confined to natural claims, because the soteriological content is delivered by means of natural and historical content — creation, the sun standing still for Joshua, the resurrection of a body, the ending of the world — and if the vehicle is accommodated, so is what it carries. (6) Therefore nothing in Scripture asserts anything, Scripture is a morally useful fiction, and Galileo's assurance that Scripture cannot err is vacuous, since a text that asserts nothing cannot err but also cannot teach. (7) Worse, the accommodation principle is itself stated in ordinary language for a lay audience, so by its own lights it too is not to be taken as asserting anything and cannot even be asserted. (8) So Galileo's hermeneutics either collapses on itself or confirms what his opponents said: once a text may be reinterpreted wherever demonstration presses, nothing holds it in place, and ecclesiastical authority is the only thing standing between revelation and arbitrary reading.

What I want from you is a diagnosis, not a defense of anyone. Identify the step that has to go — or tell me that none of them fails and that the real question is which premise is cheapest to surrender. If the fault is an equivocation, name the exact word or phrase that is doing double duty, say between which two steps it changes sense, and explain why it looked univocal in that stretch of reasoning; my own instinct is that a term in the middle is being used two ways, but every time I try to say which one, the argument reassembles itself and I lose the seam. Then price the alternatives for me: what does Galileo forfeit if instead he gives up (5)'s refusal to separate natural from soteriological content, and what does he forfeit if he gives up (7)'s self-application, and what does he forfeit if he gives up (3) and concedes that some passages do assert natural truths? For what it is worth, I would rather keep (1) and (3) — I want to hold that Scripture teaches salvation and that natural conclusions cannot be overturned by passages as written — than any of the middle steps, but I do not want that preference to decide the analysis.

Three further questions, each answered concretely rather than in generalities. First, does the argument generalize? If (2) is where it breaks, does the same move demolish any position on which a text accommodates its speech to its audience — including, uncomfortably, the premises of my own argument, which are stated in ordinary language for a non-specialist seminar? Second, Galileo's treatment of Joshua's long day, where he contends that arresting the sun as the passage describes would not have lengthened the day under a Ptolemaic arrangement and that the text is therefore better read as pointing at the sun's own motion, looks to me like a case in which he treats an accommodated passage as making a truth-apt claim about nature, and indeed one favorable to Copernicanism. Does that help me block the move from (2) to (3), or does it strengthen my classmate's charge of opportunism? Third, once the argument is repaired or rejected, what survives as a genuine difficulty — what does Galileo still owe by way of a criterion for telling which passages are accommodated and in which respect, and is there any way to state such a criterion that is not simply whatever keeps the demonstrated conclusions intact?
