Ok so, I'm a junior and somehow I got talked into being the student rep on our district's school climate committee. Basically what happens is a few times a semester somebody — a vice principal, a parent, once a company's sales rep who had gotten hold of the superintendent's email — sends the committee a proposal for some new thing that's supposed to fix behavior or safety, and we have to write a one-page recommendation that goes up to the school board. We get like three or four a semester and there is zero framework, we just argue in circles for 40 minutes and then vote, and the vote always splits exactly along "do you trust the adults in the building" lines. Our advisor reads the recommendation before it goes out and she sends it back if it's just vibes.

I've been reading Foucault's Discipline and Punish on my own — the discipline part mostly, I skipped ahead past the torture stuff — and I think it's actually useful for this, way more useful than the "is this a violation of student privacy?" framing everybody defaults to. Like the hierarchical observation thing, normalizing judgment, the examination, how a measure stops being about catching people and starts being about getting people to watch themselves, docile bodies, all of it. But obviously you can't hand a school board a paragraph of Foucault, and I couldn't cite chapter and verse in a recommendation anyway even if I wanted to.

So can you build me a decision procedure I can run on any proposal in like 15 minutes at my desk the night before a meeting? I need the questions to ask, in order; what counts as a good enough answer to each one versus a dodge; the points where the procedure just stops and says no; and the points where it has to hand the decision to a human because it's actually about values and not facts, so I'm not pretending it's objective. It has to work for the two other student reps who rotate on each year and have never heard of Foucault, so tell me which of his distinctions absolutely have to survive getting turned into a checklist and which ones I can drop without wrecking the thing.

The part I'm actually worried about: if you apply Foucault naively, doesn't every discipline proposal look bad? Cameras, tardy apps, all of it, since they're all producing self-monitoring kids and ranking everyone against a norm. So the procedure needs to be able to say yes sometimes or it's useless to me — a checklist that always answers no isn't a checklist, it's a conclusion with steps attached. And be straight with me about whether getting it to sometimes say yes means watering him down, and if so, which specific part of the view I'd be giving up.

Then run it on these three, which are all real-ish versions of stuff that has come to us:

1. A vendor wants to put monitoring software on our district Chromebooks that takes periodic screenshots during class hours, uses a model to flag "off-task" screens, and emails each teacher a weekly list of which kids in their periods got flagged the most.

2. A tardy-tracking app that scans kids in at the door and posts a running leaderboard by homeroom on the cafeteria monitors, plus an automatic text to parents at five tardies.

3. A positive behavior points thing where staff hand out points for stuff like helping a teacher or holding a door, kids bank them and spend them at a monthly store, and the top three homerooms get a pizza party each quarter.

Full disclosure on number 3: I think it's great, my little sister is in it and she's obsessed with it, and I'd be pretty irritated if your procedure kills it. Run it anyway and don't go soft just because I told you that — but if it survives, I want to know honestly why it survives, not just that it did.

Last thing: tell me what the procedure can't do and where it's going to misfire, and give me the two or three sentences I should say out loud when I present it, so the adults in the room know which parts are judgment calls wearing a procedure costume.
