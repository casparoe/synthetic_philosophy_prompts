So the stakes are real (suspensions, getting kicked off teams) but none of us have any training at all.

What bugs me is how much of what we decide comes down to luck. Two kids cheat off the same answer key, one gets caught and one doesn't, and we can only ever sanction the one who got caught. Somebody shoves a friend into the lockers as a joke — if the friend cracks his phone the sanction is way harsher than if he laughs it off, even though the shove was identical. We tried to fix this by treating attempts the same as completed stuff, and that landed us in a case last year where we sanctioned a kid for a plan he'd written down but never acted on, which honestly felt wrong to everyone in the room.

Can you turn the moral luck idea into an actual procedure we could run on a case? It has to work for students who've never read any philosophy, so tell me which distinctions from the theory absolutely have to survive being simplified and which ones we can drop. And be honest about where it'll misfire, and where it's going to give an answer I don't like.

Then run it on three cases: the two cheaters where only one got caught; the identical prank that broke a phone versus the one that didn't; and the written-down plan nobody acted on.
