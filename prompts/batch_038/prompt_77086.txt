You are a philosopher of language and literary theorist with serious working knowledge of translation theory and formal poetics — someone who has argued about fidelity and about metre at equal length, and who is willing to be exact rather than agreeable. Please think carefully before answering. I would rather have a slow, precise audit than a fast, genial one.

I am a novelist. My fourth book has a poet at its centre, and it contains some twenty poems in English that the narrative presents as translations of that poet's work. The poet is my invention and so are the "originals": I wrote each piece twice, once in the source language I have posited for the novel and once in English. Nothing here therefore depends on your knowing any actual poem, and I am not asking you to look anything up, recommend sources, or cite authorities. I want your judgement on my reasoning, not on my references.

I have been reading Hofstadter's book on Marot — the one in which a single short Renaissance French poem is turned into English over and over again under the pressure of its own form — and, partly to settle an argument with my editor, I have written down the creed I actually work by. My editor's claim is that what follows is not one position but two incompatible ones wearing a coat. I would like that tested properly rather than rhetorically.

The commitments, as near to their working form as I can put them:

1. Form is not the container of a poem's meaning but part of what the poem means. A rhyme scheme, a metrical habit, a line count, the return of a refrain at a fixed position — these set up expectations and then meet them, or meet them late, or fail them. A rendering that abandons them has not conveyed the meaning at the cost of some beauty. It has conveyed a different and smaller content.

2. Hence my rule for every poem in the book: the English version must satisfy the original's formal specification exactly. Same number of lines; same count of syllables per line, or its stress-count equivalent where the two languages do not measure alike; same rhyme scheme, including any distinction the original observes between full and approximate rhyme; same stanza divisions; same positions for any repeated word or refrain.

3. The English version must also read as though an English poet of comparable period and temperament had written it. No inverted syntax purchased to land a rhyme. No archaism used as mortar. No padding. No sense of a translator's hand resting on the reader's shoulder.

4. Fidelity is global rather than local. Where a constraint cannot be met at a given point without distortion, I compensate: I surrender a detail here and buy the loss back elsewhere, so that what I am answerable for is the total effect and not the ledger of individual correspondences.

5. A translation is a new poem, and its maker is answerable for every choice in it as an author would be; and nevertheless the translator's proper posture is self-effacement, in that nothing may appear in the target poem that the source does not license.

6. Part of a reader's pleasure in a fixed-form poem is the half-conscious perception of difficulty overcome: the sense that the poet had almost no room to move and still made something that sounds inevitable. Constraint has to remain audible in the result. A form that nobody notices is not being preserved.

7. Inside the fiction there is no apparatus: no footnotes, no facing-page original, no translator's preface, no character who explains what has been lost. The English poem alone is what the reader gets, and I hold that a reader of my novel who knows no other language still encounters the poem itself rather than a report of it.

8. Originals are not sacred across time. A translation that has gone stale should be replaced rather than reverenced, and each generation deserves its own. But a translator is never entitled to improve on the original; to smooth a clumsy line in the source is to betray it.

And two verdicts I have already given, and would defend:

A. One of the poet's pieces rhymes throughout on inflectional endings, in a language where that costs the poet nothing and is heard by its own readers as merely neat — arguably a shade lazy. The English counterparts of that procedure (an -ing set against an -ing, a -tion against a -tion) are heard in English as a failure of invention rather than as neatness. I refused them, and used instead rhymes that are surprising in their sense and that cost me a good deal of literal accuracy. My reason was that reproducing the original's cheapness would have produced in English not cheapness but incompetence, and incompetence is not what the original's cheapness does to its own reader.

B. Another piece is free verse: no metre, no rhyme, line lengths irregular to the point of appearing arbitrary, enjambments that seem not to have been chosen at all. My rule in (2) covers an irregular specification as much as a regular one, so I reproduced the irregularity exactly, and I believe I was right to.

What I want is an audit, not a lecture. Please work in this order:

- Begin with a short, clearly labelled brainstorming pass — ten lines or so — sorting out which of these commitments are making claims about the same object and which are not, and where my vocabulary is doing double duty. Then leave it behind and answer.
- Give me the strongest available case that the package is coherent: compact but genuinely argued, not concessive, and not the weak version of my editor's objection that it would be easiest to defeat. I want to know what the best defence looks like before I hear the attack.
- Then go through the commitments and tell me which pairs are in real tension and which only look it. Rank the real tensions by severity; do not present them to me as equally bad, and do not flag everything.
- For each real tension, state precisely what would have to be given up or weakened for the remainder to stand, and what the cheapest revision is — together with what that revision costs me in practice, in these poems, rather than in the abstract.
- Tell me whether verdicts A and B are deliverable from commitments 1–8, or whether I have decided one or both on grounds my own creed forbids. Where a verdict is undeliverable, name the commitment that fails to reach it and say whether the fix is to amend the commitment or to reverse the verdict. Where a verdict is delivered by one commitment but not reached by another, say so explicitly rather than treating the pair as a single question.
- Identify any commitment that is ambiguous in a way that changes the outcome of the audit, and say which reading you have assumed.
- Finish by telling me which single revision would clean up the most, and what I would be surrendering by making it.

Please do not recommend reading, do not quote authorities at me, and do not soften the answer by observing that translation is hard. I have, at this point, no view about which of these commitments I ought to be willing to lose, and I would rather you did not assume one for me.
