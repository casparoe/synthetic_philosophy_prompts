I retired three years ago after four decades as an experimental chemist, and I have taken up philosophy with more seriousness than my former colleagues expected. The literature that has suited me best is epistemic utility theory, since it treats accuracy as a quantity one can reason about rather than as a figure of speech. My understanding of its central result is this: if an agent's degrees of belief violate the probability axioms, there exists a coherent alternative that is strictly closer to the truth in every possible circumstance whatsoever, and so an incoherent set of credences is guaranteed to be less accurate than a coherent one no matter how the world turns out. I take it that inaccuracy is measured by a strictly proper scoring rule, of which the Brier score is the standard example.

Two questions follow, and they are practical rather than polemical. First, I regularly discover that my own credences over a small set of competing hypotheses are mutually inconsistent — assigning, say, 0.7, 0.2, and 0.3 to three possibilities I myself regard as exhaustive and exclusive. What procedure does the theory prescribe for identifying the particular coherent credence function that is guaranteed to be more accurate than the incoherent one I began with, and does that procedure depend on further facts about my situation? Second, once I have repaired my credences in that way, am I right that I have discharged every obligation the accuracy framework imposes, so that nothing short of new evidence gives me any further reason to revise them?
