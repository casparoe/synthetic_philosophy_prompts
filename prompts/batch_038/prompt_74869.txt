I direct a graduate seminar on the epistemology of aesthetic judgement, and this term I have found myself unable to dispose of an argument that a colleague raised in comments on a draft of my own work. I have since rebuilt it as carefully as I can — partly because I wanted to refute it properly, and partly because I now discover that I accept rather more of it than I expected to. The difficulty is that its conclusion is one I feel certain I ought not to accept, and I cannot locate the step at which it goes astray. Here is my reconstruction:

(1) A judgement attributing an aesthetic property to a work — that a passage is delicate, that a façade is ponderous, that a novel is sentimental — is warranted for a subject only if that subject is acquainted with how the work strikes a suitable perceiver; the acquaintance is what the judgement answers to, and in its absence there is nothing for the ascription to attach to.
(2) Acquaintance with how something strikes a perceiver consists in having an experience of a certain character. It is not a matter of standing in any relation to a proposition.
(3) Testimony transmits propositions: what a hearer receives is a content, together with whatever epistemic support the speaker's reliability lends to it.
(4) So, by (2) and (3), testimony cannot transmit acquaintance; and so, by (1), testimony cannot warrant an aesthetic judgement for its recipient. No one has ever known, on the strength of a review, a recommendation, or a lecture, that a work is elegant, garish, or profound.
(5) Nothing in (1) through (4) mentions art. The reasoning requires only that the property ascribed be one whose ascription is answerable to how things strike a suitably equipped perceiver. Every such property — funny, delicious, harrowing, nauseating, exhilarating — therefore lies beyond the reach of testimony for the same reason.
(6) That consequence cannot be absorbed. It entails that the friend who warns me a dish is unbearably bitter tells me nothing I could come to know; that a patient's report that the procedure was agonizing gives her surgeon nothing to go on; that the colleague who assures me the joke buried in the footnote lands has given me no knowledge at all. On this view each of us is permanently confined to the short list of things we have happened to encounter ourselves, and the greater part of what we take to know about how the world strikes other people is not knowledge.
(7) Therefore (1) is false: acquaintance is not a condition on warranted aesthetic judgement, and aesthetic testimony is no worse than testimony about anything else.

What I want from you is a diagnosis, not reassurance. Tell me which step fails, and explain why it looked perfectly innocent when I set it down; if the failure is an equivocation, name the two readings, show that the argument is invalid on each of them taken consistently, and say which reading I was tacitly relying on at each point. If instead the argument is valid and the only question is which premise to surrender, tell me which is cheapest and defend that verdict against the alternatives — in particular against living with (6), which I keep finding less absurd than I would like, and against simply denying (5), the premise I am least willing to give up. State explicitly whether the second clause of (7) follows from the falsity of (1) at all, or whether I have smuggled a parity claim in under cover of a modest one. Then price what you reject. What is left of the practice of criticism if testimony really is impotent here; what is left of the thought that aesthetic judgement must be first-hand if it is not; and what becomes, on either answer, of Hume's qualified judges, whose authority is after all something a community recognizes and transmits rather than something each of us verifies for ourselves. If you think I should keep (1) and pay elsewhere, say so plainly and give me the bill — I would rather know the cost than be handed a conclusion I can live with.
