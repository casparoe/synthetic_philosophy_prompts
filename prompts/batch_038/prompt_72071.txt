I write a science-and-ideas column for a monthly magazine and somehow I've ended up giving the 10-minute opening remarks at a one-day workshop our local uni is hosting, called "Liberalism and its Empire". The awkward bit: the whole thing is being launched by a new centre funded by a shipping family whose money goes right back to the nineteenth-century India trade, and they'll be in the front row with the vice-chancellor. Room of about fifty — two historians of empire, a political theorist, the donors, a pile of grad students, and members of the public who signed up online and have never read a word of Mill.

You're a professional speechwriter who also happens to know the Mill scholarship cold. Think it through before you write: sketch me two or three candidate framings for the remarks, a couple of lines each, then write out the one you'd actually pick.

The framing I want runs through Mill's India job rather than his essays — roughly thirty-five years at the East India Company, in as a teenager, out as Chief Examiner in 1858 when the Company lost India, drafting dispatches all day at Leadenhall Street. And the hinge should be his argument in the chapter of his book on representative government about governing dependencies: that despotism can be a legitimate way to rule people not yet fit for self-rule, provided its real end is their improvement and it genuinely achieves that end. I want the speech to spend its weight on that proviso. Half the room thinks it's a fig leaf and half thinks it's his liberalism running exactly as designed, and the remarks should make that disagreement vivid and then hand it to the historians without pretending to settle it.

Constraints: dont put anything in Mill's mouth in quotation marks, paraphrase him, I'll check any line you're tempted to quote. No naming living scholars or citing books. At most two sentences addressing the funding awkwardness, directly, without accusing anyone in the room of anything. One concrete image from the Company office — ledgers, copyists, the dispatches. And please avoid 'problematic', 'complicated legacy', and 'on the right side of history'. It has to be followable by someone who's never heard of the man, and it should end by handing over to the first speaker. About a thousand words.
