I keep circling back to the old Malcolm-versus-Dennett fight about whether dreams are experiences, and I want to force myself to actually commit to a number instead of hedging in my head forever. Here's the claim I want your credence on, stated as narrowly as I can manage:

When somebody wakes up and says they dreamt about X, there was no dream experience occuring during the sleep itself — what exists is at most a waking impression plus a disposition to produce a report, and the report itself may get assembled at the moment of waking. That's roughly Dennett's replay picture. So: P(dreams are not experiences), where what's being denied is the metaphysical claim and not a claim about report accuracy.

That last part matters. I'm not asking how reliable dream reports are. You can think they're wildly confabulated and still think something was going on in there. What I want is the number on whether anything was going on in there.

Give me the decomposition with a number for each term and a sentence or two of justification, then the structure of how they combine — I'm not assuming it's a straight product, so tell me which terms are conditional on which and which ones dominate:

1. P(Malcolm's conceptual argument succeeds): that ordinary dream talk, the way we actually use it, doesn't license an experience claim, so the skeptic about in-sleep episodes is just taking the grammar seriously.
2. P(the replay model is roughly right about the mechanism) — and be explicit about whether you're conditioning this on term 1 or holding it independent.
3. P(the evidence we already have counts for genuine in-sleep experience rather than sitting equally comfortably with replay). I have two things in mind specifically: trained lucid dreamers executing pre-arranged eye-movement signals from inside verified REM, and fMRI decoders trained on waking perception that predict above chance the coarse visual category of what somebody saw in the stretch of sleep just before they were woken.
4. P(the whole dispute is substantially verbal) — that Malcolm and the folk view are describing the same facts with different scorekeeping, so there's nothing left to be right or wrong about.

Then there's a third view I want you to price too, because I've never seen anybody state it cleanly and im not sure it's a real option. Call it the residue view: there is genuine experience during sleep, but it's fragmentary, sensory, largely non-narrative, and the dream report is a story built afterward out of that residue plus expectation and the sheer fact of having been asleep. On this view Malcolm is right that reports aren't experience-descriptions and the folk view is right that something happened. Give me P(residue view), and tell me honestly whether that's a substantive third position or just a way of splitting the difference so nobody has to lose.
