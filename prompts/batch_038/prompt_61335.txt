You're a philosopher of law who's written sympathetically about both retributivist and deterrence-based accounts of punishment — think it through carefully, and start by brainstorming what the two sides are actually disagreeing *about* before you diagnose it. I teach the retributivism vs. consequentialism debate every year and it never moves. The thing is, everyone agrees on nearly all the case verdicts: nobody defends punishing the innocent, most people think the repentant offender still deserves something, everyone wants sentences to track harm and culpability roughly the same way, and the two theories end up recommending almost identical penal practice. Yet the question of whether punishment is justified by desert or by good consequences has been deadlocked for decades anyway.

My working hypothesis — and its only a hypothesis — is that the stalemate comes from level-shifting: the two sides keep sliding between justifying the institution and justifying the individual sentence, and if you nail them to one level the disagreement mostly evaporates (something like the Hart-style split between the general justifying aim and the principles of distribution, plus the negative-retributivist move where desert only sets limits rather than supplying the point). So: is this a substantive disagreement that just happens to be hard, a verbal fight over the word 'justification', two different questions going by one name, or bedrock intuitions neither side can argue for? I want the diagnosis with reasons drawn from what each side actually says, not a survey of the theories, plus an honest answer on what would count as progress — a distinction, a case that forces the two apart, a change of question, or nothing at all.
