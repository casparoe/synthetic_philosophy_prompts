So, context: I'm on a standing city panel, sort of an "emerging tech review" group, 90 minutes a month, and honestly its mostly lawyers plus two elected council members who have never taken a philosophy class in their lives. Next month I've been volunteered to do a 25-minute intro to the precautionary principle, because we're about to vote on a pilot where the child-protection agency wants to use a risk-scoring model to triage which abuse reports get a home visit first. The room is pre-split: the public health people think the tool obviously saves kids and that caution is a way of saying no, the legal people think it will wreck families' lives, and one council member has read an op-ed somewhere arguing that the precautionary principle is incoherent because every choice carries risk, so "be careful" can never actually tell you what to do. He will say that out loud about ten minutes in, I guarantee it. There's a whiteboard, no slides, the projector has been broken since March, and nobody will have read anything beforehand.

Don't explain the precautionary principle to me — plan how I explain it to them. I want the order the pieces have to go in and why each one depends on the one before it. My hunch is that I can't get anywhere near the burden-of-proof business until they can see that "we don't have data yet" is not the same claim as "the data says it's safe," and that I need irreversibility on the board before I can explain why some risks get treated differently from others even when the probabilities look similar — but tell me where I'm wrong. I also want one case to carry the whole thing, with a list of what that case has to have and what it must not have. My instinct is to use the triage pilot itself, but I'm worried that if the example is the thing they're voting on next month, they'll just argue about the pilot and never pick up the principle, so tell me straight whether to use it or to build on something else and only switch over at the very end. Someone on the panel keeps mentioning a county that ran a similar tool years ago and insists it went fine, so the example probably needs to survive a "well it worked over there" move.

Then map the misconceptions for me, and separate the ones they walk in with from the ones my own explanation is going to create. The ones I can already predict: "precaution means don't do the new thing," "it means prove it's 100% safe before you start," "it's just cost-benefit analysis with scarier vocabulary," and the council member's incoherence objection. Ideally it's a question where the answer looks different for someone who genuinely got it versus someone who just happens to agree with me politically, because in that room those two are indistinguishable.
