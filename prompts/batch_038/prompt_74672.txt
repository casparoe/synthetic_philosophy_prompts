I have spent the last two years as the research assistant to Miriam Deleu, a literary translator from French and Polish who also consults on evaluation for machine-translation projects, and who describes herself — half affectionately, half as a fight-picking gesture — as a Hofstadterian. She pressed a copy of *Le Ton beau de Marot* on me during my first week and has never let the subject drop. I am now drafting the opening chapter of a book about what her disagreement with my side of the field actually amounts to, and the chapter turns on a claim I want to state carefully: that her repeated verdict of "no understanding" is not one verdict reached eight times for the same reason, but the common surface of two or three rather different principles that happen to agree on everything she has been shown so far. Her record, as I have it in a spreadsheet: (1) a phrase-based statistical system from around 2010 rendering a boilerplate equipment-manual paragraph with no detectable errors — no understanding; she likened it to a player piano that reproduces a waltz without hearing it. (2) A neural system that translated the opening chapter of a novel into fluent, well-registered English while silently reversing which of two named characters is in love with which, an error invisible to anyone who had not read the book — no understanding; she has called this her favourite diagnostic example, because the system never had a picture of the scene. (3) A large language model, given a Polish idiom with no literal English counterpart and no glossary, produced an English idiom that was idiomatic, right in register, and not the textbook equivalent anyone expected; she wrote that this was surprisingly good and that the system had presumably internalised something about how the two languages carve up situations, and then denied, in the same paragraph, that this was understanding, on the grounds that an idiom can be landed by proximity to enough neighbouring idioms. (4) A model asked to explain in English why a particular French sentence was ambiguous produced a correct and rather elegant analysis, including which reading was likelier and why — no understanding; she said the model was describing a game it was not playing. (5) A model that rendered a poem into what she judged to be a genuinely moving English poem bearing only a loose relation to the source — a beautiful betrayal; she wrote that liking it made it worse evidence rather than better. (6) A model that beat a professional human translator on a dense legal-technical passage, confirmed by two independent expert reviewers — she conceded the output was better and said this was beside the point entirely. (7) A model that translated a one-line joke whose effect depends on a callback to something three hundred pages earlier, and got the callback right, choosing an English word that preserved both the echo and the laugh — her written verdict was still no understanding, but the margin of her draft carries the single word triangulation followed by a question mark. (8) A model that, when asked what would be lost in translating a certain rhyming couplet, produced a careful inventory of the losses and three candidate compromises with their costs — she called this theatrical, writing that asking a system what it has lost is like asking a calculator to explain arithmetic, because it is more computation, on request.

The case I need a prediction for came out of an evaluation session last Thursday and has not been shown to her yet. A current model was given a two-page French short story with no glossary and no instruction to comment, and returned three things. First, a fluent and idiomatic English translation. Second, entirely unprompted, a translator's note saying that the story's closing line depends on a rhyme between two words that has no English counterpart, that it has chosen to sacrifice the rhyme rather than the sense, and that the alternative would keep the rhyme but turn the last line into something closer to a quip, which this story cannot afford; the rejected version was printed underneath. Third, in an unrelated paragraph, the source has a man carry a tray of glasses up a staircase and set it down on the landing outside a bedroom, while the translation puts the tray in a room that the story itself, two pages earlier, has established as being on the ground floor. Two reviewers checked the passage against the French and confirmed the relocation. When the error was shown to the system it acknowledged it at once and repaired it, though we did not test whether the repair generalises to a fresh passage, and the note it produced about the rhyme was never mentioned in that exchange.

So: what will she say, and on what grounds? I want the candidate principles laid out as competing hypotheses rather than as a list of her possible moods, and I want the case treated as an instrument for telling them apart. Please organise the answer under these headings, in this order. **Bottom line** (about 100 words): the verdict you predict, in her register, including separately what she will make of the unprompted note and what she will make of the tray. **The rules that fit her record** (about 450 words): two to four candidate principles, each named and stated as a claim about what understanding a text requires, each shown to account for all eight verdicts above; say explicitly which verdict each rule has to strain over, and whether the case-3 remark about carving up situations and the case-7 marginal note count for or against it. **Where they part company on Thursday's output** (about 300 words): what each rule predicts she will do with the note, with the misfiled tray, and with the fact that the system repaired the error once it was shown but did not connect the two. **The discriminating case** (about 200 words): one further test, concrete enough that I could run it next week, that would separate the two most likely rules — plus what she would have to commit to in advance for the result to bind her instead of being reinterpreted afterwards. **Is she unfalsifiable** (about 150 words): whether the rule you think she is most likely running leaves her position exposed to anything at all, and whether that is a defect in a position of this kind or an appropriate feature of it.
