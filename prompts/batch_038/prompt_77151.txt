The question as posed to the students was:

> Armstrong holds that universals are immanent — that for a universal to exist is for it to be instantiated by some particular — and he accordingly rejects transcendent Forms that could exist uninstantiated. One of his central supports is causal: properties are introduced in order to explain the causal powers of things, and something standing wholly outside the causal order cannot perform that task. (a) Set out this argument, and explain what it does and does not establish against Platonic realism. (b) Explain whether a trope theorist, who denies that there are any repeatable properties, can appropriate the same argument, and say what she gains or loses by doing so.

The submission read as follows:

> Armstrong's immanent realism holds that universals exist only in their instances: for a universal to be is for it to be instantiated somewhere by some particular. There are no uninstantiated universals, and so no Platonic Forms hovering outside space and time waiting to be exemplified. Armstrong's motivation here is largely causal. We postulate properties in the first place to explain the causal behaviour of things — why this electron repels that one, why this sample of metal conducts. If a property is to do that work, it has to be where the action is, that is, located in the particular that has it. A transcendent Form is by hypothesis outside the causal order: it neither acts nor is acted upon. It therefore cannot be what makes the electron repel its neighbour. Since the only reason we had for admitting properties was to explain causal powers, a property that cannot figure in any causal explanation is ontologically idle, and we should not admit it. This is a version of the criterion of being that the Eleatic Stranger proposes in Plato's Sophist, on which what is real is what has the power to act or be acted upon. Armstrong is simply applying it to properties.
>
> Notice, though, that this causal argument is really the One Over Many argument in a new costume. The One Over Many argument says that when many things resemble each other and behave in the same way, we should posit one thing over the many to explain the resemblance. Armstrong's causal argument says exactly the same thing about causal powers instead of resemblances. Nothing new is added, because causation is just the most important species of the sameness-in-behaviour that the One Over Many argument was already invoked to explain. This has an important consequence: the causal argument does no independent work in ruling out transcendence. If the Platonist is willing to say that the Form of charge is what makes all electrons behave alike, she has met the argument in the same way that any universal realist meets the One Over Many argument, namely by pointing at the shared feature. What she has not met is the further demand that the shared feature be located. But that demand is not itself an argument; it is just the immanent realist's refusal to countenance anything unlocated.
>
> The argument does have one genuine dialectical limit, which Armstrong is honest about. It depends on accepting the causal criterion of being. A Platonist who thinks there are real entities that do no causal work — numbers, say, or uninstantiated possibilities — can simply deny the criterion, and then the argument loses its grip. So it is best read as showing that if you want properties for causal explanation, transcendent Forms are the wrong candidates, rather than as a refutation of Platonism as such.
>
> Bradley's regress, by contrast, is a problem that only the transcendent realist has. The regress arises because if a Form is one thing and its instance another, we need a relation of instantiation to join them; but that relation is a further entity requiring its own joining, and so on. Armstrong's universals are wholly present in each of their instances, so no extra relation is needed to bring the universal to the particular: the particular and the universal are already together. The immanent realist therefore avoids the regress, and this is a second and independent reason to prefer immanence over transcendence.
>
> On part (b), the trope theorist cannot use the causal argument at all. Tropes are particularized properties — this electron's charge, that one's charge — and no trope is ever instantiated twice. But the causal argument, like the One Over Many argument of which it is a version, is an argument for something repeatable that accounts for sameness. Since tropes are unrepeatable by definition, they cannot supply the sameness the argument demands, and the trope theorist is driven to explain everything by resemblance between tropes instead. She therefore gains nothing from the causal criterion and loses the argument's conclusion, and her account of causation must be built out of resemblance classes of particular charges rather than out of the charges themselves. That is a significant cost, since resemblance is a much less determinate relation than causation.
