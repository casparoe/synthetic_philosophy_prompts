I'm a high school senior doing an independent study in decision theory, and my supervisor has asked each of us to bring one written argument to our closing session and defend it against attack. I would rather find out now than in front of the group, so I want a diagnosis, not encouragement.

The setting is program equilibrium, which I'll state in my own terms so that nothing in my question depends on your having read the literature. Two players each choose a computer program that will play one round of the prisoner's dilemma on their behalf. Payoffs are the usual ones: 3 each for mutual cooperation, 1 each for mutual defection, 5 to the defector and 0 to the cooperator when they disagree. Neither player sees the other's code before submitting its own; the two programs are then run, each one handed the other's source code as input, and each may use that code however it likes in deciding whether to cooperate or defect. A pair of programs is an equilibrium if neither player could have done better by submitting a different program instead. The standard illustration is FairBot, which searches for a proof that the program it has been handed will cooperate when run against FairBot, cooperates if it finds such a proof, and defects otherwise. When both players submit FairBot, each ends up proving the other's cooperation by a fixed-point route that works precisely because each program's own cooperativeness is what the other is trying to establish, so they get 3 each rather than the 1 each that two unconditional defectors would get.

Here is my argument.

(1) Rational choice in this setting means submitting the program that maximizes your payoff, taking your opponent's program as given and taking your opponent to be choosing by the same standard.

(2) By the FairBot result, conditional cooperation, meaning cooperation only with opponents whose cooperation with you can be established, looks never worse than unconditional defection when both programs are readable to each other and both players are choosing to maximize, and is much better against some opponents.

(3) Now consider TributeBot. It reads the program it is handed and cooperates only if that program guarantees its player will accept a split of 4 to TributeBot and 2 to you; otherwise it defects.

(4) FairBot accepts. What TributeBot demands is that FairBot be able to prove TributeBot will cooperate, and TributeBot's cooperating just is FairBot's accepting, which closes exactly the loop that made two FairBots cooperate. So the conditionally cooperative program pays the tribute.

(5) And paying is the right thing to do by (1). Against TributeBot your alternatives are a program that accepts, worth 2 to you, and a program that refuses, worth 1, since refusing gets you mutual defection.

(6) Nothing in that comparison depends on the size of the tribute. If TributeBot demands 4.5 and leaves you 1.5, accepting still beats the 1 you get by refusing, and likewise for any demand that leaves you above 1. So there is no tribute, however lopsided, that (1) permits you to refuse.

(7) Therefore the player who follows the rules of program equilibrium should hand nearly the entire cooperative surplus to any opponent bold enough to write its boldness into its source code.

(8) But (2) was supposed to be the discovery: that conditional cooperation beats defection, and that readable source code is what makes cooperation possible at all. If boldness is what pays and transparency is what gets exploited, the framework has not shown that cooperation is rational. It has shown that being readable is a liability.

(9) I am not willing to accept (8), so something earlier must be wrong, and I have not been able to find it.

Which step do you reject? I would most like to keep (2) and (4), since together they are the reason I found this framework interesting in the first place, and I want to know whether they survive.
