I'm in the third year of a philosophy PhD (mind and value, loosely) and I'm drafting the middle chapter of my thesis on whether the badness of pain lives in its phenomenal character or somewhere else. I spent the summer on pain asymbolia because it looks like the cleanest natural experiment we've got: people with insular lesions, people who've had a cingulotomy for chronic pain, sometimes people on high-dose opioids, will tell you they feel the pain, point to where it is, rank how intense it is, and then say they don't mind it. Some apparently grin or laugh while you're pinpricking them. My supervisor thinks this is decisive against any view that puts the disvalue in the feel itself; my friend R. thinks the argument I've built for that conclusion proves far more than I want. Here it is, and its been sitting on my desk since October:

(1) If pain is bad in virtue of its phenomenal character, then anything with that character is bad for whoever has it — the badness rides along with the feel rather than with the subject's attitudes to it.
(2) Asymbolic patients have experiences with that character: they localise the pain, they grade its intensity, they describe it in the same sensory vocabulary the rest of us use.
(3) They are unmoved by it: they don't withdraw or guard the limb, they don't ask for relief, and they report that it doesn't bother them, sometimes that they'd rather go on feeling it.
(4) Necessarily, if a state is bad for a subject then that subject has a reason to want it to stop — and a reason she could in principle be moved by.
(5) So the asymbolic's pains are not bad for the asymbolic. [from 3, 4]
(6) But by (2) those pains are phenomenally just like ours.
(7) So the character shared by their pains and ours isn't sufficient for badness. [from 5, 6]
(8) So the badness of our pains comes from the extra bit they're missing — the minding, the aversion, the being-bothered.
(9) That extra bit isn't part of pain's felt character, since by (2) they have the felt character without it.
(10) Therefore no pain is bad in virtue of how it feels, and pain as such is no bad thing at all: a pain that nobody minds costs nobody anything.

R.'s worry is the generalisation, and I think he's right that it goes through. Run (3)–(5) again with anything else: a man with a frontal-lobe injury who says he registers his wife's death and simply isn't bothered by it has, on (4), nothing bad happening to him, and on (8)–(10) grief turns out to be bad only because we happen to be averse to it. So the argument seems to show that no disvalue is bad for anyone sufficiently unmoved by it, which is very close to showing that nothing is bad. There's also a bootstrapping problem I can't shake — if badness comes from being averse to a thing, what makes the aversion itself the right sort of state, if not that it's aimed at something already bad? R. says the whole thing is just an equivocation on pain between (2) and (6), but that looks too cheap to me, because these patients really do report the sensory stuff and locate it and rank it, and if you say they aren't in pain you owe an account of why their reports come out so systematically misleading. Relatedly I can't work out from the case reports whether the ones who say they don't mind it are describing a sensation that feels unpleasant but doesn't grip them, or a sensation with no unpleasantness in it whatsoever, and my guess is that which of those it is decides where the trouble actually sits.

So: tell me which step to give up, why that step managed to look obviously true when I wrote it down, and what each of the exits costs. I can see roughly four — reject (4) and go externalist about reasons-for; reject (1) and sever the link between feel and value; reject (2) and (6) and bite the bullet about patient reports; or accept (10) and stop believing pain is bad. If I had to rank what I want to keep, it's (4) first (I'm an internalist about reasons for reasons that have nothing to do with this chapter), then the bare claim that pain is bad; the thing I'd most like to sacrifice is (1). But I want to know what I'm actually paying for each of those choices, and whether there's a fifth exit I've missed — something that keeps most of (1) by narrowing what bad-for means, say. Please write in British English, and finish with a last line of the exact form "Verdict: step N — <five words or fewer naming what fails there>", so I can compare your answer against R.'s without rereading the whole thing.
