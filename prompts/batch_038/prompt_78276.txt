So I'm TA-ing a second-year epistemology seminar — 16 students, one 75-minute session — and they've just come off a unit on knowledge (JTB, Gettier, closure, that sort of thing). The only time they've heard anything verificationist is one throwaway line from me about Dummett. Nobody has seen the Fitch proof. My supervisor wants one "active learning" session per term and I've grabbed the slot for knowablity. What I want is an in-class game where the students generate the paradox out of their own mouths before anyone gives it a name: no opening lecture, and I'd rather they didn't hear "Fitch" or see a formal statement of the knowability principle until the last fifteen minutes.

Here's the rough shape I've been kicking around — feel free to break it if you have something better. Opener is a sorting task: I hand out maybe 14 sentence cards, teams of three or four sort them into true / false / neither, with a house rule that everything they put in "true" also gets a token on the FINDABLE tray, the game's founding stipulation being that whatever is true is at least possible to find out. Some cards are ordinary, but some are things like "the exact number of dust motes in this room right now is odd", "Caesar sneezed the morning he crossed the Rubicon", and "some star that burned out before there were humans had exactly four planets" — and I want the room to squirm, because they don't want to say those are false and they don't want to commit a token for them either. Then the main event: each team has to propose a sentence they'll all agree is true but that nobody in the room knows, and a set of ledger rules I introduce as mere bookkeeping — knowing a conjunction means knowing each conjunct, knowing something puts you in a position to know that you know it, and the standing assumption that at least one card in the ledger stays face-down — should mechanically spit out a contradiction from their own proposal. I want the derivation written on the board by the students, not by me, and I want the point of maximum pain to be a "sacrifice auction" where teams bid on which house rule to abandon and have to argue what abandoning it costs them. Then a seperate third round if there's time: let them try to rescue findability by restricting it to a subclass of sentences (something like the decidable or possibly-knowable ones — I think the word people use is Cartesian) and let the other teams attack the restriction. I vaguely remember reading that restricted versions can be made to misfire anyway, roughly because the awkward sentence you need to build still gets admitted by the restriction, and it would be great if the students hit that themselves rather than me asserting it from the front.

What I actually need from you: the full card list with a note on which cards are traps and why; team sizes and roles; an integer point/token scheme that works without real money; a printable materials list; a minute-by-minute timeline for the 75 minutes; the exact wording I should say out loud for each ledger rule so they all sound like harmless bookkeeping; a facilitator script for the board work, including what to do if the room stalls or just declares the whole thing bogus; the auction rules; debrief questions that map each moment of the game onto the corresponding step of the proof and onto the real moves available in response (restrict the principle, drop KK, drop distribution over conjunction, go intuitionist, bite the bullet and say we know everything true); what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it — I already know someone's going to say "findable doesn't mean findable by us this afternoon, it means findable by somebody eventually", so build in a challenge rule for that; and finally an honest paragraph on which feature of the argument the game just can't reproduce, that I'll have to supply by hand. Also tell me how to handle it if nobody will accept the founding stipulation in the first place, because then the whole thing dies inside five minutes. No reading list, no citations, no suggestions for further background — I just want something I could run next Tuesday with index cards and a whiteboard.
