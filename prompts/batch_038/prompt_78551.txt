I am drafting a chapter for an edited collection on literature and social choice theory, and I have reached a crux I would like pressure-tested before I commit it to print. The chapter reads *Measure for Measure* against Sen's impossibility of a Paretian liberal. I know the standard terrain — the unrestricted-domain, Pareto, and minimal-liberalism conditions; Sen's two-reader example with the single copy of the racy novel, where the protected pairs generate a cycle; Nozick's reply that rights are side constraints rather than inputs to a social welfare functional; the distinction, pressed by Sen himself in later work and by Gibbard in rejoinder, between a right as a constraint on realized outcomes and a right as a protected move in a game form. What I want help with is whether my reconstruction of the play in those terms actually holds, because on my own working-through it produces something other than what I expected.

Here is the reconstruction. Take Isabella and Claudio as the two rights-holders and specify social states over two variables: whether Claudio lives (L or D) and whether Isabella's vow is broken (V or I). So x = (L, I), y = (L, V), z = (D, I), w = (D, V). Isabella's protected pairs are those differing only in her own conduct, {x, y} and {z, w}; she ranks x ≻ y and z ≻ w, and decisiveness yields x P y and z P w. Claudio's are those differing only in whether he lives, {x, z} and {y, w}; he ranks x ≻ z and y ≻ w, yielding x P z and y P w. Angelo is a meddlesome third party with no protected sphere: he ranks y above x, and once the assignation has happened he prefers z to everything. Pareto adds x P w.

The result, as far as I can tell, is not a cycle but a hole. Every pair is ordered except {y, z}, which is Isabella's conduct and Claudio's life changing together and so lies in neither protected sphere, and on which Pareto is silent because she ranks z ≻ y while he ranks y ≻ z. The one comparison the play spends its third act staging — should he die, or should she yield — is precisely the comparison that liberalism plus Pareto cannot make. Angelo then decides it by force, from outside both spheres; and the disguised Duke decides it by altering the feasible set, restoring the separability Angelo had destroyed (his offer bundles her conduct with her brother's fate) by inserting Mariana. My chapter's thesis is that this is an escape only at a price: it works by commandeering Angelo's own protected pair over his conduct, and it is nearly derailed by Barnardine, who refuses to supply the head — a man asserting a prerogative over his own life against a change every other relevant agent prefers, until the plan is rescued by substituting Ragozine, a corpse with no preferences at all and therefore no sphere.

Please tell me where this goes wrong. Specifically: (1) Is the hole genuinely the output of the Sen structure, or have I mis-specified the spheres so as to manufacture it — in particular, is it coherent to treat a novitiate's vow as a protected sphere rather than as the alienation of one? (2) Does excluding Angelo as a meddlesome preference-holder already dispose of the difficulty, and is that a principled domain restriction or an ad hoc one? (3) Does the Barnardine episode show that the Pareto condition is doing covert work in fixing whose preferences count? (4) Where does the play resist the reading — I am thinking of the Duke's unanswered proposal to Isabella, which looks like the same bundling offered again as the price of a public reconciliation — and what does the play add to the debate that argument alone does not?

Begin with a bottom line of no more than three sentences: does the reconstruction survive, and what should the chapter's thesis be? Then the argument, then the single strongest objection to it and one sentence in reply. Keep the whole response under 1,100 words, use the compact notation above for preference orderings rather than prose, and do not quote the play — I have the text open and want analysis, not citation.
