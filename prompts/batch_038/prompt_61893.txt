I am drafting the methodological chapter of a doctoral thesis in conservation science. One of the tools I rely on is Bayesian updating under ambiguous detection data: field surveyors record whether a species was probably present at a site, but no record is fully certain, so I update on weighted sets of possibilities rather than on propositions I become sure of. To explain the method to a non-specialist readership, I borrowed heavily from an introductory essay in a collected volume on uncertainty, which runs as follows:

'Bayes tells you what to do when experience makes you certain: become sure of E, and let your new degrees of belief be your old conditional degrees of belief given E. The trouble is that experience almost never makes anyone certain. You glimpse a bird through rain-streaked glass and come away 0.7 confident it was a goldfinch; you read a survey record and end up only partly persuaded that the species was present. A Bayesian who insists on certainty is driven to pretend that what you really became sure of is some proposition about how things seemed to you — that there was a goldfinch-ish appearance — and to conditionalise on that instead. Jeffrey thought this evasive, and his alternative was to drop the certainty requirement altogether.

The rule is simple. Take a set of mutually exclusive possibilities that your experience bears on, assign them new probabilities, and let the rest of your belief system be dragged along by your old conditional probabilities: new(H) = old(H|E) × q + old(H|not-E) × (1 − q), in the two-case version, where q is the new probability you assign to E. There is one further condition, which Jeffrey called rigidity: your old conditional probabilities given E, and given not-E, must be the same as your new ones. Rigidity is not derivable from the probability calculus or from anything else; Jeffrey conceded that it is a convenient assumption, and that leaves the rule looking less like a demand of rationality than like one modelling option among others.

Two consequences deserve emphasis. Because the new degrees of belief are fixed entirely by your old conditionals and the new weights, Jeffrey conditionalisation is order-independent: given several uncertain observations to absorb, it makes no difference in which order you absorb them, and the result depends only on the weights you end up with. And because nothing has to be certain, the shift from old to new probabilities need not be justified by any proposition you have come to know — you may simply find your probabilities changing. That is the spirit of Jeffrey's radical probabilism: there is no privileged class of certainties on which all learning must rest, and probability theory governs how beliefs hang together rather than dictating what to believe. The upshot for a familiar puzzle is immediate. The problem of old evidence is that a theory cannot be confirmed by evidence you were already certain of; but if belief can move without anyone becoming certain of anything, a Bayesian can allow a hypothesis to gain support from evidence it already had.'

The passage persuaded me when I read it, and I only grew uneasy when I tried to use it. What I would like is an accuracy audit rather than a restatement.

Please begin with a short bottom line: is this a broadly trustworthy guide with some loose phrasing, or does it mislead in ways that matter? Then work through it claim by claim, saying for each substantive claim whether it is (i) standard and correct as a Bayesian epistemologist would state it, (ii) a defensible simplification that nonetheless loses something worth flagging, or (iii) mistaken, or a conflation of two theses that the view keeps apart. Where a claim is mistaken, give the correct version and say what depends on getting it right — I want to know not just that the essay errs somewhere, but how much of its argument survives the corrections.

Two things I have already done with the passage are now in draft, and both need a verdict.

First, my site-visits are recorded out of chronological order, and it was convenient to re-order them so that all the high-confidence detections were processed before the ambiguous ones. I justified this to myself on the strength of the essay's remark about order-independence. Is that justification sound? If it is not, tell me whether the sequence in which I feed in my updates is a presentational matter or something that changes my final probability assignments, and whether there is any restricted condition under which re-ordering would be harmless in a model like mine.

Second, a reader of an earlier draft objected that I have simply assumed my conditional probabilities stay fixed through each update, and that this assumption is doing the substantive work in the model. My planned reply was that, as the essay says, the assumption is a conceded convenience with no independent support — a modelling choice rather than a rational requirement, and so not something I owe a defence of. Before I send that reply, tell me whether it is the right one: is the assumption the essay describes as groundless in fact groundless, and what would a philosopher of probability say to someone who treats it as a free parameter?

Finally, does the closing move — from the absence of certainty to a solution of the problem of old evidence — follow from anything true in the passage?
