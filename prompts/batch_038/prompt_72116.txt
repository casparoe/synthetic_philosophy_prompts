I'm auditing a philosophy of science seminar this term and I keep getting dragged back to one episode, so I figured I'd throw it at you rather than at my housemate again. It's the N-ray affair. As I remember it: in 1903 Prosper-René Blondlot, a well-regarded physicist at Nancy, announced a new form of radiation he'd stumbled on while trying to work out whether X-rays could be polarized; the detection method was essentially a human eye in a darkened room judging whether an electric spark or a dim phosphorescent screen looked very slightly brighter than it had a moment ago. Over the next year or so something like 120 scientists published on it, most of them apparently reporting the same barely-there brightening, while Kelvin, Crookes, Lummer, Rubens and others just couldn't see anything. Nature eventually sent Robert W. Wood over in 1904; during a demonstration he quietly took the prism out of the apparatus (and on another occasion swapped a file that was supposed to be emitting for an inert bit of wood), the observers still called out the usual readings, and his account in Nature more or less ended the thing outside Nancy, though Blondlot himself reportedly never gave it up. So the evidence base was entirely made of marginal perceptual calls of the form "I'm maybe 70% sure it brightened," never "the counter clicked."

That's the case I want you to run through Jeffrey conditionalization / probability kinematics. I know the standard textbook framing — Jeffrey's cloth-under-shifting-light example, the move from certainty to a distribution over the partition of the observation, the rigidity or constancy condition, the update P'(H) = Σ P(H|Ei)P'(Ei) — and I don't need it re-explained from scratch. What I want is the mapping onto N-rays, and then the reverse direction, where the episode puts pressure on the theory. Specifically: does the N-ray episode actually vindicate Jeffrey, in the sense that his rule is the right description of what those observers were doing and what they should have been doing, when a naive conditionalization-on-an-observation-report model would have been absurd (there was never any fact of the matter to assign probability 1 to)? Then the harder bit: where does the analysis break? It looks to me like the rigidity condition is exactly what fails over those eighteen months, because what the Nancy observers would have inferred from "it brightened" drifted as the phenomenon was elaborated — which would mean the rule wasn't even well-defined for them. And Wood's prism trick seems to be a different kind of learning altogether, evidence about the reliability of the perceptual channel rather than evidence delivered through it, and I can't see how Jeffrey's rule is supposed to absorb that without some second-order machinery over channel trustworthiness. So: could a hierarchical or higher-order-uncertainty treatment do better, and is that a friendly extension of Jeffrey or a rival to him? Also worth addressing: why did the accumulation of hundreds of these low-confidence sightings never wash out toward skepticism, given that the observers' own confidence kept rising — is that a bug in the model, a bug in the priors, or a fact about the sociology that no individual-agent update rule can capture? And finally, tell me plainly where the theory is silent: I suspect the real lesson of N-rays is that perceptual learning rules take the observation partition and its input probabilities as given and have nothing to say about when a channel should be abandoned entirely, but I want you to either defend or dismantle that suspicion rather than just agreeing with it.

One request about how you pitch it: please keep a clear line between what's settled among people who work on this and what's genuinely contested, and don't smuggle a contested position in as the consensus. I know the mechanics of Jeffrey's rule are not up for debate, but whether it's the uniquely correct rule, whether rigidity is a substantive assumption or a convenient idealization, whether the N-ray case is a counterexample or just an unmodelled mess — those are live, and I'd rather you flag the fault lines than paper over them. Same for the history: if you think I've got any of the facts above wrong or oversimplified (I'm working from memory and a lecture), say so, but don't let it derail the analysis.
