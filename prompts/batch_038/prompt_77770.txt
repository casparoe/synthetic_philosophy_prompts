I write the ideas-and-science stuff for a mid-size magazine — long features about how people argue over evidence — and right now I'm deep in a piece on why newsrooms keep reaching for "give both sides equal space" and how often that goes sideways. Somewhere around 2am I fell down a rabbit hole on Jain epistemology, anekāntavāda, syādvāda, the whole many-sidedness apparatus, because it looked like the most sophisticated thing anyone has ever built for the exact problem my piece is about. My editor loves it. She wants the piece to open on the blind men and the elephant — one grabs the trunk and calls it a snake, another grabs the leg and calls it a tree — and then use it as the spine of the argument that no single account of a contested question gets to call itself flat-out true, so the reporter's job is to hold all the partial views side by side and let the reader assemble the animal. A Jain studies person I interviewed pushed back hard: she said the story does turn up in medieval Jain writing under its own name, but the version in my editor's head — the one with the sighted guy showing up at the end and the warm moral about tolerance — is mostly a 19th-century American poem's contribution, and that it's been doing close to the opposite of the doctrine's work ever since.

So can you audit the analogy for me? I want the mapping laid out explicitly, a table is fine: what the elephant, each blind man, the touching, the quarrel, and that sighted narrator each correspond to in the actual doctrine — and which of those slots the doctrine doesn't even have. Then tell me where the mapping snaps. A few places I've half-spotted and want you to either confirm or kill. One: in the parable the disagreement is about *parts* of a single animal, each man sampling a different limb, whereas the Jain examples I've read are about the *same* thing under different standpoints — a clay pot that's permanent as substance and impermanent as mode, that sort of thing — which feels like a structurally different kind of plurality, more like a shift in the respect under which you describe something than a gap in your sampling. Two: in the parable the blind men are wrong, or at best misleading, while syādvāda seems to insist a properly conditioned claim is *true* rather than merely excusable. Three: that sighted narrator. Doesn't he reintroduce the single complete view the doctrine is supposed to deny — and doesn't he assume you can build the whole animal by stacking up enough partial touches, which I thought an inexhaustable-aspects view of objects rules out for everyone short of omniscience? Four: the parable has a Buddhist pedigree too, and in the older version I ran into the men end up beating each other up while the teller is basically laughing at them, so the moral is "these people are blind fools," not "everyone's partly right." Which tradition's moral is my editor inheriting, and does the difference actually matter for how the analogy behaves?

Then the part I care about most: separate the disanalogies that genuinely break the intended use from the ones that are just pedantry, and give me a verdict. Her conclusion is roughly that in a contested science story nobody gets to call a claim simply false, and that the reporter should present partial views as co-equal and unranked. Is there any version of that the analogy can actually carry — maybe something weaker, or something with a sorting principle attached, since the tradition apparently distinguishes a legitimate standpoint from a mere one-eyed insistence on it, and has opinions about which standpoints are admissible at all? Or was the parable doing all the work the whole time, in which case I should cut it and find a different spine. And if it really can't be salvaged, what's a better analogy for what the doctrine claims — same rhetorical pull, but one that doesn't smuggle in an omniscient spectator or a sum-of-parts picture of truth? Don't bother pointing me at sources or further reading, I have more of that than I can use — I want the reasoning.
