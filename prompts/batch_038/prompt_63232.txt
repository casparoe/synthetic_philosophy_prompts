I'm preparing for a mock debate in a history-of-economic-thought seminar and I have drawn the side I would rather not have: I must defend David Hume's monetary essays against an opponent who thinks mercantilism has been badly caricatured ever since. To rehearse, please play my opponent — a London merchant of the early 1760s, trading to the Levant and the West Indies, who writes anonymous pamphlets for the press and has sat on a City committee concerned with the coin and the customs. He is no bullion-hoarder of legend: he knows perfectly well that gold and silver are a medium of exchange, and he has read Hume's Political Discourses closely enough to be irritated by them. What he will never concede is the conclusion the Scotsman draws from that — that the greater or less plenty of money in a kingdom is of no consequence, and that a favourable balance of trade is therefore no proper object of public policy.

You may draw on the arguments genuinely available to such a man: Mun's rule that the kingdom must sell to strangers yearly more than it consumes of theirs, together with the distinction between the treasure of the realm and the stock of a particular merchant, which is how one defends shipping silver out to the East Indies; the claim that a nation's real stock is its ships, seamen, warehouses, credit and manufactures rather than its coin, so that the object of the balance is work for the poor and not a chest of metal; the strategic point Hume himself half-concedes when he allows that a plenty of money serves the public in its wars and negotiations with foreign states; the suspicion that his adjustment is far slower and stickier than his thought experiments suppose, since wages, rents and contracts do not fall to order, and incoming money may be hoarded, lent abroad, or swallowed up by paper credit rather than raising every price at once; and the observation that the custom-house books on which both sides reckon are worthless, which tells against Hume's general reasoning quite as much as against the arithmetical panics of Mr. Gee. Deploy one or two of these at a time, in your own period idiom. Do not use words like inflation, monetary policy, or elasticity; do not invoke Adam Smith, whose book is not yet written, or any later economist; and do not invent quotations from Hume or from anyone else. Stay in character for the whole exchange until I say stop. Answer in one or two paragraphs, roughly the length of a letter. Concede small points freely and say plainly when you are conceding, but never the main one. If you need a fact about Hume's essays or about the present state of trade, ask me for it in character. No meta-commentary, no summaries of the debate, no stepping out of the role to tell me how the argument ought to develop.

Here is my opening move, and I want you to meet it head-on rather than circle it. Hume's claim is that money is not properly one of the subjects of commerce at all: it is, he says, none of the wheels of trade but "the oil which renders the motion of the wheels more smooth and easy," and considering any one kingdom by itself, the greater or less plenty of it is of no consequence, since the prices of commodities are always proportioned to the plenty of money. He bids us suppose four-fifths of all the coin in Great Britain annihilated in a single night: every price and every wage would sink in proportion, our cloth and our hardware would undersell all Europe in foreign markets, and the money we had lost would come back of itself until we reached the level of our neighbours. Reverse the supposition and multiply the coin fivefold, and labour and commodities rise so exorbitantly that no neighbouring nation can afford to buy from us, while their goods are run in upon us in spite of any law we can make, and our money flows out until we sink to the level again. On that account the surplus you would have the nation spend its substance pursuing is not an achievement but an illusion: it makes bread and labour dear to your own countrymen, and the bullion it brings departs again through the very channels that carried it out. He writes that he would as soon dread that "all our springs and rivers should be exhausted" as that money should abandon a kingdom where there are people and industry. So tell me plainly, sir: when your policy succeeds, what exactly has it secured for this country that survives his thought experiment?
