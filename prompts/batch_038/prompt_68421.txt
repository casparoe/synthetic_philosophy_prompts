I facilitate a Saturday local-history workshop for about 16 adults — a mix of amateur genealogists, two people who work at a county record office, and a couple of schoolteachers. Two and a half hours, one room, no projector, pens and paper are fine, and I'm printing everything at home so keep the materials cheap.

I want an activity that makes them feel the collision between a community's memory and the documentary record — and feel that neither side can just trump the other. Set it in an invented town so nobody's actual relatives are on trial. Something like: the town is putting up a plaque about a factory fire or an eviction in the early 60s, and the participants have to agree the wording.

Here's the mechanic I want you to build out. Each small group gets an envelope of source cards: some are documents (a council minute, a page from a ledger, a newspaper paragraph, the caption on a photograph), some are transcripts of interviews recorded decades ago with people who are now dead. They should contradict each other in a specific, non-obvious way — not "one says twelve died, the other says twenty," but the sort of contradiction where the documents are precise, dated and quietly self-serving while the testimonies are warm, vivid and mutually inconsistent. Please write out the actual text of every card; thats the bulk of the deliverable and it needs to be good enough that the conflict isn't spotted in the first five minutes.

Then the plaque gets a hard word limit, so they physically cannot keep everything, plus a rule that every claim on it has to be traceable to a card in the envelope. Halfway through I hand them a second envelope — one more document, surfaced late, which undercuts whatever reading they'd settled on. I want that moment to land properly.

Also tell me whether any participants should get roles (a descendant with standing to object? someone whose job is to defend the record? a chair who has to sign off?) or whether that just derails a room of record-office people. Your call, but say why.

Debrief afterwards, mapping particular moments back onto the ideas: the memory that lives in people and dies out within a few generations versus the archived kind that survives but only in the shape institutions happened to keep it in; and the fact that a gap in the archive is not neutral evidence about what happened. Say which of those the cards can actually stage and which they can't.

Two more things. What predictably goes wrong the first time you run something like this, and what do I do about it in the moment. And be honest about which feature of the memory-versus-record problem the whole setup just can't reproduce and that I'll have to supply by talking. Nobody should hear the phrase collective memory until the debrief.
