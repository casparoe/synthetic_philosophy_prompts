For fifteen years I have been revising the same small company of characters across novels, stories, and abandoned drafts, and I have noticed that my judgments about when the character in one version is the character of another do not form an obvious pattern. I should like to know what principle, if any, they add up to — and in particular whether that principle says one and the same character exists in each version, differing only in which of her properties vary, or whether each version contains a character of its own, related to the others by something like counterpart relations whose weighting shifts with what I happen to be asking at the time.

Here are my verdicts. When I rewrote a woman's entire biography between drafts, so that nothing of her history survived but her name and her temper, I think it is the same woman, merely corrected. When I changed only her name and left everything else intact, it is obviously the same woman. When, in a chapter set in the world where my protagonist never went to war, I wrote a crueller and happier man, I do not think that man is my protagonist, though I wrote him precisely to show what my protagonist might have been. When another writer, with my blessing, continued my character under her own name and history but gave her a temperament I could never have given her, I think that is not her — and yet she descends from mine in a way the war-less protagonist does not, which is what makes me uneasy, since her origin seems preserved while the character is not. When I placed a woman of the same name and incompatible histories in two novels and afterwards declared, in an author's note, that they were one woman who had lied, I think I made them one.

Is there a single criterion of character-identity across versions and works that delivers all of these? State it precisely, with the clause that earns its keep in each case, and say what it implies for cases I have not raised: a translation in which the name has been altered, an unfinished manuscript whose thirty-year-old contradicts the fifty-year-old of the published book, a production in which two actresses alternate in the role. Then tell me plainly which of my verdicts it cannot save, and whether the price of saving all of them is a criterion too baroque to be worth holding.
