My colleague Marguerite does these informal "credence audits" for people in our department. She's a hardline accuracy-first type — thinks the only thing that can be wrong with a belief state is that it loses accuracy — and she's graded maybe thirty of them now, always just a verdict and one sentence of reason, never the underlying rule. I want to predict what she'll say about the next one before I hand it over, mostly to check whether I've reverse-engineered her correctly.

Her verdicts so far:

- A postdoc whose probabilities over four mutually exclusive, exhaustive outcomes summed to 0.86. "Dominated — someone can beat you in all four worlds." Repair it.
- Another postdoc whose three-way partition summed to 1.002. Also repair it. When he said the gap was too small to matter she said the size of the gap never matters, and added that none of her verdicts depend on which strictly proper score you happen to prefer — quadratic, log, spherical, same answers.
- Someone who'd read a lab slip but wasn't sure she'd read the number right (she put 0.9 on having read it correctly) and who spread her credence across the readings rather than going all-in. Marguerite said that was exactly right, and wouldn't hear the complaint that she should just have settled what the slip said.
- Someone who was dead certain of his evidence — held the signed letter, good light, no reason to doubt it, probability one on it — but kept 0.9 on having gotten the job. Repair it: if your evidence is E at probability one, anything short of full conditionalization on E is dominated.
- A forecaster told by three people she respects that her method runs systematically overconfident. She didn't budge and Marguerite backed her: by her own lights she's more accurate than they think she is, so there's no accuracy-based reason to defer.
- A guy who put 0 on each of infinitely many possible outcomes while insisting one of them would happen. Marguerite said he'd assigned zero to something he was sure would occur and told him to spread the mass. He protested that nothing he could ever observe would catch him out. She wasn't moved.
- Someone who said she simply had no view about whether a colleague would get tenure — no credence at all, nothing. Marguerite declined to grade it: "there's nothing to score." She also refused to say whether forming a view would be an improvement on not having one.

The new case, which is the one I actually care about: a resident who says she has credence exactly 1 that the scan showed no metastisis. She read it herself, twice, with a radiologist in the room. She'll admit out loud that she could in principle be wrong but says she has no actual reason to think she is. She's coherent everywhere else, she conditionalizes when her evidence is certain and spreads it when it isn't, and she's fully prepared to ignore anyone who tells her she's overconfident. She's also pre-loaded two counter-moves: if Marguerite tells her to drop to 0.9999 she wants to know what principle stops the slide to 0.999999, and if credence 1 is off the table for contingent claims she'd rather withdraw the opinion entirely — which she points out Marguerite has already conceded she can't grade.

So what does Marguerite tell her? I'd like the candidate rules that are consistent with everything above — I can see at least two, one where the verdicts are all just about domination and one where something else is doing work in the background — and what each implies for the resident. Then your best guess and the reason you'd bet on it, which of her earlier remarks is load-bearing for that guess, and one further case I could bring her that would separate the rules cleanly. And separately: if she does rule against the resident, which of the earlier verdicts does she have to soften or give up to stay consistent with herself?
