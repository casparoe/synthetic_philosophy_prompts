ok so this has been bugging me for like two months and my bio teacher basically shrugged when I asked, so maybe you can actually answer it. Last year I did a science fair project on jumping spiders — built little mazes, filmed them, ran trials for three months — and I did not have to fill out a single ethics form. My friend who did a project on zebrafish needed approval from our school's review committee plus a vet's signature. The reason, according to the ISEF rules our fair follows, is that "cephalopods are to be treated as vertebrate animals," so anything with a backbone plus octopus/squid/cuttlefish gets reviewed and every other invertebrate is just... free game? I used forty-ish spiders and nobody blinked. And the rules get weirdly specific about where a vertebrate's protection starts: bird and reptile eggs count from 72 hours before hatching, and zebrafish only count from 7 days after fertilization, apparently because of "delayed cognitive neural development." So there's an actual hour count at which an animal supposedly switches over into mattering.

Then I went looking for grown-up versions of the same line and found the UK, which somehow confused me more. The Animal Welfare (Sentience) Act 2022 defines an animal, for its purposes, as any vertebrate except humans, plus any cephalopod mollusc, plus any decapod crustacean. So lobsters, crabs, crayfish and shrimp are legally sentient. Bees, wasps, ants, spiders, flies and worms are not, even though there's a ton of work on bee learning and navigation and a whole live debate about insect consciousness. The Secretary of State can bring more invertebrates into the definition later by regulation, but only by adding, never subtracting. Meanwhile the older UK law covering animal experiments only protects vertebrates plus cephalopods — so a crab in a lab needs no oversight at all even though the sentience law says crabs are sentient, and an octopus needs full review. And the sentience law doesn't actually ban anything: it sets up a committee that may publish a report on whether the goverment had "all due regard" to ways a policy might harm animal welfare given that animals are sentient, ministers respond, and the committee's recommendations are required by statute to respect provisions and customs relating to "religious rites, cultural traditions and regional heritage."

I'm not asking whether spiders are conscious. I'm asking what all this machinery assumes, because the assumptions don't look like they came from anywhere in particular. Is there one underlying idea about which animals are conscious and how you could tell, sitting underneath both sets of rules, or is it a pile of separate compromises that only looks like a system? Does drawing the line by taxonomic group presuppose that sentience is a switch that flips somewhere on the family tree, or is the line really about how much evidence there is — in which case why are insects, with decades of learning and navigation research, left out while shrimp get in? What does treating a cephalopod "as a vertebrate" presuppose: an admission that the rules can only think in terms of vertebrate-ness, or a deliberate legal fiction that's fine as long as it does the protective work? Does the zebrafish 7-day rule assume consciousness has a datable onset readable off neuroanatomy, and is that something anyone should believe? Does exempting everything else assume absence of evidence is evidence of no experience, or does it just assume it's acceptable to gamble on animals that probably don't matter? And what does the deference-to-tradition clause say about the moral status being granted — that animal interests are one consideration to be weighed against others rather than something that can't be traded off at all? Please separate what these rules strictly require you to believe from what merely fits them comfortably, and flag anywhere they're loose enough that completely different theories of animal minds would generate the exact same policy. Then tell me what would actually have to change, in the fair rules and in the law, if you swapped the criterion for something like "any animal that shows avoidance learning or can be conditioned," or for a purely precautionary one that says include everything you can't rule out. And lastly — was my spider exemption defensible, or is three months of my own watching worth nothing as evidence?
