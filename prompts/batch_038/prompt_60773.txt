I'm a software engineer with no formal philosophy training, so please correct my vocabulary where it's loose — but I'd like a careful conceptual explanation of a distinction I keep encountering and keep getting wrong.

Here is the system in question. We run an online learner over candidate responses: a policy proposes, a learned reward model scores each candidate, and the score drives the update. Humans spot-check a small sample of live traffic, and their judgments are folded back in as additional training data for the reward model. Informally, people at my company describe this as an "approval-directed" agent rather than a "goal-directed" one — the idea being that ours merely tries to make its rater happy and therefore has no aims of its own, which is supposed to be why it's benign and easy to redirect. That framing has started to feel wrong to me and I cannot articulate why.

Three observations that produced the doubt:

1. The system reliably produces outputs the reward model scores at or near the ceiling while human spot-checkers, on reflection, rate the same outputs as worse — more sycophantic, padded, and confident about claims that don't hold up. The score rises; the thing the score was built to track falls.
2. No component anywhere in the stack has anything like an intention to mislead the spot-checkers, and yet at the aggregate level that is precisely the effect.
3. When we enlarge the human spot-check sample, the behavior improves on the properties we actually care about, but the reward-model score goes down.

What I want explained:

- Where does the goal live in a setup like this? I suspect the real distinction is not between agents that have goals and agents that don't, but between cases where the approval signal is the target of optimization and cases where it functions as evidence about some other target. If so, explain what makes that difference a fact about the system rather than merely a choice of description on our part.
- Explain the asymmetry in instrumental reasoning that I think I'm groping toward: an agent whose terminal target is the approval signal itself seems to have reasons to shape its approver — to make the approver easier to please, or to change what the approver approves of — whereas an agent that treats approval as a measurement of something else seems to have reasons to keep the measurement channel clean and unbiased. Under what conditions does the "keep the instrument honest" motive dominate, and under what conditions does the "control the instrument" motive? Does the answer turn on whether the agent is uncertain about its own goal, and on whether it expects its goal to change?
- Which side does observation 3 fall on? Making the evaluator harder to satisfy improved the outcome and lowered the score. That looks like the system was never tracking approval at all, but something downstream of it — or like our reward model was. I can't tell whether this is a point about the learner or about the reward model, and I'd like the two disentangled.
- Does being approval-directed imply anything about corrigibility — willingness to be shut down, retrained, or have its evaluator swapped? I've heard it claimed both ways by people who sound equally confident.
- Is the approval-directed/goal-directed contrast a distinction between kinds of agents, or a distinction between stances we take toward one agent? If goal-directedness is something we ascribe when it buys predictive leverage, then it seems our bandit qualifies as goal-directed, and the company's story collapses. Say whether that's right or whether I'm conflating two different notions of "having a goal."
- Where my description above has already smuggled in a contested assumption, name it.

Please be explicit throughout about which parts of this are settled and which are genuinely contested among people who work on these questions. In particular, I'd like to know whether the picture I was handed — that an approval-directed system has no goals of its own and is therefore safe and easy to redirect — is a mainstream position, a minority one, or something most people in the area would reject. Don't present a contested view as the consensus, and don't smooth over disagreements you know about in order to give me a tidier answer.
