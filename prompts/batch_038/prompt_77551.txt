Write a poem about the asymmetry between finding a solution and checking one — the intuition underneath the open question of whether everything that can be verified quickly can also be found quickly — under the constraints below.

**Occasion.** The speaker is a charge nurse building next month's ward roster by hand at a kitchen table after a shift. The constraints are ordinary and human: one nurse cannot work nights, two cannot be paired after something that happened in March, someone has claimed a particular weekend, everyone is owed a minimum stretch of rest between shifts, and the ward cannot be left short on any day. Give the reader enough of these to feel their weight, but do not let the poem become an inventory.

**Form.** Five stanzas of 1, 2, 4, 8, and 16 lines — 31 lines in total — every one of them in iambic tetrameter. Each stanza handles roughly twice as many people and days as the one before it, so that the growth of the poem is the growth of the problem. The meter, by contrast, never changes: the cost of a single line stays fixed while the number of lines doubles. That contrast — bounded per step, unbounded in total — is the argument the form is making, and it must be felt rather than announced. Rhyme is at your discretion, but if you rhyme, do not let the rhyme steer the sense.

**Closure.** The sixteen-line final stanza must run as a single unbroken utterance with no full stop until its last line, and that last line must repeat, word for word, the single line that opened the poem; the punctuation may differ, so that what stood alone as an assertion returns as a clause inside a much longer sentence. The effect I am after: the thing the speaker would have recognized in a glance had it been handed to her at the outset is what the search eventually arrives at, and the reader has just paid for it by reading thirty lines.

**Accuracy.** The concept must be right in these specific respects:

- The difficulty is not impossibility. Nothing here is beyond her in principle; a workable roster exists, and for four nurses she could have found one in an afternoon. What fails is the fit between the size of the problem and the time a life allows. The stanzas should show that transition from manageable to unmanageable rather than assert it.
- The gap is between search and verification. The opening line should be the sort of thing she can check instantly — the shape of an answer she cannot compose — while everything that follows is the composing.
- The blow-up is a worst-case, large-scale phenomenon, not a claim that every instance is hard. A reader who works in this area should be able to tell that you know the difference between an input size that is merely inconvenient and one that is asymptotically hopeless.
- Do not settle whether the gap is permanent. In the last movement, leave genuinely open whether this is a fact about the world or a fact about the methods available to one person at midnight with a printed template.

**Diction.** Do not use the words polynomial, exponential, complexity, algorithm, intractable, NP, or P; do not name any theorem, complexity class, or person; no jargon of any kind. The concept has to survive entirely in the images, the roster, and the shape of the stanzas. Someone who knows the field should be able to say precisely what the poem is about, and someone who has never heard of it should simply find it a good poem about a hard night's work.

**Tone.** If the roster can carry a second, human resonance — the things we would know at once on hearing them and cannot produce on demand — let it, but keep that beneath the surface; do not state the analogy. The poem should not end in consolation or in a lesson about letting go. The exhaustion is the point, not a setup for relief.

Give it a title of no more than four words, and present the poem alone, with no gloss or commentary.
