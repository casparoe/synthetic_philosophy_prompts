I am an American doctoral student in political theory, spending this academic year in Edinburgh on a fellowship, and my project has drifted into an area I am not equipped for. A few weeks ago I watched the accession proceedings on television with a Scottish friend who is an elder in her kirk, and she pointed out things that had gone completely past me: that the same person, on effectively the same occasion, swears two religious oaths in two different registers. So let me lay out the arrangement as I now understand it, and then tell you what I want from you, because the puzzle I have is not historical or legal but philosophical. The sovereign is Supreme Governor of the Church of England, must be in communion with it, may not be a Catholic, and at coronation swears to maintain the Protestant Reformed religion established by law and to preserve the rights and privileges of the bishops and clergy; at accession the sovereign separately declares, in a statutory form, that he is "a faithful Protestant" and undertakes to uphold the enactments securing the Protestant succession. But he also swears, under the Acts of Union, a distinct oath to maintain the Protestant religion and Presbyterian church government in Scotland. The Church of Scotland is the national church there and yet denies that the sovereign has any supremacy over it: he attends its General Assembly as an ordinary member, and his personal representative, the Lord High Commissioner, sits in a gallery that is architecturally outside the Assembly hall, has no vote, may not intervene in any debate, and speaks only at the opening and the close — an arrangement deliberately built to embody the church's independence in spiritual matters from state interference. Meanwhile the Church of England legislates. Its General Synod passes Measures which, once both Houses of Parliament resolve to approve them and the sovereign assents, have the same force and effect as an Act of Parliament and may even amend or repeal Acts of Parliament; before they reach the floor, a joint committee of fifteen peers and fifteen commoners, the Ecclesiastical Committee, reports on the nature and legal effect of the Measure and on its expediency, "especially with relation to the constitutional rights of all His Majesty's subjects." Parliament may not amend such a Measure, only accept or reject it. So a text about liturgy, ordination, or church property is vetted for its bearing on constitutional rights by a secular legislature full of Catholics, Methodists, Muslims, Jews, Hindus, and atheists, twenty-six of whose members in the upper house are Anglican bishops who then vote on criminal justice and taxation with everyone else. And down at the parish level, the incumbent has a legal duty to marry the people who live in the parish and to bury its dead, whatever they believe or fail to believe; belonging is fixed by address rather than conviction, so a lifelong Anglican who happens to live one street over, inside the neighboring parish boundary, has no comparable claim on the building.

What I want is an account of what this practice presupposes, not a history of how it came about. Reconstruct the theory of religion — of what religion is, what belief is, what a church is, and what a state's relationship to either can consist in — that the whole machinery only makes sense under, and separate carefully what it strictly requires from what it merely sits comfortably with. Which rival accounts does the arrangement quietly rule out: religion as inward conviction that is invisible to law, the church as a voluntary association of believers funded by free gift, the state as having no competence in spiritual things at all, or the state as having a duty to identify and support the true religion? Then take up the coherence problem, because that is where my friend and I actually got stuck. Can one natural person bear both oaths, given that Presbyterian polity denies the office the English oath secures, or does the practice only work if the sovereign is understood as something like a corporation whose capacities do not die and to which different oaths attach? Is the territorial parish an implicit theory of religious belonging, or an administrative fossil that the theory is now retrofitted to? And is the Ecclesiastical Committee's rights review an assertion that Parliament can judge religious questions after all, or a way of insisting that it judges only their civil effects? Tell me plainly where the practice is underdetermined — where several incompatible theories would license exactly what is done, so that the institutional form carries less doctrinal content than it appears to — and where it is not. Finally, work out what would have to change, concretely and not just in principle, if the presupposed view were replaced by the ordinary American one, in which churches are private voluntary bodies that the state treats identically and never legislates for: which piece of the English machinery becomes unintelligible first, and what has to be given up to make it coherent.

Please treat my description as accurate enough to reason from; you have no way to check it and I have no interest in a bibliography, a list of authorities, or a set of further readings, since I cannot chase any of them and do not want citations I would have to take on faith. If something in what I have said looks materially wrong to you, say so in a sentence and then carry on with the analysis anyway. Write to me as one would write a letter: no headings, no bullet points, no numbered lists, no bold or italic emphasis, no tables, just continuous prose from start to finish. Length is not a constraint — take as much room as the argument needs — but the shape of the reasoning matters more than the coverage, so please make the dependencies explicit as you go, telling me which claim rests on which, rather than giving me a catalogue of interesting things about establishment.
