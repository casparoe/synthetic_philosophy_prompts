I'm teaching a seminar on constitutionalism next month — 16 students, mostly senior undergrads who've had one game theory course, one 100-minute session, room with movable tables. I want an activity where they run into the problem with Ulysses-style precommitment themselves instead of listening to me describe it. No lecture up front, and I don't want the word "constitution" to be doing any work until the debrief.

The bit of the story I care about is that the device works in two places at once, and everyone only ever talks about the first. Odysseus has himself lashed to the mast so his limbs can't obey him, and the rowers have wax in their ears so his begging cant reach them — and the standing order is that if he does get through to them and asks to be released, they're to tie him down harder. So the binding isn't only on the tempted self, it's on everybody who might be moved by the tempted self's protests. The second half is where I think the philosophy actually is, because at some point a third party has to decide whether the calm earlier instruction or the desperate present plea is the one that speaks for the guy. Build both halves into the game.

What I need from you:

- a first phase where players choose, in advance and without knowing which kind of round they're in, whether to buy a commitment device and how costly to make it to revoke
- a second phase where the temptation actually shows up and the revoke decision gets made in front of the others
- enforcement handled by other players rather than by me, with their own payoffs, so the enforcers are themselves temptable
- a token payoff scheme with real but tiny stakes (I'll convert tokens into a couple of participation points) and actual numbers I can write on the board
- and the twist I care about most: in some rounds the later self turns out to have been right. As I remember it, the song in the story isn't selling pleasure so much as knowledge — the singers claim to know everything that happens on the earth — so it's not obvious the man on the mast is deluded. I want rounds where the calm earlier self simply guessed wrong about what the later self would have good reason to want. Without that the game just teaches bind yourself, binding works, which is precisely what I'm trying to put in question.

Then a debrief that maps specific moments of play onto the distinctions I want them to leave with: being unable versus being unwilling; the gap between what the device was worth when they bought it and what it's worth once they're strapped in; and the dead hand problem, where an earlier self binds someone who has by then become a different person with different and possibly better reasons.

Also tell me honestly what will go wrong the first time I run it — I'm already braced for something around enforcement getting weaponized, and for one loud econ kid doing everybody's expected-value math out loud in phase one — and which feature of real constitutional precommitment a 100 minute game with 16 consenting adults simply cannot reproduce, so I know what I'll have to supply by hand afterwards.
