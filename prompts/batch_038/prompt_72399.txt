I'm trying to get a handle on the philosophy hiding inside official state apologies, and I don't want the generic take on it — you know, the one about healing and reconciliation. Here's the practice stripped down to a typical case.

A national legislature passes a resolution apologizing, in the name of the country, for something an earlier government did — usually decades or centuries back, often to a group that still exists as a community. Almost nobody in the chamber voted for the policy, and most weren't alive when it happened. The resolution gets read aloud, sometimes with survivors or their descendants in the gallery. It's normally paired with no compensation whatsoever, and it's drafted extremely carefully: the lawyers push for wording that expresses sorrow rather than admits fault, that goes passive about what was actually done, and that includes a clause to the effect that nothing here creates legal liability. Meanwhile the same legal system has statutes keeping a doctor's post-surgery I'm-sorry out of a malpractice suit, which suggests it knows perfectly well that apologizing and admitting fault are hard to pull apart.

So what account of agency, and of harm, is this machine running on? I want the threads separated rather than blended:

1. Who is the "we"? Does the practice need the state to be a real continuing agent — a corporate entity that survives complete turnover of its members — or could it get by on something weaker, like obligations inherited along a chain of individual people, or a fiction everyone agrees to act as if it were true? And what does the legislature gain by saying we apologize instead of just saying our predecessors did something monstrous?

2. Fault without a faulty agent. Ordinary apology seems to presuppose that the apologizer is the one who did it. Here nobody in the room did. Is the practice quietly separating something that a normal apology holds together — being the perpetrator versus being answerable? And does it hold up when the apologizing public includes people whose own ancestors were among the victims, or recent immigrants whose ancestors were nowhere near it? Does a state apology have to assume that citizenship itself carries the fault?

3. The remorse problem. No parliament feels anything. Sincerity conditions for everyday apologies usually involve some inner state. So what makes the state's apology sincere or hollow — is the practice tacitly committed to a dispositional account (sincere iff the state undertakes to repair, commemorate, not repeat), or has it just given up on sincerity, in which case why does everyone get so furious about insincere ones?

4. The addressee. It's delivered to a group, on behalf of dead people, received by descendants who weren't wronged themselves and who, in a lot of these cases, wouldnt exist had the wrong never happened. Does the practice need a non-comparative or historical notion of harm to survive that — harm as violated standing or a broken relation rather than as being worse off than you'd otherwise have been? And can a descendant accept an apology for what was done to an ancestor, or does accepting on someone else's behalf require groups to be the kind of thing that can be wronged and can forgive?

5. The compensation gap. If apology is essentially about repair, the resolution is empty ritual. If it's essentially about acknowledgment, it's not clear why the state's own lawyers fight so hard over every verb. Reconcile those two, or tell me the practice can't.

6. Contrast the refusal. Plenty of governments flatly refuse, arguing that you can't apologize for what you didn't do and no living citizen is to blame. Which of the two habits — apologize-without-paying, or refuse-because-innocent — is actually more philosophically coherent? And can one state do both, apologizing for one wrong and refusing another, without contradicting itself, which is roughly what happens in practice?

Then please separate what this practice strictly requires from what merely fits it comfortably, and point out where it's underdetermined — spots where several incompatible theories would license the exact same resolution. Finally, tell me what would concretely have to change in how these documents are drafted and delivered if you swapped in a different theory of collective responsibility, one where responsibility tracks causal contribution and inherited benefit rather than identity over time.

Write it in American English, please. No reading list or citations needed — I'm not going to chase sources, I want the argument.
