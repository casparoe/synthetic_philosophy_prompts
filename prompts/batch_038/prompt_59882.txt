I am drafting a novel whose antagonist is a decision theorist, and before I can write her convincingly I need to know how much damage her doctrine can absorb before it stops being a doctrine and becomes a tautology. Let me state her position as precisely as I can, because the precision is the whole point of the exercise.

She holds what I have come to call, in my own notes, the Redescription Thesis:

For any actual agent and any decision problem that agent actually faces, if the agent's preferences are not representable as the maximization of expected utility with respect to a single probability function and a single real-valued utility function over a fixed partition of states and outcomes, then that failure is never a fact about the agent. It is a fact about our description of the agent. Some refinement — a finer partition of states, a more finely individuated outcome space, a different slicing of the person into temporal parts or sub-agents, a shift to a framework in which acts are themselves propositions — will recover coherence. Consequently no observed pattern of choice, however intransitive, ambiguity-averse, or lexicographic, can ever count as evidence against expected utility theory. It can count only against a particular formalization of it.

What I want is the exposure of this position, not a defense of it and not a tour of the literature it sits in. Do not give me references, attributions, or a reading list; argue from the structure of the arguments, and assume I already know the standard paradoxes and the standard representation theorems well enough not to need them re-explained.

First: what would have to be true for the Redescription Thesis to be false? Not what critics have said, but what the world, or the mathematics, would have to look like for it to fail — the conditions under which the supply of refinements runs out.

Second: rank the candidate defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, and for each one tell me whether my character would concede it as damaging or dismiss it as a category error. Where the position is genuinely strong, say so plainly; I would rather have an honest map than a prosecution.

Third: tell me whether the thesis has been qualified into unfalsifiability, and whether that is a problem for a claim of this kind. It is not, on its face, an empirical hypothesis about human psychology but a claim about the practice of formalization — so does it come out immune by construction, and what does immunity cost it?

Fourth, and most important for my purposes: here are four cases I have invented for the book. For each, say whether the redescription actually succeeds, what it costs, and whether that cost is local or whether it recurs everywhere.

One. An elderly collector who prefers A to B on Monday, B to C on Tuesday, and C to A on Wednesday, and who has arranged her life to savor the cycling itself, willfully re-valuing as she goes. Her defender says the outcome space is not the individual objects but histories of choice, over which she is perfectly transitive. Is the enlarged outcome space a legitimate refinement, or does it purchase coherence only by defining the incoherence away — and can the same move be applied to any preference pattern whatsoever, which is precisely what would make the thesis empty?

Two. A monk who refuses an entire class of wagers at any stake however large, so that no real-valued utility represents him. His defender retreats to utilities taking infinitesimal values. Does that retreat still count as expected utility maximization, or has the theory changed its subject? Is there a preference structure that no ordered field of utilities could capture, and if there is not, does that fact help the thesis or merely relocate it?

Three. A woman whose state space must include propositions about her own future beliefs, so that her acts help determine which state obtains. Her defender shifts to a framework where acts and states are the same kind of object, so no independence constraint is needed. The price is that such a framework leaves the split between probability and desirability underdetermined unless an extra condition — something like unboundedness or an assumption of extreme preference — is imposed. Is paying that price consistent with the thesis's promise to recover both belief and desire, and is the underdetermination a defeater for the thesis or only for its claim to be about the agent rather than about the description?

Four. A character who binds her future self by contract in a way that changes what she wants, so that no single utility function spans both of her. Her defender treats her as two agents under a transfer scheme, each internally coherent. When does describing one person as many persons stop being a description and start being a surrender, and could the thesis state its stopping rule in advance, before seeing the data — or is the number of sub-agents always adjustable afterward?

Fifth: is there any conceivable circumstance in which one could demonstrate that the refinements terminate, rather than merely failing to find one? And would recognizing such an agent require already assuming the framework one is trying to test?

Last, the novelist's question. Choose the single defeater she must concede rather than reinterpret — the one that cannot be absorbed — and tell me what she would say in the scene where she says it.
