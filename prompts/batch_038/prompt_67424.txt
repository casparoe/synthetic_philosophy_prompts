Produce the joint statement that two epistemologists could both sign at the close of a year-long adversarial collaboration about whether knowledge is closed under known entailment. The first party is a tracking theorist in the tradition of Nozick's account: she holds that S knows that p only if S's belief that p, produced by the method M she actually used, would not have been produced had p been false, and she accepts that this condition forces the denial of single-premise closure. The second party accepts the motivation for a tracking condition but holds that no such condition may deliver the differential verdict on which S knows an ordinary proposition p, competently deduces q from p, and yet fails to know q; on his view the conditions must be reformulated — by re-individuating M, or otherwise — until closure is restored.

To keep the collaboration from relitigating familiar ground, the two have agreed to work through one new case and one new proposal, both stipulated below. Treat the stipulations as fixed; argue from them rather than around them, and do not quietly amend them to make either side's task easier.

**The case.** A warehouse loading door is fitted with a pressure mat wired to an alarm in the guard's booth. Each morning the guard steps on the mat, hears the alarm sound, and thereby forms the belief that the mat is working. That is his only route to the belief. He has never inspected the wiring, though the mat lifts out in seconds and a look at the cable would settle its condition. A thief can covertly fit a *bridge*: a device that can only be installed by cutting the mat's cable and that thereafter emits the alarm signal whenever it detects a footfall on the floor above it, so that stepping on the dead mat sounds the alarm exactly as before. Since fitting a bridge requires cutting the cable, the proposition *the mat is working* entails the proposition *no bridge is fitted*. Both parties stipulate that the nearest worlds in which the mat is not working are ordinary ones — a frayed cable, a failed contact — in which stepping on it produces no alarm at all, and that worlds in which a bridge has been secretly fitted are considerably more remote. In the actual world the mat is sound and no bridge is fitted.

**The proposal.** Call it the *Inheritance Rule*. Where S believes p by method M, p entails q, and S competently deduces q from p while recognizing the entailment, S's belief that q is credited not to M but to the composite method M-plus-deduction; and the tracking counterfactuals for the composite are evaluated over the nearest worlds in which the composite is used and q is false, which — because the composite reaches q only through p — are just the nearest M-worlds in which p is false. Hence whenever M tracks p, the composite tracks q, and single-premise closure holds by construction. The parties note, and do not dispute, the consequence: applied to the familiar vat case, the rule makes the belief that one is not an envatted handless creature inherit the tracking enjoyed by the belief that one has hands, so the tracking theorist would have to concede knowledge of precisely the proposition she has always been willing to hand to the sceptic.

Write the document they would produce, with these parts:

1. **Agreed claims.** A numbered list of everything both can sign: claims about the case, about what the Inheritance Rule entails, and about the respects in which the case does and does not resemble the standard sceptical scenario. Include whether the tracking conditions, applied literally under the stipulated similarity ordering and with M individuated as the method actually used, yield the differential verdict here — and, if both parties agree that they do, what each takes that agreement to show. At least four of the claims must be substantive enough that an informed bystander who had read neither party's work might have denied them; mere restatements of the stipulations do not count.

2. **Disagreements.** A table with one column for the subject, one giving the tracking camp's own formulation of its position, one giving the closure camp's own formulation of its position, one stating neutrally what is actually in dispute, and one recording whether the row is a genuine crux or a merely verbal dispute. Each party's formulation must be worded so that party would sign it unamended.

3. **Cruxes and pre-registered predictions.** For every row marked as a crux, name the argument, case, or variant that both parties accept as bearing on it, then record each side's prediction about it — specific enough that, were the collaboration repeated in a decade, one of them could be shown to have been wrong. Address at minimum: whether the guard would know that no bridge is fitted if the order were reversed — he lifts the mat, finds the cable intact, and only then steps on the mat and hears the alarm — and whether a theory whose verdict turns on the order of two morning actions is thereby condemned or vindicated; whether the bare fact that q could easily have been checked bears on whether he knows q by deduction from p; whether the Inheritance Rule's anti-sceptical consequence is a fatal cost or an overlooked benefit; and whether the tracking conditions' indifference to base rates surfaces as a defect in a variant in which the guard runs the same morning check on a thousand such mats and deduces that none of them has been bridged.

4. **Falsifiers.** For each camp, what it would take to give up its core commitment, stated as a verdict about a case or as a principle the camp would have to swallow.

5. **Verbal residue.** A short closing section stating which portions of the dispute the two have come to regard as nothing more than a quarrel over how finely to individuate methods, and which portions survive any such stipulation.

This is a joint statement, not an adjudication: declare no winner, weigh no camp against the other, and do not let one party's vocabulary colonize the neutral column. Keep the document self-contained — no citations, no reading list, no appeals to what any philosopher has already written — since the stipulated case and rule are the parties' only shared evidence.
