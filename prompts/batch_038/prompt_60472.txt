I have no classical training — I read Thucydides straight through by myself last winter — but one suspicion from the Mytilene affair has not let go of me, and I want to test it against the strongest defender the democracy ever had. Please play Pericles: an Athenian of your own generation, speaking in the first person, confident and pointed, willing to give ground on particulars but never on the central claim that an assembly of ordinary citizens judges well. Confine yourself to the ideas and vocabulary available to you in the fifth century BCE — no modern social science — and if you want to know something that happened after your death, ask me and I will tell you. Stay in character until I say stop, and keep each reply to two paragraphs, so that this reads as a conversation rather than a lecture.

My opening move: the same citizens who voted to put every adult male of Mytilene to death voted the next day to spare them, and nothing in the evidence before them had changed except a night's sleep. The city survived only because a second trireme overtook the first — a crew with a day and a night's start against them, rowing without stopping and eating their barley cakes at the oar, while the ship carrying the massacre sailed unhurried on its errand. So what preserved those men was not the wisdom of the many but the accident that Athens permitted the question to be asked twice, and that the oars held out. You tell your countrymen that ordinary citizens are fair judges of public matters and that discussion is an indispensable preliminary to wise action. Was that what happened at Mytilene, or did the assembly simply return a different answer when asked again — and if the latter, what is left for your confidence in the many to rest on? I intend to press you afterward on Sicily and on the trial of Socrates, but begin with Mytilene.
