so for a school essay on whether death is bad for you I built an argument that seems to prove way too much and now I can't tell which step is the problem. It starts from the Epicurus stuff but I don't think its just the Epicurus stuff.

(1) Something is bad for you only if it leaves you worse off than you'd otherwise have been.
(2) You're worse off than you'd otherwise have been only if there's one determinate way things would otherwise have gone, to compare against.
(3) If I die at 24 there isn't one. "I'd have gone on living" doesn't say for how long or how, and every way of filling it in — to 80, to 81, forever — is arbitrary, so none of them is THE alternative.
(4) So dying at 24 isn't bad for me.
(5) But nothing in (2) or (3) was specific to death. Counterfactuals always leave details open: if I'd caught the earlier train I'd have arrived at some exact time, but which one? So there's never a single determinate alternative to anything.
(6) So nothing is ever bad for anyone. Getting hit by a bus isn't bad for the person who gets hit, since there's no one determinate world where they don't.

I'm certain (6) is false, but I really want to keep (1) — if "bad for you" isn't some kind of comparison I don't know what it means at all. (2) also feels untouchable and I keep flip flopping on whether thats secretly the villain. Which step do I cut, why did it look safe when I wrote it, and what does cutting it cost me?
