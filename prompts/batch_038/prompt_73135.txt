I'm doing a philosophy of physics masters at the moment and our reading group has just been through Wigner's essay on the unreasonable effectiveness of mathematics, and I've come out of it more confused than I went in — mainly because I can't work out what the actual practice of physics, which is where I did my first degree, has to say about the puzzle. The essay is all wonder and mystery, and it's clearly written from the outside, by someone looking in. But I spent three years inside a department where nobody would have blinked at any of it, and I want to know what that insouciance is resting on.

Here's the practice I mean, spelled out. In second-year quantum mechanics we were handed the Dirac delta and told to use it as though it were a function, with a breezy aside that it wasn't really one and we shouldn't worry. In field theory we expanded in perturbation series which, as everyone in the room knew, provably have zero radius of convergence — there's a nice old argument that if they converged the vacuum would be unstable — and then we truncated them at four or five loops and compared against measurements of things like the electron's magnetic moment that agree to something like ten or twelve decimal places. Path integrals are introduced as sums over histories with no measure anybody can write down. Renormalisation amounts to subtracting infinities from infinities and keeping the finite remainder, and I seem to remember one of the people who developed it thinking the whole business was intellectually disreputable even as it worked. The refereeing norms match: a physics journal asks whether the calculation is novel and whether someone else could reproduce the number, and essentially never asks whether the manipulations are licensed; a maths journal asks the reverse and would reject nearly all of the above. And the people who try to do it properly — constructing the theories rigorously rather than manipulating them — are treated as a seperate tribe with different standards, and where they've succeeded they've sometimes found that the objects the physicists talk about don't exist as described.

So: what picture of the relationship between formal structure and the world does all that presuppose? Is it that the structures are somehow there independently and the sloppy notation is just a poor map of a well-defined territory, so that getting the right answer is evidence that you've latched onto something real even when you can't define it? Or is it a straight instrumentalism on which experiment is the only court of appeal and rigour is decoration? Or is it the third thing I half-suspect, that the ill-defined objects are really compressed shorthand for controlled approximations that could be made rigorous if anyone bothered? Tell me which of these the practice strictly requires as opposed to merely sitting comfortably with, and where it's underdetermined — my guess is that several incompatible stories license exactly the same behaviour, and I'd like to know whether any conceivable observation could tell them apart. Then deal with what looks to me like a genuine tension: the same community that finds it miraculous that mathematics written for its own sake keeps turning out to describe nature also treats that mathematics as a tool whose internal standards are optional, and separately invokes beauty and symmetry as a guide when there's no data and dismisses them as irrelevant when there is. Can you hold the miracle and the instrumentality at once, or is the wonder in Wigner's essay about a different phenomenon entirely from the one physicists are actually engaged in? Finally, run the counterfactual: if the presupposition were swapped out — say a rigour requirement were imposed on physics journals, or the instrumentalist line were taken seriously — what would the discipline actually lose, and how would it cope with the cases where the honest answer is that the thing it's been calculating with has no definition at all?
