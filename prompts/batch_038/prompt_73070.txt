I am taking a seminar on objective chance and trying to determine whether a single principle of deference underlies my own verdicts, or whether some of them have to go. The cases:

1. A well-confirmed model puts tomorrow's chance of rain at 0.7 and I have nothing else bearing on it. My credence should be exactly 0.7 — not approximately, not as a default to be nudged.
2. Same model, but I am at the window and it is pouring. My credence should be 1, and the 0.7 now feels not merely outweighed but no longer a constraint at all.
3. At 9 a.m. I learn that the chance, as of 8 a.m., that it would rain by 8:30 was 0.9. I now know it did not rain. I think the 0.9 still governs what my 8 a.m. credence should have been while saying nothing about my 9 a.m. one.
4. Two live, equally supported models: one says the chance of rain is 0.7, the other 0.2. My credence should be 0.45 — the average, not either chance.
5. A readout says the chance of A is 0.7, but I learn the machine is rigged to print 0.7 only when A will actually happen. Here my credence should be well above 0.7: the same number, from a different source, stops being a guide.
6. A far better-informed colleague is 0.9 on who wrote a disputed manuscript. I defer only partly, moving to perhaps 0.8. Yet in (1) I move all the way. Chance gets exact deference; expertise only partial.

My working guess is: match your credence to the chance conditional on the whole of your evidence, except when that evidence bears on the outcome itself. That handles 1, 2, and 4, but I cannot see how it gets 5, and it leaves 6 unexplained rather than predicted.

The pair I cannot reconcile: 1 and 4 demand exact numerical matching, yet I also think a ten-million-ticket lottery, where the chance my ticket loses is 0.9999999, does not license flat-out belief that it loses. Somewhere exact matching has to give way to threshold behavior, and I do not know where or why.

Is there a principle, or a small set, that delivers these verdicts? Please state it precisely, identify which case motivates each clause, say what it implies for cases I have not considered — in particular, evidence bearing on what the chance will later be, and knowing a chance while also knowing I am about to receive misleading evidence about it — and be candid about which of my verdicts a reasonably simple principle forces me to abandon.
