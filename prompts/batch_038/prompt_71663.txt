I run a grad reading group that's half philosophy and half CS, and half the room has never actually sat through the incompleteness theorems. We're doing a session on the Lucas–Penrose line of argument — the one where you hand someone a program plus a reason to think it's sound, point out that they can then see its Gödel sentence is true while the program can't prove it, and conclude minds aren't machines. I want a self-diagnostic decision tree for the session: start from "I've heard the argument and I'm not sure what I think", end at a named position.

Some ground rules:

- No node may be deeper than six questions from the root, and no node may mention a philosopher's name or a label for a view. Every question has to be answerable by someone who has no idea which branch it leads down. Gloss any technical term in a parenthetical, since half the room will need it.
- Phrase nodes as concrete situations or yes/no commitments, not as "do you find the anti-mechanist argument persuasive?"

Build it out of the logical space before you build it out of the roster of people. So: first list the questions that actually matter and the answers each allows, sketch the resulting grid of combinations, and only then say which real positions sit in which cell. I want the distinctions I care about to be the branch points, specifically: whether the argument needs the machine to be *sound* or only *consistent* (and whether that changes what the human is entitled to conclude); whether anyone could ever hand me my own source code along with the relevant proof; whether the machine's failure to prove its own Gödel sentence counts as a deficiency at all, given that I can't prove my own consistency either; whether the escape hatch is supposed to live inside the mind (non-algorithmic insight) or outside it (non-computable physics); what counts as a "machine" in the first place — Turing-computable only, or also oracle/infinitary devices; and whether showing I'm not *this* machine licenses showing I'm not *any* machine.

Leaves I need, at minimum: the plain Lucas-style conclusion; Penrose's version split into his two separate theses so that someone who buys non-computable insight but rejects the physical mechanism lands on a different leaf than someone who buys both; the "we're machines, just not knowably which ones" reply; the "the premise fails — we're not sound, or not knowably consistent" reply; the "we can never be handed our own listing, so nothing follows" reply; the "it proves too much, it would equally show I can't be shown to differ from you" reply; and at least one cell that nobody actually occupies. For every leaf give one sentence on its most awkward commitment, and mark the empty ones as empty with a diagnosis — incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored.

Also flag any pair of leaves your tree can't really seperate, rather than pretending it can. And if you're unsure whether a given view was actually defended by the person you'd attribute it to, leave the leaf unnamed and describe it by content instead — I'd rather have an unnamed-but-correct leaf than a confidently wrong attribution.
