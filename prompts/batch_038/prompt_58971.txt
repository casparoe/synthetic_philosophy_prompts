I am a second-year graduate student in a philosophy of the social sciences seminar, and this term my paper has to evaluate the quantitative literature on long-run political instability: the program that decomposes a society into the state, the elites, the general population, and a fourth, processual component standing for instability itself, then models multi-generational secular cycles on the order of two or three centuries. My difficulty is that the standard lines of criticism I have encountered cut in both directions and neither seems to state a testable standard. Historians object that such models erase meaning, agency, and contingency; the modelers reply that their critics cannot say what would count as the critics' own account being wrong. So before I assess anything, I want a specification of what assessing amounts to.

Please take on the role of a philosopher of the social sciences who has also served as a methodological evaluator on long-horizon forecasting programs, and who is equally at home with dynamical-systems modeling and with the epistemology of evidence. Think this through carefully rather than reaching for stock objections on either side. Begin with a visible first step: list the ways a theory of this kind can actually fail — failure modes, not arguments people have made about it. I want to see that step, because I suspect that several of the demands currently made in this literature are tracking nothing at all, and your list of desiderata should fall out of the failure modes rather than out of the polemics.

Then write the desiderata. What must any theory of large-scale sociopolitical instability deliver in order to count as genuinely predictive rather than as narrative reconstruction dressed in equations? Fix the purpose as follows: I am not asking what such a theory would need in order to be true, but what it would need in order to be worth testing and worth acting on by a body that must decide whether to fund an early-warning product for fragile states at horizons of one to three generations. For each requirement, give the consideration or case that motivates it, and say explicitly whether it is non-negotiable or a preference belonging to a particular camp.

Here are the demands I collected over the term. Some I think are genuine; some I suspect are the methodological commitments of one side wearing the costume of a criterion; I cannot yet tell which is which. Sort them, and say what work each does or fails to do: the theory must be reducible to explicit microfoundations about individual actors; it must predict the timing and location of triggering events rather than only windows of elevated risk; it must be statable without reference to unique events or named individuals; its results must be invariant to who codes the historical evidence, so that a differently disposed team would assign the same values to the same society and period; its variables must be directly measurable rather than latent constructs inferred from proxies; it must survive being published and acted upon; it must beat a naive baseline — persistence, a simple trend, or a bare density-driven model — out of sample; it must state its own domain of applicability, including the kinds of societies to which it makes no claim; and it must be able to accommodate a null case in which the predicted pressure accumulated and no instability followed.

One further point deserves its own treatment, because I cannot decide whether it is a sound methodological observation or a licence for anything. In a dynamical system, two variables can be tightly coupled yet essentially uncorrelated at zero lag, because they oscillate out of phase; so the absence of a contemporaneous correlation between population pressure and internal violence is not, on this view, evidence against the coupling. Tell me whether that can be a legitimate point at all, and if so, what disciplines have to be attached to it before a theorist is entitled to invoke it — whether the phase relation and the sign of the rates of change must be specified in advance, whether the lag may be selected after the data are in, and what remains of the theory's falsifiability once the invocation is permitted.

What I most want is your view on whether some subset of the requirements is jointly unsatisfiable, in which case the list is itself the result. My own suspicion involves three: genuine prospective out-of-sample validation; the claim that the operative mechanism is time-invariant and universal across societies; and sensitivity to the particular institutions of the society being modeled. If a cycle runs two or three centuries and usable quantitative series exist for a handful of societies, there may be only one or two complete cycles per case, which looks as though it forces a choice. Say whether that reasoning holds, and if it fails, where exactly it fails.

Finally, tell me which single requirement does the most to exclude candidates, and why. Then, in one closing paragraph and not in a scorecard, apply the list to the secular-cycle program: the one or two requirements it satisfies least well, and the one it satisfies best, each with the reason.

Two constraints on format and content. I am not asking for a reading list, references, or attributions; assume I have the literature and want the analysis. Do not name works or results you are not confident about. A numbered list is fine, and a compact table for the negotiable/non-negotiable split is welcome if it stays readable. Length is not a constraint, but every requirement should earn its place, and where two of them pull against each other I would rather see the conflict stated than a harmonized list I cannot use.
