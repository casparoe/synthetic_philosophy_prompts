Quick thing for my chapter — I need someone to argue a position I half beleive, so I can see if it survives contact with a smart opponent.

Stipulate what I'll call transcriptivism: a proof is a proof only if a different mathematician could replay it from a finite transcript of what was actually written down. No appeals to what an idealized creating subject would eventually come to see, nothing that leans on Kripke's-schema-style idealizations, no ongoing minds running in the background. Brouwer obviously fails that test, and so, I suspect, does a chunk of ordinary intuitionistic reasoning that quietly assumes the mathematician is still around to keep going.

My hunch: once you strip out the idealization, the anti-realist case against excluded middle loses its engine. Dummett can still demand that a grasp of truth-conditions manifest itself in practice, but on transcriptivism the practice is finite, checkable, and transferable to someone else — and I think that's easier to satisfy with classical bivalent truth conditions than with intuitionistic ones, not harder. Something about the transcript pinning down a determinate answer that any competent replayer can read off.

So: argue that transcriptivism gives you a reason to go classical rather than intuitionistic. Strongest version please — assume your opponent accepts Dummett's manifestation challenge and won't be fobbed off with a straw intuitionist. Bottom line in one sentence up front, then the argument; cap it at 400 words.
