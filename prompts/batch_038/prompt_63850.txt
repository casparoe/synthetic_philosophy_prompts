I am a professor of philosophy preparing a chapter for a collected volume on the philosophy of history, and I want you to act as an adversary against my own position rather than as a summarizer of the literature. The position is mine; I would rather locate its fatal defect now than have a referee locate it for me.

My position — call it Khaldunian minimalism — has four commitments.

First, the cyclical view of history is worth defending only once stripped of its ambitions as a philosophy of history. What is genuinely at stake in Ibn Khaldun's dynastic cycle, as set out in the Muqaddima (1377), is not a metaphysics of rise and decline but a causal mechanism: a conquering coalition bound by group solidarity (ʿaṣabiyyah) settles onto an agrarian tax base; the ruling house monopolizes violence and substitutes cash payments and offices for kinship ties; within roughly three generations — about 120 years on Ibn Khaldun's own reckoning — that solidarity is spent, luxury expenditure and tax-farming squeeze revenues, and a fresh coalition from the periphery takes the state.

Second, the mechanism can now be stated in variables, and it is roughly what the structural-demographic theory of state breakdown says: population growth presses against the carrying capacity of the land, real wages fall, elite numbers and elite claims on the surplus grow faster than the surplus, intra-elite competition intensifies, state revenues stagnate while expenditures rise, and instability follows. The period of the resulting oscillation — on the order of two to three centuries for the long secular cycles, shorter for dynastic turnover — is an output of that model, not an assumption built into it.

Third, this places the cyclical view in a different category from its usual stablemates. Polybius's anacyclosis in Book VI of the Histories is a typology of regime forms with no mediating variables; Spengler's morphology of cultures in The Decline of the West (1918, 1922) is an organic analogy with nothing that could count as a failure for it; Toynbee's challenge-and-response has a mechanism but no principled way of individuating the civilizations that are supposed to be responding. I concede all three as non-empirical and deny that they belong to my position.

Fourth, the traditional opposition between cyclical and linear conceptions of history is therefore a category error. The linear view's characteristic variables — sustained growth in output per capita, cumulative technical knowledge — were roughly flat or very slowly rising before the modern era, so they do not compete with the mechanism; and the mechanism is switched off after about 1800 by elastic food supply and fossil energy, not falsified by that period.

There is also one corollary of my own devising that I have pressed in print: if the cycle runs on the fragmentation of landholdings within generations, then systems of partible inheritance should cycle faster than systems of primogeniture, other things equal, and the difference should be visible in dynastic durations.

What I want from you is a map of this position's exposure — what would count against it.

1. Give me the defeaters ranked from fatal to merely awkward, and state the criterion by which you rank them.
2. For each, say what the world or the historical record would actually have to look like; whether evidence of that shape already exists; and why it lands at that point on your scale rather than another.
3. Distinguish what a committed defender of this position would concede as counting against it from what they would dismiss, and say what marks the difference between a legitimate scope condition and an immunizing move. My pre-1800 restriction, my treatment of epidemic, climate, and conquest as exogenous confounds, and my exclusion of Spengler are the three places I expect to be pressed.
4. Say what the position predicts that its rivals do not. In particular: against an account on which instability is produced by exogenous shocks acting on fragile institutions, with no endogenous periodicity at all, is my view merely a competitor or is that rival a better fit for the same cases — and what would decide between them? Also address whether the specific ordering of my variables (population, then wages, then elite numbers, then state finances, then instability) is load-bearing, and what a well-documented case in which state fiscal collapse precedes elite expansion would cost me.
5. Treat what I suspect is my weakest point with full rigor: whether elite overabundance and its cognates can be measured independently of the instability they are invoked to explain; whether premodern population, price, and wage series are precise enough to distinguish a genuine oscillation from noise and from the historian's own smoothing; whether the fitted periods are predictions or postdictions; and whether the modern track record — the forecast, made around 2010, of rising instability in the United States and western Europe through the following decade — counts as confirmation or was too vague to risk anything. I want to know what it would have taken for that forecast to fail, and whether anyone has said so in advance.
6. Address two conceptual worries as seriously as the empirical ones. (a) Is my reconstruction anachronistic charity? Ibn Khaldun's own account sits inside a theology and an astrology-tinged generational arithmetic; if only the mechanism survives, one may ask whether anything of Ibn Khaldun does, and whether I have simply substituted a modern theory under a prestigious name. (b) Is my dismissal of Spengler too quick? If his seasons were recashed in demographic and fiscal variables, the distance between us would shrink, and I may be Spengler with the mysticism deleted rather than his opponent.
7. Give me a verdict on my partible-inheritance corollary: a genuine risk the view takes on, or decoration that cannot be tested because inheritance regimes co-vary with everything else that matters.
8. Close with roughly two hundred words on whether the position is well insulated, and — the part I most want answered honestly — whether that insulation is a problem for a view that presents itself as a mid-range empirical theory, as against a view like Spengler's that never claimed to be one. If you think one of my four commitments should be abandoned rather than defended, say which and why.

Constraints: write throughout in American English. Give me no bibliography, no reading list, and no citations, and no hedging of the form "some scholars argue"; where you invoke a historical case, describe the case — which polity, which variables, what the record shows — rather than pointing me to a work about it. Aim for 1,400 to 1,800 words. Do not spend words restating my position back to me beyond what is needed to keep the argument legible, and do not soften your ranking in the interest of fairness to me.
