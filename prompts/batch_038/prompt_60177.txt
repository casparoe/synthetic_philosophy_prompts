I write a science-and-ideas column that has lately been covering the institutional life of software, and I am trying to state precisely when a codebase stops being the project it was. My verdicts on the cases I have reported on are these.

A repository in which every line has been replaced, one reviewed commit at a time, over two decades, retaining its name, its history, and its maintainer community: the same project. That repository archived, then rebuilt from scratch by a team that never saw the original, keeping only the name and the feature list: not the same project, whatever it inherits legally. A hard fork carrying 95 percent of the code and most of the contributors, renamed and repudiating the old governance: a new project, while the moribund original — a handful of committers, no releases — remains the original. Yet once the last of those committers departs and the fork's team begins pushing fixes back under the old name, I find myself wanting to say the original has returned and the fork has become it; this I cannot reconcile with the previous verdict. An internal tool rewritten wholesale in another language by the same team for the same users, with an automatic importer of all existing data: the same tool. A database in which every row has been replaced by different rows, schema, name, and application unchanged: here I hesitate, and lean toward not the same.

My tentative principle is that identity follows the chain of sanctioned change: so long as each step is a modification authorized by whoever then maintains the project, it remains that project. Test that against the six verdicts, repair or replace it, and state the result precisely enough that I could apply it without consulting further intuition. Then give me its verdicts on two cases I have not considered — a project whose code is untouched but whose contributors and documentation are entirely replaced, and one that renames itself while changing nothing else — and tell me candidly which of my original verdicts the best available principle forces me to abandon. If preserving all of them requires trading off continuity of code against continuity of community, say what that trade-off would have to look like, and whether it is principled or merely fitted to my intuitions.
