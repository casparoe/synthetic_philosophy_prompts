I'm reading a trade-press philosophy book on probability, and one chapter characterizes Lewis's Principal Principle this way:

"The principle says that when you know the objective chance of an outcome, your degree of belief in that outcome should equal the chance — unless you have evidence bearing on the outcome itself. In effect, chance is just the credence a rational agent ought to have, so the principle adds no new kind of fact to the world: it shows that talk of objective chance is a way of talking about what we should believe. That is also why chance applies only to repeatable setups like coin tosses, where there is a stable tendency for the term to latch onto. Lewis eventually ran into a technical difficulty, the so-called undermining problem, conceded it, and retreated from objective chance to long-run frequencies."

The chapter then concludes that positing objective chance is never a theoretical liability, since any chance posit automatically earns its keep by fixing rational credence.

Something in the middle sentences strikes me as off — I had thought the point was that the principle constrains belief rather than analyzes chance — but I can't tell whether that is a real distinction or hair-splitting on my part. Take the passage sentence by sentence and tell me what a philosopher working on chance would accept, what they would object to, and what the qualification about evidence is actually doing there, since the book's gloss on it looks to me like the least innocent part. Then say whether the chapter's closing conclusion survives once the errors are corrected, and what a Humean about chance would have to add to make sense of the difficulty the book invokes in passing.
