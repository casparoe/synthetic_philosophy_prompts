I am revising the opening unit of a graduate seminar I co-teach with the economics department on the conceptual foundations of welfare economics, and I have run into a difficulty with a pedagogical device I have myself relied upon for years. The device is the topographic-map picture of consumer theory: preferences over commodity bundles are represented by contour lines laid over a landscape, the budget set is a fence enclosing part of that landscape, and the agent is a walker who climbs to the highest contour the fence permits, the optimum being the point of tangency or, where the fence bites, a corner of it. A widely used intermediate text deploys this picture to establish two conclusions that I would like my students to examine rather than absorb. The first is that cardinal utility is a myth: contours, the reasoning goes, tell you only which places are higher than which and never how much higher, so nothing of substance is lost by discarding the older talk of measurable satisfaction, and the numbers attached to utility are bookkeeping like the contour interval, chosen at will. The second is that this also disposes of the unobservability worry: because choice picks out exactly one contour as the highest reachable one, observed behaviour already reveals everything there is to reveal about preference, so any further question about the agent's inner magnitudes is either empty or a demand for a phantom instrument.

I have begun an audit of the picture and would like it completed and pressure-tested. Some correspondences seem secure — elevation to the utility number, a contour to an indifference curve, the contour interval to the freedom of monotone rescaling, the fence to the budget constraint, the walker's starting position to the endowment — and one disanalogy is plain enough: contour spacing on a real map encodes gradient, information the ordinalist insists is meaningless in the target. I am more interested in the places where the map appears to be doing covert work. Elevation is prior to the contour map and independent of it, and it is a genuinely cardinal magnitude measured against a shared datum; the map merely declines to print the numbers. Is the ordinalist entitled to the corresponding claim, or does the picture invite students to treat utility differences as real but unprinted rather than as nonexistent? Again, contours over any landscape never cross and are dense across the region they cover, whereas in the target those features hold only because completeness, transitivity, and continuity were assumed at the outset. Does the map therefore present substantive axioms as facts about terrain, and what becomes of preferences that are cyclic or incomplete — is there any landscape they correspond to at all? Third, elevation is public: it can be checked with an instrument independent of the contour map, and it is commensurable for hikers standing in different valleys. Neither feature seems to have an analogue the theory can accept, yet both are tacitly inherited by the illustration.

Finally, I want the audit to confront a question my colleague presses: whether this is an analogy in good standing at all. If the existence of a family of non-intersecting contours over a connected region is equivalent to the existence of a continuous real-valued representation of a complete, transitive, continuous ordering, then the map is not a comparison with something else but a picture of the target's own structure, and the disanalogies I have listed are not infelicities of metaphor but statements about which preference structures admit no representation whatsoever. If that is right, does the first conclusion become circular, the picture presupposing the very real-valued magnitude it was meant to show dispensable, and does the second conclusion lose whatever force it drew from the map's status as a mere illustration? My colleague also proposes two rival pictures, and I would like each assessed for what it can carry: the mineralogist's scratch-test scale, in which the ordering is constituted by the revealed relation itself, the numerals are admittedly ordinal, and the intervals are wildly non-uniform, the top of the scale being out of all proportion to the bottom; and the zeroth law of thermodynamics, in which the transitivity of an equilibrium relation licenses assigning each system a scalar tag and thereby justifies the thermometer, with the caveat that an ideal thermometer is defined as one that does not measurably disturb the system it measures, a condition no elicitation procedure for preference obviously satisfies. Please deliver this in a form I can circulate as a handout to students who have had one term of microeconomics and no measure theory: an explicit table of correspondences; the disanalogies, sorted into those that threaten the first conclusion, those that threaten the second, and those that are merely decorative; a list of the features of the source that the picture quietly imports; a verdict on the theorem-versus-analogy question and on whether each of the two conclusions survives once the disanalogies are on the table; and, if either rival picture is better, an account of what it licenses that the contour map does not and what it in turn forecloses. State theorems rather than prove them, and keep the whole to the length of a seminar handout.
