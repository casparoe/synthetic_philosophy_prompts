Six weeks from now I pitch our exhibitions committee and I need to walk in knowing exactly what's coming at me.

Quick setup. I'm curator of works on paper at a mid-sized university museum. We own a late-18th-century drawing, iron gall ink and wash, that hasn't been out of the dark in about twenty years because normal gallery light would wreck it. My proposal: commission a living draftsperson to remake it — period paper, period inks, worked up from our high-res imaging and IRR, and attempting to reconstruct the actual sequence of the making, including the bits that were later scrubbed out or pasted over. The remake hangs in the gallery at full light, labeled plainly and signed and dated on the verso, while the original stays in the drawer.

The part I know is going to be a fight is my reasoning. I'm not arguing "visitors need something to look at." I'm arguing the remake has a value that neither the original-in-storage nor a facsimile print can supply, and that the value comes from reperforming the making rather than duplicating the surface. And I'm biting the bullet that follows from that: the remake will show the drawing as it looked when the ink was fresh — clean sheet, full-strength wash, no foxing, no tidying up of the old repairs. So it will look, to anyone who's actually seen the original, like a different and arguably wrong object.

The committee is two paintings conservators, our registrar who handles provenance, the chair of the art history department (old-school connoisseur, thinks entirely in terms of hand and touch), our general counsel, a board member who deals in old master drawings, and the director of visitor experience. Nobody on it is a philosopher and Ive been told in advance to keep the art-theory stuff "practical."

So I want a prep sheet, not a defense of my view. Give me the eight-ish objections this room will actually make — not the ones an aesthetics seminar would raise — in roughly the order of how much damage they'd do to the proposal, and phrase each one the way that particular person would phrase it. The registrar and the dealer are worried about genuinely different things and I want to hear thier vocabularies, not mine. For each objection give me a reply I can deliver standing up in about two minutes, plus an honest line about where the reply is soft, or where I'd basically be bluffing.

Then: what I should concede in the first five minutes, before anyone has to extract it out of me; the single reply that has to land or the whole thing dies; and the one question I'm praying nobody asks.

No reading lists or article names, please — I won't have time to read anything between now and then, I need sentences I can say out loud.
