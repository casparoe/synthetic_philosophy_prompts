I've just come off four years of a novel whose narrator undergoes a callosotomy at fifteen, and the magazine that is serialising a chapter has asked me to contribute a short companion essay on the underlying science and philosophy. They want roughly twenty-five hundred words, and — because it is a literary venue — they run no footnotes and no bibliography, which suits me badly and well at the same time: it means I cannot hide behind apparatus, but it also means I need someone to tell me plainly where I have got things wrong rather than where to read about them. I would therefore be grateful if you could comment on the draft below as a reader with a working knowledge of both the neuropsychology and the philosophy of mind. Please do not suggest further reading, references, or citations; I have the primary literature and a neurologist friend to badger, and a list of titles would be of no use to me at all. What I want is your judgment.

The draft, in its current state:

---

*One Mind or Two, and How to Punctuate Either*

Sever the corpus callosum — some hundred thousand fibres, the largest single bundle in the human brain — and the two hemispheres go on working, but they can no longer compare notes. The operation was a last resort for epilepsy that resisted everything else, and the patients who underwent it became, from the late 1960s onward, the most philosophically consequential human beings of the century. The reason is a quirk of wiring: vision is crossed, so an image flashed briefly to the left of the point of fixation arrives in the right hemisphere alone. In most of us the callosum carries it across within milliseconds. In a commissurotomy patient it does not travel at all. Show such a patient a snowdrift in the left field and a chicken claw in the right, ask which goes with a shovel, and the left hand — governed by the hemisphere that saw the snow — will reach for the shovel while the speaking hemisphere, which saw only the claw, explains without hesitation that the shovel is for cleaning out the chicken coop. Michael Gazzaniga called the apparatus responsible for that explanation the interpreter. It does not witness the processes it reports on. It writes press releases.

Two things about this literature struck me with unusual force as a novelist. The first is that the patients themselves, in ordinary life, are remarkably intact; they drive, they work, they joke, and the striking dissociations appear only in the laboratory, under the artificial conditions of split-field presentation. Yet a number of them describe their days as lived from two perspectives at once, and several have spoken of waking with the sense of being two people sharing a bed — which is why the question "how many minds are in there?" cannot be waved away as a philosopher's game. The second thing is that the interpreter generalises. If my articulate hemisphere is confabulating a rationale for a choice made elsewhere, then the unity I experience is not evidence of unity; it is evidence of a very good press office.

Thomas Nagel took the bisection data to show that physicalism cannot accommodate consciousness, since no third-person account can tell you what it is like to be a subject whose experience has been cut in half. Derek Parfit went further, into the thought experiments: your brain is divided and each half transplanted, both resulting persons fully continuous with you. His verdict, as I read it, is that the counting question has a determinate answer — there are two people where there was one — and that once you see this, the puzzle dissolves into a merely verbal difficulty.

My novel had to make a decision about all this, and the decision was grammatical before it was anything else. Prose has a rule that metaphysics lacks: a single first-person pronoun governs every predicate in its sentence, and there is no notation for "half of me thinks P while the rest of me does not, and neither half is a person." So I invented a constraint I came to call commissural narration. There is one narrator, one "I," never broken, never plural, never set in two typefaces across alternating chapters. But ownership of the predicates alternates according to a rule the reader must infer from evidence: certain sentences belong to the hemisphere that can speak, others to the hand that reaches for the wrong object, and the "I" refuses, throughout, to acknowledge the arrangement. The reader is made to do the work the fibres can no longer do. The reader becomes the corpus callosum.

The thesis I want the essay to arrive at, and the one I am least sure of, is that the unity of consciousness may be a grammatical achievement rather than a metaphysical datum. If a single governing "I" is a syntactic convention that we impose on experience and cannot help imposing, then the split-brain case is not a case of one mind becoming two. It is a case in which the world declines to license the grammar, and we discover that we have no other grammar to hand. The counting question would then be badly formed, though not empty: it would be a question about which notation a life can be written inside.

I can already hear the objection, which is that this makes consciousness depend on how one writes about it, and I do not have a reply.

Some specific things I would like your comments on:

1. Accuracy, first and above all. Flag anything above that is simply wrong, and anything that is right in outline but overstated — I suspect at least one or two of my characterisations of what the patients report and of what the philosophical positions amount to will not survive contact with a specialist, and I would much rather learn that from you than from the neurologist at the launch party. If the numbers, the direction of the visual projections, or the experimental set-up are off, say so.

2. Whether the objection I anticipate in the final line is the real objection, or whether there is a more damaging one that I have not seen. In particular: does my "grammar rather than metaphysics" move quietly change the subject from the unity of consciousness at a time to the unity of a person over time, and, if it does, is the slide fatal or merely something I should own up to on the page?

3. Whether commissural narration is load-bearing. A friend who reads only thrillers pointed out that alternating narrators is a very old trick, and that what I have described may be that trick with the alternation hidden and a grand claim attached. I want to know whether the constraint actually generates a philosophical result no dual-narrator novel could generate, or whether I have decorated a device with an argument it does not need.

4. Whether the essay's spine — case report, then generalisation to normal minds, then the philosophers, then the craft problem — is the right order, or whether the craft material should come first and carry the rest. Note where the argument sags rather than where the sentences are clumsy; the sentences I can handle.

Bluntness is welcome and preferred. What I am trying to avoid is the sort of commentary that praises the ambition and then restates the draft back to me in other words.
