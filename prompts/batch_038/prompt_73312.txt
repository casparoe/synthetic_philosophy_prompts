I cover science and ideas for a small magazine, and last month I sat in on a two-day workshop where the people who work on AI safety argued with each other far more interestingly in the hallways than they did on the panels. I've been chewing on one of those hallway arguments ever since and I still don't know who won, so I want you to referee it.

The context, as best I can reconstruct it: they were talking about a proposed system that would decide *and execute* maintenance actions on a regional power grid — switching, load shedding, dispatching crews. The team's safety case, as described, certifies average-case behavior: a bound on expected harm computed over a test distribution of grid states. Nadia (not her real name) said that isn't a safety case at all. Dev said it's the only kind that exists for a learned system, and that her alternative is a slogan.

Their exchange, roughly in order:

1. Nadia: failure that matters lives in the tail. A system that behaves well 99.9% of the time and does something catastrophic 0.1% of the time isn't 99.9% safe, it's a bomb with a slow fuse. Averaging over outcomes is only legitimate when the bad outcomes are recoverable, and a cascading grid failure isn't.

2. Dev: worst case over *what* set? Over all physically possible inputs, no learned system has a worst-case bound — including the human operators we're replacing, who are worse. Demanding a guarantee that doesn't exist for anything isn't a standard, it's a way of winning the argument by making it unanswerable.

3. Nadia: fine, restrict it — worst case over a plausible family of deployment environments. But notice that his average is taken over the training/test distribution, and deployment is off-distribution. So the number he's certifying isn't the number he'll get, and worse, having a certificate makes people stop looking.

4. Dev: shift is exactly what the average-case machinery is for. You widen the mixture you bound over, you pay for it in average performance — that trade-off is well documented in adversarial robustness — and you get a real, measurable, auditable improvement. She's asking him to give up demonstrated safety on common cases to buy insurance against a scenario he's assigned negligible probability.

5. Nadia: negligible per decision. The system makes something like ten million decisions over its service life. If the per-decision failure probability is one in a hundred million, failure isn't a tail risk, it's an expectation.

6. Dev: then his own example proves her wrong. If the expected count of failures is order one, the *average* case is bad too, so the distinction she's drawing has collapsed. What she actually wants is a small expected number of failures, which is an average-case quantity by definition. And what actually protects the tail isn't a bound anyway — it's architecture: monitoring, tripwires, reversibility, a human on the loop, a kill switch. Those are empirical, and pretending they're guarantees is worse than admitting they aren't.

7. Nadia: monitoring only helps if the failure is detectable before it's irreversible, and the entire point of wanting a guarantee is to not have to rely on noticing in time. Also, safety-by-architecture is unfalsifiable in practice — after every incident you get told the state was out of distribution, which means the framework can never be shown to have failed.

Please go through this move by move: rate each of the seven from clearly mistaken to clearly right, with a sentence or two of justification for each, and flag where a move is question-begging or quietly trades on an ambiguity (I suspect at least two of them do). Then commit — who is more right overall, and on what specific point does the whole thing turn? Don't give me a both-sides-have-a-point summary; take a position and defend it, and if you think someone is right only under a qualification, state the qualification and still name a winner. Finally, tell me what neither of them said — the thing you'd have raised if you'd been standing there.
