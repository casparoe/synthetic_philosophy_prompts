I've been trying to work out why the argument about rational irrationality — the Parfit's hitchhiker / Kavka's toxin cluster — has never converged, and I don't want a survey of the responses. I want a diagnosis of the disagreement itself.

The case, from memory, so discount it if I've got a detail wrong: your car breaks down in a desert at night, a stranger is the only other driver around, you promise him a big reward if he takes you home since you can't pay now. He doesn't believe you, because you're the sort of person who never does anything that's worse for you and you can't lie convincingly, so he drives off and you're stuck there. Parfit's move is that it would have been better for you — and in some sense rational — to be someone who does keep that kind of promise, even though keeping it once you're safe is against your interest. Same shape as the toxin: the payoff attaches to having the intention, not to acting on it, and having the intention is supposed to be impossible precisely because you know acting on it would be pointless.

As best I can tell there are four camps and none of them ever make contact:

1. People who say Parfit is right and that rationality has to be assessable at the level of dispositions, plans, motives or character, not just single acts — if act-by-act assessment turns your theory self-defeating, so much the worse for act-by-act assessment. Resolute choice types live here.
2. People who say it collapses on the psychology: you can't intend at will, and you can't intend what you're confident you won't do, so the "rational irrationality" on offer is either unavailable or is just self-deception that happens to pay. On this view Parfit has shown at most that fooling yourself can be useful.
3. Consequentialists who accept the case but say it doesn't touch their theory, it just licenses its own esotericism — the theory can be true while its being widely believed or consciously applied is bad.
4. Decision theorists, a lot of them working on AI, who use the same case as a test bed for decision procedures and conclude that causal decision theory dies in the desert while something more logical or evidential-flavoured survives.

My hunch, offered only as a hunch: they aren't having one argument. Camps 1 and 2 are disagreeing about whether intending is the kind of thing you can do on purpose, which looks like a factual question about minds that nobodys actually gone and checked. Camps 3 and 4 have quietly swapped Parfit's question — can a theory of rationality be directly self-defeating? — for different ones: can a moral theory be self-effacing, and which decision procedure wins. And all four are using "rational" for at least three different relations: rational to do, rational to be disposed to do, and rational to make yourself into someone who does it.

So is that right? Is this a verbal dispute, a dispute about the object of assessment, a dispute resting on an unexamined psychological premise, four genuinely different questions sharing a costume, or is the impasse just that it's hard and there's no agreed constraint on rationality to argue from — nobody agrees on whether rationality even has to be sequentially consistent? I'd like you to work through what each camp actually argues and show where they fail to engage each other, rather than telling me who's right about the desert. Related: does the case even refute self-interest theory, or only show that such a theory can coherently recommend replacing itself in some agents? That seems to me to change what's at stake, and I can't tell whether anyone in camps 1 and 2 has noticed.

Then, given whatever diagnosis you land on, what would count as progress — a distinction, a formal result about dynamic choice and backward induction, an actual study of whether people can form and hold intentions they expect not to act on, a change of question, or nothing at all, and why not. And does your diagnosis survive swapping the case: does the stall look different if the self-defeating theory is consequentialism rather than self-interest, or if the agent is a computer program rather than a person?
