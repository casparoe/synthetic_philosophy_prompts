Two philosophers, both constitutive panpsychists, have argued for three years about whether experience bottoms out in a multitude of minute subjects or in a single cosmic one. They have now agreed to an adversarial collaboration. Write the joint statement they would produce — a document both can sign unamended.

Fix the shared framework as follows, and hold both parties to it. Each accepts that the properties physics attributes to fundamental entities are dispositional or structural in character, and that these require categorical grounds; each accepts that those grounds are phenomenal or proto-phenomenal; each rejects strong emergence, meaning that neither may posit brute phenomenal novelty at the level of brains, organisms, or anything else composite. Fix the two positions in their weakened forms as well. The micropsychist holds that fundamental subjects possess only minimal phenomenal character — a tone, a shade, a raw feel — with no beliefs, desires, self-awareness, or perspective in anything resembling the human sense. The cosmopsychist holds that the fundamental subject's experience need not be unified in the way human experience is, and that derivative subjects can be genuine subjects even if only partially integrated into the whole.

The document should contain four sections.

1. A numbered list of claims both parties sign. Include claims about what the shared framework commits them to, about what any adequate theory of consciousness must deliver, and about what each has already conceded the other has established. At least three of these must be substantive in the sense that a physicalist or a strong emergentist would refuse to sign them; mark which ones those are and say what a refuser would have to deny.

2. A disagreements table. Each row states one disagreement precisely, then gives it twice: once in the wording the micropsychist would accept for their own side, once in the wording the cosmopsychist would accept for theirs, neither caricatured. A final column records whether the disagreement is genuine or partly verbal, with reasons. At a minimum the table must cover whether a subject can literally be a part of another subject; whether considerations of uniformity and parsimony about fundamental phenomenal kinds favor one basic character or a plurality; whether the limitation of the human palette is better explained by a limited fundamental inventory or by many derivative modifications of one; and whether any notion of "combination" or "derivation" of subjects survives the ban on strong emergence rather than merely relabeling it.

3. The cruxes: the sub-questions such that settling one would actually move at least one party. For each, give the case or argument both accept as bearing on it, each party's pre-registered prediction of how that case comes out and what they take that to show, and the specific result each would count as a concession against their own view. Neither party has experiments available, so the tests must be conceptual or structural — but they must be concrete: describe each case in enough detail that both parties could evaluate it independently, not merely announce that a scenario is conceivable. At least one crux must be tested by a case constructed for this document rather than by any case already circulating in the dispute.

4. A closing section in which the two identify the portion of the argument they now believe was about words, name the term or terms responsible, and state what is left of the disagreement once those terms are fixed — including whether anything substantive survives.

Do not cite, summarize, or gesture at the literature, and do not attribute any position to a named philosopher; argue from the concepts alone.
