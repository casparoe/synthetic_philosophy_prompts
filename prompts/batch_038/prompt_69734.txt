I have spent the better part of two decades reading rationalist blogs, their forums, and their comment threads, and one of the most durable reflexes in that milieu is a brisk evidentialism about belief: beliefs must pay rent in anticipated experience, there is no such thing as a consideration that counts in favor of believing something without counting in favor of its being true, and the whole project of cultivating confidence in a proposition one does not yet find probable is waved away under the heading of believing in belief. More recently I have been reading the literature on the wrong kind of reasons, and that reflex has come to look less like a truism and more like one contested side of a real dispute. I would like a dialogue that stages the dispute at full strength.

Write a dialogue between two interlocutors, both first-rate, neither a strawman, arguing about a case I stipulate below. They are not introducing themselves or their views to each other; they know perfectly well what the other thinks, and they have met to work through the case.

The first interlocutor — label their turns CONSTITUTIVIST — holds that what makes a consideration a reason for an attitude is its bearing on the standard of correctness constitutive of that attitude type. For belief, correctness is truth, so the only reasons for belief are considerations bearing on whether the proposition is true. Practical considerations of the sort in the case below are not defective reasons for belief; they are not reasons for belief at all. They are reasons to want to believe, or reasons to bring it about that one believes — a different normative object entirely, and the confusion between the two is the source of the whole problem. A reason for belief must bear on the truth of the proposition independently of the attitude itself.

The second — label their turns PRAGMATIST — holds that reasons for belief are simply considerations that count in favor of one's being in the believing state, and that the restriction the constitutivist adds is a substantive thesis wearing the costume of a conceptual truth. Belief is not a sealed-off epistemic organ; it is the state by which an agent steers, and where believing p is an effective means to something the agent has good reason to want, that fact is a genuine reason to believe p. On this view the felt oddity of deliberating from such a consideration is explained by the contingent fact that human beings cannot believe at will, not by a difference in kind among reasons.

Now the case. Both interlocutors are agreed on all of its stipulations and neither may dispute them.

Idris's brother's light aircraft went down in remote, mountainous country five weeks ago. The official search has been suspended. The ordinary evidence about whether his brother is alive is, let us stipulate, exactly balanced: it licenses no credence above 0.5.

Idris is then approached by a predictor of quite extraordinary reliability — call her the Auditor. Before speaking to Idris, she wrote down a sealed prediction of what his sincere credence will be, one calendar month from today, in the proposition that his brother is alive. She now discloses to him both the prediction and the rule she has attached to it, and he has every reason to accept her disclosure as accurate:

If, one month from today, Idris sincerely holds a credence above 0.9 that his brother is alive, she will fund and personally direct a search that finds him alive. Stipulate that a search she directs succeeds.

If he does not, she funds nothing, and his brother dies of exposure within the week. Stipulate this too.

Idris knows all of this. He knows the prediction is already sealed and cannot be altered. He also knows that his believing the proposition is what makes it true and his disbelieving it is what makes it false.

There are two routes by which the month might end, and the dialogue must treat both.

Route one: in the third week, a hiker's account surfaces — second-hand, vague, but genuine, and by itself sufficient on ordinary evidential grounds to raise Idris's credence to 0.95. He forms the belief on that account, knowing as he forms it that doing so triggers the rescue.

Route two: no such account surfaces. Idris sits down and tries to bring himself to believe the proposition directly, on the ground that believing it will save his brother's life, and succeeds.

In both routes he ends up with a true belief, knowingly acquired, with identical consequences in the world.

Requirements for the dialogue:

Both interlocutors must commit to determinate verdicts on both routes. No appeals to vagueness, no retreat into it depends on the details, no declining to answer because the case is fantastical — they have already agreed it is stipulated.

Each must press the other's known weak point rather than their own favorite one. Against the constitutivist: the clause requiring that a reason for belief bear on the truth independently of the attitude looks suspiciously like it was drafted to produce the desired verdict, so state whether it is a principled constraint or a redescription of the datum; and in route two the resulting belief is true, so say precisely what has gone wrong and why truth is not enough. Against the pragmatist: explain the deliberative asymmetry — why Idris can deliberate from the hiker's account but cannot deliberate from the fact that his brother's life depends on his believing — without reducing it to a mere limitation of human psychology; and explain why your view does not erase the distinction between believing that p and wanting to believe that p, which is a distinction Idris plainly needs.

Each interlocutor gets at least one genuine concession, and each concession should cost something.

Include an explicit double crux: each asks the other what would have to be true, or what verdict on which further case would have to change, for them to abandon their position. Let this narrow the disagreement to something specific, but do not let it resolve it.

At one point, one of them should float the suspicion that the whole thing is a verbal dispute — that they are simply using reason for belief to mean two different things — and the other should either defeat that suspicion or fail to.

End without a resolution, on the sharpest available statement of where the fault line actually runs.

Formal constraints. Between 1,400 and 1,800 words. Speaker labels only; no narration of the room, the weather, or the characters' feelings except where a feeling is itself a premise. The interlocutors may allude in general terms to familiar landmarks of this debate — the distinction between reasons that bear on the object of an attitude and reasons that bear on merely having it, the observation that one cannot deliberate about whether to believe p otherwise than by attending to whether p, the stock examples involving threats designed to make one admire something unadmirable — but they must not quote any text, name any work by title, page, or year, or attribute a specific claim to any named philosopher. Everything must be argued from the case as I have described it. I want the dialogue I always wanted and never got on those forums: nobody scoring points by mispronouncing the other's view.
