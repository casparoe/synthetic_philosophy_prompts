I am in my final year of high school, and for about a year I have been working on an essay for my school's journal about whether life can be defined at all. I want to go into planetary science eventually, and the question keeps resurfacing, so I would like to know what would force me to abandon the view I have landed on. I will state the view as precisely as I can, and then tell you what I want out of you.

My position has three parts. First, I do not think life is a sharp natural kind with a boundary waiting to be discovered; I think it is a graded, cluster property, and that the correct response to a borderline case is a degree rather than a yes or a no. Second, I think three axes do the real work in the cluster: thermodynamic autonomy, meaning the maintenance of an internal state far from equilibrium by dissipating free energy taken from the environment; heritable variation subject to selection, meaning differences that get passed on and that affect persistence; and bounded individuality, meaning that there is a unit whose fate can be distinguished from that of its surroundings. Third, I think a definition earns its keep only if it could be run by an instrument on another world: it should say what a lander is to measure, what result counts as a detection, and how confident we should be in that result.

Please treat this as a view a serious person might defend, and map its exposure.

1. Which concrete cases does it get wrong, or get wrong in a way that is embarrassing rather than merely difficult? Include the standard virus and mule examples, but also a self-propagating crystal growing in a cooling melt, a wildfire, a prion, a desiccated tardigrade sitting in a museum drawer, a mammalian embryo at the two-cell stage, an immortal cell line growing in a flask, a computer program evolving inside a simulation, and the entire biosphere of a planet treated as a single thing. For each case, say which of my three axes is doing the work and whether assigning a degree is a genuine answer or a way of avoiding one.

2. Which empirical findings, actual or realistically possible, would count against the view rather than only complicating it? I have in mind ambiguous positive results from life-detection experiments, disputed claims about organisms with unusual biochemistries, heritable variation that involves no nucleic acids, very large viruses, and organisms that persist for long periods without metabolizing. Tell me which of these genuinely bite and which I can absorb.

3. Which rival accounts might mine collapse into? For example, a list-of-properties approach, a historical or etiological approach on which what matters is that a lineage has a past rather than that a system runs now, or an approach on which life consists of cooperating reproducers, which would let a virus inside a host count as alive. If my three axes deliver the same verdicts as one of these rivals in every case, in what sense is the view mine? What does my view imply that those rivals do not, particularly about where we should look for life and about whether any single discovery could settle the question?

4. Is the graded-cluster structure itself an escape from refutation? A critic could say that any counterexample is absorbed by re-weighting the axes, and that a definition which cannot be wrong is not a definition. Assess this honestly, say what I would have to add to make the view risk something, and say whether that addition would be worth its cost.

For each item, tell me whether a defender of the view ought to concede the point or dismiss it, and rank the points from fatal to merely awkward. Then tell me which single development over the next twenty years would most likely demolish the view. I would much rather be told that my position is indefensible than be reassured.
