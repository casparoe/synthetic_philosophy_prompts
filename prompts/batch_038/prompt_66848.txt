For the past two years I've been facilitating a Tuesday-evening reading group that grew out of a local rationality meetup: fourteen regulars, mostly working software engineers and data analysts in their late twenties and thirties, plus an econ PhD student who keeps score and a paralegal who does most of the arguing. We meet in the back room of a coworking space with a whiteboard, tables that seat sixteen, and about three hundred poker chips that we redeem quarterly against a shared coffee fund — so the group is thoroughly habituated to playing for chips and to being debriefed afterwards, and I've already run public goods games, an ultimatum tournament, and a calibration night with them. Our next session is on freedom, and I want to give them a game rather than a talk. The topic is the three-way split between negative liberty as non-interference, positive liberty as self-direction or self-mastery, and the republican conception of liberty as non-domination — the view, associated above all with Philip Pettit, that what makes you unfree is not that anyone actually blocks you but that someone is positioned to block you at their own discretion, constrained by no requirement to take your interests or your voice into account. Nobody in the group has read Pettit; one member has read Berlin's Two Concepts of Liberty and will recognize the vocabulary if I use it, so the three labels should stay off the table until the debrief. Several members lean libertarian and have an excellent nose for facilitators railroading them toward a conclusion, which matters for what follows.

My rough sketch, which you should feel free to discard if you have something better: three or four short rounds of an allocation game in which participants earn chips, plus a veto player whose authority varies across rounds while the rate at which the veto actually bites is held constant. That last clause is the whole point and the part I can't get right on my own. I want a matched pair of conditions differing only in arbitrariness — in one, the veto holder may overturn an allocation for any reason or none and owes no explanation; in the other, the veto holder must, when appealed to, cite a published criterion that tracks the interests of the person vetoed — with the observed frequency of vetoes engineered, or at minimum recorded, to be the same in both. Then I want participants to bid chips for entry into each regime, including a baseline regime with no veto holder at all, and I want the bids to be the readout on whether they in fact price non-domination separately from non-interference. If the bids come out flat — if they treat the whim-veto regime and the reason-giving regime as equivalent once expected interference is held equal — I want to report that honestly rather than massage it, so please tell me what a null result would actually license, how much within-group variance fourteen people will produce, and whether a repeated-measures design across rounds is enough to distinguish real equivalence from noise. I'd also like at least one round in which domination bites with zero interference: participants should discover afterwards that they had already reshaped their own allocations in anticipation of a veto that was never exercised, and there should be a written record of each round's choices detailed enough to show them the drift when I put it on the whiteboard.

Give me the runnable thing: roles, exact rules, chip endowments and payoff structure, a materials list, a minute-by-minute timeline for ninety minutes including the debrief, and some mechanism for keeping the veto holder's decisions and reasons opaque enough that subjects must infer the rule rather than read it off a sheet. Then the debrief itself, mapping particular moments in the play onto the three conceptions and supplying the names only at the end, together with a frank account of what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it — I assume the veto holder will go soft and decline to use the power, I assume someone will reverse-engineer the purpose from the asymmetry between conditions, and I assume the bidding stage will produce at least one theatrical outlier — with a countermeasure for each. I also want an honest statement of which features of the republican view this apparatus cannot reproduce and that I'll have to supply in words: I suspect the structural and slow-moving character of real domination, its tendency to shape what people want rather than merely to block what they want, the way Pettit ties non-domination to being able to look others in the eye without fear or deference, and the fact that an experimenter's promise to give reasons is credible in a way that actual institutional constraints are not. Finally, tell me whether positive liberty can be made to show up in the same session without turning this into two games bolted together, what the cheapest staging of it would be if so, and how you would compress the whole thing into fifty minutes for six people if my room falls through.
