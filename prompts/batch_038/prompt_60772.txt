I am a final-year philosophy student, and in three weeks I present the seminar paper for my philosophy of mind course. The seminar is small and mixed in a way that worries me: the professor is broadly sympathetic to Searle and has lectured on biological naturalism; roughly half the students are analytic philosophers of mind who take the Dennettian line for granted; one regular attendee works on teleosemantics and reads everything as a covert restatement of it; and two students come from computer science and are writing theses on trained language models, so they will want to know what my view licenses them to say about their own objects of study. I would like a Q&A preparation sheet rather than a defense of my position.

My thesis is that the two-bitser case — the device whose states acquire different contents when it is relocated to a different environment, with nothing whatever changing inside it — defeats only a claim Searle never needed to make. My argument runs as follows.

1. The two-bitser shows that what fixes the content of a state includes facts external to that state's physical constitution: its surroundings, its history, the design decisions embodied in it.
2. I distinguish two readings of "derived." On reading (a), a state's content is derived if it is parasitic on the ascriptions of actual interpreters. On reading (b), a state's content is derived if it is fixed in part by non-intentional relations to the world. The two-bitser establishes the universality of (b) only.
3. Searle's originality claim, properly stated, requires only the denial of (a). So the two-bitser is not a reductio of Searle; it is evidence that Searle chose the wrong word when he said "intrinsic."
4. I then propose a positive condition meant to separate the two-bitser from us without appealing to intrinsicness: a system has original intentionality when exactly one ascription scheme is singled out by the system's own functional organization — when getting the content wrong makes the system's behavior inexplicable, and when the scheme that makes it explicable is the scheme the system itself in some sense enforces. I call this the self-specification condition.
5. I concede in the paper that this resembles instrumentalism with extra steps, and I offer a contrast case: a creature whose every possible misinterpretation by us leaves its behavior equally predictable, versus one whose behavior is recoverable only under a single scheme.

Please give me the following. Eight or so objections this particular audience will actually raise, ordered by how much damage each would do to my paper, where damage combines likelihood of being raised with difficulty of answering. Phrase each the way its likely source would phrase it — the Searlean will not object in the same register as the Dennettian, and the computer science students will not use either vocabulary. For each objection, give a reply I can deliver in about two minutes at a seminar table, and then a frank note on where that reply is soft: which premise of mine it silently depends on, or what it concedes. Tell me separately which objections are aimed at step 2 versus step 4, since I suspect those are the two places I am most exposed and I want to know whether the audience will converge on both or only one.

Identify what I should concede in my opening five minutes to take the air out of the strongest objection, and tell me which single reply has to land for the paper to survive the hour. Name the objection I am hoping nobody raises — the one for which I have no answer — and give me the best partial holding response available. Also flag any objection that would rest on a misreading of my steps 2 or 3, and tell me how to correct that misreading early without sounding defensive.

I do not want a bibliography, a list of further readings, or citations; engage with the argument exactly as I have set it out above. I would also rather have pointed, specific objections tied to my numbered steps than general advice about defining my terms more carefully.
