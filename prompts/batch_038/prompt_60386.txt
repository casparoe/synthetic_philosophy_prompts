The case I want pressed hardest is Hess, though, and I don't think its usually read this way. In 1962 he put out "History of Ocean Basins," which is basically the seafloor-spreading paper, and the opening paragraph concedes that the birth of the oceans is conjecture and then says, verbatim, "Like Umbgrove, I shall consider this paper an essay in geopoetry." He is publishing a mechanism for moving the seafloor while saying out loud that he's writing poetry about it. And it got paid off: within about six years you have the magnetic stripes symmetric about the ridges that Vine and Matthews had predicted, and drilling ships coming back with seafloor that gets older with distance from the ridge and is nowhere near as old as the continents. So here's the distinction I want tested, because I don't think either James or Clifford draws it. James's own examples of legitimate over-belief are cases where the believing helps make the thing true — you have to trust that your comrades like you before you can find out whether they do, the group only works because people back it. But Hess's case isn't that. Whether the seafloor spreads is completely independent of anybody's attitude about it. What depends on the belief is whether anyone ever goes and gets the evidence: the cruises, the magnetometer surveys, the drilling, Wegener's own last Greenland trip — all of it was paid for out of a commitment that no evidence then available required. So there are two different things, belief-causes-the-truth and belief-causes-the-verification, and I've been calling the second one an epistemic loan: you publish ahead of your data and either pay it back or default, and the land-bridge people defaulted.
