For the past nine years I have been the volunteer who runs the monthly public lecture evenings at a small regional observatory housed in a converted water tower. My day job is in hospital administration and my astronomy is entirely self-taught, so I am accustomed to being the least expert person in the room. Next month the society's committee has asked me to speak for about thirty-five minutes, with a whiteboard and no slides, on the fact that Johannes Kepler cast horoscopes. The audience will be roughly forty amateur astronomers: people who own telescopes, photograph nebulae, read popular science, and regard Kepler as one of the first genuine scientists. Their instinct — which several members have already voiced to me at the last star party — is that the horoscopes were either a regrettable source of side income or evidence that even great minds are superstitious. I would rather they went home with something more interesting than either verdict, and I suspect the interesting thing is that Kepler did not simply practise the astrology he inherited but tried to rebuild it on a narrower foundation.

What I need from you is not the lecture itself but the plan for it: the sequence in which the pieces must be introduced and why each one depends on the previous, the single case or document that should carry the whole talk together with what it must contain and what it must not, the misconceptions I should expect and the exact point in the sequence at which each should be headed off, one question I can put to the room at the end that would tell me whether the idea had landed rather than merely entertained them, and the material I should deliberately leave out at this level — refused out loud to the audience, not quietly dropped. So that you can correct the shape of my understanding if it is wrong, here is what I have gathered on my own: that the astrology Kepler practised was not the newspaper sun-sign column but an attempt to read dispositions and events off the geometrical angles between planets as seen from Earth; that he wanted to strip out most of the inherited apparatus — zodiacal signs, houses, the prediction of particular events — while keeping a smaller core he thought defensible, arguing by analogy with the way musical harmonies act on the soul; that composing annual prognostication calendars was part of what he was paid to do in his teaching post at Graz; and that he later wrote something like a public warning to people who were dismissing astrology wholesale, on the grounds that they were discarding more than they had shown to be false. I also have to hand the horoscope he prepared for the general Wallenstein, and the years he spent defending his mother against a charge of witchcraft, and I cannot tell whether either belongs in thirty-five minutes or whether both would swamp it.

Please be decisive rather than even-handed. Choose one carrying example and one order of ideas, and defend those choices: tell me what makes that sequence work for an audience that already believes it knows what science is, and identify which of the misconceptions I listed is the load-bearing one that the others fall out of. If any of my summary above is wrong or overstated, say so plainly and build the plan around your corrected version rather than offering me a menu of alternatives. Where the plan rests on a reading of Kepler that historians dispute — for instance, on whether his proposed reform of astrology was a serious intellectual programme or a way of keeping a disreputable trade respectable while distancing himself from its worst practitioners — mark that at the point in the plan where it matters and tell me how I should signal the uncertainty to a lay audience without letting the talk dissolve into vagueness. I am not after sources, further reading, or a bibliography; I want the structure and the reasoning behind it.
