Q2. In this module we distinguish three readings of the claim that a historical agent should not be judged by the standards of a later age.
(i) Standards: whether an act is wrong depends partly on the moral norms in force in the agent's society, so an act that conforms to those norms is not wrong at all.

And the answer that came in (two-hour unseen, they'd been told roughly 40 minutes and 700 words on it):

---

The defence is almost never one claim, and the three readings in the question are useful precisely because they come apart so easily in practice.

Take the Standards Reading first. On this account whether an act is wrong depends in part on the norms actually in force in the agent's society, so a Virginian planter of the 1780s who does what everyone around him does has done nothing wrong, and there is nothing for a later age to find. The attraction of the view is that it makes our condemnation look like a confusion: we are treating a norm-relative failure as though it were an absolute one. The cost is well known — it makes moral progress unintelligible. If wrongness tracks the norms in force, then the ending of slavery was not a case of later ages getting right what earlier ones got wrong; it was a change of taste, and there is no sense in which things got better. Notice too that the cost falls on praise as well as blame. On the Standards Reading we cannot credit the abolitionists for seeing what their neighbours could not, since they too were only measuring themselves against the norms of their day. Praise and blame go together, and the reading loses both.

The Access Reading concedes the point about wrongness: the act is wrong whether or not the norms of the day permit it, and blame is a separate question about the agent's own position, namely whether they could reasonably have known better. That version is much harder to dismiss, but it has two problems. First, the same objection to the Standards Reading applies here: if what an agent is answerable for shifts with what could have been known in their age, then the yardstick we are applying to people is still moving about, and progress collapses into change all over again. Second, and more practically, working out what a person could reasonably have known is close to impossible from two centuries away; we end up guessing at their epistemic situation and then dressing the guess up as an argument.

That said, the empirical premise the defence usually leans on — that everyone at the time thought this way — is simply false in the central cases. Quakers were petitioning Congress about the slave trade within Jefferson's own lifetime, there were abolition societies in the colonies and then in the states, and Jefferson himself wrote at length about what the institution did to the character of masters and about the justice owed to the enslaved, while freeing only a handful of the people he held. So the criticism was not merely floating around in the abstract; it was available to him, and in part in his own hand. Whether availability is the same thing as accessibility is a further question, and I think the Access Reading should be read as being about the second rather than the first: what matters is not that somebody somewhere said the thing, but that a person in his position, with his education and his exposure, could reasonably have taken it up.

The Standing Reading is different in kind, because it grants the wrongness and even the culpability and denies only that we are the right people to press the charge — either because we have inherited the benefits of the arrangements we are condemning, or because we would have behaved no better ourselves. This is the version I find most sympathetic, and also the one most often smuggled in under cover of the other two: a lot of what sounds like "don't judge the past by our standards" turns out on inspection to be "you're not in a position to judge".

The Access Reading also has a far older pedigree than the contemporary debate suggests. Aristotle's account of the voluntary says that acts done in ignorance are involuntary, and that we do not hold people responsible for what is involuntary — something like the case of the man who gives away a secret not knowing what it is, or who shoots without realising the weapon is loaded. On that model Jefferson's failure is ignorance of exactly the same shape: he did not know what he was doing in the morally relevant sense. So the Access Reading is not a modern invention at all but a version of the oldest excuse there is, and the "man of his time" defence is doing something philosophically respectable rather than merely defensive.

Which brings me to the consideration that most convinces me. Most of us, dropped into Jefferson's situation — his wealth, his dependants, his economy, his education — would have done what he did. And if that is right then blame is not merely awkward, it is empty, since there is nothing there to blame: the alternative was not one any of us would have taken either. What survives of the defence is therefore narrow but real. The Standards Reading fails, because it costs us progress and costs us praise as well. The Access Reading is the more promising of the two but has to be cashed out in terms of accessibility rather than bare availability. And the Standing Reading, which is the one doing most of the work in ordinary talk, is not really a defence of the agent at all — it is a claim about us.
