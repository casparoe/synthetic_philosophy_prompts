At a philosophy-of-economics reading group I attend, the session on Harsanyi's aggregation theorem ended in a sustained dispute between two members, and I could not determine who had the better of it. Both were working from the same statement of the result, which I will give here so that the argument can be evaluated on its own terms: if each individual's preferences over prospects satisfy the von Neumann–Morgenstern axioms, if the social preference over those same prospects also satisfies them, and if Pareto indifference holds (whenever every individual is indifferent between two prospects, so is society), then the social utility function is a positively weighted sum of the individuals' utility functions; and if in addition all individuals share the same subjective probability function over states of the world, those weights are unique up to a common positive affine transformation.

Their exchange ran as follows.

1. Marcus: the theorem settles the question. It does not assume that utilities are interpersonally comparable and then aggregate them; it derives an aggregation. And the uniqueness clause shows that the weights are not a free parameter: once the individuals' utility functions are fixed, there is a determinate fact about how much each person's utility counts. A positively weighted sum of individual utilities with determinate weights is utilitarianism in its technical form.

2. Ingrid: two things are being run together. First, von Neumann–Morgenstern utility functions are each unique only up to a positive affine transformation, so the numbers the weights take depend on an arbitrary choice of zero point and unit for every person; the theorem fixes an ordering, not a scale on which the relative importance of persons can be read off. Second, the utilities being summed are defined over prospects — lotteries — not over consequences or levels of well-being. What we have is a representation theorem about social preferences under uncertainty, and calling it utilitarianism requires the further premise that those utility numbers measure well-being.

3. Marcus: on the first point you have proved too much. Uniqueness up to a common positive affine transformation is just the ordinary von Neumann–Morgenstern non-uniqueness of the social utility function, not a residual freedom in the weights. If the weights could be chosen at will, the theorem would say nothing, since any weighting would do. What it says is that, relative to the individuals' own utility functions — which their risk attitudes pin down — the social ordering depends on those utilities through one specific linear combination. That is a substantive cardinal result, and it is precisely a form of interpersonal comparability, obtained rather than presupposed.

4. Ingrid: substantive, yes; utilitarian, no. Nothing in the axioms rules out a social utility function of the form u₁ + 100·u₂; every axiom is satisfied by it. Equality of weights is the whole of the ethical content of utilitarianism, and the theorem is silent about it. To get equality you need an anonymity condition, which is an ethical premise, or else the veil-of-ignorance argument, which is where Harsanyi actually tries to get it.

5. Marcus: then take the veil argument. An agent who does not know whose place she will occupy, who assigns equal probability to occupying each of them, and who chooses rationally, will rank prospects by the average of the utilities attaching to those positions. That is not an ethical assumption; it is individual expected-utility reasoning plus ignorance of one's own identity. So equality of weights is derived, and your objection is answered from inside Harsanyi's own framework.

6. Ingrid: the equiprobability assumption is an ethical premise in probabilistic disguise. There is no fact of the matter about some process that assigns you to a person; the assignment is a device, and the reason to use the equiprobable one rather than another is that it treats persons as equally important. Worse, both the veil argument and the theorem itself presuppose that the chooser's beliefs coincide with everyone else's, and that is not an idealization you can switch off: results on the aggregation of differing beliefs show that if individuals and society are all Bayesian and Pareto is respected, common priors are forced on everyone. So the same-beliefs assumption is not a harmless simplification but a substantive and false restriction, and without it the theorem has almost no grip on real populations, whose members disagree about facts as well as about values.

7. Marcus: that is the wrong test. The same-beliefs assumption is what isolates disagreement about values from disagreement about facts, and it is what blocks the spurious-unanimity worry: when people agree on what to do because they share the same information, their agreement is real, and a social ordering that tracked it would not be deferring to collective error. An idealization that is needed in order to state the question cleanly is not a defect. The theorem's conclusion is conditional, and the condition identifies the interesting case rather than evading it.

They stopped there, each convinced that the other had simply refused to see the point, and neither would say which of the seven moves they thought decisive. I would like a proper adjudication rather than a summary.

Please take the moves in order. For each one, say whether it is clearly right, largely right, partly right, largely wrong, or clearly wrong, and explain briefly what turns on it — in particular, whether the move depends on a mathematical feature of the theorem that one of them has misstated, or on a substantive claim about the relation between representation theorems and ethics. Where a move is right for the wrong reason or wrong for the right reason, say so. Then give your verdict on the central question: does the theorem establish utilitarianism, establish a restricted version of it, or establish neither? Be explicit about what each of them gets right that the other fails to acknowledge. Finally, identify what both are missing — assumptions, distinctions, or objections that neither raises but that bear on whether the result carries the significance they are fighting over.

You may take the statement of the theorem above as stipulated. I am not asking for the history of the literature or for references of any kind, only for the reasoning.
