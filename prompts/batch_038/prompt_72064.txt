The paradox of phenomenal judgment is supposed to be fatal for epiphenomenalism — if qualia don't cause anything, then our beliefs and reports about them are caused by brain states instead, so how can they be knowledge about qualia rather then lucky accidents? Argue the other way: that this isn't a special problem for the epiphenomenalist, and if anything the physicalist about consciousness is worse off.

The line I want developed: the objection quietly assumes that knowing about X requires X to play some causal role in producing the belief, but that assumption generates a problem for any non-causal subject matter (maths, morality, modal facts), so anyone who wants to keep some of those has to drop it anyway; and once it's dropped the epiphenomenalist can say the belief is about qualia in virtue of some necessitation or covariation relation — something the physicalist can't even state, since on their view there's nothing left over for the belief to latch onto.

Build it into a real argument, not a sketch. State the causal requirement and why the paradox needs it; make the parity move; give the epiphenomenalist's positive account of aboutness; then answer (1) the coincidence worry — that it'd be a miracle for the physical reports to line up so neatly with the experiences — and (2) the charge that the whole manoeuvre begs the question. Finally, say which steps a physicalist could sign onto and where you think the argument is weakest.
