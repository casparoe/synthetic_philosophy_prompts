I'm a third-year doctoral student in a program that straddles philosophy and economics, and this term I'm auditing a reading group on social epistemology: testimony, peer disagreement, the epistemics of markets and crowds. Our session this week was on herding, and because I wanted to push back against the standard informational-cascade story rather than just present it, I wrote up the following argument. It begins from assumptions I don't think anyone in the group wants to deny, and it ends somewhere I very much did not expect.

The setup is the familiar sequential one (Banerjee's model and Bikhchandani, Hirshleifer, and Welch's are both of this shape). There are two equally likely states of the world, and a line of agents who act in order, each choosing between two options. Before acting, each receives one private signal bearing on the state; each observes every earlier choice but nothing else about her predecessors' reasoning; signals are conditionally independent given the state. All agents are Bayesian, share a common prior, know the structure of the situation, and know that all of this is common knowledge. The argument:

1. Given common knowledge of rationality, an agent's choice is common knowledge among all downstream observers to be the option that maximizes her expected utility on her posterior, and her posterior is a function of the public history together with her private signal. An observer who sees the choice therefore knows exactly which signals could have produced it.
2. Suppose the first several agents all chose A and agent *n* chooses A as well. Her choosing A tells the observer that her signal lay in the set of signals that make A optimal given that history — and that set is strictly smaller than the set of signals she might have received, since some signals would have made B optimal and we did not see B.
3. So no choice anywhere in the sequence is uninformative. Even a choice that the literature counts as occurring inside a cascade rules out a nonempty range of signals, and therefore shifts the public posterior.
4. If the public posterior shifts at every step, then there is no history after which it stops being shiftable: the crowd never exhausts what its members are able to convey to one another.
5. Because signals are conditionally independent, agent *n*'s signal is not implied by any finite public history. Hence for every history, however long, there is a nonzero probability that her signal is of a kind that would have made the other option optimal for her.
6. From (3) and (5): at every position in the sequence there is positive probability that the agent acts against the crowd. A cascade in the strict sense — a choice wholly determined by the public history, such that the agent's own signal makes no difference to what she does — never occurs with positive probability.
7. Therefore the standard claim that a group of perfectly rational agents can lock onto the wrong option and remain locked there indefinitely is false. This is not a quibble about terminology: the results establishing wrong limit cascades for learners with bounded private signals, Smith and Sørensen's among them, must be misdescribing their own models, since those agents satisfy (1) through (5) as well.
8. Nothing in the argument used any feature peculiar to these toy models. All it required was sequential observation of choices, conditional independence of private evidence, and common knowledge of rationality.
9. So it generalizes. Whenever a consensus is the aggregate of individually rational responses to shared public evidence, one fresh piece of independent private evidence always has a nonzero chance of outweighing the whole of it. Hence the size or the unanimity of a consensus is never, by itself, a reason for a rational agent to discount her own contrary evidence, and deference to consensus is never required by rationality.

Two considerations stop me from being pleased with this. First, I coded the model up and ran it ten thousand times — two states, private signals of fixed reliability, everyone Bayesian, common prior — and cascades are everywhere, including wrong ones that never break for the remainder of the run; so (6) is empirically false of the very model the argument is about, and I cannot find where the derivation goes wrong. Second, (9) is not something I could defend aloud in a department where people study testimony: it seems to imply that a single anomalous measurement never obliges a scientist to defer to a settled field, that a unanimous jury carries no more evidential weight than each juror could have assembled alone, and that accumulated agreement as such has no force whatever. My adviser's only comment on the draft was that the argument is either invalid or unsound and that I should determine which.

So, three requests. Identify the step or steps that fail and give me the mechanism of the failure rather than a label for it: I want to understand precisely what is being assumed at that point that the earlier lines do not license, and what the situation looks like when that assumption is absent. Explain why the offending step looked perfectly innocuous to me an hour ago — I would have defended every line of this on sight, and I want to know what feature of it made it seem unarguable. Then price the alternatives: if I insisted on retaining it, what would I have to surrender elsewhere in the chain, and what would I lose in the way of ordinary results about learning from others? Finally, tell me whether anything worth keeping survives — in particular, whether (9) lives on in some restricted form once the damage is repaired, or whether the whole thing collapses as soon as the early steps go. I should say that I would rather keep (9) than any of the earlier premises, since the deflationary conclusion about deference is what interested me in the first place, but I recognize that this is a preference and not an argument. Please don't bother with citations or a bibliography; I'll supply those myself.
