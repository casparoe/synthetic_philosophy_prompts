I serve as moderator for my department's annual consciousness panel, and this year's guest defends a specific numerical claim about the split-brain cases: once the corpus callosum has been completely sectioned, there are, in the single organism sitting in the chair, two distinct conscious subjects — two thinkers, two loci of experience — and describing the patient as one subject who has merely lost the ability to share information with himself misdescribes what is there. She holds this even for the patients whose left-hemisphere speech is fluent and confident while the right hemisphere, addressed through the left visual field, reports that it saw nothing at all, and she insists that the two are not related as a person and a sub-personal module.

She is a careful, well-drilled speaker. Ordinary objections will be met with rehearsed responses: the objection is really about the concept of a subject rather than about the data; unity is a matter of degree while number is not; the everyday appearance of a single person is a theoretical prejudice rather than an observation. Please write the questions I will put to her.

Requirements:

Seven questions, each accompanied by one follow-up framed as a conditional — what I say if she gives the answer she is most likely to give. The follow-ups should be keyed to specific replies, not to a vague evasion, and at least three of them should be keyed to a retreat into conceptual analysis.

Cover the following ground, in an order that makes each successive question harder to deflect than the last:

1. The individuation principle. What exactly makes the count come out at two? Ask her to state the general criterion that determines how many subjects a single body contains, and then press its verdicts elsewhere: why is there one subject in an ordinary brain with two hemispheres doing quite different work, and why is she not committed to more than two in a patient whose disconnection is incomplete in some pathways but not others?

2. The functions that did not travel through the severed bundle. Mood and affective tone, facial expression, recognition of emotion, posture and gait, arousal and orienting, and many bimanual and whole-body coordinations are at best only partly lateralized and are demonstrably not abolished by the operation; patients smile, blush, weep, walk, and remain frightened or amused as a single body. Ask what a shared affective life does to the numerical claim, and follow up on the reply that these are sub-personal or non-conscious forms of unity — asking why the sharing of information through a fiber bundle counts as constitutive of one subject while the sharing of mood, fear, and a common bodily history does not.

3. The spectrum. Sections that spare the anterior portion, sections that spare the splenium, patients whose callosum never formed, and patients who underwent the operation in childhood and have had decades to recruit alternative routes. Ask where the number changes along this series, whether there is a fact of the matter in the intermediate cases, and whether her view needs a sharp threshold; follow up on the answer that a sorites series does not embarrass a counting claim, and on the answer that the intermediate cases simply are indeterminate.

4. Normal brains. Two hemispheres with different competences, different styles of attention, and partially conflicting evaluations, integrated only intermittently and often poorly. Ask whether her criterion yields one subject there, and whether she accepts or refuses the consequence that ordinary people are borderline cases of divided agency.

5. Identity, responsibility, and prudential concern. If there are two subjects after the operation, what became of the person who signed the consent form? Ask whether one of them survives, whether both do, or whether the question has no answer, and whether the preoperative person can be held responsible for what one of them does afterwards; follow up on the reply that this is a practical and legal matter rather than a metaphysical one.

6. What would show her wrong. Ask her to describe an experimental finding or a clinical presentation that would oblige her to abandon the numerical claim — not to soften it, not to call it underdetermined, but to give it up. If she answers that no behavioral result bears on a conceptual claim, press her to state plainly whether the claim is then compatible with every possible observation, and ask what content remains.

7. Identification before the fact. Ask whether she could tell, from imaging and preoperative testing alone and before any disconnection has occurred, how many subjects a given patient contains, and what her procedure would be.

Format the result as a moderator's card: the question as I would speak it aloud, in one or two sentences and free of technical vocabulary that only a specialist would use, then the follow-up beneath it, then a single line noting the reply that follow-up is designed to block. No biographical questions, nothing about what first drew her to the topic, and nothing she could answer by restating the view.
