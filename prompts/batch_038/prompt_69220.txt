For eleven years I have chaired the joint-resources committee of the faculty senate at a mid-sized research university, and I have come to think that we are the least principled body on this campus. Between six and ten disputes come before us each academic year in which exactly two units claim shares of something jointly funded: the operating budget of an instrument used by a laboratory and by a clinical service, a half-time coordinator position attached to two departments, the second year of a bequest left vaguely "for interdisciplinary work." The committee consists of five people — a molecular biologist, two historians, a professor of law, and me — none of whom has any training in economics, and we have ninety minutes per case, at the end of which we send a one-page recommendation to the provost, who has accepted every recommendation but two in my tenure. Our actual method has been to split things down the middle where they can be split, and where they cannot, to listen to whoever is more aggrieved and then describe the result as equitable. I have lately been reading the two-person bargaining literature, and the solution put forward by Kalai and Smorodinsky in the mid-1970s — the one, as I understand it, that selects the feasible point at which each party achieves the same proportion of the most it could individually hope to achieve, measured from its walkaway position — seems to me the only candidate that is both defensible and explainable to non-specialists inside ninety minutes. I may be conflating it with the simpler rule that just divides the surplus above the two walkaway positions equally; if I am, please say so plainly, distinguish the two for me, and tell me which one the committee should actually be applying and why, because my colleagues will ask and I should not have to improvise an answer.

What I want from you is the procedure itself: the questions to be asked in order, what would count as a sufficient answer to each, and where the procedure stops and hands the matter to a person because the remaining question is one of values rather than of fact. It must tell us how to fix the disagreement point when the two units describe their walkaway positions differently and one of them is, in my judgment, bluffing. It must tell us how to determine each unit's aspiration — which is the best it could get from what is genuinely feasible, not the most sumptuous thing it has said aloud in a memo — and I would like a step that polices this, because I suspect that the rule's sensitivity to alternatives nobody would ever choose means a department can improve its share merely by nominating a grander option into the set, and if that suspicion is wrong please correct it. It must tell us what to do when the frontier is coarse, since a coordinator position cannot be cut into seventeenths, and what to do when there are three claimants rather than two, my understanding being that the two-party result does not carry over and that the obvious extension can fail even to be efficient. And it should be candid about the common measure. Our provost publishes a standard cost per released course, which we use as a rough numeraire for converting dissimilar goods into comparable ones; I want to know how much weight that number is bearing, and what the procedure should say when one party's stake — a graduate program's continuity, say, or the loss of a long-running field site — is not the kind of thing a released course measures.

Then please run the procedure on three cases of the sort we see. First: a shared imaging instrument whose annual operating cost is covered by a $100,000 line, with two departments able to take fractional shares of instrument time and of the line, a walkaway position of $10,000 for the first department (it can buy time at a neighboring institution) and $20,000 for the second (it has an older instrument of its own), and one department has asked us to consider, in addition, a scenario in which it receives the entire line plus capital funding for a second machine. Second: three departments claiming a single jointly funded staff position, none of which will withdraw. Third: a dispute over a shared archive in which, as the papers were filed, one department's outside option appears to be better for it than any allocation we could actually construct — and here I expect the procedure to tell me that the committee has no business deciding, which will not please the provost, who has already spent a semester on the matter. For each case, show the reasoning the committee would go through and state the recommendation it produces, even where the recommendation is one I will dislike, and end by telling me what this procedure cannot do and where it will misfire in practice. Please write the whole thing as continuous prose, in the manner of a letter rather than a manual: no headings, no bullet points, no numbered lists set off from the text, no bold or italicized labels, and no tables. Signpost the steps in the sentence itself — first this, then that — so that I could read your answer aloud at the table without reformatting it.
