I run a reading group that's half history PhD students and half people from the lit department, and next month we're doing Remarque's All Quiet on the Western Front alongside the Fischer controvery — Fischer's claim that the German war aims were set down early, the Septemberprogramm stuff, Mitteleuropa, the annexationist programmes, and then ran in a pretty continuous line through German politics until 1945, and the enormous fight that kicked off in the early sixties with Gerhard Ritter and company, all the way down to the more recent drift back toward shared-responsibility pictures like Christopher Clark's Sleepwalkers. One of my people keeps saying the novel basically settles the war guilt question because it shows the war from underneath, from boys who had no hand in starting it, and I can't quite articulate why that annoys me. Help me articulate it.

What I want is an actual reading, not a summary. Where does the novel put responsibility? It never names a single war aim — nobody in the book seems to know what the war is for, nobody argues about Belgium or colonies or economic unions, and the blaming that does happen gets aimed at Kantorek the schoolmaster, at Himmelstoss the postman turned drill tyrant, at the Kaiser as a running joke the men make round a fire. Is that individualising move a way of dodging Fischer's question about state-level aims, or is it the claim that aims are simply the wrong level to look at? And how does Jaspers's little postwar book on German guilt cut in here — specifically the political guilt, that citizens are liable for what's done in their name whether or not they personally willed it, sitting alongside the criminal, moral and metaphysical kinds. I want to know whether the shell-hole scene with the French printer, where Paul goes through the dead man's letters and photographs and then can't bring himself to write to the widow, is metaphysical guilt in narrative form, or something those categories just don't fit. And the ending — that flat army report on the day Paul dies. Under a Fischer picture that sentence is an indictment of a state; under Jaspers it's about who's left carrying it afterwards. Can the novel let you tell those two readings apart, or does it deliberately collapse them?
