I'm a high school junior writing a paper for my school's history seminar on accident and necessity, and I want a case my classmates can't wave away with the usual for-want-of-a-nail story. The claim I'm testing is the popular one that because history is chaotic, history is therefore contingent — that unpredictability and fragility come as a package. I'd like you to design two thought experiments that pull them apart.

Set both in the same world, so the contrast can't come from a change of subject: a small Central European state in the 1840s, bad harvests, an unpopular government, and a live question of whether the regime falls. One paragraph per case, and no time machines, no omniscient historians, no mechanism beyond ordinary social causation.

Case one: the process is chaotic in the technical sense — small differences in starting conditions amplify fast enough that nobody at the time could have forecast the details — yet the large outcome, whether the regime falls, comes out the same under almost any perturbation of those starting conditions. Case two: reverse it, a process that runs as predictably as a clock yet turns entirely on a single nail.

Then do the checking work. Show where the amplification happens in case one, and name the structural feature that makes the outcome robust — something like a basin of attraction, if that's the right notion — instead of just asserting stability. Say what case one becomes if you remove that feature, and how a defender of the chaos-means-contingency view would attack my design, including the charge that I've described the outcome so vaguely that it looks inevitable by definition. The scenarios have to generate their verdicts from the mechanisms; a case that simply announces the regime survives doesn't count.
