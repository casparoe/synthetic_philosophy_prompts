Been going through an informal philosophy-of-physics reading group (self-taught, no professor in sight) and the analogy that has completely taken over our spacetime discussion is the chessboard. The line is: spacetime is like a chessboard and matter is like the pieces. The squares are there whether or not anything is sitting on them, a knight's legal moves are fixed by the board rather than by where the other pieces happen to be, and if you sweep all the pieces off you still have a perfectly good board. The guy who is most convinced by it uses it two ways. First, against relationism: picture every piece sliding one square to the right — nothing about the relations between pieces has changed at all, yet on the board it's plainly a different arrangement, so there has to be something real over and above the relational facts, and that something is the board. Second, for the bucket: the water goes concave because it's spinning relative to the board, and that's all the "relative to what?" question ever needed an answer to.

I suspect the analogy is carrying more weight than it can hold, but "it's just an analogy" gets me nowhere in the group. So audit it properly. I want the correspondence table — what is playing the board, the squares, the pieces, the legal moves — and then the disanalogies sorted into the ones that actually break the two arguments above versus the ones that are incidental (real boards have edges and only 64 squares, who cares). Mostly I want to know which features of boards the analogy quietly imports that spacetime doesn't have.

Some places I've already gotten stuck, tell me if I'm wrong:

- On a real chessboard, what makes e4 the square it is? Only its relations to the other squares and to the edges. So does the board actually supply the extra ingredient the first argument needs — a square being *that very square* independently of how it's related to anything — or does the analogy smuggle the relationists ontology back in through the back door?
- If the one-square shift is a symmetry of the board, meaning the board looks identical before and after, shouldn't a board-believer also say nothing real changed? And if they say that, hasn't the analogy argued itself back into relationism? This feels adjacent to the hole argument but I can't tell if it's the same move or a different one.
- The board doesn't bend. In GR the geometry is acted on by the matter and can carry waves through regions where there's no matter at all. Is there any way to keep the chessboard picture for that, or does it have to be swapped out, and swapped for what?
- The board is a picture of where things are, not of how they're accelerating. Newton's own harder version of the bucket case doesn't even need surrounding bodies: two spheres tied together by a rope, spinning about their common centre in an otherwise empty universe, and you read the rotation off the rope being taut. I can't see how a static grid of squares gets you tautness, so does the analogy help with the one case it's actually deployed for?

One of the relationists countered with a phone network — the nodes are only where the calls happen, there's no grid behind them, and if the calls stop there's no network left. Is that any better or does it just have the mirror-image problems?

End with a verdict: does the conclusion (there's a fact of the matter about absolute motion and placement, so spacetime is a thing in its own right) survive, partly survive, or was the analogy load-bearing the whole way? If there's a better analogy for what's going on, give it with its own failure points flagged up front — I don't want a name for the fallacy and I don't want to be told analogies have limits, I want the mapping worked out and the joints that are bearing weight identified.
