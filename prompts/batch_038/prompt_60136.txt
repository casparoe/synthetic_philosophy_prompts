I've read a decade of open-borders vs. right-to-exclude threads and every one of them eventually cites Book II ch. 2 of Grotius's *De Jure Belli ac Pacis*, so I finally pulled up the old 1814 Campbell translation that's floating around online to see what the chapter actually opens with, instead of what people say it says. Two bits, back to back:

"AMONG the causes assigned to justify war, we may reckon the commission of injury, particularly such as affects any thing which belongs to us. Now we establish this claim to any thing as our own either by a right COMMON to us as men, or acquired by us in our INDIVIDUAL capacity."

and then, on how property got started:

"All things, as Justin says, formed a common stock for all mankind, as the inheritors of one general patrimony. [...] a general exercise of a right, which supplied the place of private property. So that to deprive any one of what he had thus seized, became an act of injustice. Which Cicero has explained in his third book, on the bounds of good and evil, by comparing the world to a Theatre, in which the seats are common property, yet every spectator claims that which he occupies, for the time being, as his own. A state of affairs, which could not subsist but in the greatest simplicity of manners, and under the mutual forbearance and good-will of mankind."

Help me with two things.

1) The theatre. The usual gloss is that this shows individual exclusion is compatible with original common ownership — no contract needed, sitting down does it. But a theatre is precisely the place where there's an usher, and i think the load-bearing words are "for the time being," which reads like a use-right that lapses when you stand up rather than a property right you can hoard, fence off, or leave to your kids. If so, the analogy gets you the power to keep people off what you're currently occupying and nothing more: a restrictionist-sounding result reached by a route that would also condemn vacant seats and inheritance. Am I over-reading it, or does Grotius mean something stronger? Relatedly — does the "greatest simplicity of manners" caveat mean the whole mechanism only runs where everyone is already being forbearing, in which case it can't be the story for a modern state, where you'd need explicit agreement, at which point the right to exclude is a consent-right and not a common right at all?

2) That COMMON vs. INDIVIDUAL distinction in the first quote. Is the common right a standing claim each person keeps against the world after property gets set up, or just the pre-property baseline that gets consumed by the individual claims replacing it ("supplied the place of private property")? The restrictionist I'm arguing with insists on the second: once property exists the common right is spent, so a would-be migrant has nothing left to appeal to. Tell me which way the text goes and where exactly his reading breaks.

Just your reading of the passage, please — don't send me off to secondary literature.
