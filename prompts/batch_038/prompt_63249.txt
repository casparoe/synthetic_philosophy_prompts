My friend and I have been circling the same argument for the better part of a decade, most recently from ten at night until two this morning, and we have finally agreed to stop exchanging essays and instead produce one document that we would both be willing to put our names to. I would like you to draft it. Please treat the drafting as the task: what is hard, and what I want from you, is finding formulations that are neither empty nor slanted — not deciding which of us is right.

The subject is whether the no-free-lunch family of results — Wolpert and Macready's theorem for search and optimization, together with Schaffer's earlier conservation law for generalization performance — constitutes a formal vindication of Hume's problem of induction.

**Camp A, whom we call the conservationists.** Any advantage an inductive method enjoys over one class of worlds is exactly paid for by a deficit over the complementary class; the equalizing identity holds for the whole family of methods, so nothing about a method *as such* can be distinguished from anything else. Hence every appearance of inductive support is produced by assumptions imported from outside the method, and the thesis that induction is rational in general, prior to such imports, is not false but contentless.

**Camp B, whom we call the deflationists.** The theorems are true and are not in dispute; they are identities about averages taken over a space of target functions that assigns the same standing to worlds that punish regularity-projecting as to worlds that reward it, they are stated relative to a fixed measurable structure and, in the learning version, to a stable sampling arrangement, and they leave untouched the only question worth asking, which is what an agent with finite resources and a description of her situation should believe. On this view Hume's problem is neither solved nor sharpened by the no-free-lunch results; it is bypassed by them.

For the record I hold A and she holds B, and she suspects — I think correctly — that my formulations keep smuggling in what they need to establish. The document must be drafted to police that tendency on both sides.

To keep the disagreement from floating free of anything checkable, we have agreed that all of it will be run through one small shared testbed. **Setting S** is defined as follows. The domain is four points, x₁ through x₄, arranged on a cycle in that order, with x₄ adjacent to x₁. A target function is any assignment of 0 or 1 to each point, so there are sixteen of them; let T(f) be the number of cycle-edges across which f changes value. Three measures over targets are in play: M_u, uniform over all sixteen; M_s, which weights each function proportionally to 2 raised to the power −T(f); and M_a, which weights each function proportionally to 2 raised to the power +T(f). The protocol is: two distinct points are drawn uniformly at random and revealed together with their labels under the target; then a third point, distinct from the first two, is drawn uniformly at random from the two remaining points; the learner outputs a guess for its label; the score is expected accuracy over draws of points and draws of the target. Two variants will also be used. In S₁ the two observed points are fixed in advance rather than drawn. In S₂ the labels of the observed points are generated by one draw from M_s and the label of the test point by a second, statistically independent draw from M_s.

Please produce the following five parts.

**Part I — Agreed claims.** A numbered list of propositions both camps can sign without reservation. It must include: a precise statement, in your own words, of the hypotheses and the conclusion of the result each camp leans on, distinguishing the weak reading (identity of average performance across two methods) from the strong reading (identity of the entire distribution over the sequences of observed values), and stating which strength each camp's philosophical argument actually requires; a formulation of what Hume's problem asks that both camps accept as the target; and the derivation of the expected accuracy under M_u of an arbitrary learner, deterministic or randomized, with the symmetry consideration that fixes it.

**Part II — The disagreement, stated twice.** Two renderings of the residual disagreement: one in the conservationists' idiom, one in the deflationists', each drafted so that its own camp would accept the wording and the other camp would concede that it is not a caricature. Follow these with a short glossary of the terms the two camps use differently — at minimum *assumption*, *prior*, *general purpose*, *justification*, and *algorithm* — giving each camp's usage and a stipulation both could work with.

**Part III — Cruxes.** A table with these columns: the crux; the construction, proof, or thought experiment that both camps accept as bearing on it; the conservationists' prediction, recorded in advance and specific enough to be wrong; the deflationists' prediction; and what each camp concedes if its prediction fails. At least one concession must be philosophically expensive — a genuine retreat, not a shrug. Include at least these four cruxes, though you may sharpen or reword them:

1. Whether the standing hypothesis that the target is fixed while the data are drawn from a stable arrangement is itself an inductive assumption of the sort Hume questioned, so that the theorems are not premise-neutral.
2. Whether the equalizing identity survives restriction of the target class to functions describable or computable within a fixed resource bound — that is, whether the symmetry that drives the argument survives the loss of closure under permutation of the domain, and if not, what becomes of the philosophical moral.
3. Whether the worst-case online results — the multiplicative-weights aggregator over a finite class of predictors, whose regret against the best member of the class grows like the logarithm of the class size uniformly over all outcome sequences, matched by a lower bound saying no aggregator can do materially better — vindicate a general-purpose inductive method or merely relocate the inductive commitment into the choice of the class.
4. Whether M_u is a faithful formalization of having no inductive assumptions at all, or is instead a positive commitment that weights anti-inductive worlds equally with inductive ones; and, relatedly, whether the claim that for any two methods there are as many priors favoring the one as favoring the other carries any epistemic content, and what would make it non-trivial.

**Part IV — The verbal component.** State which portions of the dispute dissolve once the stipulations from Part II are adopted, give the definitions that dissolve them, and identify precisely what substantive residue survives the dissolution. If the residue is small, say so, and say whether it is worth the eleven years.

**Part V — Appendix: the arithmetic.** Work through S explicitly. Enumerate the sixteen targets by their transition counts and give the normalized weights under M_s and M_a. Compute the best achievable expected accuracy under M_s and identify a learner that attains it; compute the same quantity in S₁ and in S₂ and explain what changes. Then answer the question we could not settle at two in the morning: when the smoothness-biased learner's gain under M_s is offset by its loss under M_a, is that offset a *pairwise* fact about this particular pair of learners, or does conservation hold only after aggregation over the whole class of learners, and does either answer support one camp over the other? Close the appendix with one sentence from each camp saying whether any of this arithmetic bears on the philosophical question, and why the other camp disagrees.

Constraints on the document: it must be signable by both parties, so no passage may be written from a position of adjudication; no bibliographies, reading lists, or citations of any kind, and no appeals to what a given author really meant — where you state a result, state its hypotheses and conclusion yourself, and flag any hypothesis you are unsure of rather than papering over the uncertainty; everything must be intelligible to a reader with no access to the literature. Aim for something long enough to be exact and short enough that every sentence carries weight, on the order of two thousand words for Parts I through IV, with the appendix as long as the arithmetic requires. If, having done all of this, you think one camp is better placed than the other, say so in a single closing paragraph explicitly marked as not part of the joint statement.
