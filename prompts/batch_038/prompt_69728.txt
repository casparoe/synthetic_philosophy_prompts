I work in comparative policy analysis, and there is a pattern I keep running into that I have no name for. Take two adjacent countries with near-identical legal traditions and welfare states — say Sweden and Norway — and ask which is more liberal. Every indicator we publish is agreed on by both camps; nobody is suppressing data. Yet the verdict flips wholesale depending on how the indicators are combined: weight freedoms of speech, association, and private life and one country leads; weight market entry, property, and freedom from licensing and the other leads. The weights are never argued for, only adopted.

Sharper still, and this is the part I actually want a concept for: individual commentators use the same phrase in piece after piece, but which family of indicators counts as the real test tracks the conclusion they are already committed to, and the shift is never flagged — not by them, not by their critics, who shift the same way in the opposite direction. It is not cherry-picking, since the full data sit on the table; it is not a subgroup reversal of the sort statisticians warn about; and it is not merely that the question is vague, because the vagueness is being used directionally rather than symmetrically.

Is there an established concept for a dispute that survives complete agreement on all first-order facts because the operative weighting slides toward whichever answer each side wants? Tell me which named ideas come closest, how each fails to cover the case, and if none fits, define a term for it and give me its boundary conditions — where it ends and honest disagreement about weights begins, and what, if anything, could settle the comparison short of stipulating the weights. The label matters less to me than the carving.
