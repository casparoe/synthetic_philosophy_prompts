My younger brother is awaiting trial for murder in England, and his counsel is weighing whether to run the partial defence of loss of control under the Coroners and Justice Act 2009, which would substitute a manslaughter verdict. Reading the provisions to understand what we would have to establish, I have become preoccupied by how strange their internal architecture looks. The defence requires that he actually lost self-control; that this loss had a qualifying trigger — either fear of serious violence, or things done or said of an extremely grave character that gave him a justifiable sense of being seriously wronged; and that a person of his sex and age, with a normal degree of tolerance and self-restraint, might have reacted in the same or a similar way. It is unavailable if he acted from a considered desire for revenge, sexual infidelity is to be disregarded as a trigger, and the Act expressly says it does not matter whether the loss of control was sudden.

What account of the emotions does this machinery presuppose? The first limb sounds as though anger is a pressure that overwhelms a separate faculty of control, yet the trigger limb and the objective limb ask whether his anger was directed at something that warranted it and whether an ordinary person would have felt it too — as though emotions were the kind of thing that can be justified or unjustified. Is the statute committed to both pictures, and can they be held together? What explains the revenge and infidelity exclusions under each — in particular, why should the content of a trigger matter at all if the operative fact is simply that control was lost? And which limbs would have to be rewritten if the law's implicit view were replaced by one on which an emotion is a perception of value rather than an inner surge?
