A mid-sized school district piloted a math tutoring app last year. The vendor's press release says it raises pass rates. The district's own year-end data, which I got, looks like this:

| school | app users | pass rate | non-users | pass rate |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| North | 300 | 92% | 2,700 | 90% |
| Central | 1,000 | 72% | 2,000 | 70% |
| South | 1,700 | 54% | 2,300 | 52% |
| whole district | 3,000 | 63.8% | 7,000 | 71.8% |

So in every single school the app kids do two points *better*, and district-wide they do eight points *worse*. Background facts I've confirmed with the district: the app was handed out building by building starting with the lowest-performing ones, which is why South has the most users; use within each school was voluntary — families opted in; and the district can't tell me whether its test-attempt pipeline dedupes student IDs correctly across years.

Here's what I've collected from people I talked to. Eight claims, roughly as they said them:

**A — vendor's head of data:** "There's no paradox here at all. The district number is just a weighted average, and the two columns have different weights because the app went to the low-baseline schools. Do the arithmetic and it comes out exactly as predicted. Nothing weird is happening."

**B — a causation person I emailed:** "Both tables are true, they answer different questions. The district-wide gap is the policy number — it's what actually happened when you ran this rollout in the real world, sorting and all. The per-school numbers are a hypothetical about a kid in a fixed school. If a journalist prints only the second table she's committing a category error."

**C — district IT lead:** "It's a pipeline artifact. We dedupe test attempts by student ID and a couple of the app schools recycled IDs from the prior year, so the app column likely has double-counted kids and wrong denominators. Fix it and the reversal goes away."

**D — an education economist:** "The number to distrust is the +2, not the aggregate. Use was voluntary, so within each school the app cohort is the motivated kids with the engaged parents. Two points is inside what self-selection buys you for free. The aggregate reversal is a real compositional fact; the +2 is not a real effect."

**E — a parent advocate:** "The app is hurting the schools that need it most. Look at South — worst numbers in the district. If it worked, South would have improved."

**F — a second analyst at the vendor:** "Standardize it. Reweight both groups to a common school mix and the app is ahead by about two points everywhere. The honest headline is that it helps."

**G — a statistician I quoted in an earlier piece:** "Textbook Simpson's paradox. The moral is that aggregate statistics mislead and you should always disaggregate."

**H — a grad student who read that piece and wrote in:** "It depends whether school is a confounder or a mediator. If the app caused kids to change schools — say families transferred in to get access — then school sits on the causal path and conditioning on it is exactly wrong, you'd be blocking part of the effect you're trying to measure." (For what it's worth: transfers into app schools were up 6% that year and the district cant say why.)
