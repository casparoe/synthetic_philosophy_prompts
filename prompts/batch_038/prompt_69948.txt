I'm a senior in a philosophy seminar on responsibility and punishment, and my final paper — about four thousand words, due Thursday — is an argument about the Dennett–Caruso disagreement over just deserts. My seminar partner read the central section and told me it was clever but empty, that I had simply given the familiar compatibilist-versus-skeptic split a new coat of paint, and I have not been able to shake the worry since. Before I commit another week to it, I'd like a serious critical read. Below is the section roughly as it stands; it is meant to occupy about twelve hundred of the paper's words and to carry the whole thesis. I realize it is compressed and that the opening sections, which I have not included, do more work setting up the two views.

SECTION 3: THE DIRECTION OF JUSTIFICATION

Both Dennett and Caruso want punishment to be constrained, and I want to argue that they disagree less about metaphysics than about where the constraint comes from — and that this second disagreement is the one that matters.

Caruso's rejection of basic desert is driven by what I will call a metaphysical precondition. To deserve something in the basic sense, on his account, an agent must be the ultimate source of her conduct; because neither determinism nor indeterminism nor the sheer luck of one's neurological endowment leaves room for that, no one deserves to be punished, and any practice that distributes suffering on the ground that it is intrinsically fitting is thereby unjustified. What takes its place is forward-looking: the state may restrict the liberty of the dangerously criminal for reasons structurally like the reasons it may quarantine the dangerously contagious. The constraints on that power are real and demanding — least restrictive alternative, genuine duties of care toward the detained, no gratuitous hardship, release once the danger has abated — but their source is the detainee's standing as a person with rights and needs, not anything she has earned.

Dennett's 'just deserts' is not purchased with a precondition of that kind. Desert attaches to competent agents: agents who can detect reasons and adjust their behavior in light of them, and who are therefore proper addressees of the practice of holding one another responsible. On this picture what a person deserves is fixed largely from inside the practice — by what that practice counts as competence, by the norms it applies to its participants, and by the reactive attitudes of people who are themselves embedded in it. That, I suggest, is why Dennett can be comparatively relaxed about ultimate origination: the question of whether she is really the source gets answered at the level of capacities rather than at the level of the causal history of the universe.

Here is my worry. A standard that is fixed from inside a practice is revisable from inside the practice, and revisable in precisely the direction that consequentialist pressure pushes. If desert is a matter of what our practice of holding responsible licenses, then the practice may be re-engineered: we can narrow or widen who counts as competent, alter what the reactive attitudes track, recalibrate the penalties attached to given conduct, and the deliverances of the revised practice count just as fully as 'what is deserved' as the old ones did. The ceiling that desert is supposed to supply — no more suffering than the offender deserves — turns out to move with the very considerations it was supposed to be insulated from.

Caruso's constraints have the opposite structure. They are stated in a vocabulary — rights, dignity, basic needs — whose application to a given person does not depend on how the practice currently understands itself. A quarantine model can therefore say that continued detention of someone who is no longer dangerous is wrong even if the practice, revised under institutional pressure, has come to regard it as fitting. The skeptic about desert has the harder-edged constraint.

So the two positions trade places relative to our ordinary expectations. The side that affirms desert supplies a ceiling that bends; the side that denies it supplies one that does not.

Four things I would like from you, in this order:

First, accuracy. I am not asking you to correct my interpretation with citations — please do not send me a reading list, do not suggest sources, and do not supply titles or page numbers, since I will not be able to check any of it before Thursday and would rather not have half-remembered references embedded in my footnotes. What I want is a judgment about whether either characterization is seriously overstated as it stands, and if so, a description in your own words of what the fairer version would look like. In particular I suspect the obvious reply on Dennett's behalf is that competence is not practice-relative at all — that whether a given person can detect and respond to reasons is an empirical fact about her that the practice merely registers, and that nothing about desert is fixed from inside anything. Please tell me whether that reply is available to him, and if it is, what it costs him: does it collapse his view back into the metaphysical precondition he is trying to avoid?

Second, the emptiness charge. Is my 'direction of justification' framing doing independent work, or is it a redescription? Concretely: name a substantive consequence that my framing has and that a plain statement of the disagreement does not, or else concede that it does not and tell me what the section should be reduced to.

Third, the symmetry objection, which I think is the most dangerous one and which I have not yet dealt with in the draft. My argument says that a practice-internal standard bends under pressure. But dangerousness is also an empirical and revisable judgment, and prediction is revisable upward as readily as downward: a quarantine model can be stretched to detain people whose risk is modest, contested, or merely projected, and it has no natural upper bound at all once desert is gone, whereas a retributivist ceiling at least purports to cap the amount of suffering by reference to the offense. Tell me whether this defeats my conclusion or merely relocates it, and say which of the two models is better protected against expansion in practice rather than in principle.

Fourth, structure. Would the distinction between the general justifying aim of a punitive practice and the principles governing its distribution give Dennett a clean way to block the revisability move — that is, can he hold that the aim is practice-internal while the distribution is constrained from outside? If so, I would like to know whether that concession is stable for him or whether it quietly imports the very thing he rejects.

Finally, please write out the single strongest objection to my thesis as a paragraph of about one hundred and fifty words in a register I could drop into the paper more or less as it is, followed by one or two sentences on whether you think that objection can be answered.
