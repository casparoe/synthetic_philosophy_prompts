I've spent the last year reporting a long piece on how psychiatric diagnosis actually works, and one feature of the practice has resisted every explanation I've tried. A single professional association publishes an official manual listing the recognized mental disorders, each defined by an operational criteria set: a list of symptoms, a minimum number of them that must be present, a required duration, and a clause demanding distress or impairment. Those criteria are periodically rewritten by appointed committees, which merge, split, rename, delete, and add disorders, citing new research and clinical experience as their grounds. The resulting manual is not treated as a specialist's reference work. Insurers decide reimbursement by it; courts and schools use it to allocate rights and services; pharmaceutical trials use it to select participants; epidemiologists count how many people have each disorder by it. So the criteria are doing enormous practical work, they change on a schedule set by a committee, and everyone involved appears to regard each revision as progress in knowledge rather than as a change of policy.

What I can't resolve is the following. Whenever a revision loosens a category and the number of people diagnosed with it climbs steeply over the next few years, commentary divides into two camps. One says the old criteria were too narrow and that we are at last detecting a condition that was always there and always about this common: the rise reflects better detection, and the underlying phenomenon did not change. The other says we manufactured the rise by naming it — that publishing a label, with its criteria and its treatment, changes what people notice about themselves, what they report to clinicians, what clinicians listen for, and eventually who those people are, so that the new numbers describe a world that now contains more of the thing because the classification helped create it. Both camps treat the same statistics as evidence about the same underlying reality, and they cannot both be right, yet the practice seems to accommodate both readings without strain, and the manual's authors appear to think they can remain neutral between them. There is a related wrinkle I keep running into: the evidence used to validate the next revision comes largely from patients describing themselves in a vocabulary they partly learned from the current edition, and from clinicians coding to the current criteria, and I am told this is not circular, though I cannot see why not.

Please reconstruct the account of the classified world that this practice presupposes. What must the manual's authors believe about the relationship between a diagnostic category and the people who fall under it for the whole arrangement — committee revision, downstream legal and financial deployment, prevalence counting — to make sense? Which rival accounts does it rule out outright, and which does it merely fail to favor? I would like a firm separation between what the practice strictly requires and what it is simply consistent with, because I suspect a good deal of the claimed neutrality comes from running those two together. Then tell me whether both camps above are available to someone working inside the practice, or whether one of them is committed to something the practice denies — and whether my own inclination, that the second camp is right and the neutrality claim is a dodge, is a defensible reading or an overstatement. Finally, suppose we decided to build the system explicitly on the second camp's assumption, that classifying changes the classified. Describe what would have to change: how criteria would be written and revised, what would count as evidence for a revision, what the numbers in a prevalence survey would then mean, and how courts and insurers could continue to rely on a manual whose categories they knew were partly self-fulfilling.
