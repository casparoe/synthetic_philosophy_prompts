For several years now I have been reading about evolution on my own, without ever having taken a course in it, and I have gotten myself tangled in a question I cannot seem to think my way out of. I would be grateful for a serious essay that helps me settle it, and I will try to describe the difficulty precisely enough that a general answer about group selection would not actually touch it.

The question is what it means to say that selection operates "at" a level, and whether the level attribution can ever be more than a matter of bookkeeping. I understand the broad outlines of the dispute: on one side, the view that there is really only one kind of selection, differential reproduction of replicators, and that talk of groups, organisms, or species being selected is at best a convenient redescription of what genes are doing; on the other side, the view that a population structured into interacting groups genuinely contains more than one selective process at once, so that describing a trait as favored within groups but disfavored between them is a substantive claim about the world rather than a choice of notation. I also know the intermediate position that both descriptions are always available and equally correct, differing only in usefulness. What I cannot decide, and what I would like the essay to decide, is whether that intermediate position is actually true, or whether it quietly concedes the point to one of the other two sides.

To make the problem concrete rather than abstract, I have constructed a case. I invented the species, so please treat every detail below as stipulated and argue from it directly rather than trying to match it to something in the literature.

Consider a social wasp. Each spring, a nest is founded by two to four queens drawn essentially at random from the overwintering population, so nestmates are related but not closely, and relatedness varies from nest to nest. All males come from unfertilized eggs; the queens lay the great majority of eggs, but workers can and do lay male eggs of their own. Now suppose a heritable tendency appears such that a worker who encounters an egg laid by another worker eats it, while leaving queen-laid eggs alone. Stipulate the following three facts:

1. A nest containing at least one lineage with this tendency produces about forty percent more reproductive offspring than a nest containing none, because internal egg-eating otherwise consumes enough worker effort and brood to cripple the nest.
2. Within a mixed nest, a worker of a non-policing lineage leaves roughly three times as many sons as a policing worker of the same size, since policing costs time and the policer's own eggs are eaten by her nestmates in turn.
3. Because every nestmate polices every other, the cheating advantage in clause 2 disappears once policing is common in the nest: a nest founded by policing queens becomes uniformly policing, and policing is not a sacrifice the policer makes for others so much as a mutual constraint all parties impose on one another.

Note also that the nest does not reproduce. It dissolves each autumn, and nothing corresponding to a nest produces daughter nests; only individual queens and males carry anything into the following year. The forty percent advantage in clause 1 is real, but the entity that enjoys it does not persist or have offspring.

Write an essay of roughly 2,200 words on whether, in a case of exactly this shape, the between-nest difference described in clause 1 counts as selection at the level of the nest, or whether the honest description is that only individuals and their genes are being selected and the nest figures only as part of the environment they happen to share. The essay should engage clause 3 seriously, because it seems to me to cut against both of the standard answers at once: policing is not obviously altruism, so the familiar story about self-sacrifice being repaid by group advantage may not apply, yet the group-level correlation between trait and productivity is undeniable. It should state the strongest argument available to the side it rejects and explain why that argument fails or falls short, and it should say explicitly what would have to be true of the case for the verdict to reverse.

Please do not end in a balanced shrug. I want a defended conclusion, even if it is a qualified one of the form "this counts as group selection only under such-and-such a description, and that description is doing real work because so-and-so." Define any technical term the first time you use it, and write for a careful reader who has no training. Please do not include a bibliography, a list of further reading, or footnote-style attributions; you may name a familiar position or a well-known argument in the body of the text where that genuinely helps, but do not build the essay out of references. Write in American English.
