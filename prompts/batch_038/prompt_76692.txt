Building this chain for a seminar on international criminal law and the ending makes me uneasy, but I can't find where it snaps:

1. Nobody may be criminally punished for conduct that wasn't criminal under law binding on them at the time they acted.
2. In 1939 no international criminal statute or court existed and there was no settled practice of trying individuals for starting a war; the charter that created the offence of crimes against peace was drawn up by the winning powers after the events and bound only the loosers.
3. So convicting the major war criminals of crimes against peace breached (1).
4. A proceeding that punishes people for what wasn't criminal when they did it isn't a criminal trial but force in judicial dress — and since the court's jurisdiction over the accused was created by the very party prosecuting him, not even a fully prospective statute would have cured that.
5. So Nuremberg wasn't a trial in the sense that carries authority.
6. Every institution since — the ad hoc tribunals, the permanent court, the whole idea that individuals can be charged over aggression at all — claims descent from that precedent and borrows its standing from it.
7. So the enterprise rests on a founding act that was itself lawless and inherits the defect: the honest conclusion is abolition, not reform.

I want to keep (1) if I can — it's the only thing I can see standing between law and violence. So which step do you reject, why did it look perfectly fine when I wrote it down, and what does rejecting it cost me? And if you think the chain is valid as it stands, tell me which premise is cheapest to lose and price the alternatives against each other rather than just listing them. Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then the reasoning — no preamble, and don't restate my question back at me. British English please.
