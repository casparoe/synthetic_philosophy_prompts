I'm auditing a joint seminar run by our economics and philosophy departments this term, on the foundations of welfare economics, and I come at it from philosophy with almost no formal economics — which is why I'm writing this up rather than trying to settle it myself. Last week a visiting speaker presented survey evidence about automatic enrollment in workplace retirement saving: employees enrolled by default, with an opt-out, accumulated far more than those who had to opt in; and when the speaker's team later interviewed both groups, large majorities of the opt-in group — the ones who had never signed up — said they wished they had saved more, while almost nobody in the default group wished they had been enrolled. The speaker concluded that the defaults made people better off. Two other members of the seminar, both graduate students but on opposite sides of the disciplinary divide, went at it afterward in the hallway and kept going for the better part of an hour. I'm reporting the exchange as neutrally as I can; I didn't take a side, and I still can't tell who was ahead when it ended.

(1) Nadia said the speaker's conclusion contradicts the theory he is invoking: since the ordinalist turn, welfare economics has measured a person's good by her choices rather than by some felt magnitude inside her; a woman who declined to enroll revealed that she preferred consumption now to consumption at seventy; so calling her worse off appeals either to a notion of utility the theory explicitly discarded or to the speaker's own values about thrift. (2) Owen said that misdescribes revealed preference: it was never a definition of well-being but a consistency theory with testable content — choices satisfy the axioms or fail to — and it is an empirical fact that people violate them. The same person who skipped enrollment at thirty will tell you at forty-five, unprompted and calm, that she regrets it; that is not confusion about her own good but a conflict between two of her temporal selves, and there is a real question about which of them is better placed to judge. (3) Nadia said fine, but then which one, and on what grounds? Owen has posited a preference over preferences and now needs a rule for picking the authoritative level, and no such rule can be read off choice data alone. Worse, the rule is unstable in both directions: if the later self can veto the earlier, every lapse is retroactively nullified and nothing anyone does is safe from being reclassified as a mistake by some future self who has changed her mind; and if you index the choice situation finely enough to include which self is doing the choosing, the axioms can never be violated — at which point the theory she accused it of being, an unfalsifiable restatement of whatever happened, is back. (4) Owen said not any rule, a specific one: endorsement under reflection, adequate information, and the absence of distorting conditions — and it stays inside the framework, because the endorsement is itself something people choose. They reveal in cool states that they want their hot states constrained: they sign advance directives, put money in accounts with withdrawal penalties, ask friends to hide their keys, and, in this study, said they were glad someone else's decision had bound them. A preference for being bound is still a revealed preference. (5) Nadia said that concedes the point instead of answering it. Run the identical machinery the other way: someone who resolves, calmly and informed, to quit smoking, fails, and then at sixty says she is glad she smoked because it carried her through the worst years of her life. Owen's rule pronounces the resolution her real preference and the retrospective gladness a rationalization — but nothing observable makes the cool self authoritative rather than the self who speaks last, or the one whose values happen to match the policy under discussion. He has reinstated a utilitarian judge inside the agent's skull, and the judge's verdicts always coincide with what the policymaker wanted. (6) Owen said the asymmetry is not a priori but empirical, and it runs one way: people in cool states systematically ask for their hot-state choices to be overridden, while almost nobody pays to be made less patient, less sober, or less informed. The retrospective-gladness cases exist but are a minority, and much of that minority looks like preference formation under deprivation — precisely the distorting condition his rule excludes. (7) Nadia said "deformed by conditions" is the escape hatch that makes the whole apparatus unfalsifiable: whichever preference survives the test counts as authentic, whichever fails counts as corrupted by a condition named on demand. And she said the deeper cost is to the word itself — utility began as a felt state, became an index of choice precisely in order to stop being a felt state, and Owen has now made it a third thing, the verdict of a hypothetical better-informed chooser, which is neither observed nor defined by choice and therefore carries no more empirical content than the notion the ordinalists were trying to eliminate. He has not rescued welfare economics; he has changed the subject and kept the name. (8) Owen said he would rather have a concept that can be wrong than one that cannot, and pointed out that Nadia's version can criticize no choice at all — not one made under duress, ignorance, exhaustion, or delirium — cannot say that a policy improved anyone's situation, and cannot compare one person's lot with another's. It describes markets and grounds no normative claim whatsoever; and then, when a speaker uses the label to say that defaults helped people, her objection is not to the claim but to the word.

What I want from you is an adjudication of the argument as it was actually made. Please take the eight moves one at a time and say who is right at each rather than who wins overall — rate each move somewhere on a scale from clearly mistaken to clearly right, and justify each rating briefly. I'm especially interested in the technical questions underneath, since I can't tell whether either of them is misstating a standard view: whether revealed preference theory really is falsifiable given its axioms, whether fine-grained description of the choice situation destroys that falsifiability, and whether privileging a reflective or calm self over an impulsive one can ever be justified by choice data alone or always requires an independent normative premise. Then tell me what both of them are missing — is there a third account of what utility is supposed to mean that neither one articulated, and would it dissolve the disagreement or merely relocate it? Finally, please be explicit about your confidence: separate what is settled among people who work on these questions from what is genuinely contested, do not present a contested position as though it were the consensus, and do not dress up your own verdict as the standard view. I'm not asking for references and I have no way to check them, so please argue it through from the reasoning rather than from names and titles.
