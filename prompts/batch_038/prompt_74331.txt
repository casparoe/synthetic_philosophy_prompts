I sit on the admissions panel for a small magnet program at our district's only high school. This year one seat was left and two files came back identical on every criterion in the rubric: same composite score, same two blind readers rating the essays as indistinguishable, same walking distance, same household income band. The handbook sends ties to a drawing of lots, and the drawing falls to me to conduct. One of the two applicants is my niece — which is why I recused myself from the scoring but not from the drawing, a line I can no longer defend even to myself. During the meeting we rejected the obvious alternatives: alphabetical order by surname, timestamp of submission, whoever was listed first in the export. Each was dismissed for the same reason, that it lets a difference with nothing to do with the children decide between them; the lottery was preferred precisely because a coin has no reason to favor either, and so, we told ourselves, remains impartial all the way down.

Write a short story of about twelve hundred words out of this situation. What I want it to think through, without ever stepping outside the scene to explain itself: whether a lottery resolves the tie or merely relocates it to a device that has no interest in the outcome; whether exact indifference between two options is something a person can actually have, or only assert — and what it means that eight of us found no difference while I, holding two folded slips, cannot pretend to have none; whether searching for a reason to prefer one of them would be a corruption of the procedure or the honest thing the procedure was built to hide. Underneath it all I want the old puzzle of the ass between two identical bales of hay: the story should let the reader wonder whether that animal dies of symmetry, or of a mistaken claim about its own mind.

Keep the philosophy in the furniture — the slips, the envelope, the two families in the hallway, the panel chair who wants to be home by six. End at the moment of the drawing and not afterward.
