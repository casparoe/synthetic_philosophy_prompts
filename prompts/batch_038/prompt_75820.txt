I am in the second year of a philosophy master's programme, and this term I am taking a seminar on concepts, categories, and what fixes the extension of a category. Our final session is an adversarial collaboration. Because my seminar partner and I have been on opposite sides of the same argument since week three and have spent the whole term talking past each other, the convenor has asked me to draft the joint document we ought to have produced. I would like your help drafting it properly.

The question is the one we were set in week two: **what makes every 'a' an 'a'?** Consider the range of shapes that count as the letter a — roman, italic, blackletter, uncial, a child's unsteady manuscript, a wordmark with the crossbar removed, a stencil with the counter left open — and yet literate readers recognise all of them instantly, and agree that a given one is a good 'a', a marginal 'a', or not an 'a' at all.

Two camps. I will label them for convenience, noting that each objects to the other's label:

**The Archetype camp.** There is something real that all 'a's have in common and that explains both the recognition and the grading: a flexible, high-pressure *spirit* of the letter — not a list of strokes, but a characterisable cluster of competing demands (legibility, ease of the hand or of the tool, descent from earlier forms, the need to stay distinguishable from neighbouring letters) that jointly carve out a region of shape-space. Variants differ in *fidelity* to that archetype; a borderline case is borderline because the demands genuinely conflict there, not because we lack information. They hold the archetype is articulable in principle, and they resent being called template-mongers: nothing is stored, and the archetype constrains production and recognition rather than being a picture to be matched.

**The System camp.** There is no intrinsic a-ness in the ink. A glyph is an 'a' only relative to a writing system and a trained community: it occupies the a-slot in an alphabet, it is picked out by contrast with the other members (o, e, d, q, the capital A), and it is fixed by readers' dispositions, the habits of scribes and typefounders, and the rulings of standards bodies. Apparent fidelity to an archetype is the shadow the system casts — shapes converge on a region because of shared functional pressures, not because they track an essence. They resent being called formalists: the "system" is a living practice, not a code.

Neither camp is a definitionalist about strokes; both agree that no finite feature list captures the category. That agreement is real and the document should record it, because the interesting disagreement lies elsewhere.

Both camps accept the following as data. Please build on these rather than reaching for others, though you may add cases of your own if you flag them as additions:

1. Lowercase a has two standard shapes — the double-storey form of most roman type, and the single-storey form of italics, handwriting, and the faces set in children's reading primers. Type designers have distinct names for them and treat both as the same letter.
2. Lowercase g has a looptail and an opentail form. The looptail is the older; most serif faces use it and most sans-serifs do not; Unicode treats them as glyph variants of a single codepoint while supplying a separate codepoint for the script g where it must be distinguished.
3. In the International Phonetic Alphabet the script a is a *different letter* from Latin a, and for a period around the turn of the twentieth century the IPA assigned the two g forms different sound values. The same shapes have been one letter and two.
4. The lowercase a of the Cyrillic alphabet is, in most typefaces, indistinguishable in shape from the Latin a, yet they are different letters of different alphabets, and a plain text search for one will not find the other.
5. The German ß began life as a ligature, functions as a letter of its alphabet, and until recently had no accepted capital form.
6. A proposed experiment both camps would agree to run: ask fluent adult readers, outside any word context, to reproduce the looptail g from memory and then to select it from an array of g-like shapes; separately, have them read a paragraph set in that face at normal speed. The expectation under test is an asymmetry between fluent reading and isolated recognition and production.

Please deliver the document in exactly these sections, in this order:

**1. Shared Ground** (numbered, roughly 300 words). Claims about letters, variation, and recognition that both camps can sign. Every claim must be phrased so that neither camp would feel the need to append a footnote. Include at least one claim that a naive stroke-definitionalist would reject, since agreeing against that position is part of what these two have in common.

**2. The Disagreements** (a table, five to seven rows). Columns: the disagreement in one sentence; the Archetype camp's preferred formulation of its own side; the System camp's preferred formulation of its own side; and a verdict of *substantive* or *verbal*. Each camp must be able to read its own column without feeling caricatured.

**3. Cruxes** (roughly 500 words; three or four, ranked by how much the debate would move if each were settled). For each: the precise question; a concrete case or experiment — drawn from the list above or clearly marked as a new proposal — that both camps would accept as bearing on it; each camp's prediction, written down in advance; and what outcome would oblige each camp to revise.

**4. What Is Merely Verbal** (roughly 200 words). Which parts of the dispute turn on the words *essence*, *spirit*, and *representation*, and which parts survive a complete renomination.

**5. The Residual** (roughly 150 words). One or two sentences per camp stating the disagreement that remains once the verbal parts are stripped away, each phrased so that the other camp could read it aloud and say: yes, that is exactly what I deny.

Please do not cite sources, name real scholars, or attach a bibliography — I want the reasoning, not the references. Invented cases and experiments are welcome provided they are flagged as inventions. Aim for roughly 1,300 to 1,500 words in total.
