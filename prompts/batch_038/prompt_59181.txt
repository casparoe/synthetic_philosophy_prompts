I spent thirty-eight years directing an analytical chemistry laboratory, and since retiring I have been working through the philosophy of mind on my own. One argument I keep returning to is the paradox of phenomenal judgment, which I understand roughly as follows. If phenomenal properties are causally inert, then the physical processes that produce our reports and judgments about experience do their work without any contribution from experience itself. It follows, apparently, that the accuracy of those reports is a standing miracle: we would say precisely what we say even if the phenomenal properties were rearranged or absent, and the correspondence between what we report and what there is would be, at best, the byproduct of a common physical cause. What I cannot determine is whether this is a special objection to epiphenomenalism or a generic difficulty attaching to any property that is real, accurately reported, and causally idle. I should like a thought experiment that decides the matter for me, or at least makes the shape of the difficulty visible.

Please construct a case to the following specifications.

The setting: a realistic working laboratory or field station — people, instruments, data, ordinary time pressure — not a cosmic or science-fictional scenario, and no immaterial souls, no philosophers' zombies, and no exotic physics. The scenario itself should run to a single paragraph.

The content: within that setting there must be a property P that is (i) genuinely instantiated, (ii) covariantly and non-accidentally tracked by the reports of some agent or instrument in the case, and (iii) causally inert with respect to those reports, where "inert" is to be understood interventionally: no intervention on P, holding fixed everything physical, alters what is said, written, or registered. P must be a wholly physical-world property — nothing dualist — so that the case is available to a materialist and cannot be dismissed as smuggling in the very dualism at issue.

Constraints I care about most:

1. The inertness of P must follow from the construction rather than be asserted. My own first attempt fails this test, and I should like you to do better. I imagined a simulation in which a quantity is computed at every step and written to a log that nothing ever reads, so that a report generated from the same inputs is a perfect shadow of the logged quantity. But that property is inert only by an accident of wiring; reconnect the log and it becomes causally live, whereas phenomenal properties are supposed to be inert in principle. Your P must be inert in something like that stronger sense, and you should tell me honestly how much strength you have achieved and what remains contingent about it.

2. The tracking must be counterfactually robust, not lucky. There must be possible situations in which P would take a different value and the reports would differ accordingly. A property that is always instantiated, or a report that would come out the same however things stood, tracks nothing, and I would count it as cheating.

3. Nothing may be settled by stipulation. In particular, you may not simply announce that the reports are accurate; the accuracy has to be earned by the structure of the setup.

4. You must confront the access problem directly. If P is causally inert, nobody inside the case can measure it, and it is hard to see on what grounds we — the readers, standing outside the case — are entitled to call the reports accurate rather than merely correlated. Explain where the warrant for "accurate" comes from in your construction, and say whether the phenomenal case enjoys a warrant of the same kind or of a different kind.

Then, after the scenario:

- Give me the causal audit. Trace the chain that produces the reports, link by link, and point to where P does and does not appear in it.
- State the paradox of phenomenal judgment as a short numbered argument, and say precisely which of its premises your case instantiates and which it does not.
- Identify the load-bearing feature — the one element of the construction without which the case stops biting — and ablate it: tell me what the case becomes once that feature is removed.
- Give the strongest reply available to a defender of epiphenomenalism who wants to block the transfer of the paradox to your case. I expect such a reply to appeal to something like acquaintance, or to the claim that the relation between experience and judgment is constitutive rather than causal, or to the claim that P is a mere redescription of its physical correlate rather than a further fact about the world. Take the best of these seriously, say whether it succeeds, and say whether it can be advanced without presupposing the falsity of epiphenomenalism.
- Close with your own verdict on what the case is worth: does it show that the paradox is a generic puzzle about causally idle yet accurately reported properties, and therefore a cost to be weighed against the benefits of the view rather than a refutation of it — or does the phenomenal case possess some feature that no constructed analogue can reproduce?

Please write in American English, in plain prose, without citations or a bibliography; I am interested in the argument and not in the references, and I will do my own reading afterward. Something on the order of 1,200 words would be about right. If you judge that the specifications cannot all be satisfied at once, say so explicitly and show me the closest case you can build, rather than quietly relaxing one of them.
