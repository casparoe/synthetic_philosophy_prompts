I am writing the philosophy-of-biology chapter of my thesis on natural kinds, and I have become more interested in the nomenclatural codes than in the metaphysics literature, because the codes seem to encode a working theory of kinds that nobody is ever required to defend.

Consider the type-specimen practice. A new species name is anchored permanently to a single designated name-bearing specimen in a museum collection; the diagnosis may be rewritten as often as the evidence demands, and routinely is, but the name never moves with it. The specimen is not required to be representative of anything — it may be a juvenile, an aberrant individual, or a bone fragment — and the subsequent discovery of a far better one does not displace it. If the holotype is later found to belong to a different species, the name goes with the specimen and the familiar organism has to be renamed. Where the original type is lost, or proves to lack the features needed to separate the taxon from its relatives, the codes permit a stand-in to be appointed decades after the fact, chosen for its consistency with the original description and locality and with prevailing usage rather than for any privilege of its own. And where strict priority would resurrect an obscure senior synonym at the cost of overturning settled usage, the codes allow the familiar name to be conserved and the earlier one set aside, expressly in the interest of stability.

My supervisor treats all of this as bookkeeping with no metaphysical content whatsoever. I suspect otherwise. What theory of natural kinds does the practice presuppose, if any? It has the shape of a baptismal account, with reference fixed by ostension to a sample rather than by a description; but baptism seems to presume that there is a joint there to be named, whereas anchoring a kind-term to one arbitrary individual makes the boundaries of the kind hostage to museum history. Please reconstruct the implicit view as carefully as you can, distinguishing the commitments the practice cannot do without from those it merely sits well with; say which rival accounts — homeostatic property-cluster kinds, species-as-individuals, or a plain descriptivism about kind terms — it genuinely excludes rather than merely fails to suggest; indicate where it is underdetermined, so that two incompatible metaphysics would generate precisely the same code; and tell me what taxonomy would have to abandon if names were made to track property clusters and nothing else.
