I've read the rationalist corpus more or less continuously since the Sequences, and over the years I've followed a good deal of the adjacent writing on climate, geoengineering, and the long-term future. Lately I've been working back through the coverage and comment threads surrounding the March 2024 vote in which the stratigraphers declined to formalize the Anthropocene as an epoch, fifteen-odd years after the working group started pursuing it. What stuck with me wasn't the outcome of the vote but a pattern I kept encountering in the argument leading up to it, and I have no word for it.

The pattern is this. All parties treat the question of when the Anthropocene began as a factual one, to be settled by ice cores, lake sediments, and the historical record — and yet the start date a given writer proposes tracks, almost without exception, the moral indictment that writer most wants to lodge against the human relationship to the planet. Those who think the crime is colonialism and the destruction of Indigenous American societies point to 1610 and the dip in atmospheric carbon dioxide that followed the post-Columbian population collapse. Those who think the crime is industrial capitalism point to the mid-twentieth-century radionuclide and plastics record, or back to the steam engine and the Industrial Revolution around 1780. Those who think the trouble is simply that tool-using hominids got loose on this planet point to the onset of rice irrigation and the methane rise that followed it some eight thousand years ago, or to the Neolithic more broadly. Every one of these markers is real and datable; the evidence exists whether or not you accept the framing attached to it. And each camp presents its own choice as forced by the record while characterizing the other camps' choices as politically motivated. There's a parallel strand that seems to do the same work by other means: the dispute over what to call the period at all, so that pressing for Capitalocene or Plantationocene in place of Anthropocene is a move in the same argument, and roughly the same people resist both. What I find striking is that the dispute never surfaces as a dispute about what periodization is for; it is conducted throughout as a disagreement about facts, with the normative commitments operating as the tie-breaker that nobody acknowledges is doing the tie-breaking.

So: does this configuration have a name? I can list the neighbours and I don't think any of them quite covers it. Motivated reasoning is a story about individual believers rather than about a contest between informed people who are each holding genuine data. Underdetermination explains why the evidence fails to select a unique answer but is silent on why the tie gets broken in the direction it does. The experimental literature showing that people's causal attributions shift with the moral valence of an outcome is about single events and single agents, not about how to periodize a centuries-long process. And the standing complaint that historians write the past in the image of the present is close, but seems to concern interpretation rather than the placement of a boundary. What I want is the carving, not the label: what exactly is the thing I've described, what is it not, and where are its edges? In particular, I suspect there are two versions of it that need separating — one in which importing the normative commitment is a straightforward methodological error, and one in which it is unavoidable or even correct, since choosing a boundary for a period always encodes a claim about what mattered. If some established concept in philosophy of science, in historiography, or in the Anthropocene debate itself captures that specific structure — a factual question underdetermined by the available evidence and therefore settled by normative commitments, with every party insisting the settlement was evidential — name it and tell me precisely how it differs from the neighbours above. If nothing established fits, say so plainly and coin a term, defining it tightly enough that I could apply it to a new case, and give me two or three boundary cases showing where it stops applying. I'm not looking for a reading list; I'll chase down whatever you name on my own. I would ask only that you be explicit about the epistemic status of what you tell me: distinguish what is settled among people who work on this from what is genuinely contested, don't dress a contested view up as the consensus, and flag any point where you are reconstructing a concept you aren't sure exists rather than recalling one that does.
