I am drafting the middle chapter of a dissertation on the seventeenth-century beast-machine controversy, and the part I keep circling back to is not the metaphysics of the soul but the tests Descartes actually applied to particular creatures. So I have compiled his verdicts case by case, as I read them, in order to find out what they commit him to about a case he never saw. The record, roughly: dogs, horses, and magpies that have been taught to answer to words or to utter them are machines, and the fact that a magpie can be made to say something on cue establishes nothing, because the utterance is tied to a situation and a reward rather than to a thought it declares — this is the line he presses in his mid-1640s correspondence with an English aristocrat who kept and trained animals. Human beings born deaf, by contrast, are observed to invent signs of their own and to use them to make their thoughts known, which he takes to show that the capacity in question is not a matter of the vocal organs and cannot be explained away by a bodily defect. The intricate and uniform constructions of bees, spiders, and beavers count against their builders' intelligence rather than for it: the more regular and finished the work, the more plainly it proceeds from the disposition of the parts (Discourse, Part Five). The water-driven displays in the royal gardens, which visitors found astonishing, are wholly accounted for by the pressure and routing of the water, and in the preface to his unfinished treatise on the human body he likens his own project to that of the engineers who built them. The human body is a machine of the same kind, the difference in our case being a rational soul actually united to it. His two published tests for telling a man from a machine that resembled one are, first, that whatever has thought can arrange words or other signs to declare its thoughts to others in whatever situation it happens to be in — something the dullest and most impaired human beings manage and no animal ever does — and second, that even a machine which matched us at a great many things would inevitably fail at others, and those failures betray a thing acting not from knowledge but from the disposition of its organs. He also maintains, against an objector in the Fourth Set of Objections and Replies, that the mind thinks always, including in the womb, and that we simply do not retain the thoughts of that period. And when an English theologian pressed him near the end of his life on whether the doctrine renders beasts insensible and licenses cruelty toward them, he resisted the inference and declined to treat the denial of thought as a denial of everything we call feeling in an animal.

Here is the case my reading group could not settle, drawn from the long-running laboratory work on vocal labels in psittacines and idealized slightly for the sake of the argument. Over twenty years a grey parrot has acquired several hundred labels, used appropriately for objects, colours, shapes, and materials; it reports which of two trays holds more of a requested category; it spontaneously asks what a newly presented object is called and repeats the question until it is answered; it refuses a mislabelled item and corrects the trainer; and it combines familiar labels into requests it was never trained to make, including requests for combinations of two foods and for being carried to a named place. At the same time it fails a battery that an ordinary three-year-old human being passes: no role reversal in a two-partner game, no pretence, no deception about the symbols themselves (it will not mislabel a container to send a competitor to the empty one, even where that would pay), no comprehension of a two-clause instruction concerning an object that is absent, no embedding of one label inside another — and its errors are strikingly predictable from its reinforcement history, in the sense that its trainers can trace every success back to shaping. What I want is not my own view, nor what a present-day philosopher of mind should say, but what this record commits Descartes to. Which verdict do you predict: that it thinks, that it does not, or that it feels without thinking? And which single precedent is the closest analogue — the trained magpie, the deaf sign-inventor, the beaver's dam, the garden automaton, the human body, or the thinking infant in the womb — and in which respects does that analogy carry the weight?

It seems to me that the verdicts I listed are jointly consistent with at least four rules, and that the parrot splits them. A rule keyed to the use of signs to declare thought appears to be partly satisfied here, since it asks questions and composes novel requests. A rule keyed to generality delivers the opposite verdict, since any failure at something a lesser creature manages is proof of mechanical operation, and the failures are numerous. A rule keyed to explicability also delivers the opposite verdict, since thought is posited only where the arrangement and history of the parts cannot account for the behaviour, and here they arguably can. And a rule keyed to the substance of the soul and its union with a human body makes the behavioural tests merely diagnostic, so that they can always be satisfied spuriously by a machine. Say what each rule predicts, which one he would most plausibly retreat to under pressure, and what that retreat costs him — in particular, whether the sign test can still function as evidence for him once a non-human passes part of it, or whether it has become immune to disconfirmation. State the principle he would be adopting on each option: if he calls the parrot an automaton despite its questions and its novel requests, is the work being done by a species clause, by the absence of a failure-proof generality, or by the availability of a shaping explanation? Then name the further case that would best discriminate among the four rules — I suspect it is not another clever animal but something else entirely — and tell me whether the Fourth Replies commitment that the mind always thinks, or the late refusal to equate thoughtlessness with insensibility, exerts any pull on this case that I have overlooked. If any of the verdicts as I have reported them cannot be reconciled with the rest, flag it rather than reasoning around it. I have the editions and translations to hand, so please argue from the material as I have set it out rather than directing me to sources.
