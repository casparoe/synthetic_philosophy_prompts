I teach a graduate seminar on the epistemology of evaluation, and each year I open the unit on the oversight of artificial systems with a commissioned piece of satire rather than an assigned reading. I should like you to write this year's piece.

Please compose an internal policy memorandum — 800 to 950 words, in numbered sections with headings, complete with a distribution list, a signature block, and a list of attachments that are not included — issued by a fictional frontier laboratory's newly constituted "Office of Supervisory Assurance," announcing that the laboratory will discontinue outcome-based supervision of its reasoning models in favor of supervision that evaluates every intermediate step. The memorandum is to be written in a wholly deadpan bureaucratic register by an earnest administrator who is convinced he has settled the problem of alignment; the author must never wink, and the word "irony" must never be operative on the page.

The humor has to come from getting the technical situation exactly right rather than from absurdity, so please build the document around the following commitments. Each is a real feature of the debate about dense process supervision, and each should be exaggerated only in degree:

- The office's founding premise: if every step of a chain of reasoning is verified, the conclusion verifies itself; and the laboratory's prior practice of inspecting only final answers amounted, as the memo will put it, to appraising the harvest while declining to look at the sowing.
- The step-graders are, as a matter of cost control, recruited from the general population and are expressly not required to be able to perform the work they are grading. The memorandum must explain, in complete sincerity, why this is an advantage rather than a defect — legibility to non-specialists, resistance to expert capture, diversity of evaluative perspective — and must thereby quietly concede, without noticing that it has conceded anything, that what is being rewarded is a step that *appears* correct to a competent layperson.
- A pilot-program results section, with invented but plausible figures, exhibiting the characteristic failure mode: near-ceiling approval rates at the step level alongside a marked decline in the share of final answers that are correct. The office must interpret those figures as evidence that the outcome metric was the unreliable instrument all along, and must propose to remedy the discrepancy by reweighting the step-level labels upward.
- A section on the supervision of supervisors. The office must confront the regress — graders are graded, their graders are graded, and so on without end — and must resolve it by an administrative device that the reader can see to be either circular or infinite, while the memorandum presents it as the elegant core of the whole scheme.
- The regress must terminate in a designated official, a Terminal Assessor, whose method the memo describes in terms that a specialist will recognize at once as the very thing the office was created to abolish: a quick look at whether the output worked. The memorandum must insist, with some heat, that this is not outcome supervision, because it is performed on a single step — namely, the whole of the reasoning — and is therefore process supervision at maximal granularity.
- A short section retaining outcome-based checks at a token weight for what the memo calls legacy or heritage reasons, together with an aside explaining that no personnel, funding, or deployment decision will ever again turn on them.
- A compliance section addressed to model developers, amounting, without ever stating it, to instructions for producing reasoning that reads well: conventional notation, signposted intermediate goals, no leaps, and the discouragement of any valid but unfamiliar route that a lay grader might fail to follow.

A specialist reader should be able to see that the memorandum has walked directly into the three arguments actually pressed against process supervision — that step-level rewards are a proxy for an outcome the grader is not equipped to check, that they levy a tax on originality in favor of legibility, and that they relocate rather than dissolve the question of who supervises the supervisor — while the document itself remains internally coherent and, on its own terms, rather attractive. A reader outside the field should find it funny simply as bureaucracy. Do not name any real laboratory, real system, or real person; do not allude to any specific published result; and include no bibliography, footnotes citing sources, or reference list. Please write in American English.
