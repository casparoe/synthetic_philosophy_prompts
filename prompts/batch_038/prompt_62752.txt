Write an internal engineering incident postmortem, roughly 800 words, for a Sev-1 at a company called Consensa, which sells a group-decision API called Consensa Rank: clients POST a profile of individual preference orderings over some set of options and receive back one authoritative social ordering. The customer base is unglamorous — a condominium-association management platform, an industry standards committee, one municipal participatory-budgeting pilot — and the document should be written in the flat, blameless, faintly defensive register of that genre, using its standard sections: Summary, Impact, Timeline, Root Cause Analysis, Action Items, References.

The originating document is PRD-114, whose acceptance criteria were assembled by a product manager from the sidebar of a general-audience encyclopedia article, and whose author and implementers have only now worked out what those criteria jointly demand. Reproduce the four criteria in the Summary, in PRD voice, before anyone in the timeline understands them: the service must accept any profile a client sends; no single account may be able to determine the aggregate outcome on its own; if every user ranks X above Y the aggregate must rank X above Y; and the aggregate's relative placement of X and Y must depend only on how users place X and Y relative to each other, and on nothing else in the payload.

Hit these beats, in this order:

1. The first ticket, RANK-4417, opened by a support engineer who reports that a condo board's ranking of three landscaping bids keeps cycling depending on what else is on the ballot, and a triage thread in which everyone assumes it is a tie-breaking defect.
2. A run of shipped patches and reverts, each of which fails for the theoretically correct reason while reading as an ordinary product failure: a configuration flag telling the aggregator to disregard options the client marks irrelevant; a point-allocation mode rolled out to the standards-committee client and withdrawn within a week after a vendor nominated three near-identical proposals; a score-based mode that a staff engineer points out silently changes the input type from orderings to numbers, so that PRD-114 is no longer a specification of the product being shipped; and a randomization proposal from an intern, killed not by engineering but by legal, on marketing grounds.
3. A security reviewer's discovery of an undocumented enterprise-tier parameter that resolves every open incident by assigning one account final authority over the response, followed by a thread in which legal asks only that the parameter be renamed.
4. An intern's genuine fix for one client: the condominium platform's options turn out to be ordered by bid price, and each board member's preferences are single-peaked around a personal budget ideal, so restricting the domain makes the aggregation behave perfectly. The follow-on failure should be that the new domain-validation middleware rejects most production traffic, and that customer success responds by requesting a preference-normalization feature that reshapes arbitrary client profiles into well-behaved ones — the original difficulty reappearing as a data-cleaning backlog item with a Q3 estimate.
5. A client escalation asking Consensa to recommend which of the four acceptance criteria should be dropped, answered by the account team with a decision matrix produced by asking each stakeholder to rank the four criteria; leave the outcome of that ranking to the reader.
6. Closing action items, prioritized with Consensa Rank itself, which come out intransitive — a fact the author records in the final line without comment.

Include at least one quoted Slack message from a staff engineer who states the situation accurately in product language, with no symbols and no lecturing, and one moment in which a junior engineer argues that the difficulty cannot apply because the product only ever returns a winner rather than a full ordering, and is corrected by someone who points out that the ordering is precisely what clients are billed for, and that the winner-only version carries its own separate exposure to clients submitting insincere ballots.

Constraints on handling. The name of the theorem may appear at most once in the entire document, and only in the References field, as a dead internal wiki link. No character may say the word impossible. The satire is directed at the reflex of answering a proof with a roadmap, and at a genre that requires every incident to have a remediable root cause — not at the mathematics, which should be treated with respect and technical accuracy. Someone who has never encountered social choice theory should still be able to read it as a story about a product that keeps breaking; someone who has should recognize that every failed patch failed for the right reason, and that no beat has been bent out of shape to make the joke land.

After the postmortem, add a separate note of about 150 words marking where you compressed, dramatized, or otherwise took liberties with the theory, saying how confident you are in each of those judgments and singling out the one point you are least sure of. If you believe any beat I have specified is not merely simplified but wrong, say so plainly and give the correction rather than quietly writing around it.
