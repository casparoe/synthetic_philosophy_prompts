Our faculty newsletter's style subcommittee spent its entire last meeting on one procedural question and ended up evenly split. I was there only to take minutes, so I would like an outside adjudication of the arguments as they were actually made.

Background: our new managing editor proposed that the newsletter adopt what she called a default-assumption audit, modeled on the satirical column Douglas Hofstadter wrote under a pseudonym in the early 1980s and reprinted in *Metamagical Themas*, in which a self-appointed guardian of linguistic purity complains about people who insist on inserting race-words into positions where the language's unmarked term for a person is already doing the work. Her proposal: when an editor suspects that a phrase is silently supplying a category, substitute a term marking a category the phrase is officially silent about and see whether the sentence becomes objectionable; if the substituted version is objectionable and the original is not, that asymmetry is evidence that the original was supplying the category by default. (If my description of Hofstadter's piece is inaccurate in any particular, please say so rather than reasoning from it; nothing in the dispute below depends on the wording of the original.)

The exchange, roughly:

1. Rosalind said the audit is a method and not a stunt: it converts an invisible presupposition into a visible one, and it does so by using the reader's own response as the instrument.
2. Omar said the instrument is contaminated. The shock elicited by the substituted sentence is borrowed from moral norms the reader already holds about race, and it therefore says nothing about the original sentence, whose innocence the same reader reports without hesitation. On his account, the audit demonstrates only that race-words are charged and sex-words are not.
3. Rosalind replied that this is the finding rather than an objection to it: that a reader can process the unmarked sentence without hesitation while flinching at the substitution is precisely what it means for a default to be operative yet unnoticed.
4. Omar said the test then overgenerates. If unnoticed historical exclusion is what disqualifies a term, then "person," "citizen," "human," and "people" all fail, since each has at some point been withheld from somebody; a criterion that condemns the entire vocabulary cannot guide an editor's decision.
5. Rosalind offered a control. She said Omar's explanation predicts that any marked-but-morally-inert substitution — a term marking hair color or handedness, say — should produce comparable shock; if the flinch instead tracks moral charge rather than mere markedness, the audit is measuring the reader's commitments, which is what she had claimed all along.
6. Omar answered that this concedes his point: if the effect is produced by the reader's commitments, the audit cannot be turned against a term, because whether those same commitments extend to the sex-word case is exactly what is in dispute. He added that a satire of this kind succeeds only on readers who already share its target norm, which makes it preaching rather than argument, and that a diagnostic explaining both the flinch and its absence — the latter being attributed to blindness — is a diagnostic that cannot fail.

They asked me to break the tie and I declined. I would rather have the argument graded.

Please adjudicate move by move: for each of the six, state whether it is clearly right, partly right, or mistaken, and justify the rating. Then answer three further questions. (a) Which distinct claims — psychological, semantic or conventional, and normative — are being run together by one or both of them, and does separating those claims dissolve any part of the dispute? (b) On Omar's unfalsifiability charge in move 6: is there an answer available to Rosalind that she did not give, and if so, does it rescue the audit? (c) Setting the general argument aside, is the proposed audit defensible as a standing editorial procedure — that is, as a rule that a non-specialist editor could apply to a sentence and get a usable verdict from?

Finally, say what both of them overlooked entirely, if anything, and give your overall verdict on who came out ahead and on which single point the disagreement should have been joined.
