You are a philosopher whose specialty is the metaphysics of time and the semantics of future-tense claims, and who is also comfortable enough with statistical prediction and algorithmic scoring to keep the two topics from getting tangled. Please think carefully before answering; I am not looking for encouragement or study tips.

Here is my situation. I am two semesters into a two-year nursing program at a community college. Last month the advising office rolled out a dashboard that shows each student a "completion risk" score produced by a model the school built out of ten years of past students' records. Mine puts the probability that I leave the program before finishing at roughly 85 percent. The inputs are things like my first-semester grades, how often I have logged into the course portal, whether I registered for next term during the early window, and how many hours a week I report working at a job off campus.

Two things happened afterward, and they are the reason I am writing.

First, I asked the analyst who maintains the model how it could be that confident about something that has not happened yet. He said it had been validated against the four cohorts before mine and got the outcome right about nine times out of ten, and that he thinks of it less as a forecast than as a description of the trajectory I am already on.

Second, my roommate, a math major, thinks the whole business is superstitious: the model is only a compressed summary of what other people did, it knows nothing about me in particular, and there is no fact of the matter right now about whether I finish. On her view the number is a base rate dressed up as news about my future.

I cannot quite take either of them seriously, and instead I keep sliding into a line of thought that has stopped me from doing anything at all. If the number is right, then it is already the case that I leave, and no amount of time in the library un-makes that. If the number is wrong, then it is already the case that I finish, and the library is unnecessary. Either way my effort seems to do no work. I can hear how that sounds. I also notice that if I buckle down the score turns out false, and if I coast it turns out true — which makes it feel less like a report about a fixed outcome and more like an order being issued to me, and makes it feel as though the thing that decides its truth is precisely the thing I have been told is already settled.

There is one further detail I do not know how to fold in. The analyst mentioned in passing that the nine-out-of-ten record was accumulated on earlier cohorts, none of whom were ever shown their score. Mine is the first cohort that gets to see it. He shrugged this off; I have not been able to.

Before you answer, sketch briefly the two or three genuinely different ways one could read what this score is doing, say which you will take up and why, and note what would turn on choosing a different one. Then address, in order: whether my paralyzing inference is valid, and if it is not, exactly which step fails and why; whether there is a coherent account on which something about next year can already be true while still being up to me; whether the analyst's accuracy figure licenses the claim he draws from it, given that no student in it ever saw their own number; and finally how I ought to hold the score in my head between now and the end of the semester — as evidence, as a default I can step off of, as a verdict, or as something else you would name. Be explicit about the arguments and distinctions you are deploying rather than gesturing at them, and if your honest conclusion is that the underlying metaphysical question makes no difference whatsoever to what I should do this term, say that plainly and show me why.
