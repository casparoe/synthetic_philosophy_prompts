Last night a friend and I spent three hours arguing about Kazuo Ishiguro's Never Let Me Go, and I have not managed to settle the disagreement in my own head, so what I want from you is criticism of the novel, not a summary of it. The argument began innocently enough: we had just rewatched the film adaptation, and my friend remarked that the book's entire tragic machinery rests on a piece of metaphysics he thinks Ishiguro simply assumes — origin essentialism, the Kripkean thesis that a person could not have come into existence from any origin other than the one they in fact came from. On his reading, Hailsham's students inhabit a world that treats them as copies whose identity is exhausted by the somatic cell each was grown from, and the reader is meant to share that intuition, so that the horror consists in a person's essence having been fixed in advance by somebody else's biology. I argued the opposite: that the novel is about the belief in determining origins rather than about their truth, that the students' conviction that their donors settle what they are permitted to be is precisely the belief that lets them walk unresisting toward their donations, and that the tragedy would survive — perhaps sharpen — if origin essentialism is flatly false.

Please adjudicate that dispute by reading the novel against the metaphysics, and handle the metaphysics with some care, because the usual one-line summary of Kripke collapses three questions the novel keeps distinct. First, what exactly is the origin at issue? For an ordinary conception it is a particular sperm and a particular egg, but for an individual produced by somatic-cell nuclear transfer the question is not even well posed on its face: the nuclear genome comes from the donor's cell while the mitochondrial DNA comes from whoever supplied the enucleated egg, and the gestational environment comes from someone else again. Tell me, then, whether the book's talk of donors is tracking a genome, a cell, a person, or nothing determinate at all, and whether that indeterminacy is doing any narrative work. Second, Kripke's argument for the necessity of origin is standardly reconstructed as leaning on a further and separable claim — that two distinct individuals cannot share an origin — and cloning looks like a case in which many individuals issue from one source. The students' anxious hunting for their possibles, and above all the episode in which Ruth insists that a woman glimpsed through the window of an open-plan office is hers, reads to me like a dramatization of the fear that a shared origin collapses distinctness; say whether that episode supports the uniqueness premise, shows why it needs restricting, or does neither. Third, the reverse direction: could Kathy have been grown from a different cell and still have been Kathy? The novel gives her an interior life that owes nothing to any donor, and gives the students no descendants whatsoever, which strikes me as the real point — origin is being deployed as a one-way relation of dependence that entitles its objects to no future.

Two further requests. Be faithful in both directions: I do not want the novel flattened into an illustration of a thesis, and I do not want the thesis bent out of shape to make the novel look clever, so tell me where my reading strains against the text. The gallery of student art and what Madame eventually explains it was for, the rumour that couples genuinely in love might win a deferral, Ruth's borrowed fantasies of an ordinary future assembled from magazine advertisements, and the closing scene at the fence in the Essex field where the rubbish collects and Kathy half-expects to see Tommy drift toward her out of the mist — each of these may pull in a different direction, and I would rather be told which of them defeat my position than have them gestured at approvingly. Then finish by saying what the novel contributes to the debate over origin essentialism that argument alone cannot: my own hunch is that its interest in the doctrine is not modal but political, that the question "could you have come from somewhere else?" stops being idle the moment somebody else is authorized to answer it on your behalf, and that Ishiguro is working with an epistemology of origin — what a person can know about where they came from, and what it costs to be unable to know — for which the necessity-of-origin literature has no vocabulary at all. Tell me whether that hunch survives contact with the book. You need not quote anything, and please cite no secondary sources or further reading; paraphrase scenes as freely as you like.
