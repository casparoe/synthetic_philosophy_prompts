I'm trying to work out whether my sister, who's a pain researcher, runs one rule or several about when pain is bad for the person having it. Her track record, from years of dinner-table arguments:

1. Man with a left insular stroke, classic pain asymbolia: says the pinprick is sharp and painful, chuckles about it, refuses anything for it, says he wouldnt mind if it went on for years. Verdict: barely bad for him at all.
2. Woman after a cingulotomy for cancer pain: still reports the pain, says it just doesn't bother her anymore, sleeps fine, declines further treatment. Verdict: not bad.
3. Locked-in patient who reports through eye movements that his pain is excruciating and that he minds it enormously, but shows no distress and never asks for anything. Verdict: clearly bad.
4. Chronic pain patient, ex-soldier: says the pain is genuinely awful and he'd take a cure in a second, but has trained himself to show nothing, works full time, calls his life good. Verdict: still bad, as bad as anyone's.
5. Masochist's pain during a negotiated scene, wanted and enjoyed. Verdict: not bad, good for them actually.
6. Person born unable to feel pain. Verdict: worse off — and she insists it isnt only because of the injuries they'll rack up.
7. Rat with induced arthritis that will press a lever for analgesia. Verdict: its pain is bad.
8. Patient under heavy post-op sedation, grimacing, full autonomic response, but as far as anyone can tell no experience at all. Verdict: not bad, nothing there to be bad.
9. Vivid, distressing pain produced by hypnotic suggestion with no tissue cause. Verdict: bad.

The new case I'm going to hand her: a woman with a small frontal lesion and a badly arthritic knee. When the physio moves the knee she says, perfectly calmly, that it doesn't bother her at all, that she doesn't mind it, that she wouldn't take a pill for it. But over the next hour she guards the leg, cant settle, is irritable, sleeps badly that night — and when she's offered the choice between the movement continuing and stopping she picks stopping every single time, instantly, without hesitation.

Predict her verdict and say which rule you're betting on and why. Then: state the candidate rules that fit all nine earlier verdicts equally well (no names, no citations, no lit review — just the rules, precisely), say which earlier verdict is in the most danger if she adopts a rule that makes the new case bad, and give me the one further case that would best separate the survivors. And if you think no single rule captures all nine, tell me which pair of verdicts breaks it first — I suspect 6 and 8 are the trouble but I want to know if you agree.
