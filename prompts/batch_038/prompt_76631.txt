i'm putting together a small evening on fakes and originals and i want a poem that makes Nelson Goodman's autographic/allographic distinction felt rather than explained. setup: a master forger — thirty years of perfect fakes, undetectable under x-ray, provenance cooked up to match — who takes a crack at poetry and finds it's the one art he cant forge. the reason should come through structurally: a painting is autographic, its history is part of what it is, so even an atom-for-atom duplicate is a fake and worth a fraction of what it copies; a poem is fixed entirely by its sequence of words, so an exact copy isn't a copy of the poem, it just IS the poem again, full value intact. to land that i want one stanza repeated verbatim later in the piece, and the whole point of the repeat should be that nothing has been lost or counterfeited by the repeating. 20-28 lines, form is up to you, and the turn should be the moment the forger realises his skillset has gone worthless: there's no move left that could make a poem inauthentic.

but don't let it coast to an easy win. his last try is the obvious one — a brand new poem written in a dead poet's hand and slipped into an archive as a lost manuscript — and that's still a fraud in a way that's genuinely hard to locate on Goodman's picture, since no existing work is being counterfeited at all. end on the awkwardness instead of resolving it. someone who knows the distinction should be able to tell you've got it right; someone who's never heard of it should just get a good poem.
