I'm a third-year PhD student in philosophy of cognitive science, and I've been asked to write a review of a new trade book on AI and common sense for our department's blog. The author is a well-known science writer, not a philosopher, and the first chapter is a big deflationary swipe at the frame problem. I need to be fair in print, so I want to know exactly what I can push back on and what I'd just be nitpicking.

Here's the passage I'm stuck on (I'm paraphrasing closely but the wording is his, and it's all one continuous argument):

'When McCarthy and Hayes coined the term in 1969, the frame problem was a bookkeeping headache with a price tag. A logic-based robot had to be told explicitly that pulling a wagon out of a room changes what's on the wagon but not the colour of the walls, and the number of these non-change axioms multiplies with every new fluent and every new action, so the whole enterprise was doomed by arithmetic. Their own diagnosis was that this is really the qualification problem: you can never finish listing the conditions under which an action works. Philosophers then took a technical curiosity and inflated it into a deep puzzle about relevance and meaning — how does any system decide which of the indefinitely many things that stay the same are worth checking? — and Dennett's story about a robot blown up by a bomb it failed to notice made that inflated version famous. But the inflation was a mistake, and I want to seperate three things people run together: the bookkeeping problem, the relevance problem, and the meaning problem. Only the first is the frame problem, and what I'll call the persistence tax — the cost of stating what doesn't change — was always just a scaling problem. Nonmonotonic logics and Reiter's situation calculus discharged it in the eighties, and the Yale Shooting Problem was the moment the community admitted the residue was computational rather than conceptual: you can always write down a consistent set of defaults, you may just need a lot of them. So the philosophers were chasing a ghost, and Dennett's robot that carefully derived where the bomb was but never derived that this mattered is a parable about a bug, not about the human condition. Today's large language models finish the job, because their weights encode a vast stock of learned defaults about what normally stays the same; they pay no persistence tax at all, since they never enumerate frame axioms.'

Can you go through this claim by claim and tell me what someone who actually works on this would accept, what's a defensible simplification I shouldn't complain about, and what's wrong? A few things I specifically can't pin down:

- He says McCarthy and Hayes's diagnosis was that the frame problem is really the qualification problem. Are those the same thing, or has he collapsed two distinct problems (and if so, which is which)?
- The robot story. Which robot fails for which reason, and does his description of the one that "derived where the bomb was but never that this mattered" match any of them? It feels off to me but I've only read the story secondhand, so I want to be sure before I say it in print.
