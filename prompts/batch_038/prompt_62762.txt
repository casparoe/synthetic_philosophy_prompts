I'm setting the final for an upper-level philosophy of religion seminar and I want one essay question on Lewis's 1997 paper "Do We Believe in Penal Substitution?" Everything I've drafted so far has a fatal flaw: it can be answered by a student who just recites the taxonomy of atonement theories — satisfaction, moral influence, Christus Victor, substitution — sprinkles in some Pauline proof-texts, and says something thoughtful-sounding about whether a loving God would need blood. I want a question where that stuff earns exactly zero.

Work from this sketch of the argument and don't go hunting for the paper; I want the question to be answerable closed book in 45 minutes, so any premise a student needs has to be in the question itself. Lewis opens with a mundane case: an offender justly sentenced, a devoted innocent friend who volunteers to take the punishment in his place. Most of us think the friend's suffering gives no reason whatsoever to let the offender go. But notice that Lewis's diagnosis isn't that it's unfair to the friend — it's that punishing an innocent substitute wouldn't do any of the work punishment exists to do. It doesn't reform the substitute, since there's nothing in him to reform; it doesn't protect anyone; it can't be counted on to deter, because the deterrence would depend on a deception that couldn't reliably be kept up; and if punishment is essentially the guilty getting what they deserve, then suffering borne by someone who isn't guilty just isn't that thing. So the real question turns into: what function would you have to give punishment for transfer to make sense at all? Lewis's answer is a compensatory one — punishment conceived as amends, something like a debt owed on account of the offence — and debts, of course, are exactly the sort of thing a third party can discharge. And the title isn't rhetorical: he thinks a one-sided diet of mundane examples conceals how far we actually are from a uniform refusal of substitution.

Structure it in two parts. Part (a) should force them to say why this is a conceptual problem rather than a moral one, and to explain what turns on the difference — a student who writes "Lewis thinks penal substitution is unjust" has missed the paper. Part (b) should hand them one concrete mundane case to wrestle with (a fine that a friend pays on your behalf is the obvious candidate, but pick whatever you think is sharpest) and ask them to decide whether it's a genuine counterexample to the initial intuition or a disanalogy that does no work for the theologian. The pressure point I want them to hit is that the compensatory move looks like it saves the doctrine only by changing the subject: if punishment is just compensation, is it still punishment, or has penal substitution quietly collapsed into something Anselmian? And what could possibly license switching your concept of punishment when you get to God, other than the desire to make the doctrine come out coherent?

Deliverables: the two-part question with mark allocation and a word limit; a model answer of about 750 words; and a marking scheme out of 25 built from five or six must-hit moves, with the marks each carries and a one-line note on the specific mistake that should cost marks there — including the ones I expect, like defining punishment so that guilt is analytically required and then declaring victory, or treating the volunteer's consent as though it did all the work on its own. Finish with a short paragraph saying what the solid-but-B answer does that the A doesn't, because I need to be able to defend the grade boundary to a moderator.
