**Hume's Maxim as a Triage Rule**

**1. The stalemate.** Section X of the *Enquiry* has generated two dominant readings, and I think both are wrong in the same way. On one side there is the Bayesian indictment — Earman's reconstruction, which finds the argument largely unoriginal and, where original, without merit once you actually do the probability calculus. On the other side there is the defensive reading, most associated with Fogelin, which takes the maxim about testimony whose falsehood would be more marvelous than the event it reports to govern the assessment of the *particular* testimony in front of you, and which treats the four considerations in the second part of the section as separate, defeasible reasons to distrust miracle-reports rather than as premises in a single deductive argument. The circularity charge is usually aimed at the space between them: Hume appears to discount miracle-testimony on the strength of the uniformity of experience, and to establish that uniformity partly by noting how much miracle-testimony there is that has proved false.

**2. The proposal.** Both readings assume the maxim is an *alethic* rule — a rule about what it is reasonable to believe given the evidence. I want to suggest it is better read as an *allocation* rule: a rule about what to investigate when your budget of epistemic attention is finite and non-refundable. On this reading the maxim is not justified by maximizing true belief but by minimizing expected loss across a stream of incoming claims. That is a much weaker standard, and Hume may well be meeting it.

**3. The screening model.** Call a *screening function* any rule that sorts incoming claims into "investigate" and "dismiss" before investigation, using only cheap surface features. Suppose claims of a category T have a very low base rate of truth, that investigating one costs c, and that a confirmed true one would be worth v. The naive rule is to investigate whenever p·v > c. But that rule assumes you can estimate p for the individual claim, and the interesting cases are precisely those where you can't: where the surface features that mark out a claim as credible — vividness, number of witnesses, apparent sincerity, willingness of the reporter to suffer for it — are the same features produced in abundance by the false-positive mechanism of the category. Then the category isn't merely low-yield; it is low-yield in a way that cheap inspection cannot improve, and the rational policy is to set the threshold so high that no member of the category is ever admitted. This is what people on the forums have called epistemic learned helplessness, and I think it is a better model of Section X than anyone has said.

**4. Why this fits the second part of the section.** On my reading, Hume's four considerations are not premises in a probability argument at all. They are estimates of the false-positive-generating process of a category of claims. The credulity of the vulgar and the pleasure people take in the passion of surprise and wonder: that is the generative mechanism. The observation that such reports cluster in remote times and ignorant places: that is a claim about the distribution of a report-type, not about any individual report. The point that rival religions each produce their own miracles and that these cancel one another: that is a demonstration that the category's output is uncorrelated with the truth, since the same mechanism yields incompatible claims with equal conviction and equal surface credibility. I think this last one is decisive for my reading and against the defensive one. Mutual cancellation cannot function as a premise about the particular testimony before you — the particular testimony is not thereby made less probable in any ordinary evidential sense — but it makes perfect sense as a reason to stop admitting the category at all.

**5. Why the circularity charge loses force.** The charge assumes Hume needs a prior on the supernatural, or on laws of nature being violated, and that he gets it illegitimately from the very testimony he is discounting. The allocation reading needs neither. It needs a comparative base rate about *reports* — how often claims of this shape have turned out to be mistaken, deluded, or invented — which is an empirical statistic Hume thinks history supplies in bulk. He is not arguing that a miracle is improbable; he is arguing that a category of claims is not worth your remaining attention.

**6. The price.** This reading saves Hume from circularity by making his conclusion local, indexed to the inquirer and to the cost of checking. It is a rule for a lone person with a finite lifespan, not a metaphysical verdict on the possibility of the supernatural. It follows that the threshold moves: as investigation becomes cheaper and, crucially, shareable, the rational screening function admits more. Which implies something Hume would find obnoxious — that his argument is weaker now than it was in 1748, not because the supernatural has become likelier but because checking got cheaper. It also implies that the modern arguments with the same shape (parapsychology, and the entire genre of extraordinary claims that turn on whether anyone will fund the replication) are mostly arguments about who pays the cost of checking, dressed up as arguments about priors.

**7. The objection I can't yet answer.** Individually rational screening can be collectively catastrophic. If everyone dismisses a category, nobody investigates the true positives, and the base-rate estimate that justified the dismissal is never corrected — a prior that seals itself against disconfirmation, structurally a frozen information cascade. Hume has no resources for this, because his rule is addressed to the individual wise man and he never asks what happens when everyone follows it. So my reading rescues him from circularity and hands him a different and arguably worse problem.

**What I'd like from you:**

1. **Textual fit.** Does Section X contain anything that *contradicts* the allocation reading, as opposed to merely failing to support it? I'm particularly worried about the passage where Hume concedes that testimony could in principle be so extraordinary that its falsehood would be more marvelous than the fact it reports. On my reading that concession is costless — he isn't claiming the event is impossible, only that distinguishing the true cases is unaffordable — but I can see an objector saying the concession shows Hume thinks the question is settled by evidence rather than by budget.

2. **Try to reduce me.** Make the strongest case that §3 is just the defensive reading with a footnote about attention costs, and that once you allow the cost of investigation into the utility function the whole distinction between alethic and allocation rules evaporates. I suspect the reduction fails because of the point in §3 about surface features being produced by the false-positive mechanism as well as the true-positive one, but I want someone to push on it rather than agree.

3. **Mutual cancellation.** Is §4's claim about the rival-religions consideration actually sound? My whole thesis leans hardest there, and if that argument can be run as a premise about a particular testimony after all, §4 loses its force and I'm back to a redescription.

4. **The collective-action problem.** Is §7 fatal, a separate paper, or a feature — i.e., does locating Hume's real weakness in the collective rather than the individual count as a successful defense? I'd like your honest view on which.

5. **Terminology.** Is "epistemic learned helplessness" doing genuine work here, or is it a fashionable label for something that should be described mechanically and left unnamed? I'm attached to it for audience reasons and that's exactly the sort of attachment I should be suspicious of.

6. **Register.** The intended readership knows Bayes' theorem cold and has probably never read Hume. Flag anywhere I'm assuming too much about the *Enquiry*, and anywhere I'm condescending about the probability theory.

7. **Bottom line.** Is the thesis interesting, or is it the well-worn "Hume was a fallibilist all along" line with a decision-theoretic coat of paint? I would rather hear the second answer bluntly now than after I've spent another month on it.
