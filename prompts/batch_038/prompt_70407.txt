For some forty years I worked as a structural chemist, principally determining molecular geometries by diffraction methods, and I have come to philosophy only since retiring. I now record a monthly series of unedited hour-long conversations with working philosophers, and I should like assistance in preparing for the next of them.

My guest defends a particular answer to Bradley's regress concerning the unity of the proposition. As I gather it from our correspondence, her position is that propositional unity is not achieved by any further constituent that binds subject to predicate. Relations and properties, on her view, come already marked for the positions they admit; a relation cannot be entertained in abstraction from the way its relata stand in it, and therefore the arrangement of the relata is not something over and above the relata plus a tie. The regress, she holds, never genuinely starts: the demand for a tie between the tie and its relata is not a demand that could in principle be satisfied, and its apparent force derives from a misleading picture of relations as items that could be listed alongside their relata in an inventory.

Please compose a sequence of ten to twelve interview questions, ordered so that the pressure increases rather than merely accumulating, on the following lines.

1. A question asking what the marking of positions consists in, and how it differs from merely asserting that concepts are unsaturated — whether she has explained the unity or rechristened it.
2. A question forcing her to choose between two concessions about the regress: that a further tie is intelligible but otiose, or that it is unintelligible. I want the follow-up to press the cost of whichever she selects.
3. A question putting the problem of converse relations to her in my own idiom. I intend to offer her an analogy from my former trade: enantiomers, and likewise cis–trans isomers, have an identical inventory of atoms and an identical pattern of bonding, and differ only in arrangement; and that difference is not an additional ingredient in the molecule. If she can distinguish aRb from bRa without admitting the arrangement as a constituent, why cannot precisely the same manoeuvre dispose of Bradley's demand? Prepare a rejoinder for the likely response that the analogy fails because molecular arrangement is fixed in space while propositional arrangement is not.
4. A question about falsehood: if unity is not conferred by a corresponding fact, what makes a false proposition one thing rather than a list?
5. A question about whether positions are filled by acts of predicating, and if so whether propositions exist unentertained; the follow-up should pursue whichever horn she takes.
6. A question about how her account secures our capacity to understand a novel sentence upon first hearing it.
7. A question asking why one should not simply deny that propositions are structured at all and treat them extensionally, thereby dissolving the difficulty — and what structure buys that is worth its metaphysical price.
8. A question airing a suspicion of mine that the whole problem is a shadow cast by the medium. A spoken sentence is a linear string unrolled in time, whereas what it says appears to have more dimensions than one, which is much why my diffraction data had to be reconstructed into an arrangement rather than read off as a list. Would the problem evaporate for a species whose language were natively non-linear, and if not, what does that show about its source?
9. One question she is entitled to answer by saying that I have misdescribed her view, followed by a second-order question: what is the most persistent misreading of her position, and what in her own exposition invites it?
10. A question about accounting: which standing assumption she is prepared to abandon — that every relation might be conceived apart from its relata, that all constituents of a proposition are separately referable — and what else in metaphysics becomes harder once that is given up.
11. A question on what could tell against the view: what argument or consideration would oblige her to relinquish it, and whether any evidence could favour the rival account rather than merely fail to support hers.
12. A closing question that is not a request to summarise.

For each question, supply one sentence stating what it is intended to test, one sentence giving the reply she is most likely to make, and then a follow-up keyed specifically to that reply rather than to the topic in general.

Two constraints. First, no biographical, institutional, or otherwise ceremonial questions; I am not interested in what drew her to the subject. Second, write for an intelligent questioner who is technically untrained: do not include a primer on the history of the problem, since I know perfectly well what the regress says, and assume throughout the idiom of current analytic metaphysics without apology.
