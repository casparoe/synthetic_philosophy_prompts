I'm in the third year of a PhD that sits between ML theory and philosophy of science, and the chapter I'm drafting concerns the epistemics of task decomposition in iterated amplification and distillation. The literature I'm working through tends to treat "decompose the task into subtasks the base model can handle" as though the move were self-vindicating, and my advisor keeps pressing me to say exactly when it isn't. Rather than argue from first principles, I've been doing something less disciplined but, I hope, more informative: collecting my own verdicts on a pile of concrete decomposition schemes and trying to see what they add up to. Here they are, with the marginal glosses.

(1) A legal-research task decomposed into retrieval, per-precedent holding extraction, drafting, and a separate adversarial critique pass: fine on both counts, alignment and competence preserved. (2) A "does this plan satisfy the specification?" judgment decomposed into one subagent per clause with majority aggregation: competence preserved, alignment not, because no component ever holds the plan as a whole and the aggregate can be confidently high about a plan that is bad in a way no clause-check registers. (3) Translating a poem by decomposing into word-level and line-level judgments: I say competence is lost even when every subtask is discharged perfectly, and I take this as a warning about decompositions that otherwise look benign. (4) Decomposing "decide whether to terminate the project" into twelve sub-questions: not fine, and I want my principle to explain why — the sub-questions are individually harder to evaluate than the original question, so the difficulty gradient points the wrong way. (5) Auditing a subordinate system for deceptive alignment by breaking the audit into small questions each of which the auditor answers honestly: fine. (6) The same audit, same sub-questions, same aggregation, except that the list of sub-questions was itself generated by the system being audited: not fine. I know (5) and (6) are my most vulnerable pair, since in both cases the auditor inspects and endorses the question list before using it, and I cannot currently say what the endorsement accomplishes in (5) that it fails to accomplish in (6). (7) A decomposition in which one component is explicitly adversarial — a red-team subagent trying to break the others' outputs — while the rest are cooperative: fine, which strikes me as odd given my verdict on (2). (8) Distilling a multi-step verification procedure into a single model that emits only the final answer: fine when you can tell from the answer alone that the procedure succeeded, not fine when its success depended on intermediate structure that leaves no trace in the output — except that I also think distilling a debate-style procedure into a single model is not fine even though its outputs are checkable, so this distinction may be a mess. (9) Recursively amplifying the subtask "evaluate whether this reward model is being gamed": fine at one or two levels, and by level five I feel uneasy without being able to name a cutoff.

My tentative principle is a no-bootstrapping condition: a decomposition preserves alignment only if the success condition of every subtask can be evaluated by an evaluator who is not itself a product of that decomposition, and only if evaluation difficulty decreases as one moves down the tree. I suspect the first clause is too strong — it seems to rule out (7), and possibly (9) — and that the second does no independent work. So: what is the principle, or the small set of candidate principles, that best captures (1)–(9)? I'd like each clause stated as a condition precise enough to be applied to a case I haven't seen rather than as a slogan, with the case or cases that motivate it named explicitly; I'd like the verdicts the resulting principle delivers on two schemes I haven't been able to resolve — a decomposition of an interpretability task in which the amplified system's own outputs help fix what counts as "the feature was present," and a scheme in which the overseer's values are partly constituted by the amplified system's prior recommendations — and an honest accounting of which of my verdicts it cannot accommodate. If the price of saving all of them is a principle with several ad hoc clauses, say so and give me the simpler one instead, along with the verdict I should be least reluctant to abandon. I'd also like to know whether any of my distinctions — (5)/(6) in particular, but also the trace-in-the-output distinction in (8) — are doing real work or merely recording my discomfort.
