I am a doctoral candidate in philosophy of biology, and this term I am co-running an upper-level seminar on the metaphysics of biological classification with a colleague from the ecology department. There are eighteen students — roughly half have had a solid course in systematics or population genetics, and half are philosophy students whose biology stops at the modern synthesis — and we meet once a week for 100 minutes. They have now read the standard material on the species problem and can recite the rival concepts fluently, but the discussion has degenerated into a kind of scoreboard exercise: each student picks a favorite concept in week two and then defends it for the rest of term, and nobody seems to feel the deeper worry, which is that the concepts are not competing answers to the same question posed to the same evidence. A friend of mine who sits on a real taxonomic revision committee tells me that in practice the outcomes are driven as much by nomenclatural inertia, by who holds the type, and by the fact that some regulatory trigger keys on rank, as by any criterion — and I would like the students to run into something like that pressure rather than hear me describe it.

So I want an in-class simulation in which the students, working as a revision committee for an invented genus, are forced to produce a single official checklist of that genus before the end of the session, and discover in the course of doing so that their criteria are not merely weighted differently but are in some cases answering different questions and attending to disjoint bodies of evidence. Please invent the clade and the dossier; do not use a real published case study in disguise, and do not use real taxa as examples anywhere in the packet. I would like the genus to be a group of montane stream-dwelling frogs distributed across a fictional valley-and-ridge system, with seven populations labelled A through G, and I would like the dossier to include at least the following tensions, which you should flesh out with concrete numbers, sample sizes, and study designs plausible enough that the biology students will not spend the session picking holes in them: A and B are parapatric with a narrow contact zone in which about nine percent of sampled adults are morphologically intermediate, and captive crosses yield fertile backcrosses in one direction only; C is a high-elevation population diagnosable by a single fixed character in advertisement call, but its mitochondrial haplotypes are nested inside B, so B is not monophyletic unless C is sunk into it; D is an all-female, sperm-dependent population whose nuclear genome is essentially clonal and which occupies a distinct thermal regime; E is a recently founded island population with a strong drift signature and very low heterozygosity, yet niche modelling shows near-complete overlap with A and genetic assignment indicates two migrants per generation; F is known only from nine museum specimens collected across a century plus a single environmental-DNA detection from a stream where nothing has since been found; and G is the terminal population in a chain of valleys such that every adjacent pair exchanges genes but G and A do not interbreed where they come into contact. Layer two pieces of external pressure on top of this. First, a statutory listing process recognises only species, a comment period closes the day after our meeting, and a road-and-hydroelectric concession turns on whether the G valley populations count as one species or three. Second, the oldest available name in the genus was described from a type locality that our data now assign to B, so any decision to split B moves the name and strands a large published literature under a new combination.

Design the whole thing as a runnable packet for eighteen students in six groups of three, 100 minutes, in a normal seminar room with no computers and no specimens. What I need from you: (1) six role briefs, one per group, each assigning an operational procedure for delimiting populations — reproductive isolation under natural and experimental conditions; diagnosability by fixed characters; ecological distinctness; multilocus genotypic clustering; cohesion through a specified mix of intrinsic and extrinsic mechanisms; and a seventh-chair role that insists only on separately evolving lineages and refuses to endorse any criterion — together with a nomenclatural-stability officer and a listing-agency representative who rotate in as individuals rather than groups; crucially, the briefs must state the procedure and a slogan only, and must nowhere name the concept, its author, or its standard objections, because I want the labels attached only in the debrief. (2) A private evidence-card mechanic, in which each group starts with one or two data items that the other groups have not been given, chosen so that each card is decisive for its holder's procedure and either irrelevant or actively damaging to someone else's, and with an explicit rule for when and at what cost cards may be traded or demanded. (3) A first silent round in which every group writes its partition of A through G on a card and all cards are posted simultaneously, so that the disagreement is visible in the room before anyone argues; then a negotiation round governed by a chair and a fixed decision rule that yields exactly one checklist; and a rule that abstention is not neutral, because any population the committee fails to rank defaults to being lumped for listing purposes. (4) A point-based payoff scheme for each role and each individual — no money — that rewards fidelity to one's assigned procedure, penalises having one's partition rejected as arbitrary by a reviewer, and rewards the listing officer and the nomenclaturalist asymmetrically, so that a coalition that maximises protection also maximises nomenclatural disruption. (5) A minute-by-minute timeline and a printable materials list. (6) A debrief that maps specific moments of the session onto the conceptual terrain: the discovery that the groups were not reading the same evidence; the exercise in which each group writes its implicit definition of species on the board and the class notices that two of the definitions are not definitions of the same kind of thing at all, one being a class-membership condition and the other a description of a historical entity, which is where I want the species-as-individuals thesis of Ghiselin and Hull to arrive from the students' own sentences; de Queiroz's separation of the species category from the criteria for recognising it, and whether that move dissolves the dispute or merely relocates it; pluralism of the Kitcher and Mishler-and-Brandon variety against monism; Ereshefsky's proposal that we abandon the rank altogether; and the value-ladenness of the checklist, including how quickly the listing pressure made splitting look attractive and whether that is a corruption of the science or an honest acknowledgement of what taxonomy is for. Please also build in a mechanic and a scripted facilitator response for the student who will inevitably propose integrative taxonomy — pooling every line of evidence — and who must be made to see that pooling requires a weighting rule, and that a weighting rule is itself a concept choice, so the proposal does not escape the problem. (7) An honest section on what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this and how to handle it in the moment: I expect the diagnosability group to split everything and declare a trivial victory, the reproductive-isolation group to try to abstain on D, F and G, the nomenclatural-stability officer to be treated as comic relief until someone realises the leverage, at least one group to break character and argue from wolves or Darwin's finches, and the reveal round to eat the debrief. (8) A frank closing note on which feature of the real problem this activity cannot reproduce and that I will have to supply myself — I suspect it is the diachronic dimension, since a frozen snapshot of seven populations never shows the lineages as separately evolving over time, and also the fact that practicing taxonomists are far less articulate about their criteria than six clean role briefs imply — plus a note on how the activity should be modified if only twelve students show up.
