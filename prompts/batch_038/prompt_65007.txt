I am in my third year of a political theory degree and this term I am taking a seminar on democratic innovations, where a large share of our reading has been on sortition — citizens' assemblies, jury-style panels, and the more ambitious proposal of a permanent second chamber selected by lot. Last week we discussed the model adopted in 2019 by the German-speaking Community of Belgium, in which a standing citizens' council sets the agenda for subsequent randomly selected citizens' assemblies that feed directly into a regional parliament of a community of roughly 77,000 people. I raised what I thought was a competence objection: such a body is small rather than crowd-sized, its members deliberate together for weeks under facilitators and briefed by invited experts, its remit is contested and value-laden policy rather than a factual quantity, and since only a small fraction of those mailed an invitation actually accept, the members who turn up are self-selected in ways that a uniform draw would not produce. A classmate waved all of this away by saying that what I had just described was simply "the wisdom of crowds at work," that the objection assumes an individualistic picture of knowledge which the slogan already refutes, and that the assembly will be right for the same reason the crowd at Galton's county fair got the ox's dressed weight right when the median of their guesses was taken. I did not have an answer at the time, partly because I suspect he was sliding between several different claims that all travel under that one phrase.

So I would like a careful disambiguation of the slogan before I go back into seminar. Please lay out the distinct precise theses that "the wisdom of crowds" can be used to assert, in the sortition debate and more generally, as a numbered menu ordered from the weakest and most defensible to the strongest and most fragile. I have taken an intermediate statistics course, so I am comfortable with the machinery: I know the Condorcet jury theorem and its assumptions of independence and individual competence above chance; I know Surowiecki's four conditions for a wise crowd — diversity of opinion, independence, decentralization, and some aggregation mechanism — and I know the bias–variance story about why averaging many noisy estimators cancels error. What I cannot do on my own is sort out how many genuinely different claims are hiding behind the phrase, including the ones I have probably not thought of: the formal results about diverse collections of problem-solvers outperforming collections of the individually best problem-solvers; the claim that aggregate forecasts from prediction markets or panels of ordinary forecasters beat individual named experts; the claim that majority rule tracks an independent standard of correctness rather than merely aggregating preferences; the comparative and negative versions, such as "crowds are less bad than elites" or "no identifiable expert is reliably better than the average citizen"; the claim that errors are uncorrelated with the truth rather than small; the reading on which the result is an average over very many questions rather than a claim about this question; and any purely political reading on which the phrase is doing normative or anti-oligarchic work — about standing, inclusion, or legitimacy — rather than epistemic work at all. For each reading, tell me what exactly it claims, what it presupposes, whether it is a platitude, a substantive and defensible empirical or mathematical result, or simply false, and — most importantly — whether it would actually license my classmate's dismissal.

Two further things. First, give me a residue report on the case itself rather than on the slogan in the abstract: for each of the three features I raised — small size, sustained expert-guided deliberation among the same members, and self-selection among those invited by lot — say which readings it breaks, which it leaves intact, and whether any reading survives all three together. If deliberation is sometimes defended precisely because it pools private information and corrects individual error, I want to know whether that defense is one of your readings or a different claim that merely sounds like one. Second, tell me what people who repeat the phrase in the sortition literature most likely mean by it, whether they are usually careful about which reading they need, and where the interesting equivocation lies — I suspect somewhere between "the many are smarter than the few on average across many questions" and "this particular assembly will get this particular contested question right." I am not looking for a bibliography or a reading list, and I would rather you not cite anything you are unsure of; I want the distinctions argued out explicitly, and I want you to say plainly, at the end, whether my classmate's use of the slogan holds up or whether it was doing nothing but sounding decisive.
