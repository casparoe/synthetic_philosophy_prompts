Our department is retiring Professor Ilse Marchetti this May, and I have been handed the tribute at her final faculty dinner. Roughly ninety people will be there: about twenty-five philosophers, a dozen of whom work in metaethics or nearby and have actually read her; the rest are her children and grandchildren, former students who went into law and public health, and a scattering of emeriti who remember her arrival. I get six minutes, so somewhere around eight hundred words. What makes this hard is that Marchetti spent thirty-five years defending a hard-line expressivist line and refused, publicly and repeatedly, to take the quasi-realist turn — where Blackburn, and later Gibbard, wanted to earn back the right to call moral judgments true, she held that saying her late husband Amos was a good man was no more a claim about the world than a gasp of delight is a claim about the world, and that she would sooner be accused of losing the plot inside a conditional than of smuggling in a moral property.

The dinner has two built-in requirements that pull against each other, and neither is negotiable as far as I am concerned. Her daughter has asked me to work in the forty-year argument between Ilse and Amos, who was a realist of the most old-fashioned kind, and specifically to do it in the conditional form Amos liked to deploy at their kitchen table: if goodness really were out there to be found, then Ilse spent her life insisting that the thing she kept bumping into was not there. The metaethicists will hear that sentence and instantly hear the problem it instantiates — an evaluative content sitting unasserted in an antecedent, feeding a valid inference, doing precisely the work her semantics was built to struggle with. Meanwhile I have to praise her, and praise is exactly what her own view says cannot be a description. So the speech must commend her in a register she would recognize as legitimate, expressing my attitudes rather than reporting moral facts about her, while staying perfectly intelligible and moving to her granddaughter, who will hear that she was kind as a plain fact and should not be made to feel corrected by me. There is a third wrinkle: I want a passage on what she taught her students, which means putting her moral claims inside belief and memory reports — she thought cruelty was the worst thing, she used to say that — and attitude reports are another place her position comes under pressure. I would like the speech to get through all three of these without a limerick, without jargon, and without a payoff that only the philosophers in the room can catch.

Please write the speech, and then append a short map of it for me: which sentences are avowals of my own attitudes, which place an evaluative content in an embedded position, which are reports of her states, and where, if anywhere, I have quietly committed myself to the realism she spent her career denying or made a move that only a quasi-realist could license. I would much rather be told that now than be walked through it over dessert by a colleague with a grudge. And if you think the task as I have framed it cannot be done well at all — that no six-minute tribute can simultaneously meet a hard-line expressivist's standards for legitimate praise, carry an unasserted moral antecedent, and comfort a room full of non-philosophers — say so plainly at the top rather than quietly dropping one of my constraints, and tell me what the closest achievable speech looks like and which constraint would have to go. If I have also mischaracterized what her view actually forbids, correct that first.
