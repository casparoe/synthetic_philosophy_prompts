I run the research desk at a small documentation NGO — five analysts, we write reports on things like mass detentions, forced displacement, attacks on hospitals — and every report goes through legal review before publication, then usually gets a furious rebutal from the government involved within a week or two. Our recurring problem is the gap between "we have a lot of evidence for this" and "we can say we know this," and right now that call is made by whoever is most senior in the room, which means it's inconsistent from report to report. A few years back I did an evening philosophy course and got hooked on Nozick's tracking account in *Philosophical Explanations* — the idea that knowing is believing truly by a method that wouldn't have let you believe it if it were false. Sensitivity, he calls it. And then the mirror-image one, adherence: if it were true, you'd believe it. Both of those feel like things you could actually ask an analyst to do with a claim, unlike "justification," which nobody in my office can cash out.

So: write me a procedure my analysts can run on a single factual claim from a draft report, in about fifteen minutes, with no philosophy background, producing something a lawyer can read and challenge. I want the questions in the order they should be asked. That includes: how you construct "the nearest situation in which the claim is false" without just imagining whichever false-world is most convenient; how narrowly the method has to be specified before you test it (the grandmother case is supposed to show what goes wrong when you let the method drift, and I want that lesson turned into a rule rather than a parable); when sensitivity comes out trivially satisfied because the false-world is so far away that no method would fire there anyway, and why that shouldn't be scored as a pass; when adherence is the test really doing the work — for us that's often "if the pattern were true but slightly different, would our collection method have picked it up at all," which is basically a coverage question; and where the procedure has to stop and hand the claim to a human because what's left isn't a tracking question but a question about how much reputational and legal risk we're willing to carry.

Then be honest with me about closure. If sensitivity is the standard, it looks like we can know each of twelve individual testimonies without knowing the summary claim they jointly entail, and our reports are almost entirely summary claims — so tell me whether that's a bug to design around, a reason to use a different condition for the summary layer, or just something we live with and disclose. Finally, run the procedure on three cases and make sure one of them comes out against us: (1) twelve witnesses interviewed independently describe the same checkpoint routine, and we'd like to say we know it was standing policy rather than improvisation; (2) a satellite image series showing dated new earthworks at a site a defector described as a detention facility — the earthworks are real, but nothing in the imagery says what happens inside; (3) one named source, corroborated only by documents we can't authenticate, saying a specific order was given in writing — the government's rebuttal will be that we coudn't have known, and honestly I think they're right, but I want the procedure to show me why instead of just agreeing with my gut. Also point out which steps an analyst who wants a particular verdict can game, and what the procedure can't deliver no matter how carefully it's followed.
