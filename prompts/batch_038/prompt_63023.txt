I was a low-temperature physicist for forty-one years. I retired three years ago and have taken up philosophy the way other men take up the cello: badly, but with pleasure, and with the old habit of writing everything down. What has hold of me is the paradox of the doubling coin — the one with a city's name attached to it, in which a fair coin is tossed until the first head appears and the player receives two dollars if that happens on the first toss, four on the second, eight on the third, doubling without end, so that the expectation is infinite and yet no one will pay much to play. I have not worked through the standard replies at length and I do not want them recited to me: no survey of the field, no roll call of names, no bibliography, since I could not check one and would not trust it if I could. What I want is what I have always wanted from a colleague who arrives with a notebook: a law that fits the data, or a demonstration that some of the data is bad.

Here is the data. Each figure is what I would pay, in dollars, for a single play, after sitting with the game for a while. They are measurements, not definitions, and they carry an error bar of perhaps twenty per cent; please treat any difference smaller than that as noise rather than as a fact about me. For scale: the house is worth about nine hundred thousand dollars, the pension and savings bring the total to a little over two million, my income is secure, and I have neither debts nor dependants.

1. The doubling game as described. Twenty dollars. I should feel I had overpaid.
2. The same game cut off at twenty-five tosses — doubling through the twenty-fifth, and nothing at all if no head has appeared by then. Its expectation is twenty-five dollars. Twenty-two dollars; that is, more than I would pay for the game whose expectation is infinite.
3. The doubling game for the first forty tosses, and thereafter a payoff of two raised to two raised to the power n on the nth toss. Twenty dollars. Not a cent more.
4. Two raised to two raised to the power n from the very first toss. Twenty dollars. My son, who is quicker with a pencil than I am, insists this figure cannot survive contact with the arithmetic of the fourth and fifth tosses. I have stared at his objection for a week and cannot feel its force. Treat this entry as disputed.
5. A control set, to discover whether any of my behaviour has to do with doubling as such. One prize of one hundred thousand dollars at four different odds: at one chance in two, twenty-nine thousand dollars; at one in ten, three thousand nine hundred; at one in a hundred, three hundred and sixty; at one in a million, nothing whatever — I would decline the ticket even gratis if claiming it required filling in a form. I note in passing that the fraction of the expectation I am willing to pay falls from about three fifths to about two fifths and then stops falling, until it falls off a cliff. I cannot tell whether that is a fact about money or a fact about me.
6. On the loss side I am another animal. For nineteen years I have paid a premium against a flood that my insurer prices at one chance in five hundred years. I look both ways before crossing an empty street. Probabilities of one in a thousand move me plainly when the outcome is a loss, while one in a million moves me not at all when the outcome is a gain.
7. A ticket entitling me to play case 1 once a week for the remainder of my life at no further charge. I would refuse it as a gift. Half the weeks I collect two dollars; those weeks I can see. The average I cannot see.
8. The case I keep returning to. Case 1 played against a house that cannot cover more than ten million dollars in total. Some mornings my price is twenty dollars, unchanged, on the ground that the house's limit is reached only on branches whose probability is one in a million, which I have just told you does not move me. Other mornings it is eleven dollars, on the ground that a prize nobody can pay is not a prize. I have never yet managed to hold both thoughts at once.

My own tentative law, which I offer to be broken rather than admired: price the game as its expectation, computed after two amendments — strike from the sum every branch whose probability lies below one in a billion, and enter no payoff at more than the value of my house. This reproduces 1, 2 and 3 to within my error bar, and it prices the last line of 5 at ten cents, which I am willing to accept as a rendering of my refusal. It prices case 4 at a figure I am too embarrassed to write down. It prices the middle lines of 5 at their full expectation, which I know from experience to be wrong. It cannot see case 8 at all, since its ceiling already lies below the house's limit. And it values each weekly play in case 7 at about twenty-one dollars, which is the opposite of what I actually do.

What I should like:

(a) A principle — or at most two or three competing principles — stated precisely enough that I could hand it to my son and he would compute the prices I have reported. If it is a formula, give the formula and the values of its parameters; if it is a procedure, give the steps in order.

(b) For each clause and each parameter, the case above that forces it. I want to see which datum is doing which work, and whether any clause is doing none.

(c) The price your principle puts on three games I have not costed: the doubling game run in reverse, in which I pay out two dollars on the first toss, four on the second, and so on, and what I ought to demand in order to play it once; the doubling game with the stake denominated not in money but in barrels of the isotope I spent my career on, or in years of my remaining life; and case 1 with the coin replaced first by an apparatus I calibrated myself and second by a stranger's thumbnail, the stranger being at liberty to stop tossing whenever it pleases him.

(d) A plain statement of which of my verdicts your principle cannot accommodate. Where you overturn one, say whether you take it to be a genuine measurement that your principle is simply wrong about, or an artefact — a case in which the number I reported was a number about something other than what I supposed I was reporting. I expect to lose case 4 and case 7, and I should like to know whether they can both go for the same reason, or whether my principle must pay for each separately.

(e) If two of your candidates fit my eight cases equally well, name the ninth case that would decide between them and state in advance what each predicts. I have spent my life being told that two curves which coincide over the measured range are not thereby the same curve, and I should like the extrapolation made explicit.
