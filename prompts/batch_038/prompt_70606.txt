I am in the middle of a seminar paper in metaphysics and I need help building a case rather than arguing about one. The paper pushes back on a move that is close to a default in the persistence literature: the claim that four-dimensionalism wins on economy, because it gets change, coincidence, and vagueness more or less for free while the three-dimensionalist still owes us an account of what unifies an object across time. I want to press on a place where I suspect the asymmetry runs the other way, namely objects that exist at discontinuous times. My working thought is that the endurantist, for whom an object is wholly present at every moment at which it exists, can simply concede that there are moments at which the object exists and moments at which it does not, whereas the perdurantist, who identifies the object with a sum of temporal parts held together by some criterion of unity — immanent causation, or maximal continuity, with the best candidate selected when several overlap — has to answer the question which sum the object is, and in some settings that question has no good answer. My supervisor's standing objection to this sort of exercise is that any case I bring will turn out to be a relabelled ship of Theseus, so the puzzle has to turn on the gap and on the pooling of components, never on gradual replacement.

Please construct a thought experiment that makes the asymmetry bite, to the following specifications. The object is an ordinary middle-sized artifact in a realistic, non-sci-fi setting, and the scenario itself runs to no more than two paragraphs: no teletransporters, no fission or duplication, no persons, brains, or survival cases, no exotic physics, no time travel. There must be a genuine gap — a stretch of time at which the object is not merely scattered or dispersed, since I do not want the four-dimensionalist able to say that it exists throughout as a scattered object, but a stretch at which it does not exist at all, followed by a later time at which something answering to the same description does exist. The reason for the gap should be that the object's components have been pooled with numerically indistinguishable others, so that the resulting tie among candidate sums is generated by the situation rather than asserted by me; think along the lines of an auditorium's stock of identical folding chairs, a prop shop's bin of identical daggers, or a rental yard of interchangeable boats, though you should choose your own setting and keep it describable economically. Hold the qualitative description of the situation fixed throughout — same components, same records, same interval — so that nothing but the two theories of persistence can explain any difference in verdict. The four-dimensionalist, applying the unity criterion and nothing else, must end up either with genuine indeterminacy about which object the later thing is, or with a determinate verdict that is flatly less intuitive than the endurantist's; the endurantist's verdict must come from standard resources only — wholly-present-at-a-time, sortal-relative identity, the dominant-kind reply — and not from any principle you quietly invent for the occasion.

After the scenario, walk me through the design. Say which temporal parts there are at each stage, what the unity criterion makes of them and why it cannot select among them, what verdict the four-dimensionalist is left holding, and then give the endurantist's reasoning and verdict in the same case. Then be honest about the engineering: name the single feature of the story that is doing the work, say what happens to the case if I remove it — if, for instance, exactly one candidate sum is available after the gap — and give the four-dimensionalist's best attack, which I expect will be either that my endurantist is smuggling in a criterion of diachronic unity they cannot state, or that the indeterminacy is a welcome result rather than an embarrassment, and tell me whether you think that attack succeeds. Please write the whole thing as continuous prose, the way you would write a letter: no headings, no bullet points, no numbered lists, no bold text, and no tables, even for the walkthrough. Close by telling me how confident you are that the case actually does what I have asked, and by singling out the one step in it you are least sure of — I would much rather know where the load-bearing assumption is weak than be handed a tidy example I cannot defend in seminar.
