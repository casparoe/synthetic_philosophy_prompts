You are a philosopher of physics specializing in probability within Everettian (many-worlds) quantum mechanics, and you have run reading groups on precisely this debate. Please think carefully and work stepwise; I am not looking for a survey of the literature but for a diagnosis of an argument of my own construction. Where you are unsure of a technical detail, flag the uncertainty rather than smoothing over it.

**The setup.** Let W be the complete physical state of the universe at some early time and L the dynamical law — in the Everettian case, the universal wavefunction and unitary evolution; in the classical case, an exact point in phase space and Hamilton's equations. Call a proposition p *D-fixed* if p follows from W and L together. I deliberately leave open whether p is a claim about the world as a whole or about some region, some time, or some person's situation; policing that is part of what I am asking you to do.

**The argument.**

(1) If p is D-fixed, then any rational agent who knows W and L must assign p probability 1 (or 0, as the case may be). Probability tracks ignorance about which possibility is actual; where W and L admit only one possibility, there is nothing to be ignorant of.

(2) In Everettian quantum mechanics there is no collapse and no selection of one branch: W and L generate a superposition in which every outcome of nonzero amplitude is realized on some branch.

(3) Therefore, for a measurement with outcomes i = 1, …, n, each having nonzero amplitude, every proposition of the form "outcome i occurs" is D-fixed.

(4) By (1), each of those propositions has probability 1. But a probability rule over the n outcomes requires the probabilities to sum to 1, and here they sum to n. So the Born weights |cᵢ|² cannot be the probabilities of the propositions in (3).

(5) Hence the Born rule, if it measures anything at all, measures a non-probabilistic magnitude — branch weight — and Everettian quantum mechanics contains no genuine probabilities.

(6) A probabilistic conclusion cannot be extracted from wholly non-probabilistic premises. Any attempted derivation must either introduce a proposition that is not D-fixed, which (2) and (3) rule out, or introduce a probabilistic premise in disguise.

(7) Each of the standard repair strategies does one of those two things. (a) Decision-theoretic derivations build the weights out of rationality axioms for a pre-branching agent — branching-indifference conditions and their relatives — but every motivation on offer for those axioms already quantifies over branch weight. (b) Symmetry-based arguments from entanglement-assisted invariance convert an indifference between physically interchangeable situations into a numerical probability, and that conversion is itself a probabilistic principle. (c) Self-locating-uncertainty accounts require an agent, after the measurement and before they look, to be genuinely uncertain which branch they are on; but which branch every future copy occupies is D-fixed. (d) Typicality accounts require a measure over branches in order to say that branches with anomalous frequencies are rare — and supplying that measure is exactly what was to be done.

(8) So Everettian quantum mechanics is a theory without probabilities, and the Born rule is descriptive bookkeeping.

(9) Nothing in (1) through (7) is specifically quantum. Substitute an exact phase-space point for W and Hamilton's equations for L, add a low-entropy boundary condition on the early universe, and every proposition about the future macroscopic behavior of a gas is D-fixed; so by (1) and (6) the probabilities of statistical mechanics are not probabilities either. The argument shows that no deterministic physical theory can harbor genuine probabilities.

**My difficulty.** I can live with the first clause of (9) — perhaps deterministic theories are not the natural home of objective chance. I cannot live with its consequence, since I am unwilling to say that a century of statistical mechanics rested on a category error, and I am still less willing to accept (8): I want to remain an Everettian who treats the Born rule as a probability rule, not as a measure of how much of something there is. Of the steps above, (1) and (6) feel to me like the least theory-specific and therefore the ones I would most like to keep; I say this as a statement of my preferences, not as a hint about where you should land.
