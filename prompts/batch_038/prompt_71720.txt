**The Ass Never Gets There: coin flips, and why the symmetry is always somewhere else**

[1] The setup is familiar: an agent faces two options it has no more reason to take than the other, and starves between them. There are three standard responses. Either the symmetry is never really perfect (one pile is a centimetre closer), or paralysis is so clearly irrational that something must be licensed to break the tie, or the whole story is a reductio of the idea that choice has to be driven by reasons at all. I want to push a less popular line: the situation isn't just hard to get out of, it isn't reachable. And a related claim — the coin flip that's supposed to rescue the ass is doing two completely different jobs, and only one of them is a rescue.

[2] Call them the diagnostic and the constitutive use of a randomizer. Diagnostic: I flip because I suspect I already have a preference I can't articulate, and the flip is a cheap way of finding out — you watch your own gut react to the result. Constitutive: I flip because there's genuinely nothing to find, and I've bound myself in advance to abide by whatever comes up, so the flip manufactures a reason where none existed. My cousin had two job offers that were identical on paper — same money, same city, same title, same boss problem — and after forty minutes she flipped a coin, saw it, felt a lurch of disappointment, and took the other job. That's the diagnostic use, and it worked. But notice what it means: the flip didn't break the tie, it exposed that there hadn't been one.

[3] Which suggests the Buridan case has no observable signature. If a choice gets made, we can't tell from outside whether it revealed a preference or was pure fiat. If no choice gets made, we can't tell whether that's because the options are exactly balanced or because the agent is exhausted, or frightened, or bad at deciding. The economist will object that indifference is *defined* by choice data: take A from {A,B}, take B from {B,A}, and you've demonstrated indifference, observable and repeatable. I think that objection fails, but I'm least sure of my reasoning here. Reversed choices require facing the same menu twice, and by the second pass the menu has changed: you remember the first choice, you're partly satiated, you're annoyed at being asked again. And for one-shot decisions — which lease to sign, which offer to accept — the reversal test can't be run at all, so it never gets to deliver a case of indifference.

[4] Now the constitutive flip. If it's rational, its rationality has to come from somewhere, and the only place available is a prior commitment: I decide now that I'll obey the coin. But that decision is itself a choice made for reasons — dithering is costly, so binding myself beats going on weighing. So the choice that mattered happened earlier, and at that earlier moment the options weren't balanced at all: "flip and obey" beat "keep weighing." The symmetry hasn't been resolved, it's been relocated. And the relocation repeats indefinitely, because at every moment the ass is really facing "act on fiat now" versus "wait a bit longer," and that pair is never a tie — waiting has a price and acting has a price, and the prices differ. So the ass never stands between two piles of hay. It stands between hay and time.

[5] That gives a rival diagnosis of what the ass gets wrong, and I like it better than the coin. Suppose perfect indifference is unstable: tastes drift, moods shift, information arrives, and what's exactly balanced at noon isn't balanced at three. Then under genuine symmetry the right move is to defer — and deferring isn't paralysis, because it's chosen for a reason, namely the expected improvement in the quality of the eventual choice. Call it the delay dividend. The costs are obvious: hay rots, apartments get rented, offers lapse, and waiting can just be an expensive form of not choosing. So delay is rational only while the option is still live and while there's a realistic chance the tie breaks on its own. On this picture the coin flip is a way of ending a deliberation you'd rather not end; delay is a way of letting it end itself. The uncomfortable consequence is that people who agonize for weeks over two "identical" options were right to agonize, and their only mistake was not noticing that they were waiting for something.

[6] I'm not making any claims about what the historical Buridan or anyone else actually said, and I don't want the essay to depend on that.
