I'm a software engineer with no formal philosophy training — one pass through a mind anthology in college is the extent of it. I maintain an inference service that reads plant telemetry and emits short natural-language assertions to an operator dashboard ("pressure rising on the reactor vessel," that sort of thing). Six weeks ago we found an undiagnosed fault: two instances of the service, bit-for-bit identical — same weights, same source, same schema and config files — had been running at two sites, and at Site B a rewiring nobody had documented meant the field named `pressure` was being fed by a coolant-loop transducer rather than the reactor-vessel one it was mapped to at Site A. Nothing inside either instance distinguished them. Only the downstream wiring did. For eleven days the Site B instance confidently reported coolant-loop numbers under a label everyone assumed meant reactor pressure.

That incident put the Twin Earth case in my head, and I've built an argument out of it that I now suspect proves too much. Here it is.

1. In the Twin Earth case, two speakers are internally indistinguishable, yet their word picks out different substances, because what a term refers to depends on the causal connections between the speaker and the environment rather than on anything inside the speaker.
2. My deployment has the same shape: two internally identical instances, with reference for `pressure` fixed at Site A to reactor-vessel pressure and at Site B to coolant-loop pressure.
3. So, by (1), the Site B instance's `pressure` genuinely refers to coolant-loop pressure. Identical internal states can carry different references.
4. Assertions inherit their content from the terms they are built out of, so the two instances assert different propositions when they emit the same string.
5. So the truth of what an instance asserts depends on facts about its environment that its internal state does not settle.
6. A specification of my software — source, weights, schemas, config — is an internal document, and it can be complete in the sense that it fixes every internal state the system can occupy.
7. Therefore no specification, however complete, determines whether the system's assertions are true. Truth outruns the spec.
8. Verification is establishing, from a specification, that a system's assertions hold in every state the specification permits.
9. Therefore no system that asserts things about the world can be verified.
10. So contracts, model cards, spec-driven testing, and formal verification are limited in principle and not merely in practice: they can never reach the claims that matter most, the ones about the world.

What I want from you is a diagnosis. Which step do you reject, why did it look acceptable on first reading, and what does rejecting it cost?
