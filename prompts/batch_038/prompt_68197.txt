Ok so I've settled on a position about revealed preference and utility and I want someone to take a real run at breaking it. The view:

On the revealed-preference reading, utility is a theorem rather than a hypothesis — you get a utility function back out of consistent choice, so there's nothing extra being posited. Which means the old charge that the whole thing is an empty tautology (people choose what they prefer because they prefer what they choose) is aimed at the wrong target. The empirical content isn't in utility at all. It's in the consistency conditions — WARP, SARP, GARP, whatever rung of the ladder you need — applied to a *description* of the agent's choice situation. And here's the bit I actually care about: a violation of those conditions only counts against utility maximization if the description of the choice situation was fixed independently of the choices you're trying to explain. Call it description-independence. If the budget set, the set of feasible alternatives, and what counts as one alternative rather than another can only be pinned down by looking at what the agent picked, then the test was never run. On my view that's where revealed preference is genuinely exposed — not in the unobservability of utility, but in whether anyone can hand you a description of a choice situation that isn't already contaminated by the behaviour it's supposed to test.

Concretely: say a lab study has subjects choose between bundles of two snacks at a bunch of different price ratios, and a fifth of them end up violating GARP. My line is that the interesting question isn't "does this refute utility theory," it's whether the experimenters' description of those bundles — that the same snack is the same good across trials, that nobody's choosing partly for the benefit of being watched, that nobody's treating it as a repeated decision about tomorrow's budget too — was nailed down before the choices came in.
