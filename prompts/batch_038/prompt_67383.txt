I work on a small forecasting team, not in academia, so this may have a standard name I have simply never picked up. Twice in the last year I have watched the same thing happen in our post-hoc reviews. Forecasters are scored afterwards on whether the outcomes they called likely actually occurred. A person who says seventy-thirty and is wrong loses noticeably less than a person who says ninety-five-five and is wrong, so over several review cycles the numbers migrate toward the middle: everything becomes sixty-forty, then fifty-five-forty-five. Point out that the forecasts have stopped discriminating between outcomes at all, and the reply is that nobody was overconfident and nobody was badly wrong. The part that puzzles me is that the forecasters are not lying. Asked directly, they report genuinely feeling less sure than they used to; the incentive seems to have worked its way into the confidence itself rather than sitting behind it as a calculation. And the reviewers reward the drift, because the review penalizes a bold miss far more heavily than it credits a bold hit.

Is there an established concept for an evaluation practice that, by punishing confident error disproportionately, drags reported confidence toward the midpoint and is then credited for having done so? I am after the carving, not just a label. How does the thing I am describing differ from deliberate understatement for safety, where the sharper view is still held privately? From a genuinely agnostic judgment that happens to sit near the base rate? From cases where caution is simply correct, because in the real world a confident miss really does cost more than a missed opportunity? And where does the interesting defect sit — in the scoring practice, or in an agent whose felt confidence bends to meet it? If nothing established covers it, coin a term and define it tightly enough that I could test whether a given review scheme has the property, and say where the boundary cases fall.
