I am completing a mechanical engineering degree and, this term, taking a metaphysics seminar on dispositions, conditionals, and modality, where we have worked through the conditional analysis of disposition ascriptions and the standard objections to it: Lewis's reformed analysis and its demand that the stimulus conditions be spelled out without circularity, Martin's electro-fink and the question of whether the interfering change is intrinsic to the object, the cases the literature calls masks or antidotes, where the disposition is present but something blocks its manifestation, and the mimicking cases, where the manifestation occurs although the disposition was never there. The reason I am asking for help is that I also work twenty hours a week as a reliability technician at a company that assembles lithium-ion packs for e-bikes, and I have come to think that a large fraction of what I do there is disposition ascription in a lab coat, carried out by people who do not realize that it is a philosophical activity at all.

On average twice a month I have to close out a test anomaly report. Something unexpected happened on the bench — a unit passed an abuse test it was expected to fail, a unit failed in a way the design was supposed to prevent, or a field return resembles a laboratory failure but arrived by a different route — and I have to state what the event licenses us to say about the design: whether the pack has the disposition in question, and what claim, if any, may go on a datasheet or into a certification statement. Our quality manager signs every report, has no patience for metaphysics, and expects me to spend about an hour per report; if a step needs a designed experiment that will take a quarter, the procedure should say so rather than pretend the question is settled. The stakes are not academic. Two years ago a competitor printed an abuse-test claim resting on a single passing run under conditions that were not our standard conditions, and the resulting recall is the reason our legal department now reads everything we assert about behavior under abuse.

What I want is a decision procedure my team could actually run: an ordered list of the questions to ask, in the order to ask them, with a statement of what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure terminates in a firm yes or no, and where it hands the decision to a human being because the remaining question is one of policy rather than fact. It must preserve the distinctions that do the real work — which object the disposition is being ascribed to (cell, pack, pack-with-its-protective-electronics), how to fix the stimulus condition and the manifestation without smuggling the disposition into the description of the conditions, what to do about the ceteris paribus clause and whether "normal conditions" can be specified finitely at all, and how to tell a fink from a mask from a mimic — and it must be explicit about which steps are judgment calls wearing a procedural costume, including any step where the answer silently encodes a choice about what the company is willing to assert. If the binary conditional cannot be resolved on the available evidence, I would like to know whether the graded alternative, on which dispositions come in degrees of potentiality, offers a usable fallback, and what data a degree claim would require. Please gloss every technical term at first use; I do not want a bibliography, a reading list, or citations, and I do not need the history of the debate.

Then run the procedure on three cases I am holding right now. First: our 14 amp-hour pack passed three nail-penetration tests, but because of a scheduling error the bench was at 4 °C and the units were near a quarter state of charge, whereas our protocol calls for 23 °C and full charge; marketing wants a datasheet line saying the pack does not undergo thermal runaway under nail penetration. Second: the bare cell vents when overcharged at twice the rated current, while the assembled pack never vents under the same abuse because the current-interrupt device opens first and the pack is inert thereafter; marketing wants "the pack does not vent when overcharged," and the cell supplier's datasheet says the cell does. I suspect the procedure will land somewhere our quality manager will dislike, because the defensible line seems to be roughly that the pack vents when overcharged provided the interrupting device fails to interrupt — a claim about a disposition of the protective device for which I have no test data at all. Third: eleven packs from one production month swelled, and four vented, all of them while charging on a particular third-party fast charger whose voltage profile sits outside our charge specification; on our compliant charger those same returned units pass everything we can throw at them. Two readings are available — the non-compliant charger produced the manifestation and the packs lack the disposition, or the packs had it all along and the compliant charger was masking it — and I want the procedure to tell me what would distinguish them, and what to write this quarter if the distinguishing test cannot be run.

Close with a short statement of what the procedure cannot do, and the kinds of anomaly on which it will predictably misfire. Then give a one-sentence justification for each of the three cases, and end with a single last line in exactly this form: Verdict: A: <label> — B: <label> — C: <label>, where each label is one of LICENSED, NOT LICENSED, or INDETERMINATE, standing for whether the disposition claim at issue in that case is supported by the evidence as I have described it.
