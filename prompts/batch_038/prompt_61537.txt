I'm running a two-day workshop for project leads at a county government — mostly people who manage capital work: road resurfacing, IT migrations, a wastewater plant upgrade — and one of the sessions is supposed to be about escalation of commitment. The slide deck I inherited says, flatly, that a sunk cost is never a reason to keep going and that anyone who thinks otherwise is being irrational. A participant who's been running the same treatment-plant project for nine years pushed back hard last time we ran it. She said there are projects where the money already in the ground is exactly what makes walking away the wrong move, and my co-facilitator's only answer was that the money's gone either way, which is true and also obviously not a reply to her. I don't want to walk back in with the same slide.

So: build me a thought experiment in which honoring a sunk cost is the right call, and right *because of* what's already been spent rather than because of anything that spending buys downstream. Constraints, and I want you to say for each one whether you actually met it or quietly bent it: realistic setting (a household, a small firm, a municipal project — no trolleys, no sci-fi, no deathbed melodrama); the expenditure is genuinely irrecoverable, no resale, no salvage, no insurance payout, no residual asset; it carries no information, so nothing about how much you've already spent is evidence bearing on whether the project will pay off; nobody else knows the figure and nobody will update on your decision, so no reputation, signaling, or commitment effects on other agents; no self-prediction channel either — it can't be rational because finishing buys credibility with your future self or guards against a later weakness of will; and no psychic payoff, meaning no guilt relief, no dodged regret, no narrative tidiness, no feeling of integrity that could be booked as a benefit on the keep-going side. The promise-to-a-dead-partner route is also out in its plain form: a promise generates its own reason, and that reason isn't the sunk cost. Scenario should run a paragraph or two, then show the reasoning — what a purely forward-looking expected-utility calculator does in the case (it should quit), what your agent does, and why quitting would be a mistake rather than just an option.

Then tell me which single feature of the case is doing the work and what happens if I delete it. And be straight with me about the meta-question, which is really what I'm after: my hunch, and I may well be wrong, is that these constraints are jointly unsatisfiable, that every apparent counterexample leaks through one of the channels I banned, and that the orthodox slogan isn't a substantive thesis at all but a consequence of how we've already defined a reason. If you think that's right, say so and give me the best near-miss plus a precise account of the exact step where it leaks, rather than a case that cheats without noticing. If you think it's wrong and you've actually built one, tell me what principle generalizes out of it — what the past cost has to be, or do, for it to reach forward like that. Either way I want the epistemic part spelled out: what would change your mind here, which consideration would flip you from impossible to possible or back again, and where in your own construction you're most likely to be fooling yourself.
