**Barycenters: a two-body reading of the center of narrative gravity** (working title, I know)

§1. The setup, which I want to keep short. Dennett's move is to treat a self the way a physicist treats a center of gravity: an abstractum posited because it makes the behavior of a complicated thing tractable, not a bead hiding somewhere inside the thing. Nobody thinks the center of gravity of a suitcase is an extra part of the suitcase. On the standard gloss this is a deflationary, anti-realist-ish position: the self is a useful fiction, a theorist's convenience, and any felt intuition that there's a subject in there is a residue of bad metaphysics. I'm not going to argue with the analogy, I'm going to argue that people read it as doing less work than it can do.

§2. My first claim, which is I think the least controversial part. The deflationary gloss underplays how much objectivity the analogy buys you. Given a mass distribution, where the center falls is not up to anyone. It isn't a matter of interpretive charity, or of which story you prefer, or of what the object thinks about itself. It can be miscomputed. Both an outside observer and the object's own owner-estimate can be flatly wrong about it, and there's a fact of the matter that settles the dispute. If the analogy is real, the same holds for the narrative self: given the distribution of what a person does and says — including what they say about what they do and say, which is where the narrativity comes in — there is a fact about where the center falls. So the view is not anti-realism about a thing, it's realism about an abstraction. The self is objective but not concrete. That reordering matters, because most of the objections I know of to this sort of view are objections to the anti-realism, not to the abstraction.

§3. Here's the part I'm most attached to and least sure of. If the analogy is load-bearing rather than decorative, it should transmit further constraints, and I think it transmits a conservation constraint. In a closed system the center of mass moves uniformly: internal forces can't accelerate it, and it can't teleport. You cannot lift the center of mass of a closed system by pulling on your own bootstraps. And that, I suspect, is exactly the physical content of the very old intuition that self-reinvention is bootstrapping — that a person cannot pick themselves up by their own narrative. So: if selves can be re-authored but centers can't jump, then change has to be paid for by redistributing mass, i.e. by changing which past events and commitments carry weight, and that redistribution is itself constrained by what has already happened. Two predictions fall out. (i) Rapid self-transformation should be rare and expensive, and where it looks cheap we should suspect we missed a mass transfer. (ii) Where it looks genuinely instantaneous, we have mis-modelled the system, not witnessed a jump.

§4. Which is the binary-system move, the thing my friend called an epicycle. Take the apparent discontinuities: the sudden conversion, the person who leaves the marriage and the vocation in the same month, the trauma case, the early dementia case where the family says they aren't themselves anymore. §3 says the center can't have jumped. So my proposal is that in these cases we discover the person was never a single body. They were a binary system, and what we had been calling the self was the barycenter of the pair — sitting between the two components, weighted toward the heavier one, moving as the masses move. When one component sheds mass (the marriage ends, the vocation dies, the memories go) or the other quietly accumulates it, the barycenter slides along the line joining them, and from inside a one-body model a sliding barycenter looks exactly like a teleporting center. Nothing jumped; the second body was there the whole time and our model was wrong. I think this also gives the narrativity-friendly realist a datum that Dennett's deflationary readers usually have to explain away: when someone says they finally became who they really were, that can be true on this view — not because a buried essence surfaced, but because the heavier component finally dominates the center.

§5. The physics problem I actually need help with. I keep writing center of gravity because that's the phrase in the title of the paper and it's the phrase everyone uses, but §3 needs center of mass: the conservation result is about the mass distribution, and the center of gravity coincides with the center of mass only in a uniform field. In a non-uniform field they diverge, and the divergence depends on how the body is oriented relative to the gradient. I don't know whether to fix this as a slip or keep it as a gift. The gift version: a person sits in a non-uniform normative environment — different audiences pulling with different gradients, in different directions, from different distances — so their center of narrative gravity would fail to coincide with their center of narrative mass, and the size of the gap would be a measure of how much of the self's location is being contributed by the audience rather than by the distribution of the person's own doings. That sounds like a big idea to me at 2am and might be complete metaphor soup by daylight.

§6. The objection I know is coming. The anti-narrativity pushback: some people simply don't live their lives as stories, don't experience themselves as protagonists stretched across time, and are none the worse for it — so narrativity is a temperamental imposition on them, not a structure they have. My reply sketch is that the barycenter model never required the person to be telling stories. The distribution is fixed by what they do and say, not by whether they experience themselves as narrating. The non-experiencer still has a center; what they lack is a self-image, and on this view a self-image is a representation of the center, which is a seperate thing from the center. I can see this looks like it makes the view compatible with everything, which would be fatal.

§7. What I don't do, and I want to flag it rather than hide it: I have no account of what makes an event mass-bearing in the first place, or of whether the weighting is normatively loaded (does the distribution include what the person ought to have cared about, or only what they did care about). If the weighting is up for grabs then §2's objectivity is much weaker than I claimed, and I think that's the hole the whole thing sits on top of.
