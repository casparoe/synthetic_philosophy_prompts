I'm one of two analysts on the capital planning team at a regional water utility — roughly 250,000 connections, and the assets we appraise are supposed to last 50 to 100 years. We do about a dozen appraisals a year, the board votes quarterly, and there is no economist anywhere in the building. Right now everything goes through the same discounted cash flow model with a point forecast and a sensitivity table bolted on the end, and it hands back a confident number for every proposal, including the ones where we basically know nothing. I've been reading Keynes on probability — the Treatise, plus the chapter on long-term expectation in the General Theory — and I think he's saying something much more specific than "be humble under deep uncertainty," and I want to build our appraisal procedure on it.

I want the Keynesian machinery, not the Knightian slogan. So: probability as a logical relation between a body of evidence and a conclusion, which needn't be expressible as a number and needn't be comparable to some other probability resting on different evidence; weight of argument as a dimension separate from the balance of the evidence, so that you can have a great deal of relevant evidence pointing slightly one way, or almost none of it pointing hard the other; whatever his attack on the principle of indifference gives you for refusing to split the difference between outcomes you have no grounds to rank; and the thought in the long-expectation chapter that what actually carries a decision through is convention — assume the existing state of affairs continues indefinitely unless there's some definite reason to expect a change — together with the fact that conventions fail at particular identifiable junctures rather than eroding smoothly. I know Ramsey thought the non-numerical probability business was a mess and that the profession went with Ramsey. Build it on Keynes anyway, but tell me straight what that costs me.

What I need is something my colleague and I can run in three weeks per proposal, that ends in a recommendation a board can vote on. Steps in order. For each step: the question to ask, what counts as a sufficient answer, and what happens if the answer isn't forthcoming. I need to know where the procedure terminates in a no, and where it hands the decision up to the board because what's left is a question about values or about who bears the risk, not a question about the evidence. And I need you to flag the steps that are judgment calls wearing a procedure's clothes, because otherwise we'll both pretend the thing is mechanical.

Then run it on two live ones:

- Case A: a desalination plant sized against a fifty-year hydrological projection. Huge capital cost, essentially irreversable once poured, and we have a very large amount of quite good data that is all about a climate that isn't the one we'd be operating in. The team has already defended this project publicly for two years.
- Case B: a thirty-year offtake contract with a proposed data-centre campus that would be our anchor customer. Whether the campus gets built depends on where a handful of firms expect the industry to go, which depends on where they expect each other to expect it to go. Almost no data, and what there is is about other people's forecasts.

At least one of the two has to come out an answer my team will hate. Don't sand the edges off to spare us, and don't hand me a compromise recommendation that lets everyone keep their existing position.

No citations, no reading list, no pointers to the literature — I can't look things up on the clock and I don't need to be told to go read the Treatise. And take a position: I want definite choices with reasons behind them, particularly about which Keynesian distinctions you decided had to survive the translation into a checklist and which you dropped as unusable, not a menu of ways to build the thing with pros and cons of each.

Please use these sections, in this order:

1. **What I'm committed to** — the handful of claims from Keynes that the procedure is built on, in plain language, and which standard appraisal notions get thrown out as a result. ~250 words.
2. **The procedure** — numbered steps, each with its question, its sufficiency condition, and its exits. ~900 words.
3. **Where it isn't really a procedure** — the judgment calls, and what a bad actor or a lazy analyst does with each one. ~250 words.
4. **Case A: desalination** — ~450 words, ending in a clear recommendation.
5. **Case B: offtake contract** — ~450 words, ending in a clear recommendation.
6. **What Keynes costs me** — where following him makes our appraisals worse than the standard method would, and what I should tell the board about that. ~250 words.
