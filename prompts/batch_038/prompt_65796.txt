I've become a reasons internalist of a specific stripe, and I want a map of where the view is exposed.

As I hold it, the position has four parts. (i) A consideration is a normative reason for an agent to act only if there is a sound deliberative route from that agent's subjective motivational set — call it S, understood to include not merely desires but dispositions, loyalties, projects, and emotional tendencies — to the agent's so acting. (ii) This is not a claim about the meaning or conceptual role of the phrase "has a reason to"; it is a substantive claim about what reasons are, since reasons just are the considerations that figure in routes of that kind. The internal/external distinction is therefore metaphysical for me, not semantic. (iii) Because the route is deliberative rather than merely causal, it may run through imagination, through the correction of false belief, and through the agent's own higher-order attitudes; S is elastic enough that the view does not trivially deliver the verdict that a sadist, or someone who has never once considered the interests of strangers, has no reason to stop. (iv) External reason statements are not false but rather fail to express anything truth-apt at all, and it is this — rather than any argument about the nature of blame — that dissolves the question of why one should be moral.

Tell me what would count against each of these.

1. What would have to be true for each part separately to fail: which arguments, which cases, which results in neighbouring areas (metaphysics of explanation, the philosophy of the attitudes, the psychology of deliberation), which empirical findings? Rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, and say for each which parts of the view it threatens.
2. Which of these would a committed internalist of my sort concede as genuinely counting against the view, and which would they absorb, and by what move? I want the concessions, not only the criticisms.
3. Is the view falsifiable in the relevant sense, or has it been insulated by qualification? Specifically: once S is made elastic enough to survive the sadist objection, and the deliberative route rich enough to include imagination and self-correction, does the view leave open any possible agent-and-action pair of which it can say there is no reason there? If it says that of nothing, is that a defect, or is it what a correct account of this kind should look like?
4. What does the view risk that its two main rivals — an externalism on which reasons are supplied by facts about value, and the idealised-desire account on which reasons track what one would want were one fully rational — do not risk? Name at least one thing internalism predicts that neither rival predicts, and say whether that prediction could be disconfirmed, including by evidence about how actual rationalisation, self-deception, and moral conversion work.
5. The objections I already know — reasons for attitudes rather than actions, the standing of third parties to blame, the worry that a sufficiently idealised internalism collapses into externalism — should not be restated at length. Say instead whether, given the four parts above, they still bite, and which of them I should be most afraid of.

Please be explicit about how confident you are in the main claims you make, and single out the point in your assessment you are least sure of. I would rather be told which of these verdicts rests on a shaky reading of the dialectic than have them all delivered with equal firmness.
