So I lost most of last night to an arguement with a friend about internal versus external reasons — the Williams thing, whether a reason has to hook onto something already in your subjective motivational set — and somewhere around 2am I realized we weren't even disagreeing about the same claim. He kept sliding between like four different theses and I kept answering a fifth one, and neither of us could say which.

I help run a little philosophy meetup and next week's session is on exactly this, so I want to turn the mess into something useful: a decision tree that sorts people into a named position based on what they say about cases, not on what label they're comfortable with. Call it eight to ten nodes, two or three answer options each, and roughly ten leaves. Nothing at a node may use the words internalism, externalism, or desire-based, and no node may just ask whether reasons depend on motivation — the point is that you answer the cases first and get sorted afterwards.

Build it out of the logical space before you build it out of names. I want the axes stated up front, then the answer-combinations they allow, then the actual positions located in that space.

The axes it needs to turn on, because these are the places we talked past each other:

- whether the motivation has to be one the agent actually has right now, one they'd have if they were fully informed and fully rational, or one they could be brought to have just by being handed a good argument that works on them;
- whether the constraint applies to which reasons exist at all or only to how heavy a reason is once it exists — half our fight was about this and neither of us noticed;
- what's supposed to happen to a claim that some totally unmoved agent has a reason: does it come out false, true but motivationally inert, not truth-apt, or true-and-motivating. This is where I think the error theorist, the expressivist, and the plain externalist part company, and nobody in our group realized they were three different people;
- whether believing you have a reason to do something has to move you even a little, which is a separate question from whether the reason depends on your motivations, and which my friend treated as the same question;
- whether a reason has to be the sort of thing that could explain an action to count.

Cases I want in there, or close equivalents: the deathbed promise to destroy an unfinished manuscript, where you now feel nothing either way about it; the fully informed, perfectly rational bystander who watches a child drown and has nothing in his motivational make-up served by saving her; two people who both have some reason to visit a sick relative but for only one of whom it's decisive; someone who has a reason they could never be argued into acknowledging, only converted into; and at least one node where the internalist and the externalist give the *same* verdict about the case and disagree only about what makes it true, because that's the node that separates people who think this is a first-order fight from people who think it's a fight about grounding.

Leaves have to include: Williams-style internalism with the motivational set stretched as wide as it will go; the Smith-style idealized-desire version; the exists-only and weight-only hybrids; straight externalism of the Scanlon or Nagel flavor, where facts about other people's needs hand you reasons whether or not you care; a constructivist leaf if it earns one; the reasons-are-evidence leaf; the error theory or nihilism leaf; and at least one leaf for a combination you think nobody actually defends. Every leaf gets the name of the position (or the word unoccupied) plus one sentence on the commitment that will be most embarrassing for that person in open discussion.

Then flag every pair of leaves your tree can't really separate — I'd bet broadened-set Williams and the idealized view are near-indistinguishable from the inside — and for each empty cell say whether it's empty because the view is incoherent, because it's unattractive, or because nobody's gotten round to it yet.

Start straight in with the tree itself. No intro, no here's-your-taxonomy, no repeating back what I asked for. Save any commentary for the very end and cap it at two sentences, ideally telling me which node my friend and I should have hit first. American English, please.
