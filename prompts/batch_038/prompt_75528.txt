ok so I'm in a philosophy of history seminar this semester (undergrad) and we've spent the last three weeks on whether things like germs, cold snaps and volcanoes can count as historical actors, and for the creative assignment I want to submit a parody. Treat yourself as a professional historian of the environment AND a philosopher of explanation AND actually funny, which is a rare combo, so take it seriously.

First, before you write a single line of the piece: think carefully and brainstorm out loud for a few paragraphs. List the rhetorical moves the genre I'm describing makes, then seperately list the objections a careful historian or philosopher of causation would raise against it, then pair them up — each objection gets smuggled into the piece as a feature of the prose, never argued. Show me that mapping before the piece itself.

What I want is the complete front matter and opening of a blockbuster big-trade popular history bestseller, the kind of book whose whole thesis is that nobody ever decided anything: weather and pathogens made the world and humans merely showed up. Include all of these, in this order:

- the jacket copy (that breathless back-cover paragraph)
- an epigraph
- the first 350 words or so of the prologue
- two or three blurbs from invented famous people — a laureate, a TV presenter, and a rival author who is being grudgingly generous
- the author bio, which should be doing a lot of quiet work

About 700 words for the whole thing, deadpan from start to finish, no winking, no explanatory footnote at the end telling me what the joke was.

The comedy has to come from the material being right rather than from being silly, so use real episodes and use them accurately: Tambora in 1815 and the summer that failed in 1816; the dust veil of 536 and only what you can actually defend about it; plague reaching Europe in 1347 and what happened to wages and to serfdom afterwards; the cold centuries and the general crisis of the seventeenth century; 1918. The book's narrator should glide between these with total confidence and no hedging at all.

And the piece should make a specialist *feel* — never state — the real problems with the style. The difference between identifying a cause and giving an explanation. The escape hatch where climate is decisive only in the last instance. The awkward fact that the same shock produces wildly different outcomes in two neighbouring societies, which is hard to square with the shock being the explanation. The way this register quietly reimports the old geographic and racial determinism it thinks it left behind decades ago. The way the archive preserves the doings of the powerful and almost nothing else, so that structural causes end up looking like the only visible ones. And the question of who, by the end, is left with any capacity to respond to anything.

Fairness matters more than edge here: a real environmental historian should read this and laugh, recognising that you're exaggerating something the genre genuinely does (the prose rhythm, the recurring nobody-chose-this refrain, the dust-jacket certainty) rather than something you invented. Don't make the invented author a fraud or a cartoon villain — make him completely sincere, and let the sincerity be the problem.

Two hard rules. Don't quote any real book, article, or historian: the epigraph and the blurbs must be invented, and if you can't invent a convincing fake source, cut the epigraph. And don't name any living writer, real or thinly disguised, as the target — the thing being satirised is a style of argument, not a person.
