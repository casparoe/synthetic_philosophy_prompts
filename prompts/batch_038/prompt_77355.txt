Compose a joke about the Hart–Fuller debate — the 1958 exchange in the Harvard Law Review between H. L. A. Hart and Lon Fuller over the relation between law and morality. The audience is a room of jurisprudence people at the end of a long workshop, and the humor must come out of the substance of the disagreement: not a pun on either surname, not the tired observation that philosophers quarrel about words, and not the reflex that lawyers will litigate anything.

Build the joke out of Hart's own illustration of open texture — a municipal ordinance forbidding vehicles in a public park — and let the difficulty escalate: a motorcar, a motorized children's toy, a bicycle, a skateboard, an electric wheelchair, a decommissioned army truck bolted to a pedestal as a war memorial, an ambulance arriving for an injured visitor. The comic engine I want exploited is this. On the easy items the two men agree entirely about what a court should do; the whole dispute concerns how to describe what happens once an item stops being obviously inside the rule and obviously outside it. Hart thinks the judge is then left without a determinate answer by the rules and must make law; Fuller thinks the judge remains answerable to something — to the purpose the ordinance serves, and more broadly to an inner morality he took to be built into the very idea of law. Add a third character who holds that both are wasting their breath, since what the court does is settled by the judge's sympathies and only afterwards clothed in reasons.

Setting and staging are up to you: a park gate, a courtroom, a seminar, a waiting room in the afterlife. You need not keep to 1958 chronology; Hart's later apparatus is fair game.

Constraints:

1. Under 150 words, deliverable aloud in a single telling, with a recognizable setup and a final line that functions as the punchline.
2. The punchline must be a sentence spoken by one of the characters. It may not be a school of thought deployed as an epithet, and the joke may not end on the word positivist or the phrase natural lawyer.
3. One beat must land only for a listener who knows what Hart means by a rule of recognition, and one only for a listener who knows that Fuller thought a regime could fail so comprehensively — curing its irregularities retroactively, officials simply disregarding the rules they profess to apply — that it forfeits the name of law. Neither beat may consist of a character explaining the notion in question.
4. Invent all dialogue; do not reproduce or closely paraphrase either author's prose. Anything put in Hart's or Fuller's mouth should be consistent with what he actually held, and is funnier if it is.
5. The joke must not adjudicate the debate. Ideally a positivist and a Fullerian both laugh, though not at the same moment and for different reasons.
6. Avoid the three-philosophers-walk-into-a-bar frame unless the bar is doing work in the joke.
7. It must be an actual joke, with a turn. A neat summary of the disagreement followed by an exclamation point does not qualify, and neither does a witticism whose entire content is that both men are dead and the argument goes on.
