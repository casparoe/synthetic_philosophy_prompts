I run the operations side of a mid-sized nonprofit, and for the past two years I have been reading, mostly on my own and mostly in blog posts and technical write-ups I only partly follow, about how AI labs supervise their models. The distinction that has stuck with me is the one between process-based and outcome-based supervision: grading a system only on its final answer versus grading each step of the work that got it there, with the familiar trade-off that outcome supervision is cheap, scales, and doesn't require the supervisor to know how to do the task, while process supervision catches errors early and gives denser feedback but costs a great deal and bakes in the supervisor's own idea of what good work looks like. A while back my sister, who teaches fourth grade in a public district, mentioned Atlanta in passing, and I went and read up on the school cheating scandal there. It struck me that the episode is something like a natural experiment in outcome-based supervision, run on human beings instead of models, and that almost nothing in the alignment literature engages with it. I would like you to analyze it that way. Not a summary of the scandal and not a general history of federal test-based accountability — I know the outline of both. What I want is a disciplined mapping, and then a candid account of where the framework fails.

The facts as I have them, and please tell me if I have any of them wrong: Atlanta Public Schools, under Superintendent Beverly Hall from roughly 1999 to 2010, was nationally celebrated for raising standardized test scores, and Hall was named national superintendent of the year in 2009. That same year the city's newspaper published a statistical analysis of state test results — including, as I understand it, implausible wrong-to-right erasure patterns on answer sheets — that flagged dozens of schools. The governor had the state investigative bureau look into it, and its report in July 2011 concluded that cheating had occurred at 44 of the 56 schools examined and implicated 178 educators in altering answers entered by students. Thirty-five educators were indicted, most took plea deals, and in April 2015 eleven were convicted of racketeering; Hall had been indicted too but died of cancer before the trial ended. Teachers who confessed pointed to district score targets and to what happened to people who missed them, all under a federal regime that tied school survival to annual proficiency numbers.

Start by laying out the mapping explicitly, and say plainly where it strains: who is the policy being trained, who is the supervisor, what exactly is the reward signal, what counts as the environment, and what would count as the specification rather than the optimizer. I suspect the strain points matter more than the fits — a model that games a reward is not deceiving anyone, whereas these teachers were, and I want you to say whether the reward-hacking vocabulary does violence to the case or whether that is a difference without analytical significance. Then take up three things in particular. First, the detection: the cheating was found by hunting statistical anomalies in the outcome data, not by anyone competent to evaluate the teaching itself, and I have been calling that a retrospective process audit — reconstructing how the output was produced from the artifacts the outcome left behind, after the fact and at scale. Tell me whether that is a real third category or just weak outcome supervision with a new name, what it requires to work (a baseline distribution, a sloppy actor, a willing investigator), and whether anything like it exists or could exist for AI systems. Second, and this is the part that puzzles me most: Atlanta did not lack process supervision. It had scripted pacing, frequent benchmark testing, data reviews, target-setting meetings, and classroom walkthroughs, arguably more process oversight than most districts in the country, and it still failed. My hypothesis — a phrase I made up, so feel free to reject it — is what I would call the inherited rubric problem: when the people who define the process criteria are themselves judged on the outcome, the process rubric is only the outcome metric in slower motion, so process supervision collapses into outcome supervision with extra cost and extra intrusiveness. Test that against the episode, and against the AI case, where process rewards for things like mathematical reasoning are often themselves derived from models trained on outcome rewards. If the hypothesis holds, what independence condition would a process rubric have to satisfy, and is that condition realistically satisfiable? Third, the attribution gap: outcome supervision tells you that something failed without telling you which step failed, so blame attaches to whoever is most visible. In Atlanta that meant teachers and principals facing racketeering charges, while the superintendent who set the targets died untried and the regime that mandated the targets was federal. Does the framework have anything principled to say about where responsibility sits when the reward is set far from the agent that games it, and does the familiar split between blaming the objective and blaming the policy map onto the split between blaming the accountability system and blaming the educators? Say where that way of putting it starts to sound like an excuse, if it does.

Aim for something on the order of fifteen hundred words, organized with a few headings, in plain language — name the concepts you are using, but do not assume I know the machine-learning jargon. Where the framework has nothing to say about a feature of the case, say so rather than forcing it to speak; I would rather have three honest gaps than a tidy story. And please close with a short calibration section of your own: how confident you are in each of your main conclusions, which single claim you would be least willing to defend under pressure, and whether any of the Atlanta facts I gave you above are wrong, contested, or oversimplified in a way that changes the analysis.
