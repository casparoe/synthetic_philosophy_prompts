I built the following argument for a paper in my food ethics seminar, and it now seems to establish something I can't accept, so I want it diagnosed before I hand it in.

(1) What is morally at stake in choosing what to eat is only how many sentient animals my choice causes to die.
(2) Cultivating plants kills a great many of them: tillage, harvesting, and rodent control kill mice, voles, and other field animals on essentially every hectare under crops.
(3) Cattle grazing land too arid or too steep to cultivate eat grass that people cannot eat, and involve no crop deaths at all.
(4) So beef from such cattle causes fewer animal deaths per calorie than plant foods do: the plants carry field deaths on every hectare, and this beef carries none.
(5) The same metric ranks beef above chicken, since one steer yields hundreds of meals whereas every chicken meal costs a whole bird.
(6) Therefore, if (1) is true, I ought to eat beef from cattle grazing non-arable land in preference to both plants and poultry.
(7) And the reasoning generalizes: if pasture beats cropland on deaths per calorie, then the more land we convert from crops to rangeland the fewer animals die, with no point at which further conversion stops being an improvement.
(8) That cannot be right, since pasture yields far less food per hectare than cropland, and feeding everyone on pasture would take vastly more land, killing vastly more wild animals and leaving people hungry besides.
(9) So (1) is false.

Which step should I reject, and what does rejecting it cost me? If I have to give something up I would rather give up (9) than (1), since I don't want to abandon the idea that animal deaths are what is at issue here, though I recognize that preference may just be prejudice rather than a reason. I would like a committed diagnosis rather than a survey: name the step you think fails, explain why it looked sound when I wrote it, and then price the exits I didn't take, saying what I would be giving up by abandoning (3), or (4) and (5), or (1) itself.
