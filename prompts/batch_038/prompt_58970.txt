QUESTION 3 (25 marks)

The Bhāṭṭa Mīmāṃsakas admit arthāpatti (postulation) and anupalabdhi (non-apprehension) as means of knowledge over and above those Nyāya accepts.

(a) [8 marks] Distinguish arthāpatti from anupalabdhi, identifying for each the cognition it is meant to produce and the example traditionally used to exhibit it, and say what Kumārila takes to mark arthāpatti off from inference.

(b) [10 marks] Explain the Nyāya strategy for refusing both additions, and say what that strategy commits Nyāya to concerning the reality and the knowability of absence (abhāva).

(c) [7 marks] Dignāga holds that only perception and inference are means of knowledge, and treats the cognition arising in the standard gavaya case as the product of inference together with testimony and memory. Would Nyāya accept this treatment? Answer with reference to the criterion Nyāya uses for deciding whether a candidate means of knowledge deserves a place on its list.

---

CANDIDATE SCRIPT

(a) Arthāpatti, usually rendered postulation, is the means by which a fact is posited in order to remove an incongruity between things already known. The standard example is Devadatta: we know that he is stout and we know that he does not eat during the day, and these two cannot stand together unless something further holds, so we arrive at the cognition that he eats at night. What marks it off from inference, on Kumārila's account, is precisely this trigger. Inference runs from a perceived reason to a conclusion by way of a pervasion, whereas postulation begins from the awareness that the known facts do not fit. Anupalabdhi, non-apprehension, yields cognitions of absence. Its stock case is the empty ground: I look at the floor and see no jar, and I come to know that there is no jar there. The absence itself, rather than the bare floor, is what the cognition is about.

(b) Nyāya accepts only four means of knowledge, so both candidates have to be absorbed. Arthāpatti is treated as a case of inference: the incongruity between stoutness and not eating by day licenses an inference to eating by night, once the appropriate pervasion is in place. The familiar objection, which I think a strong one, is that the pervasion required is not obviously available to anyone who has not already made the postulation; but Nyāya can reply that the pervasion in question is of an ordinary sort. Anupalabdhi is treated as a case of perception. This is not straightforward, since the senses cannot make contact with what is not there, so the perception of an absence has to be a non-ordinary, alaukika perception. Within Mīmāṃsā itself the two sub-schools divide on the point: Prabhākara is content to reduce non-apprehension to a perception of the locus as merely bare, whereas Kumārila insists that absence is given in a cognition of its own. Nyāya sides in effect with Prabhākara.

It is worth stressing that this is an epistemic concession only. Nyāya-Vaiśeṣika does not count absence among the padārthas, so there is no ontological commitment standing behind the claim that absences are known; the point is merely that a cognition whose content is negative can be produced by an already admitted means. The same reasoning is what closes the list: a candidate pramāṇa is admitted only if its objects are real over and above the objects of perception and inference, which is why Nyāya stops at four and the Buddhists stop at two.

(c) I would say that Nyāya accepts the Buddhist treatment. Nyāya itself breaks the gavaya case into steps: a reliable statement that the gavaya is like a cow, the perception of resemblance on encountering the animal in the forest, the recollection of that statement, and the resulting cognition that this animal is the one spoken of. Every one of these steps is the work of an already admitted means, so nothing is left over to be assigned to upamāna. Prabhākara, the Nyāya commentator who defends the independence of upamāna, tries to resist this by claiming that resemblance is itself an object of knowledge, but on the analysis just given the resemblance is perceived, and perceiving it is what the inference runs from. So the Buddhist has the better of it here.
