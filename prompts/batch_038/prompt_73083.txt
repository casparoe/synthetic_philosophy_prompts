For the past year I have sat in on our department's foundations reading group, in which Professor Varga, a functional analyst, has been recording theorem by theorem which choice-dependent results she is willing to call true. Her ledger so far runs as follows.

- Baire's category theorem for complete metric spaces: accepted. She noted that it spends only dependent choice, and that she has no quarrel with dependent choice.
- Vitali's construction of a non-Lebesgue-measurable set of reals: refused. For her purposes, she says, such a set does not exist.
- The Banach–Tarski decomposition of the ball: refused, and not merely avoided. She called it evidence that our working notion of set has outgrown any justification we can give it.
- The Hahn–Banach theorem in full generality; weak-star compactness of the dual unit ball; Tychonoff's theorem for arbitrary products of compact Hausdorff spaces; the existence of maximal ideals in unital commutative rings with identity: all accepted and used freely. Pressed on why these survive when Vitali's set does not, she said that each of them can be proved from the ultrafilter lemma, which is strictly weaker than the full axiom of choice.
- The well-ordering theorem, and in particular the claim that the continuum can be well-ordered: refused, and refused as meaningless rather than false.
- The claim that every vector space has a basis, hence that the reals have a Hamel basis over the rationals: refused. She will not permit one in her course.

She also insists that the ledger is not closed under logical consequence and must not be made so: what she records is what each individual proof actually spends, not everything her acceptances quietly entail.

Last week a visiting logician proposed that the group adopt a single official foundation and sign a statement endorsing it: Zermelo–Fraenkel set theory with dependent choice, together with Solovay's result, relative to an inaccessible cardinal, that one may consistently hold every set of reals to be Lebesgue measurable. His pitch was that this universe preserves classical analysis, eliminates precisely the pathologies she objects to, and forces on her nothing she has already refused. She has not said whether she will sign. He did concede one point, which she wrote in the margin of her copy: in that universe there can be no free ultrafilter on the natural numbers, since an ultrafilter on the naturals, coded as a subset of Cantor space, would itself be a non-measurable set of reals; and the standard proofs of Hahn–Banach, of weak-star compactness, of Tychonoff for compact Hausdorff products, and of maximal ideals in unital commutative rings all run through the ultrafilter lemma.

I have a bet with another student on whether she signs. Answer as exactly five numbered items of two or three sentences each, with no introduction and no conclusion.

1.–3. Three candidate rules, each phrased as the criterion she is actually applying, each consistent with every entry in the ledger above, and each paired with the verdict it yields on the logician's proposal. They must be genuinely distinct criteria rather than relabelings of one another, and at least one of them must predict that she signs.

4. Which of the three you would bet on, and the one consideration from the ledger that settles it.

5. A single further theorem she has not yet ruled on — say what its standard proof spends and what it delivers — that would separate your top two rules, together with the verdict each of those two predicts for it.
