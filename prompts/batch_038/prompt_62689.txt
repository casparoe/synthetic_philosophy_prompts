I am in the final year of a joint politics and philosophy degree, and this term I am taking a course on the state's right to coerce, which includes a comparative block on the Nordic and Alpine defence models. For next week's seminar each of us has been asked to put our own position in writing so that the rest of the group can attack it, and I would much rather be attacked on a set of claims I have already tested against one another. My worry is that these views were assembled over two years out of different arguments — some broadly republican, some straightforwardly liberal — and were never designed to fit together. A course-mate who read an earlier draft told me that I was being romantic about the militia and libertarian about everything else, which sounds unkind but does not tell me where the fault actually lies. What follows is the position as honestly as I can state it; I would be grateful if you would audit the set rather than argue with any single item, and I have no view in advance about which of them I ought to abandon, so please do not assume that any of them is beyond revision.

Here is where I have landed. (1) The only justification for conscription that I find convincing is a civic one: its point is that it lifts an entire age cohort out of its own social and regional sorting and makes it answerable to the political community as a whole. Strategic considerations — how many soldiers, how quickly, at what cost each — are real but secondary, and a state that adopted conscription purely because it was the cheapest way to field a large reserve would have missed the reason for having it. (2) A standing professional army is in principle the more politically dangerous institution: its members' livelihoods, status and peer groups are all supplied by the state's armed apparatus, and their loyalty is concentrated and organised, whereas a force drawn from the general population and dispersed back into civilian life between exercises is harder for an executive to turn against its own citizens. (3) Universality is nonetheless not required. The Swedish arrangement, since conscription was reintroduced there in 2017, looks to me roughly right: the whole cohort is obliged to present itself and be assessed, but only a few thousand of the most suitable and most willing are actually trained each year out of an age group of perhaps a hundred thousand. The civic good comes from everyone being counted and anyone being liable, not from everyone serving. (4) Coercion is legitimate only where it is the least burdensome means of securing a benefit that cannot be had without it and that the coerced themselves endorse; I do not think case-by-case consent is needed, but I do think that if the same benefit could be had by asking, the state must ask. (5) The civic good described in (1) is exactly of that kind — non-excludable, since nobody can opt out of living in a polity whose citizens are answerable to one another, and very widely endorsed across the political spectrum — so (4) is satisfied and conscription is permissible. (6) Conscientious objection must be accommodated by a genuine substitute civilian service of comparable length and difficulty, with nothing punitive about it and no lasting disadvantage to the objector. (7) The Swiss treatment of those who perform neither military nor civilian service — a levy on taxable income, payable over a stretch of early adulthood — seems to me an elegant answer to the fairness complaint that selective service generates: those who serve carry a burden the rest of us ought at least to pay something towards. (8) Armed neutrality is the most defensible posture for a small European state, because its defence is credible precisely in virtue of not being tied to anyone else's commitments; Finland before it abandoned non-alignment and joined NATO in 2023, and Switzerland today, are the two serious examples, and I would prefer my own country to imitate them rather than join an alliance. I note in passing that Finland trains upwards of twenty thousand conscripts a year and plans for a wartime establishment in the region of 280,000, while Sweden's intake is a small fraction of that. (9) I am strongly opposed to the general diffusion of weapons among the civilian population: I support strict firearms control and I do not think an armed citizenry is a safeguard worth its cost.

What I want from you is an audit, not a survey — please do not spend time on the general literature for and against conscription, which I know well enough. Take the commitments in pairs and triples and tell me which of them genuinely cannot all be held at once, which only look irreconcilable and dissolve once you distinguish something I have run together, and, for each genuine tension, what the cheapest revision would be and what that revision would cost me elsewhere in the set. Rank the tensions by severity rather than listing them as equally bad, and be explicit about which single change to the list would repair the most damage. Where a tension turns on a factual premise rather than a normative one — for instance, whether the civic mixing I prize in (1) is actually produced by a system of the kind described in (3) — please say so and tell me what would have to be true for the pair to be consistent. Finally, since the handout will be circulated to two visiting fellows from an American department, please write your response in American English.
