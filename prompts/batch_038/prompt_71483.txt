I'm a TA for a first-year seminar on war and politics — 18 students, one 80-minute Friday section — and this week is nuclear deterrence. I'll be honest, it stopped feeling like coursework for me over the holidays. My uncle, who spent a career in the Air Force, told me at the dinner table that deterrence only fails if somebody blinks, that the whole game is convincing the other side you wont. I couldnt answer him, and I've been chewing on it ever since, because I think he's half right in a way that's worse than being wrong.

So: design me a game for that section. Not a lecture, not a clip, something they actually play, where they run into the problem before anyone names it. What I want them to hit on their own is the difference between a threat you're still in control of and a threat you've deliberately made uncontrollable — that credibility comes from visibly wrecking your own options and from manufacturing a shared risk neither side actually wants, so that "I'd never really do it" is precisely the sentence that gets you killed. And the nastier version: the player who looks least in command of their own side is the one the other side has to take most seriously. Plus the part I most want them to feel, that once it's over nobody can tell from the outcome who won.

Build the loss of control into the rules rather than having them act it: each team's leader should not fully command their own moves. I also want one irreversible public self-binding move available, and I want you to argue with me about whether it should be literally irreversible or just very expensive to reverse — tell me which choice teaches the better lesson and why.

Give me roles, materials, a payoff scheme that runs on candy or participation points instead of money, a minute-by-minute timeline for 80, and a randomizer that's public and watchable by the whole room, since secret dice would ruin the point. Make the disputed prize small enough that they notice afterwards the stakes came from the escalation, not from the thing they were fighting over. Then a debrief that maps specific moments of play onto specific pieces of the idea, and only at the very end hands them the vocabulary and the argument my uncle was half making.

Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this with 18 people who've never met each other, how to fix it live, and what the design looks like if only nine show up. And be straight with me about which feature of the real thing the game cant reproduce — the part I'm going to have to supply by talking. American English please, and dont send me a reading list.
