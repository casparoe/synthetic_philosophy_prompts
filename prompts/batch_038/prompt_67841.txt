I help run a board game design meetup in my city and there's this one regular, a guy who got maybe halfway through an econ PhD, who has a single line he reaches for constantly: "a rational player never plays a dominated strategy." He deployed it three separate times last Thursday and I'm now pretty convinced he meant three different things by it, and that at least one of them is just false. I have a math degree but never took any game theory, so I can feel that something's off without being able to name it.

Use one: we were playtesting a worker-placement game I made and he said one of the actions should be cut from the box because its dominated, and when I asked him which other action beats it everywhere he kind of waved and said it doesn't have to be a single one. I went home and built a toy version to check myself: my actions A, B, C against his two possible setups, where A pays me 1 if he does the first thing and 0 if he does the second, B pays the reverse, and C pays 0.4 either way. There is no single row that beats C everywhere, so on the plain reading C survives — but I also can't shake the feeling he's right that C is junk. Use two: someone asked whether the order you delete the bad actions in matters, and he said of course not, dominated is dominated, order never matters. Use three: a total beginner beat him at a game afterwards by playing something he'd announced beforehand was "obviously irrational," and he said she got lucky, the maxim still holds, and anyway you can't argue with people who play bad moves.

Can you pull apart the distinct precise claims that one sentence could be making? I want the axes kept separate rather than blended: strict versus weak dominance; beaten by one alternative versus beaten by a lottery over alternatives, and what you have to believe about a player for that lottery to count as something she could actually do; dominated against every profile the opponents could choose versus dominated only once you've already crossed off what they'd never choose; and exactly what you're assuming about the other players knowing that you know when you run the deletion more than once. Then stamp a verdict on each reading — trivially true, true but only under an assumption he never stated, true and interesting, or false — and say which of them, if any, actually licenses each of the three moves above. I'd also like to know what the right diagnosis of my little A/B/C toy is, since that one's still bugging me. Give it to me as a list of readings with a verdict on each and a line about the three uses, not a general lecture on how to solve games.
