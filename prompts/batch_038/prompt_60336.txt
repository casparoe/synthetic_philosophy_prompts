Longtime lurker of the rationalist blogosphere here (OB in the old days, SSC, MR comment threads, the works) and I think I've finally got a clean dissolve on the whole "is the American or the European attitude toward inequality more defensible" question. My roommate says I'm smuggling something in. I've tried to steelman his objection for like a week and cant find it, so: help.

My move, which I call the instrument-substitution argument, or ISA (yes, I know):

Step 1. Almost every headline claim that America is wildly more unequal than Europe is a claim about *market income* Gini — pre-tax, pre-transfer. That's the number that makes the US look like an insane outlier.

Step 2. But "the European attitude toward inequality" just IS a tax-and-transfer attitude. Swedes aren't claiming wages should be compressed by a moral force field; they're saying let the market run, then redistribute the output.

Step 3. So compare like with like. On post-tax-post-transfer disposable income, the US and Sweden converge to basically the same place.

Conclusion: Americans and Swedes don't actually disagree about how unequal a finished society ought to be. They agree on the target and disagree only about which pipeline gets you there — wage structure vs. the tax code. And thats a technical question about optimal instruments, not a moral one. So the "which attitude is more defensible" framing is a category error: there's one shared attitude, two pipelines, and whichever pipeline is more efficient wins on the merits. No philosophy required.

(And before anyone reaches for wealth inequality: wealth is just deferred consumption, so its the same distribution sampled at a different time — it doesn't add a new axis.)

Two questions. Given that the step-3 convergence is real, how would you tighten up ISA so it survives contact with an actual political philosopher? And if my roommate is right that there's a hole in it, where exactly is the hole — is he just quibbling with my empirics, or am I doing something worse than that?
