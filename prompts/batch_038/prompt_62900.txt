Question 3 (20 points). Ellsberg's single-urn experiment: an urn holds 90 balls; 30 are red, and the remaining 60 are black and yellow in an unknown proportion. A bet pays a fixed prize if the specified event obtains and nothing otherwise.

(a) [6 points] State the modal pattern of choices across the two choice problems (Red versus Black; Red-or-Yellow versus Black-or-Yellow), show that no additive probability measure over the three colours can generate it, and identify which postulate of Savage's theory the pattern contradicts.

(b) [8 points] Consider two proposed ways of accommodating the pattern: (i) maxmin expected utility over a convex set of priors, and (ii) an orthodox Bayesian who treats the unknown composition of the urn as a random variable and evaluates each bet by averaging expected utilities across the possible compositions. For each, say whether it can in fact generate the pattern, and what it costs in axiomatic terms.

(c) [6 points] Choquet expected utility with a non-additive capacity is commonly offered as a third route. Explain what independence-type condition it preserves, and say what the coexistence of several such models implies for the claim that the urn data settle how ignorance should be represented.

Student's answer:

(a) In the first problem most subjects bet on Red, and in the second most bet on Black-or-Yellow. Suppose the subject's beliefs are given by an additive measure P over the three colours. Preferring Red to Black means P(Red) is greater than P(Black). Preferring Black-or-Yellow to Red-or-Yellow means P(Black) + P(Yellow) is greater than P(Red) + P(Yellow), and cancelling the common term gives P(Black) greater than P(Red). No single P satisfies both, and a Bayesian is required to use one P for both problems, so the pair of choices cannot be rationalized. The prize is identical throughout, so this cannot be a matter of risk attitude: the bets differ only in the events they are tied to.

What fails is Savage's sure-thing principle. It is worth stressing that nothing here involves intransitivity or incompleteness: the four acts can be ranked in a perfectly coherent order, and the difficulty is not that the subject cannot make up her mind. But because the sure-thing principle is a condition on how acts are ranked rather than a condition linking two separate choice problems, the breach is already present in the first problem on its own. Preferring Red to Black is to rank an act tied to an event of known probability above an act tied to an event whose probability cannot be pinned down, and P2 rules that ranking out before the second problem is ever posed. The second problem merely makes the same breach visible a second time.

(b) The maxmin route generates the pattern without difficulty. The decision maker entertains a convex set of priors, wide enough to include compositions in which black is scarce and others in which yellow is scarce, and scores each act by the least favourable expectation over that set. Red then has a guaranteed probability of one third, whereas Black can be made arbitrarily unlikely by an admissible prior; and Black-or-Yellow has a guaranteed probability of at least two thirds, whereas Red-or-Yellow can be made equally unattractive. The ranking comes out as observed.

This route is also axiomatically conservative, which is its main selling point. Maxmin expected utility keeps the ordering, continuity, monotonicity and independence requirements of the standard theory intact; its one heterodoxy is a refusal to commit to a unique prior. The prior is a set instead of a point and the evaluation rule is a minimum instead of an expectation, but no requirement about how mixtures of acts are ranked is surrendered. Ambiguity aversion, on this picture, is a claim about the representation of belief rather than a revision of the rationality axioms.

The second route looks more surprising but works as well. A Bayesian who treats the split of the sixty balls as a random variable, say uniform over the sixty-one possible compositions, faces a compound lottery rather than anything unmeasurable. She evaluates the Red bet by averaging its expected utility over compositions, and evaluates Black-or-Yellow the same way. Averaging over the second-order distribution is just an application of the law of total expectation, so all of Savage's axioms continue to hold, and the model can still separate the two bets: the spread of expected utilities across compositions enters the evaluation and penalises the ambiguous ones. The urn therefore does not force us outside orthodoxy. It shows only that people are sensitive to second-order variability, which a fully Bayesian account is able to represent.

(c) The third route replaces the additive measure with a capacity and integrates with respect to it, using the Choquet integral. What survives is comonotonic independence: the usual independence condition is retained for mixtures of acts that rank the states in the same order, and dropped elsewhere. Since additive measures are a special case of capacities, this framework nests subjective expected utility rather than competing with it, and it delivers the observed pattern by assigning subadditive weight to the events whose composition is not fixed.

The fact that maxmin, Choquet and the second-order families all reproduce the same two choices shows that the urn data underdetermine the representation. All three agree with subjective expected utility when ignorance is absent and all three diverge from it when ignorance is present, so they are observationally equivalent on any static choice problem of this kind. Distinguishing among them requires dynamic settings, where the models are forced to say how beliefs are revised after news arrives; a static experiment with more balls or more colours cannot separate them, because each family can be parameterised to fit any finite set of such rankings.
