I am a militia battalion commander in the Swiss Army; in civilian life I teach history at a cantonal secondary school. Tomorrow I give the address at the swearing-in of my recruit cohort on the parade ground of the barracks. The recruits — nineteen and twenty, all male conscripts plus a handful of women who volunteered — stand in formation; their families stand behind the roped line. By custom we also have guests: two foreign defence attachés and a visiting delegation of staff officers from a country with an all-volunteer force, several of whom have said to me this week, politely and with real evidence, that a part-time citizen army is an obsolete instrument.

Please write the speech, in English, about nine hundred words. It must be deliverable aloud by a schoolteacher who is not a politician: short sentences, concrete nouns, no rhetorical questions stacked more than one deep.

The speech has to be built on two distinctions, and the order of its parts should follow them rather than follow a list of sentiments.

First: neutrality as a status conferred by other powers, versus neutrality as a posture a country holds by itself. The great powers agreed on Swiss neutrality at Vienna in 1815 and confirmed it at Paris; in 1889 several of those same powers threatened to rescind what they had granted, and Swiss historians have argued ever since about whether the thing was given to us and could therefore be taken back, or whether it was always our own decision and no one else's to cancel. Second: an army that the state owns and hires, versus a citizenry under arms. The founding federal constitution of 1848 forbade the Confederation to maintain a standing army and permitted each canton only a few hundred men, while making service an obligation of every citizen; the article that now establishes the army still provides that it is organized primarily on a militia basis. My claim, which the speech should argue rather than assert, is that the second kind of army is the only one that makes the second kind of neutrality credible — because what deters an invader is not a declaration but his estimate of whether there will be anyone left with the authority to accept a surrender, and whether the population will keep the roads and tunnels unusable for years.

Constraints on what the speech may and may not say:

1. No philosopher, jurist or political theorist may be named. At most one proper name in the whole speech, and it must belong to someone standing in the room.
2. No current conflict, alliance or adversary may be named. The speech must nevertheless acknowledge, without naming anything, that the company Switzerland keeps in its doctrine has thinned dramatically in the last few years, and it must treat that fact neither as a vindication of us nor as a reproach to them.
3. The strongest form of the visiting officers' objection must be stated aloud and fairly: that part-time soldiers are less trained, that a conscript force is likelier to violate the laws of armed conflict because it is less drilled, and that fixed defences and riflemen are beside the point against precision munitions, drones and cyber effects. The answer may not appeal to morale, spirit, tradition or the will of the people. It must be a strategic answer about what a militia is *for* — denial by duration, the impossibility of a capitulation that binds, an armed population that is not a target set.
4. Two passages must address different groups. One, in the second person, to the recruits. One to the families. They must not make the same point in different words; what the recruits need to hear and what the parents need to hear should come apart, and the seam should be visible.
5. Among the families are people who voted for the 2013 initiative to abolish compulsory service, which roughly three-quarters of the electorate rejected, and who voted last autumn for the initiative that would have replaced it with a general civic obligation for both sexes, which more than four in five rejected. The speech must concede the strongest version of their case: that the draft is a compelled transfer of months of a young person's life, that it falls on men alone, and that the state's claim on those men is not self-evidently a claim of justice. It may not address them as losers, as sentimental, or as people who have changed their minds.
6. Use the fact that under our regulations a soldier keeps his personally assigned weapon at home, while the sealed box of ammunition has not been issued since 2007–08. The rifle in the wardrobe, in other words, arrives without its ammunition. This must be used to make the point that the object is not the guarantee and that the state has deliberately left something out that only a decision by the person holding it can supply. It may not be turned into an argument about gun policy in either direction, and it may not be sentimentalized.
7. The wartime plan of withdrawal into the alpine fortifications may be referred to, but only together with the admission that it was never a plan to protect most of the population or the industrial plateau. It may not be used as a comfortable emblem of national virtue.
8. The word "freedom" may appear at most once, and where it appears it must mean that no one outside this country gets to decide what happens in it — not the absence of constraint on the individual.
9. The speech must not end with a prayer, a slogan, a benediction, or a call to arms. It should end by locating the gap in the doctrine inside the people standing in formation: the point at which armed neutrality stops being a matter of law and equipment and becomes a matter of a decision that cannot be conscripted, legislated, or made by anyone on their behalf. Hand that problem to the recruits rather than solving it for them.

Give me the speech text only — no title, no stage directions, no notes explaining your choices.
