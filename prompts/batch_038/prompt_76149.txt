I am running a seventy-five-minute, whiteboard-only session for third-year students in a joint physics-and-philosophy programme. Every one of them has completed a course in classical mechanics and one term of introductory philosophy; none has read metaphysics, and none has met the word "supervenience" except as a noise. The topic is the dispute between Humean and anti-Humean accounts of the laws of nature: whether a law is nothing over and above a complete catalogue of the local matters of particular fact that obtain, or whether lawhood involves something further — a best-systematisation of that catalogue, relations of nomic necessitation among universals, an irreducible governing relation, or dispositional essences.

What I need from you is a plan for how to explain this, not the explanation itself. Please address the following.

1. **The order of exposition.** Give me the sequence of components, and for each, state what must already be in place before it can be understood and what would be damaged if it arrived later. Justify the ordering rather than listing it. I expect the sequence to have to accommodate at least: the difference between a generalisation that seems lawlike and one that is merely a true summary of what happens to occur; the claim that the two camps agree on every local fact; the claim that they nonetheless disagree about something; and the direction in which explanation runs, which is opposite on the two sides. I also want your view on whether the students should meet the Humean position or the anti-Humean one first, and why.

2. **The carrying example.** I want a single scenario that carries the whole session rather than a sequence of illustrative anecdotes. It must be a world containing no observers, no scientists and no instruments, because I want to foreclose at the outset the reading on which "law" just means "our best description of the world." A full specification of that world's history — every particle's position at every moment, exhaustively — should leave the students feeling that something has been left unsettled, and both accounts should be statable about it. Tell me what the example must contain to do that work, what features would spoil it, and what foil regularity I should set beside it so that the students can feel the difference between lawlikeness and mere universality.

3. **The misconception map.** Design around the picture they will arrive with: they have been taught that a law is a differential equation which, together with initial conditions, yields the subsequent history, and they slide unnoticed between "the equation lets us compute the future" and "the equation brings the future about." On top of that, I expect at least the following, and you may add others you judge more dangerous:

   - hearing "Humean," they conclude that laws are conventions, impositions, or useful fictions, and that physics has been debunked;
   - hearing "anti-Humean," they conclude that this is simply what physics already says and that the philosophers have manufactured a dispute out of vocabulary;
   - collapsing lawhood into determinism, so that an indeterministic law looks to them like a contradiction;
   - importing the juridical image of a legislator, so that the debate sounds like a quarrel over whether the universe has a lawmaker;
   - once told that laws summarise the mosaic, objecting that a summary cannot explain what it summarises, and treating that as a knockdown refutation rather than as the most interesting objection available.

   For each, tell me at which point in the sequence to head it off, whether I should raise it or let them raise it, and the one or two sentences I should say at that point.

4. **The check for understanding.** I want something I can put to them near the end whose answers will separate students who have grasped the dispute from students who can recite "one side adds something." My own candidate is: given that Humeans and anti-Humeans agree on the entire local history of the world, name a question to which they give different answers. Tell me whether that question works, what a good answer sounds like, what a bad-but-predictable answer looks like, and, if you judge it unsuitable, what to ask instead.

5. **What to omit, and how.** I am willing to leave out the technical prerequisites and the live objections: the worry that simplicity and strength are well defined only relative to a vocabulary and the metaphysics of naturalness required to fix one; the charge that nomic necessitation is an unintelligible brute relation; the question whether laws can be said to explain their instances if they are systematisations of them. I do not want to omit them silently. Tell me which to drop entirely, which to flag as deferred, and the sentence with which to flag each.

Please do not recommend readings, sources, or further study, and do not write out the explanation itself — I want the plan, the ordering, and the diagnostic.
