Two research groups studying classical Athenian political institutions have spent a year arguing past each other over the same four cases: the Mytilenean debate of 427, the Sicilian expedition of 415–413, the collective trial of the generals after Arginusae in 406, and the trial of Socrates in 399. Their positions have hardened into two camps, which for this exercise I will call the corrective-design camp and the constitutive-risk camp. Please draft the joint statement their adversarial collaboration ought to have produced.

The corrective-design camp holds that the Athenian apparatus — open assembly debate, a Council of Five Hundred framing the agenda, large allotted juries, sortition, and the after-the-fact accountability of speakers and magistrates — was well built for pooling dispersed knowledge and for catching its own errors, and that each catastrophe traces to an identifiable local failure: information that never reached the deciding body, a check that existed but was not engaged, a normal sequence compressed or suspended under panic and wartime pressure, or a motion carried without the interval the system's own conventions would have supplied. On this view the reversals — above all the second assembly on the day after the first Mytilenean decree — are evidence of a polity correcting itself rather than merely of a polity being volatile.

The constitutive-risk camp holds that the catastrophes were generated by the same features that made the system work, functioning normally: an agenda any citizen could move onto, no standing body empowered to hold up a decree before implementation, a deciding mass wholly dependent on what persuasive speakers elected to tell it, responsibility diffused across thousands and settled on named individuals only afterward, and no procedural brake between a motion and its execution. On this view a reversal is not self-correction but proof that the first decision issued from conditions that would equally have produced the opposite one, and that the undoing was itself a contingency of who got to speak second.

Deliverables:

1. **A numbered section of agreed claims, ten to fourteen of them.** Each must be concrete enough that one could tell whether it were false; no shared platitudes about the complexity of the evidence. This section must record what both camps accept about the institutional inventory — the character of the indictment against an illegal proposal, against whom it lay, whether it operated before or after implementation, whether a decree could stand above written law, what the restoration settlement of 403 changed about that relationship, what the Council could and could not do to the assembly's agenda, and what the surviving accounts permit us to reconstruct — and what both accept counts as a failure and what would count as a correction. Where the camps in fact disagree about a point of institutional fact, do not smuggle it in here; flag it and route it to the table.

2. **A disagreements table**, one row per live disagreement, with these columns: the issue; the corrective-design camp's formulation of its own position, in wording that camp would sign; the constitutive-risk camp's formulation likewise; a classification of the disagreement as substantive, partly verbal, or merely verbal, naming the term or terms doing the work wherever it is verbal at all; the crux — the sub-question whose settlement would move at least one side; the case, counterfactual, or argument both accept as bearing on that crux; the corrective-design camp's prediction, recorded in advance; and the constitutive-risk camp's prediction. No strawmen in either self-formulation column, and the two camps' rows must be symmetrical in specificity: if one side's cell contains a mechanism and the other's contains only an adjective, you have drafted the wrong document. "Reliable," "systemic," and "self-correcting" are exactly the contested words; if you judge that part of the quarrel turns on whether a system is to be assessed by the distribution of its outputs or by its capacity to revise them, say so plainly, and then say which parts of the dispute survive that clarification.

3. **A shared instrument: three stipulated modifications,** to be applied uniformly. (a) A mandatory interval between a motion's passage and its execution, with a second assembly obliged to take the matter up again. (b) A reviewing body able to suspend a decree's force pending a legality check, as distinct from prosecuting the proposer afterward. (c) A requirement of itemized accounting — cost, force size, logistical support — attached to any motion committing large resources. For each of the four cases and each modification, both camps state whether Athens had anything resembling it, whether it would have been engaged on that occasion, and whether the catastrophe would have been averted. Render this as a compact grid. Mark cells on which the two agree; any divergence should surface as a row in the table above rather than sitting in the grid unexplained.

4. **Two control cases apiece, chosen to be inconvenient.** For the constitutive-risk camp: the decision to put the Laurium windfall into hulls rather than into citizens' hands, and the codified limits on decrees adopted at the restoration. For the corrective-design camp: the final ostracism, that of Hyperbolus, and the recall of Alcibiades from an active command mid-expedition on religious charges. Each camp must give its best account of both of its own control cases and say what that account costs it.

5. **A section of falsifiers.** For each camp: what discovery about the institutional record, or what pattern across the cases, would oblige it to abandon its position — specified concretely enough to be recognized if it turned up. Then, separately, one thing each camp concedes it cannot presently explain.

6. **A closing residual statement:** a single sentence both camps would sign, stating the disagreement that survives everything above, containing no term either side could reasonably read two ways.

Constraints: no bibliography, no reading list, no citation apparatus, and no quotation from the ancient authors. Name a work in passing only where its evidential status matters — for instance where two surviving accounts of the same events diverge about who did what, and that divergence is itself part of the problem — but do not quote and do not give chapter and verse. Write for a reader who knows the period well and would spot both a vacuous agreement and a slanted one.
