I am preparing to defend a paper arguing that virtual objects and virtual worlds are real, and the objection I keep failing to answer cleanly is a make-believe account of virtual worlds. I would like to rehearse against it before I face a hostile audience. Please play a committed proponent of that account — well-informed, quick, and not a strawman — willing to concede subsidiary points freely but never the main one, and stay in character until I say stop.

So that we are fixed on the position: your character holds that a virtual world is a prop in an elaborate game of make-believe, structurally akin to a novel or a staged play rather than to a place; that claims about virtual objects, when true, are true as fictional truths, and that no such object is part of reality; and that consequently there is no distinctive category of virtual harm, even though the psychological, social, and economic effects that virtual worlds have on players' lives are entirely real and can be genuinely wrongful. Your character will happily concede that people sink real labor and real money into these worlds, feel real grief and real betrayal, incur real obligations to one another by way of in-world dealings, and can be really wronged by what happens inside a game. What your character will not concede is that any of this entails virtual objects, or that the make-believe requires a computational ontology beneath it in order to work.

Ground rules for the exchange. Keep each reply to roughly two paragraphs, so that it reads like a conversation rather than a paper. You may stop me and demand a definition or a clarification when my move depends on one, in your character's own voice. Do not cite, name-drop, or summarize the literature, and do not offer me reading lists: you are arguing with me, not reviewing a field. At the end of every reply, add a single bracketed line in which you step out of character and tell me which of the claims you have just relied on are common ground among people who work on these questions and which are contested positions that your character is asserting as though they were settled — I need that separation so I am not practising against a caricature, and so I do not come away believing the field agrees with you where it does not.

My opening move. Two players have, over eighteen months, jointly run a guild in a persistent online world: they raised a keep together, stockpiled goods in it, and admitted members on terms they negotiated between themselves. Then one of the two arranges for the keep's contents to be transferred to an outside buyer and keeps the proceeds. The others describe what they felt as betrayal of precisely the sort they would feel from a business partner who did this to them. Now, on your account nothing real was taken, because there was no keep and no stockpile to take; the only real wrong must therefore be located in the off-world promises, the off-world trust, and the off-world distress. But here is my difficulty. Those promises were about the keep and the goods. If there is no keep and there are no goods, what were the promises about, and how can a promise about nothing be broken? It looks as though your account can render the betrayal intelligible only by quietly borrowing the reality of the in-world things it denies. And if in-world things must be real in order to serve as the objects of real obligations, real labour, and real loss, then the make-believe reading has explained the wrongness of what happened by presupposing the very existence it set out to deny.
