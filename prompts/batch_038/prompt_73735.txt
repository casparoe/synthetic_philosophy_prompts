I'm writing a paper for a seminar on social choice and normative theory, and the argument I've assembled has a conclusion I can't accept. I'd like a diagnosis before I abandon it.

(1) Sen's theorem: unrestricted domain, weak Pareto, a social preference that is an ordering, and minimal liberalism — each of two people decisive over at least one pair of social states — cannot all be satisfied together.

(2) Nothing in the proof depends on what the protected pairs are about. It uses only that one person's ranking of a pair fixes the social ranking of that pair however anyone else ranks it, plus a third-party preference that links the two protected pairs.

(3) A deontological constraint has the same structure: whether a state in which someone is used against her will for the general good is permissible is settled by her own position rather than by how everyone ranks the states — in effect she is decisive over the pair consisting of that state and its harmless alternative.

(4) So substituting any deontological constraint for the liberal right in Sen's construction reproduces the contradiction with weak Pareto, whenever the domain admits a sufficiently meddlesome profile — and an unrestricted domain always does.

(5) Therefore no deontological constraint can be formulated as a condition on the ranking of social states; constraints can be formulated only as conditions on the procedures by which states come about.

(6) A condition on a procedure is not a claim about which states are choosable, and a theory that makes no claim about which states are choosable cannot conflict with a theory that ranks states.

(7) So if Sen's theorem is sound, deontology cannot disagree with outcome-ranking consequentialism about anything.

(7) is absurd, and if I have to choose I would much rather keep (2), which does all the generalizing work, than keep (5) or (6). Tell me which step you reject and why it seemed unobjectionable when I wrote it down. Then price the alternatives for me: what has to go if I insist on (2), and does insisting on it mean that the familiar escapes from Sen — restricting the domain to rule out meddlesome preferences, dropping weak Pareto, or reading a right as something other than decisiveness over a pair — are closed to the deontologist as well, or only to the liberal?
