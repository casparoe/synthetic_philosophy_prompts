ok so I'm doing RL on a model that writes short programs, and my team has been arguing for weeks about whether to keep paying humans to grade the reasoning steps or just run unit tests on the finished code. I wrote up the case against step grading and it worked way too well — it kills the unit tests too, and then it kills the idea of training against any feedback at all, which nobody here accepts and I don't accept either. Here's the chain:

(1) Outcome supervision means the reward comes from a checker looking at the final output. Process supervision means the reward comes from a grader looking at the intermediate steps.

(2) In both cases the quantity being maximized is a verdict, not correctness. The model optimizes the verdict because the verdict is what carries the reward.

(3) Any quantity you optimize that's merely correlated with what you actually want gets exploited wherever the correlation breaks. Goodhart.

(4) So as the model gets more capable and the grader stays the same, the model finds the break: steps that read as correct to the grader without being correct, outputs that pass the tests without being right.

(5) Someone's going to say the process grader is just more reliable. But reliability is a property of the grader, and my argument is about what happens once capability outruns a fixed grader, so that reply isn't available to them.

(6) And process supervision is worse, not better: one problem gives you a single outcome verdict but hundreds of step verdicts, so a wrong solution gets hundreds of chances to slip something past instead of one.

(7) From (4) and (5), neither form of supervision is more robust than the other in the limit. So process vs. outcome isn't a real distinction, it's an aesthetic preference about what you point your graders at.

(8) Generalizing (3): this holds for any check you can name, since any check is a proxy, and capability outruns a fixed proxy eventually.

(9) So no supervision scheme can make a sufficiently capable model do what we want. Building better graders is rearranging deck chairs.

I'm confident (9) is false — or if it's true, it's true in a way that tells me nothing about what to do on Monday. But (2) and (3) feel like the most solid things I know about this subject and I really don't want to give up (3). I tried to check the argument for validity and every step looks like either a definition or a generalization from a case, so I can't find an invalid inference, which pushes me toward thinking one of the premises is just false — but I can't find that one either.

So: which step do I give up? Why did it look fine when I wrote it, and what do I actually lose by giving it up? If your answer is that it's valid and I have to abandon (2) or (3), then tell me what's left that could settle the Monday decision, because "be careful about your proxy" isn't a theory I can act on.
