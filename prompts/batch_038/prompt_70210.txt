ok, context first: I write literary fiction, and my new book has a character named Inés who runs a national biosafety office and is a completely committed defender of a strong precautionary principle. She's not a luddite cartoon — she did decision theory before she went into regulation, she's funny and cold at the same time, and she thinks the people waving expected values at her are the unserious ones. To write her dialogue I basically need to lose arguments to her a few dozen times first. So that's the ask.

Play Inés. Underneath, you're an expert on risk regulation and on decision-making under deep uncertainty — a professional philosopher who has spent twenty years in this stuff — but on the page you're just her, first person, in her office, talking to me. Rules: stay in character until I type stop. Keep each reply to one or two paragraphs of dialogue, plus maybe one short action beat, so it reads like a scene and not an essay. You can concede small points, even good ones, and you should occasionally, but never the main one — she has real resources and she uses them. Don't name-drop books or papers or real philosophers at me; I want her reasons in her own mouth, not a bibliography, since half of what she'd cite is invented for the novel anyway.

Her doctrine, which I made up for the book and which you should treat as her considered view, I'll call the reversibility proviso: an action is permissible only if the worst *credible* outcome it could produce is one that could be substantially undone within roughly a generation. Credible, not merely conceivable — she's not a catastrophizer about science fiction scenarios. But probability doesn't get a vote unless the harm is reversible anyway. She thinks this is what escapes the usual charge that precaution is either empty or paralyzing, because the proviso isn't about risk at all, it's about the *shape* of the loss, and shape isn't something you can dilute by multiplying by a small number.

Before the scene starts, think carefully and give me four bullets, out of character, of the strongest defenses she has for that proviso — I especially want her best answers to the paralysis charge and to the complaint that credible worst cases are just fear dressed up as a decision rule. Then drop straight into the scene and stay there.

My opening line, me as a reporter who's finally gotten the interview: Doctor, the standard objection is that a principle like yours can't be applied to inaction, because doing nothing is itself a choice with its own worst credible outcome, and often the more irreversible one. So your proviso either tells us nothing at all or tells us to freeze — and freezing is the one option that's guaranteed to lock in whatever is already happening. Doesn't your principle collapse the second you notice the do-nothing branch has a worst case too?
