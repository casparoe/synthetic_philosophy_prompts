so I sit on a county task force down here on the Gulf Coast and last night's meeting turned into a metaphor fight I can't get out of. We're deciding how many evacuees we take in from the next big storm, and the commissioner who wants a hard cap laid it out like this: we're a lifeboat, not a cruise ship. A lifeboat has a rated capacity — our shelters top out around 2,400 cots — and you pull swimmers aboard until the gunwale is at the waterline, after which every extra body drowns everybody already in the boat, including the person you were trying to save. He says you also have to hold some of that rated capacity back as a safety factor, because the next storm is coming and a boat running exactly at its number is a boat that's already lost. And then the ratchet: every rescue teaches more people that this boat picks people up, so more people get in the water, and you can never go back down. He says this is just Hardin and he'd rather be honest about an unpleasant arithmetic than drown politely. He also waved off the other side's spaceship-Earth answer — one vessel, one crew, no passengers, nobody gets thrown out the airlock — on the grounds that there's no captain of that ship and no one to make anybody share.

Our reply was: it's not a lifeboat, it's a house with a spare room and a working kitchen.

Couple of background facts that didn't come up in the meeting: two neighboring counties have a mutual aid agreement with us, the feds reimburse most of the sheltering cost after a declared disaster, and we've doubled cot capacity twice in the last decade by opening school gyms. Also two of us do volunteer SAR on the water, and my understanding is that in an actual boat the decision isn't yours to make — you render assistance, and then you're on the hook to get those people to a place of safety, which is a seperate argument about who pays.

Here's what I want from you, and please don't make it a lecture on whether Hardin was a decent guy. Give me the correspondence table for the lifeboat: what's the hull, what's the rated capacity, what's the waterline, who are the swimmers, what's the safety factor, and what plays the role of the crew's authority to say no. Then tell me which of the disanalogies actually bite against the use he's making of it — justifying a hard cap — and which ones are cosmetic. Two things I specifically can't work out. First, does the ratchet step need the boat picture at all, or is it an independent claim about incentives that would still be standing if you dropped the metaphor entirely? Second, is there some subset of what our task force actually decides where the boat really is a boat — where capacity is genuinely fixed and hard and the drowning-everyone logic goes through — and if there is, how do I tell that apart from the commissioner just picking the picture that does his work for him? And finally, is our house-with-a-spare-room any better as an argument, or is it the same move in a nicer mood? If you think there's a better analogy for the actual decision on the table, give me one and say what it lets you conclude that the other two don't.
