His whole argument rides on one analogy: money is basically a language, a set of symbols that work only because everyone agrees they do and that have no intrinsic content. He reckons Aristotle backs him up, because of the bit in the Nicomachean Ethics on exchange where money turns out to exist by convention rather than by nature and can be changed or rendered useless. From there he runs three steps: money's value is purely collective agreement → so saying bitcoin has "no intrinsic value" is a category mistake → so a decentralised ledger is at least as legitimate as a state currency, maybe more so, since the agreement is out in the open. He's asked me to sanity-check it before it goes out, and I'd rather hand him an audit of the analogy than a vibe.

What I want:

1. The mapping laid out explicitly. A table, money → language: unit of account, medium of exchange, store of value, issuer, general acceptability, debasement and counterfeiting, dialects versus a standard, and whatever else you think belongs. Mark the rows where nothing in the source domain actually corresponds — those empty cells are the point.

2. The breakdowns, ranked by whether they matter for *his* use of it. Some disanalogies are harmless decoration (money having no grammar doesn't obviously block anything he wants to conclude); others do real damage. I suspect the store-of-value row is the dangerous one: words don't have to be repaid, don't go bankrupt, carry no default risk across time, and nobody's balance sheet is exposed when a meaning drifts. Does that sink the analogy, or just one function of it?

3. A verdict on each of the three inferential steps separately. My hunch is step one survives and the analogy is quietly carrying steps two and three. Tell me if I'm wrong.

4. What Aristotle's own analogies for money are — my recollection is that he reaches for a measure or unit, and for demand as the thing money stands in for — and whether swapping my roommate's analogy for Aristotle's changes the verdict on any of the three steps. This is the bit I most want you not to fudge: if the text doesn't say what I remember it saying, say so plainly rather than papering over it.

5. A better analogy if there is one — something he could substitute that doesn't overpromise — or the straight answer that no analogy carries step three and he should drop it.

Hard limit: 900 words, table included. He isn't a philosopher, so gloss any jargon you can't avoid.

One more request, and it's the one I care about most: seperate what's actually settled here from what people who know these texts genuinely argue about — whether that convention passage is about state fiat or about grown custom, whether the exchange discussion amounts to a demand theory of value or is really about proportionate reciprocity in justice, whether the material on money breeding money in the Politics constrains any of his steps. Don't hand me a contested reading dressed up as the textbook view, and label anything you're reconstructing rather than recalling. And flag it if you think his best move is to not cite Aristotle at all.
