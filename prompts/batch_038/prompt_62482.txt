I am applying for a two-year postdoctoral fellowship with a group that works at the interface of formal philosophy and the philosophy of social science. The application calls for a proposal of roughly 1,500 words covering the research question, the method and why that method, the deliverables, and an honest risk assessment. It does not ask for a bibliography and I will supply references myself, so please do not produce any or gesture at particular papers.

Here is the half-formed idea I want turned into a proposal.

Lewis analyzes a convention as a regularity in the behavior of members of a population that solves a recurring coordination problem of common interest, and that is sustained by the fact that each member expects the others to conform, prefers general conformity to partial conformity, and — in the strong version of the account — takes all of this to be commonly known. The signaling-game tradition that grew out of his own examples, in which populations converge on a signaling system under replicator dynamics or simple reinforcement learning, is usually read as showing that Lewis's epistemic conditions are not necessary for a convention to arise: no individual need represent the game, the others' expectations, or the coordination problem. I want to press the converse question, which I believe has been left in the background — not whether those conditions are needed to get a convention off the ground, but whether they do any explanatory or justificatory work in keeping one alive after the payoff structure that generated it has changed.

Concretely: consider a Lewis–Skyrms signaling game that switches partway through the run, so that the states acquire new action-relevant meanings, or the receiver's interests partially diverge from the sender's. The population is sitting at a signaling-system equilibrium of the old game, and is now playing a game in which that regularity no longer resolves any coordination problem of common interest. On Lewis's analysis it should stop counting as a convention; in practice the regularity keeps being followed, often for a long time, and agents keep justifying their conformity in the same way as before, by appeal to what everyone expects everyone else to do. Call this interval, and the regularities that persist through it, normative residue. My working hypothesis is that residue is not a marginal curiosity but a structurally predicted feature of Lewis's account, and that it is where the interesting cases of norm change live.

The proposal should do the following.

First, state a precise research question with a determinate answer. I suspect it has to be split in two: a conceptual question about whether Lewis's conditions can come apart in this way, and what the analysis should say when they do; and a formal question about whether adding higher-order expectation-tracking to the agents changes anything measurable in such a model — for instance, the rate at which the residue decays once the payoffs switch, compared with a population of agents who merely have reinforced habits and no representation of others' expectations at all. Say what the possible answers are and what would decide between them.

Second, explain why the existing positions leave this open: Lewis's account itself, the evolutionary modeling tradition, and the rival family of views on which a social norm is sustained by expectations about approval and disapproval rather than by expectations about behavior. For each, indicate whether it predicts the wrong thing, is silent, or absorbs the phenomenon so readily that nothing is left to investigate.

Third, set out the method and defend it against the obvious alternatives — patching the definition with an extra clause, a purely exegetical reconstruction of what Lewis could have said, and a qualitative case study of norm change inside real organizations. Explain what philosophical argument contributes here that simulation alone does not.

Fourth, give a phase-by-phase plan across twenty-four months, and specify deliverables that a hiring committee would recognize as achievable: I am thinking two papers and one workshop.

Fifth, describe what a successful result looks like and what would count as failure. I want the failure conditions to be genuinely falsifiable rather than decorative — including the case in which residue turns out to be already handled by resources Lewis provides for regularities that have stopped solving their coordination problem, in which case the project collapses into exegesis, and the case in which the formal comparison shows decay rates that are invariant to whether agents track others' expectations, which would undercut my hypothesis even if it would still be worth reporting.

Finally, assess scope honestly: which component is most likely to balloon (I expect the question of what makes a regularity the same convention over time, and any move to structured rather than well-mixed populations), and how the proposal should be trimmed if the fellowship committee signals that two years is optimistic.
