I direct a graduate seminar on the moral standing of everyday conventions, and I would like your help pressure-testing a view I have come to hold, or at least to defend provisionally in print. The view concerns the familiar class of local conventions: whether one tips and how much, whether one queues and in what order, whether one crosses against a pedestrian signal, whether one speaks to a shopkeeper in a certain register. My students assume such matters are outside morality altogether; I have come to think that assumption is too quick, and I need to know how much weight my own account can bear before it snaps.

Call it the participatory account. It has five commitments.

1. Participation, not consent. The obligation does not arise from any agreement, promise, or acceptance of benefits. It arises from taking part in a shared social space in which a determinate practice is generally followed. One who stands in the queue, sits at the table, or walks on the pavement has already enrolled.

2. Accessibility. The obligation attaches only where the agent could reasonably be expected to know that the practice obtains. A visitor who has been in the country nine hours and faces a signal convention unlike anything at home is not thereby excused if the convention is visible in the behavior of everyone around them; they are excused where it is genuinely opaque.

3. Local determination. The content of the obligation is fixed by the local practice, not by the agent's home practice. There is a no-export clause: the visitor may not discharge the obligation by doing what would have been correct back home, and may not plead that their own norm is more reasonable.

4. Detachment from particular outcomes. The wrongness does not depend on anyone's expectations actually being frustrated or anyone actually being harmed in the case at hand. It depends on the practice's being of the sort that generally coordinates expectations. Hence crossing against the signal at three in the morning on a wholly empty street in a city where pedestrians wait is still a wrong, though a slight one; so is abandoning a place you hold in a queue when no one will notice or mind.

5. Pro tanto status. The obligation is defeated only by competing moral considerations of comparable weight, not by inconvenience, by a dislike one has done nothing to inform, or by ignorance one could cheaply have cured.

The rivals I take seriously are: reliance-and-cost accounts, on which obligations arise only when someone's expectations are actually disappointed; fair-play or benefits accounts, on which the obligation flows from having accepted the fruits of a cooperative scheme; the descriptive social-norms approach, on which conventions of this kind are backed by sanctions and disapproval but are not moral norms at all, so that violating them is impolite rather than wrong; and expressive or respect-based accounts, on which the wrong consists in the message sent to others, and so evaporates when no message is sent.

Now tell me what would count against the participatory account. I am not interested in who has said what: no references, no reading list, no names of living philosophers. I want the dialectic, constructed from the materials I have given you. Specifically:

(a) A ranked list of defeaters, running from fatal to merely awkward. For each, state the case, argument, or empirical profile that generates it and say in a sentence or two why it bites, or why it fails to. Supply your own cases; I want at least two you have constructed rather than the stock ones everyone reaches for, and I want at least one drawn from a convention type I have not mentioned.

(b) For each defeater, whether a committed defender would concede that it counts against the view or would explain it away. Where it would be explained away, say what the explanation costs: does it require a new clause, a narrowing of the no-export rule, or a retreat from commitment 4?

(c) What the view predicts that its rivals do not, and which of those predictions could in principle come out wrong. In particular, the view seems hostage to a certain empirical profile concerning how people judge violations that no one observes and no one minds, and how children distinguish rules that remain binding absent an authority or an explicit rule from those that do not. Say whether a robust separation of that kind would be a genuine defeater or merely a different question, and why.

(d) Two anxieties of my own, which you should assess and then exceed. First, commitment 5 seems to leave a residual pro tanto obligation to comply with conventions whose content is odious — segregated queueing, say, or a local custom of not serving a certain group — even where the balance of reasons decisively favors violation; I find that residue repellant, and I cannot see how to remove it without collapsing into a content-filter that looks ad hoc. Second, if participation in shared social space enrolls one in every local practice, then a single act may enroll one in conflicting practices at once, as when a city has no single settled convention for whether one tips at a counter, and commitment 3 offers no way to choose because it simply defers to a practice that is not there. Tell me whether either anxiety is fatal, and then tell me what I have failed to worry about.

(e) An honest assessment of insulation. Between the accessibility condition, the pro tanto structure, and the defeasibility clause, I suspect the view may be able to absorb any counterexample by redescribing it either as a defeated obligation or as an inaccessible norm. Determine whether that is so, and if it is, whether it is a defect in a view of this kind or an appropriate response to the heterogeneity of the phenomena — a normative theory of conventions may legitimately be permissive without being empty, and I want to know where that line falls.

Lead with the defeater you judge most dangerous to the account, not the most obvious. Be willing to conclude that no decisive objection exists, or that the account has been qualified into vacuity; either conclusion is acceptable if argued for. Tell me plainly which single consideration, if it turned out right, would make me abandon the view.

End your response with a final line in exactly this form:

Verdict: <LABEL> | <no more than twelve words naming the single most damaging consideration>

where LABEL is one of AT RISK (at least one defeater the view cannot absorb without ceasing to be this view), WELL ARMORED (every defeater is absorbable by qualifications that are independently principled), or VACUOUS (every defeater is absorbed only by qualifications that leave the view without content).
