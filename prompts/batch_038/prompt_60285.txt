I work in clinical risk management at a mid-sized hospital and chair our serious-event review panel, which meets fortnightly; I read philosophy informally on the train commute and have gotten into the experimental-philosophy material over the past year. There is a pattern I keep seeing in these reviews that I have no word for, and I cannot tell whether it is a recognised one. When a bad outcome has several contributing conditions — typically four or five, each individually unremarkable, none sufficient on its own — the panel does not treat those conditions as equally eligible to be singled out as the cause. It searches, and the search stops the moment it lands on a condition attached to a person who did something they ought not to have done. Everything else, including conditions that were doing far more of the mechanical work in producing the outcome, gets demoted in the write-up to "background" or "context," and nobody experiences that demotion as a loss of explanation; the report reads as though it has answered the question.

The part I find harder to place is what happens when the search comes up empty. If no such person is found — the individual followed the protocol exactly, the protocol was current, the equipment performed to specification — the panel does not fall back on the mechanical account it had been perfectly content to leave unspoken a week earlier. It does not say that the cause was the interaction between the protocol and the equipment, even though that interaction is right there in the file, fully described and causally sufficient. Instead the event is reclassified as an accident, or as a "systems issue," and the question of what caused it is treated as no longer having an answer that could be written down. So the inquiry appears to have two exits rather than one: it terminates at a culpable agent, or it terminates by dissolving the question altogether. The physical facts are known in both cases; what varies is only whether a blameworthy candidate turned up. A smaller example from outside the hospital, which I raise because it suggests the pattern is not specific to medicine: our software team's incident postmortems behave the same way. When somebody deployed without review, the root cause is that person's shortcut, and the absent automated test that would have caught the bug becomes a footnote; when nobody skipped a step, the postmortem records that there was "no single root cause" and closes, although the absent test is still absent and still perfectly identifiable.

I am aware this sits next door to a finding I have come across, the one about people being readier to say that an agent brought about a harmful side effect deliberately than a helpful one, and I am also aware of the general point in the philosophy of causation that among many necessary conditions we tend to pick out the abnormal or unexpected one. But neither seems to capture what I am describing: in my cases the mechanical story is complete and available, and what varies is not whether a factor is abnormal but whether the demand for a cause can be brought to rest. So my question is whether there is an established concept that picks out this specific thing — the way the demand for a cause is satisfied by a culpable agent and, failing that, is abandoned rather than answered mechanically. If there is, tell me what it is called and where my description overreaches. If there is not, propose a term and define it tightly enough that I could apply it: what must be true for a case to count, what it has to be distinguished from — importantly, including the familiar situation in which there is a perfectly good causal explanation but nobody who can fairly be held responsible, since my phenomenon looks like something close to the reverse of that, with the explanation being what goes missing — and one or two boundary cases that would fall outside it. I would also like to know how you would design a study capable of separating my pattern from the two neighbouring accounts, given that they should make different predictions about what happens when the culpable candidate is removed from an otherwise identical vignette. Please give me your bottom line in the first sentence — an established concept, or no established concept and the term you would coin — and then the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of my question.
