So I could really use an outside brain on this, sorry in advance for the length. I grew up in a mill town in northern Maine — call it Westfield, pop. about 1,900 now, was 4,300 when I was born in '91. The paper mill closed in 2004, the high school consolidated with the next town over in 2011, the little hospital is now an urgent care clinic open untill 8pm, the movie theater is a self-storage place, and of the the three churches only one still holds services. I left at 18 like everybody does, got laid off from a logistics job in the spring, came home to help my mom after my dad's stroke, and somehow ended up appointed to the town council to fill a vacancy. My vote is very likely the tiebreaker on something and I don't feel equipped for it.

The state has a $2.3M economic revitalization grant we're shortlisted for, and there's two ways to spend it. Option A: put it, plus a town match we'd have to bond for, into an industrial park — grading, three-phase power, water and sewer stubs, a road cut off the state route — and use it to recruit a distribution warehouse. The pitch is 120 jobs and a broader tax base. Option B: no match needed, use it to buy out and retire the water and sewer district's debt, which is eating roughly a third of the annual budget in debt service, then purchase and demolish something like 60 vacant houses on the north side where the road plowing, water lines, and school bus routes cost the most per household, and consolidate the remaining people into the core. The town gets smaller on paper but stops bleeding. Half the council calls A fighting for the town and B euthanasia by spreadsheet. The other half calls A a boondoggle we'll be paying for in 2050 on behalf of a warehouse that leaves in 2031, and B finally living inside our means.

Here's the part I can't get straight: is there actually a fact of the matter about whether this town is collapsing versus just turning into something smaller? Because depending which number I look at I get opposite answers. Population halved, tax base halved, no local paper, no local high school, one church, no grocery store — but median household income of the people who stayed is up, the property tax rate is lower than when I was a kid, ambulance response time is better than it was, and my grandmother, who is 84 and has never lived anywhere else, insists she is less lonely now than she was in 1990. Meanwhile one of the other councilors keeps bringing up Jared Diamond's Collapse and the Easter Island story as a warning about societies that keep doing what they're doing right up until they can't, and people nod at it, and I can't tell whether that's a legitimate thing to be worried about in our situation or whether he's just reaching for a scary story. My dad's old friends treat the shrinkage as a humiliation that has to be reversed if we're going to have any self-respect; my grandmother treats it as the town finally settling down. I want to know whether that disagreement is even the sort of thing that numbers can settle, or whether they're describing different things and talking past each other — and if it's the latter, which description is the one my vote should be answering to. Then, given whatever you think about all that: what should I actually be weighing when I try to figure out whether A is a rescue or a trap, and whether B is adaptation or just surrender with better accounting? And what evidence, if I could get my hands on it before the meeting in three weeks, would change your mind about which one it is?
