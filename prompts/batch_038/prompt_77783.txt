For eleven years I have run an evening seminar at our county library called "Splitting the Difference." It draws about a dozen adults — two nurses, a retired machinist, a woman who came to it after a contentious divorce, a couple who run a food truck. I have no degree and never took a course in economics; I learned this material on my own, over twenty-odd years of working as a community mediator, and I teach it the way I learned it, from primary documents and arithmetic. This term we read the two founding papers of bargaining theory — Nash's 1950 article and the 1975 reply by Kalai and Smorodinsky — and we have spent five sessions drawing feasible sets and locating both solutions when the frontier is a straight line. I am now writing our final, and I want one compulsory question built on a curved example, because I have found that people who can recite the axioms usually cannot tell you what the axioms do.

Here is the material I want the question built on. In both problems there are two parties, utilities are normalized so that the disagreement point is (0,0), and free disposal is assumed: if an outcome is available, so is any outcome that gives neither party more.

Problem A: the feasible set consists of every pair (x, y) with x ≥ 0, y ≥ 0, and y ≤ 4 − x².

Problem B: the feasible set is the smallest convex, downward-closed set containing everything in Problem A together with one new option, (1.8, 2.2).

I have worked both by hand and I want the question to preserve what I found, so let me state it plainly. In Problem A the two solutions disagree: one favors the first party, the other the second. In Problem B the new option raises neither party's best possible payoff — the ideal point is still (2, 4) — and yet the Nash solution moves so that the second party ends up materially worse off than in Problem A, while the Kalai–Smorodinsky solution moves so that both parties are better off. That contrast is the whole point of the exercise, and I want the students to produce it themselves rather than be told it.

The question should have four parts, roughly: compute both solutions in Problem A, giving exact values where they are clean; compute both in Problem B and say precisely who gains and who loses relative to Problem A; construct a third feasible set, contained in Problem B, that shows the Kalai–Smorodinsky solution failing independence of irrelevant alternatives, and explain why the very same pair of problems does not show the Nash solution failing it; and a short closing argument, of perhaps 250 words, on whether the monotonicity requirement states a principle of fairness or is only a technical repair to Nash's axiom set, using their own numbers as evidence. I would also like the closing part to require them to explain why the two solutions can never disagree about a symmetric problem, and what that implies about where the real dispute lies.

I will supply the class with a sheet of axioms in my own words, and I want your version of that sheet to appear in the question as the students will see it: Pareto optimality; symmetry; invariance to rescaling of each party's utility; independence of irrelevant alternatives; and monotonicity, which I have written as follows — if the set of feasible outcomes grows but neither party's best possible payoff changes, then neither party should finish worse off than in the smaller problem. I am not confident that last one is the axiom as economists actually state it; I suspect the real version is about what each party can obtain at every possible level of the other's payoff, not merely about the two maxima. Tell me whether mine is a legitimate weakening, whether it is the standard formulation, and whether it still does the work in Problem B.

The constraints on the paper are these: two hours, open book, calculators permitted, twenty-five marks. My students have no university mathematics, so nothing may require more than solving quadratics and finding where two lines meet; if a step needs a differentiation rule, the sheet must hand it to them, together with any derivatives they would otherwise have to invent.

Please give me three things. First, the question exactly as it will be printed, including the formula and axiom sheet. Second, a model answer of about nine hundred words that shows the working a student is expected to show, with exact values where they exist and decimals to three places where they do not. Third, a marking scheme allocating the twenty-five marks across the four parts, which separates an outstanding answer from a merely competent one by naming the specific moves each must contain, and which lists the errors I should expect — for instance, reporting a maximum of the product without checking that the point lies on the frontier of the enlarged set, or claiming that Nash violates independence of irrelevant alternatives because its old solution is still feasible in Problem B and simply is not chosen. For each such error, say what it reveals and what it costs. If you think my example misbehaves in some way I have not noticed, or that a small change to the numbers would make the arithmetic kinder without dulling the point, say so before you write anything else.
