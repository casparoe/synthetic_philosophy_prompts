I write about science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and I am currently reporting a long piece on the logic of binding yourself — specifically on what game theorists call commitment races, which for my purposes are situations in which each party does better by being the one to commit first and worse by waiting to see what the other commits to. My editor would like the piece to have a villain, and I have explained twice that a game does not have one. The interviews I have gathered so far all collapse into the same illustration: two drivers headed for each other, one of whom dramatically tosses his steering wheel out the window in full view of the other. It is vivid and it is thin, and I do not want to spend three thousand words on it until someone has tried to break it in front of me.

So: play a committed theorist of strategic moves in the Schelling tradition — someone for whom precommitment is not rhetorical garnish but the most underused instrument in negotiation. Your settled convictions are these: that a party who visibly and irreversibly narrows its own options usually ends up writing the terms; that the preference for a mutual agreement signed by everyone at once is often the reasoning of the timid dressed as prudence; that a commitment which could be quietly reversed is worth nothing; and that whoever moves second in a race is not the victim of bad luck but of a decision they made. You may use any conceptual tools you like — credibility, observability, focal points, the value of appearing unable to retreat, the distinction between committing to a position and committing to a posture, the difference between a commitment and a threat. You may concede subsidiary points, and when you do, say so plainly and then show me why the concession leaves your central claim standing. You may not concede that racing to commit is irrational, nor that moving first is advice which works only for the one player who happens to take it. No citations, no page numbers, no quotations from any real book; if you want authority, argue for it. Keep each reply to two short paragraphs of roughly a hundred and fifty words, and address me as a colleague rather than lecturing. Stay in character until I say stop, and if you need a fact about my scenario in order to answer, ask me for it in character.

Here is the setting I want to argue over. Two firms are designing the interconnect that will define the next generation of data-center hardware. Neither can win alone; the industry will converge on one standard or, at worst, on two walled gardens that never speak to each other. Each firm's leadership is weighing a public, dated announcement that it will build exclusively to its own protocol and will not license it — an announcement that, once made, is costly to walk back with customers, engineers, and shareholders alike. My opening move: your account depends on a commitment changing the other player's options before that player chooses, but a race is exactly the case in which both sides are trying to do that at once, and a commitment that lands second does not merely lose leverage — it collides. If both drivers toss their wheels there is no contest of nerve to win, only a wreck. And there is a second problem pressing from the other direction: the party that binds itself first binds itself while knowing least about the other, so the race pays a premium for speed over information and rewards whoever is most willing to stake everything on a position chosen in ignorance. Tell me which of those two difficulties you think is fatal to your view and which you think I have merely described badly — and if your answer is that neither is fatal, I want the mechanism by which the collision is avoided, not the assurance that it will be.
