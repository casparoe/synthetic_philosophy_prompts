I retired in 2019 after thirty-four years in marine stratigraphy — mostly describing Quaternary cores from the North Atlantic, and teaching undergraduates what has to be true of a boundary before it can be fixed and correlated. Since then I have been auditing seminars in the philosophy department of the university where I still hold borrowing rights, and I have acquired a taste for the literature on classification, natural kinds, and the extent to which our categories carve nature at its joints rather than merely sorting it for our convenience.

Last month one of the doctoral students in the group circulated a chapter draft arguing that the Anthropocene controversy is a rerun of the Pluto controversy. His gloss runs roughly as follows: in both cases the observations were never in dispute; in both cases the quarrel concerned the extension of a word; in both cases the matter was closed not by discovery but by a vote of an international body — the astronomers' resolution of 2006, and the rejection in March 2024 of the geologists' proposed mid-twentieth-century boundary marker; and in both cases a new category was subsequently invented to house the dissenters, dwarf planet on the one hand and the Anthropocene-event proposal floated since by some of the same geologists on the other. From this he draws two conclusions. The first is that humanity's planetary impact is as settled as the fact that Pluto is round, and that fifteen years of stratigraphic procedure was pedantry of no epistemic interest. The second, which does the real work in his chapter, is that because the placement of the boundary is definitional, historians and the public may date the onset as they wish — with the spread of agriculture, with the depopulation of the Americas, with the steam engine — without thereby putting themselves in conflict with geology, since no committee's ruling carries more weight than any other convention.

I would like an audit of the analogy rather than a defence of my old profession. Please lay out the correspondence explicitly: what in the Anthropocene case plays the role of Pluto, of the word planet, of the third criterion about dynamical dominance of an orbit, of the union, of the ballot, and of the compromise category. Then identify where the mapping breaks down, and please separate the breaks that bear on his two conclusions from the ones that are merely picturesque. I am especially interested in whether the analogy silently assumes that both cases sort objects all of which are present at once, and what becomes of it if the geological case instead concerns a chronology whose boundaries must survive physically in an archive and be correlatable across the globe. Do address the obvious retort: that the 2006 resolution was itself widely judged procedurally botched, carried by a few hundred of the union's several thousand members, so that he is comparing two political processes and not a political process to a scientific one.

Finally, tell me whether either of his conclusions survives once the disanalogies are counted, and whether a better analogy exists. My own candidate, offered in all humility, is that fixing the start of the Anthropocene resembles fixing the onset of an illness: one may ask when the patient was infected, when the first symptoms appeared, or when the diagnostic test first turned positive, and the three answers differ without any of them being arbitrary or merely stipulated. Say whether that improves on Pluto, or whether it smuggles in difficulties of its own — and if so, what would carry the weight better.
