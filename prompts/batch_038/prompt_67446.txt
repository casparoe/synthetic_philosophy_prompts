I have been arguing with a friend for about a week now about an application of the strategy-stealing argument, and I want someone to tell me where the reasoning actually goes wrong, because the two of us keep talking past each other and I am no longer sure which of us has identified the weak point. Start with the version I feel confident about. In a symmetric connection game like Hex — two players alternately claiming hexagons, each trying to complete a chain between their own two sides — one can show that the second player has no winning strategy. Suppose she did. The first player opens by claiming an arbitrary cell, then follows her strategy, treating himself as though he were the second player and simply ignoring the fact that he already owns one extra cell, playing somewhere else at random whenever the stolen strategy calls for a cell he has already taken. Since an extra stone of your own colour can never make your position worse in a game of that kind, the first player would win by means of a strategy that was supposed to be a guaranteed win for the second player, which is absurd. The same shape of argument applied to tic-tac-toe strikes me as yielding something weaker: it rules out a second-player win, but under perfect play the game is drawn rather than won. I am not sure how much of the force of the Hex case depends on Hex being a game that cannot end in a draw, and my friend says that is a detail.

My friend works on model evaluations at a lab, and she wanted to transfer the structure to competition between AI developers. Her claim is that a lab which spends part of its budget on safety work can always adopt whatever plan a less careful competitor adopts, because the safety work only ever adds information, options, and understanding of failure modes, and extra options cannot hurt; therefore carefulness carries no competitive penalty, and the race-to-the-bottom pessimism that has been a staple of these debates for a decade is unsound. I accepted that for a while. Then I hit what I take to be a fatal problem. The Hex argument is non-constructive: it establishes that a winning first-player strategy exists without exhibiting it, and on boards too large to be settled by search nobody knows what the winning line is. So the lab version appears to establish the existence of a win-the-race-while-being-careful strategy that no actual lab could execute, and in a real competition an unexecutable winning strategy loses to an executable strategy that is merely very good. Her response is that this is a mathematician's scruple with no practical content, since a lab does not need a solved board: it can watch its competitor, copy the plan, and pocket the difference. I am not persuaded, but I cannot articulate why, and there is a second worry she waved away — that in chess there are positions in which the player who is obliged to move would rather pass, so the premise that an extra move can only help is not a law of nature but a feature of particular games.

So I would like an audit rather than a summary. Where does the strategy-stealing argument get its force, and which of Hex's structural features does the lab application inherit rather than merely assume? Please lay out the premises the transfer depends on and take them one at a time — including the one she leaned on hardest, that safety work is an extra stone rather than a stone spent somewhere else, the tic-tac-toe point about drawability, and the chess point about being obliged to move. Then tell me which of our objections bites and which only sounds worrying, what the argument licenses at most once its premises are made explicit (in particular, whether its conclusion is about anyone winning at all, or only about who cannot be guaranteed to win), and whether any defensible version of the claim that safety work costs nothing competitively survives the audit — or whether the transfer from a solved board game to an unsolved race was a category error from the start. For what it is worth, I have read a great deal of combinatorial game theory in popular form and rather too many years of forum arguments about takeoff speeds, so I am happy to be told that my objection is the wrong one, provided I am shown which objection is the right one.
