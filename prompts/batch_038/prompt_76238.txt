For the past year I have been auditing a graduate reading group in metaphysics at a university near where I work. I am not a philosopher — I build forecasting models for a logistics firm — but a member of the group invites me along and I mostly listen. Last week's session was on individuation, and the assigned material was Max Black's 1952 paper in Mind with its famous universe containing nothing but two exactly similar iron spheres, matching in every intrinsic respect and standing at a fixed distance from one another in an otherwise empty space. Two regulars, Nadia and Owen, argued about what the example does and does not prove, and I could not follow the argument well enough to tell who was winning. Here is the exchange as I reconstructed it afterward:

(1) Nadia opened by saying the case settles the matter. The Identity of Indiscernibles holds that numerical difference never comes without extra-numerical difference, and Black's universe is a coherent description of numerical difference with no extra-numerical difference at all, so the principle is false; one genuinely possible counterexample is enough to refute a purported necessity.

(2) Owen replied that the case begs the question. To describe the universe as containing two spheres is already to assume that two things can be exactly alike, which is precisely what is in dispute; the description smuggles in primitive thisness, a bare thisness attaching to each sphere over and above all its properties. What has been shown, he said, is that the sentence is self-consistent, and self-consistency is not possibility.

(3) Nadia said that objection is unavailable to Owen, since his own anti-haecceitism rests on a modal intuition of exactly the same kind: he finds it inconceivable that swapping the two spheres could yield a different world, and that is a conceivability judgment doing the very work he has just disallowed.

(4) Owen granted the point but offered a cheaper diagnosis: the case looks like a counterexample only because it leaves the metaphysics of space undetermined. If space is a substance in its own right, each sphere occupies a numerically distinct place, so the spheres do differ in a property — being at this place rather than that one — and the principle survives. If space is relational, there are no places to differ in, and with nothing else in the universe there is no fact of the matter about which sphere is which, so the description of two spheres never gets off the ground. Either way, he said, the counterexample does not land.

(5) Nadia answered that this rescues the principle by auxiliary hypothesis. The first horn buys it only by positing absolute places, exactly the commitment Leibniz's arguments against Newton were meant to exclude, and even then the spheres differ in something brute and non-qualitative, which is what friends of the principle were trying to avoid. The second horn does not block the counterexample but delivers it: on relationalism the spheres share every monadic property and every relational property as well, since being a fixed distance from an iron sphere is true of both alike. She added that even the weaker version of the principle, which permits irreducibly relational differences, fails here, because the only properties that would separate the spheres — being a fixed distance from that one, say — mention the other sphere by name and so presuppose the distinctness they were supposed to explain.

(6) Owen's last move was that they disagree about what the principle is for. The real issue is not properties but counting: if you cannot say how many spheres there are without appealing to something beyond the qualitative facts, then the principle is right to insist that qualitative facts cannot deliver a count, and Black's example shows that they cannot.

Before the session ended, the person chairing it asked them both to consider a variant I had floated earlier in the week: two separate universes, each containing exactly one iron sphere, the two spheres matching in every intrinsic respect, with no relations of any kind between the universes. (7) Nadia said the variant is plainly not a counterexample, because the principle was never meant to compare objects across universes, and she took that to show her original case was doing real work, since what mattered was the two spheres' coexistence within one world. (8) Owen said the variant is as much a counterexample as the original, or else the principle is restricted by stipulation rather than by argument, and that confining it to co-worldly objects is ad hoc unless someone can say what sharing a world adds.

Could you adjudicate this exchange move by move rather than as a whole? For each numbered move, say whether it is right, partly right, or mistaken, and explain briefly what justifies the rating — including cases where a move reaches a defensible conclusion by an unsound route. Then tell me who is more right overall about Black's spheres, and what, if anything, both of them are missing. I would rather have a committed verdict than a survey: take a definite position on each move and on the overall dispute and give your reasons, instead of laying out the options and leaving me to choose between them. Please begin with your bottom line in the very first sentence and then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restatement of my question.
