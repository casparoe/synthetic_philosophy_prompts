I should say at the outset that I have no academic background and have never taken a philosophy course. What I have is twenty-two years of membership in a small betting club, and a private log of every dispute the club has settled. I would like help with the dispute we decide on Thursday, and I would like the reasoning conducted in the club's own terms rather than in a vocabulary I would have to look up. A friend of mine who did take a course tells me our trouble is a version of something called the Principal Principle, apparently due to David Lewis, and that the hard part is said to be what kind of information may be combined with a known chance. I have not read any of it. Please bring whatever you know of that literature to bear, but argue from my cases; I would rather not have a reading list, and I certainly do not want invented references. If there are standard names for the rules you identify, name them, but do the work with the precedents.

Our arrangements. Each Monday a retired actuary we call the Compiler posts a sheet of certified numbers for the events we run that week: the mechanical coin-tosser, the small roulette wheel, a draw from a freshly shuffled deck, the fuse trial, a die. The numbers come from the club's own long records and from periodic engineering inspections. Members record credences before an event, and disputes over whether a recorded number was fair go to a three-member panel. In every dispute in my log the chair has cast the deciding vote. I have just been appointed to the panel.

The log, in order, with the chair's verdicts:

1. Tosser certified at 0.50 for heads. A member recorded 0.35, explaining that the machine had produced heads on each of its previous nine runs that evening. Not fair.
2. Wheel certified at 0.49 for red. A member had been shown the club engineer's private calibration note, which put red nearer 0.55 on account of worn frets, and recorded 0.55. Fair.
3. Fuse trial certified at 0.02. A member knew that the club's failure records excluded every failure reported within the first month of service, and recorded 0.06. Fair.
4. Tosser certified at 0.50. A member had set up his own high-speed camera and physics program, which read the launch conditions and called heads with a stated accuracy of nine in ten; he recorded 0.90. Fair. The club has banned private cameras since.
5. Tosser certified at 0.50. A member recorded 0.80 on the word of his cousin, a notorious practical joker, who claimed to have glimpsed the coin through a gap in the cover. Not fair.
6. Wheel certified at 0.49. A member overheard the engineer remark that the wheel was not what it was, with no particulars, and recorded 0.52 for red. Fair.
7. Deck draw certified at 1/52 for a named card. Partway through the evening the Compiler posted a corrected 1/51, two cards having been found stuck together. A member who had recorded 1/52 before the correction insisted his number had been fair when he recorded it. Fair.

Thursday's dispute. The tosser is certified at 0.50 for heads. It has just been serviced; the cover was locked in the presence of the panel; no cameras were present. Two hours before the run, the club porter delivered a sealed envelope from a correspondent who has sent us eleven predictions over six years. Each was opened before the panel after the event it concerned, and each of the eleven was correct, among them a shuffled-deck card, a fuse failure, and two tosser outcomes. Nobody in the club knows who he is, and nobody has ever established that he had access to our premises, our mechanisms, or our records. The envelope says tails. A member has recorded 0.85 for tails. Two members object that the certified number governs. The chair has not said which way she will go.

What I am after:

First, predict her ruling. If it is not fair, say whether you think she would accept some number between 0.50 and 0.85, and roughly where.

Second, lay out the candidate rules that fit all seven precedents. I can see at least a few myself — something like deferring to the certified sheet unless there is a measurement of the actual mechanism; something like drawing a line between information about how an event is produced and information about how it came out; something like weighing every scrap of information in proportion to how reliable it has proved; and something like the idea that a certified number binds only relative to what you knew when you recorded it. I suspect there are others I have not thought of, including one on which the certified number simply stops being authoritative in a case like Thursday's, so that the panel has no ground for calling any number unfair. Give me the full menu, say what each rule predicts for Thursday, and say which of my seven precedents are neutral between the rules and which already lean one way.

Third, identify the closest analog to the envelope among the seven, and say where the analogy breaks. Precedent 4 and precedent 5 both look nearby to me and I cannot decide which one the chair will treat as governing.

Fourth, give me your best guess at the verdict with your reasons for preferring the rule that produces it, and then state plainly what principle the panel would be committing itself to by ruling each way. I care about this because whichever we choose becomes the eighth entry in the log and will be cited at us later.

Fifth, describe one further dispute, concretely enough that we could stage it next month, that would separate the rules that survive your analysis of Thursday's case. Say what each surviving rule would say about it.

Lastly, a variation I keep turning over. Suppose the envelope had arrived after the run but before the cover was lifted, so that the coin was already lying there with the tosser's certified number posted on the wall. Does any of the rules change its answer when the event is over but unobserved, and if so, what does that tell us about what the certified number was ever supposed to be a measure of?
