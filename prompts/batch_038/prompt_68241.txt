For eleven years I have designed charts and pictorial notices for a municipal benefits agency, and last month I was handed the redesign of the eligibility letter that goes out to every household in this city — some two hundred thousand of them, in five languages, to readers whose literacy in any of those languages cannot be assumed. An external audit had found that a large share of eligible households never act on the notice at all. This ceased to be an abstract professional problem for me two winters ago, when my mother missed the deadline for a heating subsidy because the letter informing her of it was accurately drafted, correctly translated, and impossible for her to parse. I am now, unwillingly, someone who has to take a formal position on whether pictures can carry legally operative content.

A colleague who found me flailing suggested that a philosopher attempted to build precisely such a thing a century ago: Otto Neurath and the picture-language program that grew out of his social and economic museum in Vienna — first the Vienna Method of pictorial statistics, later Isotype, with Gerd Arntz drawing the signs and Marie Reidemeister performing the transformations from source material to finished chart. I have skimmed enough to know the rules of thumb that get repeated (repeat an identical sign rather than enlarge a single one; keep quantities within a countable range; make the display self-explanatory; the museum's standing line that it is better to remember simplified pictures than to forget accurate figures), and enough to suspect that a substantive theory sits underneath those rules that I am not equipped to read in the original.

So I would like you to restate Neurath's picture-language program in the vocabulary I actually work in: glyph libraries and design systems, encoding channels, legends and keying, redundancy, small multiples, direct labeling, accessibility conformance, and above all the distinction I try to hold onto between a chart type and the claim a chart makes. Work through it claim by claim rather than as a narrative, and then give me the residue report. Specifically:

1. Which of his commitments translate cleanly into my terms, and into which terms.
2. Which translate only by distortion — where the nearest item in my vocabulary quietly carries assumptions he would not accept. A design-system component and what he means by a self-same repeated sign are not obviously the same thing, and I want the difference stated rather than papered over.
3. Which have no counterpart in my vocabulary at all.

Please keep the translation honest in both directions. Do not launder my field's defaults into his program: where Neurath would reject a standard piece of contemporary practice — encoding quantity by area or length, for instance, or our premium on unrounded precision — say so and explain the reasoning. Conversely, where modern information design has a resource he lacked, label it as mine rather than presenting it as an implicit part of his view.

Three points deserve particular pressure:

First, the logical one. Can a picture language express negation and generality? The operative content of my notice is a conditional with a negative consequent — a household that does not respond by a given date forfeits its position in the queue — and I cannot see how any arrangement of self-same signs states that rather than merely suggesting it. What does his system do with such content, and what is the cost?

Second, the institutional one. My committee wants to know whether pictograms may stand alone, with no accompanying text, in a document that has legal effect. My impression is that Neurath treated pictures as an auxiliary to words rather than a replacement for them. Was that a limit he argued for on principle, or a concession to the audiences and reproduction technology of his era?

Third, the semantic one. Does a Neurath chart assert something capable of being false, or does it only display? My discipline has no settled answer to that question, and I suspect his program does.

Close by telling me which single step in your translation you consider most likely to be wrong, and whether the residue — the parts that refuse to fit into my vocabulary — indicates that information design is missing a piece of theory it needs, or that Neurath's program was looser than its rhetoric suggested. I would rather be given the second answer if it is the true one, since I have a recommendation to file and it should not rest on a philosopher's ambition.
