I am preparing a session on Bayesian social epistemology for my graduate seminar, and I have an argument I intend to put to them whose conclusion I cannot myself endorse. I would like your diagnosis of it before I commit it to the handout.

(1) Aumann's result: if two Bayesian agents share a common prior, and their posterior probabilities for some proposition are common knowledge between them, then those posteriors coincide.

(2) Call an exchange exhaustive when both parties have laid all of their evidence before one another and each knows that this has been done, and knows that the other knows it, and so on without limit. In an exhaustive exchange, each party's posterior for any proposition under discussion is common knowledge between them.

(3) Rationality requires a common prior. A prior is by definition what one holds before conditioning on evidence, so no evidence can require it; two rational agents must accordingly begin from the same distribution.

(4) Hence, after any exhaustive exchange, rational agents agree about everything they have exchanged evidence about.

(5) Yet exhaustive exchanges between people I take to be epistemic peers plainly occur and end in disagreement: a colleague and I have spent a year trading everything either of us can adduce on a contested empirical question in the psychology of reasoning, and we remain on opposite sides of it.

(6) Therefore (3) is false, and rationality places no constraint whatever on priors: every probability distribution over the state space is an admissible starting point for an ideally rational agent.

Tell me which step to reject, and why it seemed unobjectionable to me when I wrote it down — I want the diagnosis to explain the appeal of the faulty step, not merely to locate it. Then price the alternatives: I am most attached to (1) and to the report in (5), so if either must go, say what epistemology loses; and say, for each of (2), (3), (4), and (6), what I would be giving up by abandoning it, including whether Aumann's result retains any purchase on real disagreement once the argument is repaired. Finally, rank the candidate repairs by the cost to me of accepting them, and say which one a Bayesian about rationality who is not prepared to embrace (6) should prefer.
