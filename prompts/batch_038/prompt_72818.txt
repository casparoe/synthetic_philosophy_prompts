Last month my grandmother, who is 84 and in the late stages of Alzheimer's, developed a chest infection, and my aunt and my uncle spent the better part of two days arguing in a hospital family room about whether to treat it. I was there only because I had driven my uncle over and stayed behind to take notes: the palliative care nurse had asked someone to write down what each side was actually claiming, since the ethics consultation the next morning would be working from that record. What follows is my reconstruction of the exchange, in order and as fairly as I can manage. Some background first. When my grandmother was 61, still entirely herself, and having just watched her own mother decline, she wrote an advance directive refusing antibiotics for pneumonia or any other infection once she reached a state in which she could no longer recognize her children. She also added a sentence that has dominated the entire dispute: that if in that state she appeared content — if she smiled at music, laughed at simple things, seemed untroubled — the appearance should be read as information about the disease rather than about her wishes, because, in her words, "the woman who would be content in that condition is not the one signing this." I am deliberately not telling you which of my relatives I found more persuasive, because I would rather the assessment not be anchored by mine.

Their moves went like this. (1) My uncle said the directive is a forecast and forecasts can be falsified: my grandmother predicted that the state she feared would be unbearable, and that prediction has turned out to be wrong, since she is demonstrably not miserable; a forecast that is wrong about the patient's own experience loses its authority. (2) My aunt said this is a category error — the document is not testimony about how things will feel but an instruction from the one person who has standing to give it, and treating it as a prediction hands the decision to whoever happens to be at the bedside, which is exactly what it was written to prevent. (3) My uncle then went after personal identity: if you take Parfit's reductionist line seriously, that what matters is psychological continuity rather than identity, then advanced dementia severs the connection that would make the cheerful woman in the bed the person who signed anything, so the directive is not an exercise of self-government at all but a request by one person that we let someone else die — and we should be disturbed by how readily such a request is honored when its target cannot object. (4) My aunt said he is entitled to that argument only if he will follow it down, and he won't: if the present patient is a different person, then his own "my mother would want to live" is a claim on behalf of a woman who is gone, so the metaphysics defeats his position as much as hers. She added that he had produced the identity argument only at the moment it served him, since an hour earlier he had been insisting on continuity — that she was still his mother, still the same person she had ever been — when arguing that the family must not give up on her; she called this opportunistic metaphysics. (5) My uncle conceded the inconsistency charge had some force but said his conclusion did not depend on it, because there is a plain asymmetry of error: treating the infection risks a bounded wrong (a course of antibiotics, some days of discomfort, continued life in a condition his sister considers worthless), while withholding it risks an irreversible one, and when you cannot tell which of two contested pictures of the patient is correct you should not make the mistake that cannot be repaired. He added that we ought to default to the interests of whoever is actually present to be helped, since the signer of the document can now neither be helped nor harmed in any way anyone could verify. (6) My aunt replied that his asymmetry runs the wrong way and that his default rule would abolish advance directives as a class, since every directive is by construction about a person who is absent — and if absence from the room disqualifies an interest, no directive has ever had authority. She said the harm he calls bounded isn't bounded: what is in front of the family is not one course of antibiotics but a standing policy of restarting them each time the same infection returns. And she said the contentment he keeps appealing to endorses nothing — it settles no question, states no view, and cannot be interrogated — whereas the directive was written by someone who anticipated precisely this appearance and pre-empted it in writing; on her account, a passing look of ease cannot revoke a considered refusal. The infection was treated, my grandmother recovered, and the argument has been suspended rather than resolved.

Please adjudicate this move by move rather than in the aggregate. For each of the six numbered claims, tell me whether it is clearly mistaken, partly right, or clearly right, and say briefly what would have to be true for it to hold; I am particularly interested in whether (3) and (4) simply cancel each other out or whether one of them leaves a residue the other fails to answer. Then tell me which of the two is more right overall, and whether the sentence my grandmother added about contentment should carry any weight at all — or whether it is an attempt to legislate in advance about a question that can only be answered from inside the later life. Finally, tell me what both of them are missing: whether some consideration, had either raised it, would have changed the shape of the dispute, and whether there is a point in the exchange on which they cannot be separated by argument at all.
