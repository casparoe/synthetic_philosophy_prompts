Last month I sat in on a workshop talk and then stayed through about forty minutes of argument between the two respondents — a computational neuroscientist and a philosopher of mind visiting from another department. I have tried to reproduce the exchange faithfully and in order, without adding anything either of them did not say.

**Round one (the philosopher).** He described the color-phi display: a green dot flashed at one location, a red dot flashed a short distance away a moment later, with the spacing and interval set inside the range where apparent motion occurs. Observers, he noted, report a single object traveling between the two positions and switching color at the spatial midpoint of its path — that is, they report the switch before the second flash has happened. He took this to settle a substantive question. A report cannot be about content that does not yet exist, so the content of the experience must have been altered after the second flash arrived. That is an Orwellian revision, history rewritten in place, and it shows there is no stable stream of presented contents for a subject to inspect.

**Round two (the neuroscientist).** She denied that the report licenses any inference about the experience. On her account, the color switch could genuinely have been represented at the midpoint, because predictive machinery carried the trajectory forward from the first flash, with the verbal report merely issued later. That is the Stalinesque alternative — the show is right, the record of it is delayed — and it accommodates every datum he cited. She added that the two hypotheses predict identical reports in every version of the display anyone could run, so no experiment could ever separate them; that, she said, is her reason for treating the dispute as empty rather than as unresolved.

**Round three.** He granted the empirical equivalence and said it was his conclusion, not an objection to it. If two stories about when an experience happened are indistinguishable by every observation anyone could make, the question they disagree about has no content. And her predictive-mechanism story, he went on, does precisely the work his does: saying the trajectory is carried forward before the second flash is only another way of saying the experienced content was not a function of the stimulus sequence.

**Round four.** She said that was too quick. The hypotheses differ about something real and measurable — where in the processing stream the color representation gets settled — and the display's dependence on spacing and interval is a parameter-shaped prediction, not a shrug. Neural timing, not verbal report, is the right instrument: you can measure when the relevant activity occurs relative to the second flash, and a theory that ties the experience to that activity is committed to something.

**Round five.** His reply was that this is exactly the mistake the display was meant to expose. A neural measurement locates a brain state in time; it does not locate an experience in time. If you assume experience has a temporal address that such measurements can find, you have reinstated a place where contents are presented to someone — the very thing the illusion was supposed to rule out. Her appeal to the timing data, he said, smuggles the theater back in.

**Round six.** Her last move was that if there is no fact of the matter about when the experience occurred, then the question that generated the whole problem — whether the observer saw the color change before or after the second flash — has been dissolved rather than answered. And dissolving a question that ordinary people can perfectly well ask, and that both of them had been happily asking in the first two rounds, is a worse outcome than conceding that we do not yet know.

They were then called to dinner. I could not referee it at the time, and I am not certain I can now.

Please adjudicate this move by move. For each of the six rounds, give a rating from clearly mistaken to clearly right, state what exactly the point succeeds against if it succeeds, and identify where it overreaches; a sentence or two of justification for each rating is enough. Then tell me where the argument actually turns: which single claim, if it were settled, would decide most of the rest, and why the others depend on it. Finally, name anything both of them assumed without arguing for it — I suspect the stalemate rests on a shared premise neither one stated.

Begin your response with your overall verdict in the first sentence — who is more right, and about what — and then give the move-by-move reasoning. No preamble, and no restatement of my question.
