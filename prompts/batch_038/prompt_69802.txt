I am assembling a reading packet for a graduate seminar on intergenerational ethics, and the session I have in mind concerns the choice of a pure rate of time preference. My students arrive able to recite the Ramsey decomposition but unable to see that the quarrel over the pure rate is a moral argument conducted in the vocabulary of calibration. Prose summaries have not dislodged this, so I intend to hand them a piece of satire instead, and I would like you to write it.

The form I want is a Swiftian modest proposal of roughly eight hundred words — call it seven to nine hundred — in the first person and in a tone of unruffled administrative reasonableness, as though it were to run in the op-ed pages of a serious financial daily. Its author should be the chief economist of an invented treasury-like body; give the body some plainly bureaucratic name of your own devising, but do not use any real institution, ministry, review, or consultancy, and do not target any living person. He has just been delegated authority to dispose of the twenty-third century, and the piece is his explanation of why this is not merely permissible but efficient. The scheme itself should be concrete and should escalate: an auction of future decades, perhaps a class of instruments by which the claims of unborn persons are securitized and sold forward, and a closing paragraph in which the reader discovers what has been done with the residual risk. The proposal must follow its own logic to a conclusion the reader recoils from, and the recoil should be the point rather than an accident.

The humour has to come from the economics and the moral philosophy being right, so that a specialist laughs rather than winces, and so that she judges the piece unfair only in its exaggeration. Deploy, as the material of the jokes and not as decoration: the fact that once growth projections flatten, the pure rate is doing all the work in the Ramsey equation; the fork between calibrating the social rate to observed market returns and deciding what is ethically permissible, and the way the author slides across it without noticing; Koopmans's old result that a coherent ranking of infinite utility streams must, under stationarity and continuity, embody impatience, together with Diamond's charge that those axioms presuppose the conclusion — your economist should invoke the first as though it were a theorem of nature and remain serenely deaf to the second; the finding that genuine uncertainty about the rate itself drives the certainty-equivalent rate down as the horizon lengthens, until the far future is priced nearer to the lowest rate anyone seriously entertains than to the central one, which is a disaster for his auction and must be explained away by some technocrat's device worse than the problem; the observation that at a pure rate of zero with an unbounded horizon the aggregate diverges and the calculus ceases to rank anything at all, which he should treat as a bookkeeping inconvenience rather than as an argument; quasi-hyperbolic present bias, the resulting time inconsistency, and the money pump to which a naive discounter is vulnerable — he may regard the pump as a revenue opportunity; and, circling beneath the whole thing, Sidgwick's insistence that mere position in time is not a morally relevant distinction, Pigou's diagnosis of myopia as a defect of the imaginative faculty rather than a preference to be respected, and Ramsey's conviction that discounting for nearness alone is indefensible. He must talk around these last three without ever conceding them.

He should never break character: no winking, no hedge, no aside admitting the objection, no sentence in which the author of the proposal is visibly separate from you. Where he meets a difficulty he should answer it in his own idiom, and the answer should be more alarming than the difficulty. Keep the whole thing deadpan and yet legible — a reader should be able to tell what is being ironized without being told.

Two constraints of housekeeping. Do not put any words in quotation marks as though they came from a real author; where your economist alludes to Sidgwick, Pigou, Ramsey, Koopmans, Diamond or anyone else, let him paraphrase in his own voice, and let him cite no page, date, journal or title. And give me the piece itself and nothing else: no preface, no framing note, no postscript identifying which joke corresponds to which argument. Write it as continuous prose, the way one would write a letter — no headings, no subheadings, no bullet points, no numbered clauses, no bold or italic emphasis, no tables, and no line breaks other than those between paragraphs.
