So I'm the token philosopher on a seven-person working group our state judicial conference set up to write a short bench guide on neuroscience evidence for trial judges — six pages total, no footnotes, and staff counsel never lets us forget that the reader is a judge with one afternoon and no science background. Most of what we cover is pretty dry: brain-injury mitigation at sentencing, fMRI lie detection, the usual. Last month a criminal court judge actually sent us a live question, which basically never happens, and it has the group split in a way I've failed to talk my way out of. I got assigned to circulate something before Thursday's call.

The case is an aggravated assault after a bar argument. The defense expert, a well-published cognitive neuroscientist, ran the defendant himself through a self-paced button-press task fourteen months after the incident: averaged readiness potential starting around 470 ms before muscle onset, defendant's reported first awareness of the urge around 190 ms before. His affidavit says this "matches the classic pattern" and concludes that the defendant's acts are initiated unconsciously before awareness, that his capacity to inhibit is measurably impaired, and that this goes to whether the assault was a voluntary act at all. He leans on the original 1983 study (readiness potential beginning roughly half a second out, reported urge around 200 ms), a later scanning study that predicted which hand subjects would use several seconds ahead at maybe 60% accuracy, and an fMRI study of withholding an already-prepared movement. The prosecution has a rebuttal expert and the judge wants to know whether to admit it — but our actual job is whether the bench guide should say anything about this whole category. Two members want flat language saying this evidence never bears on responsibility and should be excluded as a matter of course; one member, a neuroscientist, says we have no business pronouncing on a live scientific fight and wants either silence or a shrug saying "weigh it like any other expert testimony"; the chair wants a single paragraph that all of us can vote for, which is maybe asking for a miracle.

Draft the memo I'll send round. I need you to actually commit to one of three concrete options — a guide entry with specific cautionary language, a neutral entry that just hands the judge a list of questions to put to the experts, or nothing at all — and to include the proposed bench-guide paragraph itself (say 60 to 120 words, no case or study names in it, since footnotes aren't allowed) that we could vote on verbatim. Then give the reasoning, and please keep it in the weeds rather than at the level of "the science is contested": I want the memo to work through why the inference from that recording to that night is or isn't licensed, which to me means the difference between a trial-averaged waveform and a single-subject claim, the reinterpretation of the readiness potential as what you get when you average backwards from movement onset over a noisy ramping process near threshold, how much weight a clock-reading report of an urge can bear at all, the gap between initiation and inhibition (including that cancellation seems to hit a point of no return only shortly before the movement, roughly where the reported urge sits), the mismatch between an arbitrary self-timed button press and a bar argument unfolding over minutes, the fact that a lab task done fourteen months later is not a measurement of the defendant's state on the night, and whether any of this "fits" the legal question about a voluntary act as opposed to the philosophical question about free will. Also handle the strongest case against whatever you recommend — the neuroscientist member's point that we'd be freezing a snapshot of an unfinished dispute into judicial guidance, and the worry that a guide entry saying "exclude" is really a covert ruling on the metaphysics of free will that most of us don't think the courts should be making.

Bottom line in the first sentence — the recommendation — and then the reasons. No preamble, no restating the question back to me, and flag anywhere you think I'd need to go get a real citation before this circulates.
