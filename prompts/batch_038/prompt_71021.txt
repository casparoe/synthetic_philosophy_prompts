Here's the question, worth 25 marks:

"Question 2. Two strangers, neither of whom speaks the other's language, find themselves both heaving on the same stalled car at a junction, and between them they get it into a lay-by. They never exchange a word and part without acknowledgement. (a) What does a theory of collective intentionality owe us by way of an account of this episode, and what would count as showing that a given theory can't deliver one? (b) Do the two thereby acquire any obligations to one another, and does your answer to that depend on which theory is right?"

And here's the student's answer, in full:

"The case is constructed to make trouble for any account on which acting together requires an exchange of words. If a theory can only get two people into a shared activity by way of an agreement, a promise, or a communicated proposal, then it has to say that these two were not pushing the car together, which seems like the wrong verdict and certainly not a verdict the theory should be proud of. So the first thing a theory owes us is an account of how a plural 'we' can be in place before anyone has said anything.

Searle can supply this most easily. On his view collective intentionality is a biological primitive: the we-intention is a state in an individual head, with a collective content, and it is not to be analysed into an I-intention plus beliefs about what the others intend and believe about what I intend. That reduction is exactly what he rejects, so nothing about mutual belief needs to be established between the two pushers. Each can simply intend that they push the car, against a Background that makes the other's contribution intelligible as part of the same activity. The difficulty, as Searle knows, is that nothing in the shape of his account guarantees that my we-intention and yours hook onto one another rather than onto two merely coincident projects; and he does not, so far as I can see, offer a positive mechanism to close that gap.

Bratman's account is more demanding but more informative. Shared intention is a structure of interlocking individual intentions that we J, together with mutual responsiveness in sub-plan and in action, so that the sub-plans mesh, and with the relevant interdependence and common knowledge. The two pushers qualify to the extent that each is tracking and adjusting to the other's effort, and it is a virtue of the view that this comes in degrees — they may be pushing together more or less fully. It also avoids positing any subject over and above the two of them. But it is a purely descriptive account. Because a shared intention is nothing but a pattern of individual planning states, there is no normativity in it at all, and so nothing that would give either pusher standing to object if the other simply let go.

That is what Gilbert is for. On her view the two are jointly committed, and thereby constitute a plural subject — a group agent in something like the sense List and Pettit argue for, an entity over and above its members whose attitudes need not match those of any of them, which is precisely why the members can be bound by something none of them individually endorsed. The obligation generated by a joint commitment is essentially the obligation you take on when you promise, only a tacit one: it can't be shrugged off unilaterally, and it gives the other party the right to rebuke you. So the answer to (b) is yes, they are obliged to keep pushing for as long as the joint project requires, and one who walks off mid-lay-by is open to criticism.

Interestingly this also cuts against Gilbert's usefulness here. A joint commitment requires that each party express readiness to be jointly committed, and these two never exchanged a word, so on her account there may be no plural subject after all. If that's right, only Searle can handle the spontaneous case, and the answer to (b) becomes no.

One further possibility is worth flagging. It may be that this isn't shared agency at all — two people each doing what they can for their own reasons, in helpful parallel — and a good theory should be able to say so rather than being forced to find a 'we' wherever bodies are coordinated."
