Bit of an odd request. I'm helping run a first-year seminar on the philosophy of social norms and we've just spent a couple of weeks on Lewis's account of convention — the coordination-game picture where a regularity counts as a convention when it's common knowledge that everyone conforms, everyone expects everyone else to conform, everyone's reason for conforming is conditional on the others doing so, and there's some alternative regularity that would have served just as well. The students can recite the conditions but none of them really feel it in their bones, and honestly I suspect I'm in the same boat. So instead of another handout I want to give them a story.

Could you write me one, maybe 1,500–2,000 words, that dramatises all of this without ever naming a philosopher or pausing to lecture about conventions? Here's the shape I had in mind, change the details as you like: a small harbour town on an island (invent both) where for as long as anyone can remember vessels going out take the eastern channel and vessels coming in take the western one. Nobody ever signed anything, and the town is absolutely certain the arrangement was settled by a formal vote of the harbour guild in the 1840s, an event that gets retold every winter with slightly different details each time. A visiting clerk arrives to codify the town's local rules for a new regional maritime authority and finds, in the archive, that no such vote ever happened: the practice grew out of one bad November night when a single boat did something that happened to work and everyone else copied it, and the precedent simply hardened over a generation or two. I'd like the founding-vote legend to still be alive at the end rather than being debunked on the page — that's part of what I want the students to notice.

Then the crunch: a dredging scheme means the two channels have to swap roles, and the town tries to change the rule and mostly fails. I want the failure to feel mechanical rather than tragic. The notice pinned up in the harbourmaster's office isn't enough because half the skippers don't know that the other half know, and the new arrangement isn't conspicuous in any way that would make it an obvious place for everyone to land at once; meanwhile the one skipper who decides to just go first on his own account discovers that deviating unilaterally isn't punished, its simply useless — nobody's cross with him, he just becomes an obstacle. Maybe somebody eventually gets the swap to happen by making it impossible to miss, a bell or a green flag or a light in the window, whatever you fancy, so the story shows what actually does the work. Two extra asks: build in one contrast case, some other regularity in the town that looks superficially like the channel rule but is a completely different kind of thing because nobody's reason for doing it depends on anybody else doing it; and keep the ending slightly unresolved, because the real puzzle is how a first convention ever gets going when there's no prior convention for making them. British English please, and no footnotes or references at all — I'll be photocopying this straight onto A4.
