You are a philosopher of religion with substantial experience teaching graduate seminars on the cosmological argument, and I want a hard-nosed, engineering-style judgment from you rather than a survey of the terrain.

Consider the family of premises that can serve as the load-bearing premise of a cosmological argument. I have labeled six candidates and deliberately arranged them from maximally ambitious to maximally modest:

- **P1 — Unrestricted PSR.** Every true proposition, including negative and modal ones, has an explanation, either in the necessity of its own content or in something distinct from it.
- **P2 — Contingent PSR.** Every contingent fact has an explanation.
- **P3 — Modal (weak) PSR.** For every contingent fact, it is at least possible that it has an explanation.
- **P4 — Aggregate premise.** Every being is either a dependent being or a necessary being; and if every being were dependent, then the fact that there are any dependent beings at all — the existence of the whole collection — would itself stand in need of an explanation that could not be supplied from within the collection.
- **P5 — Kalam causal premise.** Whatever begins to exist has an efficient cause of its existence.
- **P6 — Minimal existential demand.** No general principle at all: rather, the concrete universe as a whole is the sort of thing whose existence calls out for explanation on broadly abductive grounds, even though many lesser things within it do not.

Score each of the six on four axes, 0 through 10, using these weights:

1. **Dialectical availability (0.35).** How readily a sophisticated, non-theistic opponent could grant the premise without thereby conceding anything close to the target conclusion. A premise that is trivially true but grants nothing scores low; so does one that no opponent would ever accept.
2. **Objection-resistance (0.30).** How well it survives the best-known attacks, including the point that explaining each member of an infinite collection seems to leave nothing further to explain, the complaint that a principle invoked only to launch an argument and then abandoned once the desired terminus is reached is doing illicit work, and the objection that physics supplies counterexamples to unrestricted causal or explanatory demands.
3. **Yield (0.25).** How far the premise gets you down the road to a recognizably theistic conclusion — a necessary being is not yet an agent, an agent is not yet a creator, a creator is not yet a personal God.
4. **Independence (0.10).** Whether the premise draws support from cases and intuitions that have nothing to do with the argument it is being used to power.

Work in two stages.

**Stage one, brainstorming.** Before assigning any numbers, list for each premise the two or three objections that in your view actually have a chance of doing real damage. Be specific about the mechanism: for each objection, say whether it defeats the premise, merely forces a narrowing of it, or relocates the difficulty elsewhere and leaves the premise intact. Do not pad this with objections you think fail.

**Stage two, scoring and ranking.** Give a table with the four scores, the weighted composite computed to two decimal places, and one sentence explaining why each premise lands where it does. Then give a tier list — S, A, B, C — ordering all six, and state where your closest call was and what broke the tie.

Then commit. Pick the single premise you would actually build an argument on if you were forced to, and defend that choice in roughly two hundred words, meeting head-on the strongest objection you identified for it in stage one rather than a weaker one. Finally, state whether you think any of the six is strong enough to render a cosmological argument cogent for a philosophically well-equipped naturalist, and give a numerical probability for that verdict.

Do not provide citations, a bibliography, or a reading list; I want your reasoning, not your references. Do not hedge by observing that the ordering depends on one's background metaphysics: select a background in one explicit sentence and rank relative to it. Take a definite position throughout, including on the premises you rate lowest.
