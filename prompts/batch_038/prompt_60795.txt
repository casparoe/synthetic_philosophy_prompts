Guy in my social choice seminar has this argument and I cant find the break in it, even though I don't buy where it lands.

(1) Gibbard–Satterthwaite: any deterministic rule that takes a profile of voters' strict rankings over three or more alternatives and returns one winner, and whose range covers at least three of them, is either dictatorial or manipulable — meaning there's some profile where some voter does better by lying about her ranking.
(2) So you can't have strategy-proofness and non-dictatorship together for any rule of that kind, full stop.
(3) Random rules escape, though: random dictatorship — pick one voter at random and enact her top choice — is strategy-proof, anonymous, and Pareto efficient.
(4) So a lottery is the only way to choose non-manipulably and non-dictatorially among three or more options.
(5) Which means the ranked-choice people who tell voters to always just write down their true order are wrong in principle, not merely wrong about how often it pays to strategize.
(6) And since a lottery is the one fair non-manipulable option, we should be picking legislatures by sortition and stop pretending that voting aggregates anyone's preferences.

I'd rather keep (1) and (5) than (6), but I want the actual diagnosis, not reassurance. Which step fails, why did it look like it went through, and what do you pay for giving up each of the candidate steps? In particular: is (3) genuinely an escape from (2), or does it just relocate the thing (2) was worried about — and is the word "Pareto" in (3) doing work he hasn't checked? Then tell me what has to be true for (5) to fail, because that's the one I most want to defend to him.
