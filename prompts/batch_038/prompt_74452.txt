You're a philosophy professor who's taught the Scottish moralists for a decade and you're grading the essay section of a history-of-ethics midterm. Think carefully about this one, I want a real assessment and not a vibe.

Here's the question I set:

Q. Explain Smith's account of how we come to approve or disapprove of other people's sentiments and conduct, using the notions of sympathy and the impartial spectator. Then (a) distinguish what Smith means by propriety from what he means by merit, and (b) say how his account differs from Hume's on the source of moral approval. ~600 words.

And here's what one student wrote, transcribed as-is:

Smith thinks we approve of other people's sentiments and actions when we can sympathize with them, and sympathy for him just means fellow-feeling. It isn't restricted to pity, so we can sympathize with someone's joy or their anger or their gratitude just as much as with their sorrow. Since I can't literally feel what you feel, the only route I have to judging your reaction is imagination: I put myself in your circumstances and ask what I would feel if the same thing happened to me. If what I'd feel lines up with what you're actually feeling, I approve of it as proper; if it falls short, I disapprove. So moral approval isn't the perception of some property in the act, it's a verdict about the fit between two people's feelings.

The spectator's imagined feeling is always fainter than the real thing it copies, which is why Smith thinks we can never fully enter into the most violent passions, and why our approval tracks how much of the agent's feeling we manage to recover.

Because we grow up among people who are constantly judging us, we learn to see ourselves as they see us, and this builds up into what Smith calls the man within the breast, the impartial spectator. It isn't a literal person and it isn't God, though Smith does bring in religious ideas around it. It's an internalized representative of the standards of the people around us. That's why I think his account ends up conventional: the impartial spectator is a generalization of the actual spectators we've happened to meet, so what counts as proper in one society can be quite different in another, and there's no standpoint outside of that from which to criticize a community's standards. The only check on a corrupted spectator is another spectator.

On (a): propriety is about the motive, whether the agent's affection fits its cause, judged by whether I can enter into it. Merit is about effects on the person acted on, whether the action deserves reward or punishment. Merit needs sympathy running in two directions at once, since I have to feel along with both the agent's motive and the gratitude or resentment of whoever was affected. Smith uses this to explain justice: because we can sympathize with the resentment of someone who's been injured, we approve of punishing the person who injured them, and this is why he thinks the rules of justice have an exactness the other virtues lack.

On (b): Hume also grounds moral approval in sympathy, and he also thinks we have to leave our private perspective and adopt a common point of view so that our judgments can agree with other people's. Smith differs mainly in adding the impartial spectator as a worked-out model of what taking up that general standpoint involves, and in insisting that we approve of motives and not only of outcomes. But underneath they hold the same thing: we approve of the virtues because they're useful or agreeable, either to the person who has them or to other people, and sympathy is just the mechanism by which that usefulness becomes pleasing to us as spectators.
