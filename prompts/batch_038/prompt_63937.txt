You are the grader for the final examination in a second-year philosophy of art course. Below are the examination question and one student's response. Assign a grade out of 100, then supply point-by-point comments in the form they would take on the returned script: what the student has got right, what is wrong or unsupported, and where a conclusion does not follow from the premises offered. Please separate errors of substance from mere infelicities of phrasing, and pay particular attention to any mistake that would not be obvious on a first reading.

**Question.**

(a) State Goodman's distinction between autographic and allographic arts, and explain why the criterion cannot simply be whether the art form yields one object or many.

(b) Explain the role of notation in the account: what a notational scheme has to accomplish, and why compliance with it is supposed to rule out forgery in an allographic art.

(c) A forger produces a highly convincing manuscript of a previously unknown Beethoven string quartet, and a professional ensemble then performs the work from that score. Say precisely what, if anything, has been forged here, and whether the performance is genuine.

**Student's answer.**

(a) An art is autographic when the difference between an original and a forgery is significant, and allographic when it is not. Painting is autographic: even an indistinguishable duplicate of a Vermeer is not thereby genuine, because what makes the work the work is its having come out of Vermeer's studio. Music and literature are allographic: there are countless scores and countless performances of a symphony, and no one of them is more genuine than another. So the criterion is not the number of objects, since a print run also produces many objects; what matters is whether the identity of the work depends on its history of production. In an autographic art it does, and in an allographic art it does not.

(b) An allographic art is one that has a notation. A notational scheme sorts marks into characters that do not overlap and are not so finely divided that one cannot tell which character a given mark falls under, and each character determines exactly one compliance class, so it is never ambiguous which instances belong to the work. Compliance therefore settles genuineness: a performance that gets every notated property right is a genuine instance of the work, whoever conducts it and whenever it is given, and there is nothing left to forge, since a forgery has to pass itself off as the original and in an allographic art every correct instance already is the original. A copied score may be wrong, but a wrong copy is not a forgery, only a defective copy.

(c) In the Beethoven case nothing has been forged in the sense that matters. The quartet is a musical work, music is allographic, and the ensemble's performance complies with the characters in the manuscript, so the performance is a genuine instance of the quartet. The manuscript itself is merely a copy of a score, and by (b) copies of scores cannot be forged. Nor is anything lost artistically: an audience cannot hear any difference between this quartet and an authentic Beethoven, so its aesthetic value is untouched, the notes being what they are regardless of who wrote them. The only defect is a false claim about provenance, which is a matter for historians and dealers rather than for aesthetics.
