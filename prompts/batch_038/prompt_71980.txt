I am preparing the introductory handout for a second-year philosophy of logic seminar, and a colleague's draft contains the following paragraph on Carroll's Tortoise dialogue:

"Carroll's dialogue shows that no deductive argument can ever justify its own conclusion. The Tortoise grants Achilles the two premises of a Euclidean inference, and grants as well the conditional to the effect that if those premises are true the conclusion must be true, yet he still declines to accept the conclusion; each time Achilles supplies a further conditional the Tortoise accepts it and requires another. The series of conditionals demanded has no end, and an infinite supply of reasons is no justification at all, so Carroll has proved that the rules of logic cannot be known to hold but can only be trusted, or followed out of habit. His error, as later commentators observed, was to treat a rule of inference as though it were one premise more: a rule is not the kind of thing that can be added to a stock of assumptions, so the Tortoise's demand is incoherent and the difficulty disappears. What remains is the modest moral that deduction rests on a non-rational leap of faith in our inferential habits."

My suspicion is that this is nearer the truth than my students will assume, but I cannot say where the trouble begins, so I should like an accuracy audit rather than a general essay about the dialogue. Please work through the paragraph claim by claim, distinguishing what a philosopher of logic would endorse, what is a tolerable simplification, and what either misreads the exchange or fails to follow from what has been granted. Two points matter most for the seminar. First: does the final sentence's conclusion follow from anything the paragraph gets right, or does it introduce a sceptical thesis that no step in the argument supports — and is the paragraph correct to treat the Tortoise's predicament as Carroll's own considered view? Second: are the two responses it gestures at, namely that a rule is not a premise and that inference rests on a kind of trust or entitlement rather than on further beliefs, rivals for the same explanatory job or complements, and if the first does dissolve the regress, what is left standing by way of a justification of deduction? Please close with a corrected version of the paragraph, three or four sentences, that I could hand to the students without qualification.
