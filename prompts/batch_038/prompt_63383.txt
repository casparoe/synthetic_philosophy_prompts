The thesis: the Fischer controversy never got resolved and I don't think it can be, because "responsibility" is doing at least three different jobs in it and the two sides have been answering different questions with the same word the entire time. My version of the argument, rough:

- There's causal contribution (what your choices made more likely), culpability (the moral-blame notion, needs something like a guilty mind), and liability (who owes what, which can get assigned by contract or treaty to parties nobody thinks is blameworthy at all). These three come apart cleanly in 1914-1919. The Weimar government accepted the reparations article under formal protest while every German party denied the blame reading of it; the Belgrade officers behind the assassination were culpable in a way that generates no liability for Serbia at all. So you get a little matrix of dissociations, and my claim is that the whole war-guilt literature is an argument about which cell of that matrix is supposed to be load-bearing.
- Article 231 was drafted as a liability instrument — it's the opening article of the reparations section and its job was to cap and ground the bill, not to render a moral verdict. The verdict reading is a Weimar-era political artifact that then got baked into the research program, and Fischer inherited the framing from the people he was arguing against.
- Fischer's actual evidence — the Septemberprogramm, drafted by Riezler in September 1914 after the early victories — supports a claim about wartime expansionism and about a disposition of the decision-making system. It doesn't support the premeditation claim he was read as making, and the standard critic's move, that you can't run the inference backward from September to July, is correct but doesn't touch the dispositional claim. Both sides think they're contradicting each other. Mostly they aren't.
- Constructive upshot: state-level attributions of responsibility for a war should be read as liability claims with a dispositional backstop, not culpability claims. So "who was to blame for 1914" is malformed while "who owed what in 1919" is well-formed and has a defensible answer.
