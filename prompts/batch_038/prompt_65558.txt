I am at work on a novel set in an invented republic, and I need a piece of political scaffolding built to order rather than borrowed. I refuse to write a roman à thèse: a reader must not be able to tell from my chronicle whose side the author is on. So the case has to generate its verdicts from the theories, not smuggle them in through my adjectives.

Build me a chronicle — no more than six hundred words, in the flat register of a year-by-year record of enactments, rulings, and appointments — of a constitutional democracy transformed over roughly eight years by a government that wins every election it contests. Constraints, all of which must hold:

1. No violence, no coup, no state of emergency, no imprisonment of opponents, no shuttering of newspapers, no formal disenfranchisement. Every measure is ordinary legislation or regulation.
2. Every measure is upheld by the republic's constitutional court on the merits, with reasoning a decent lawyer could sign — not merely tolerated, but affirmed.
3. At the close of the chronicle, elections remain genuinely competitive and the incumbent still faces a realistic prospect of defeat.
4. The republic is not a thinly disguised Hungary, Turkey, Poland, or United States: invent its institutions, its geography of media ownership, its civil-service traditions.
5. At least one measure plausibly remedies a real abuse committed by the previous administration, so that an unsympathetic reader can say the critics are sore losers.

Then show that the design works. State the verdict the chronicle earns under each of three accounts: a procedural minimalist, for whom nothing counts as backsliding while elections stay free and fair and power can still change hands; a polyarchy account in Dahl's sense, where the dimensions are public contestation and inclusiveness of participation; and a retrogression account of the kind Huq and Ginsburg describe as constitutional retrogression, or Scheppele as autocratic legalism. The three must not converge — I want at least one to come out clean and at least one to come out condemning — and for each divergence I want you to name the single institutional feature of my chronicle that is doing the work.

Next, a removal test: identify the one sentence of the chronicle which, if deleted, collapses the disagreement between the accounts, and say why that sentence rather than another carries the weight. Then a control: give me a variant of two or three sentences in which the identical measures are enacted by a government that loses the next election and hands over power, and tell me whether the diagnosis should change — that is, whether backsliding is a property of individual acts, of sequences, or of outcomes.

Finally, and honestly: does the objection that this is nothing but a partisan label survive your case? If it survives, tell me what I would have to add to the chronicle to defeat it; if it does not, say what in the case earns the diagnosis independently of who is narrating. No bibliography, no reading list, and no real-world parallels beyond the frameworks I have named.
