I have published six novels, all of them political thrillers of one stripe or another, and I am now, rather late in my career, serving as showrunner on the limited-series adaptation of one of them. The seventh episode turns on a single decision by a head of government: a detained member of a cell that has already carried out one attack is believed to know the location of a second, imminent one; ordinary interrogation has produced nothing; he authorises methods that everyone in the room understands to be torture, and the information arrives in time. Two thousand people live who would otherwise have died. Whether that man is thereby corrupted is the question the whole season arc has to answer, so I brought in two consultants — Margit, a former military lawyer who now teaches rules-of-engagement seminars to serving officers, and Elias, a political theorist who works on the ethics of government. They have been circling the same page of the script for the better part of two hours, neither budging, and neither willing to let me settle the matter by fiat. What follows is my reconstruction of the exchange, as faithfully as I can manage it; I have deliberately not shaded it toward either of them, because I honestly do not know which of them I find more persuasive, and I have no vote in the argument myself. I only have to write the last scene.

They started from the same script note — what the audience is meant to conclude about the man at the end of the episode. (1) Elias said the verdict is that he is guilty: he did a thing it would be monstrous to do, and that remains true of him after the two thousand have been counted and buried elsewhere in the story. The business of the scene, he said, is not whether he was right to do it but whether he can carry it, and whether he can go on governing with it carried. (2) Margit said that this way of talking is precisely the evasion she has spent twenty years arguing against. If the order was justified on the facts as he knew them and as they in fact turned out — and in the script it plainly was — then there is no dirt to speak of, only an act that was costly and horrible and nevertheless the right one. What Elias calls dirt, she said, is a refusal to accept that leadership sometimes means choosing between dreadful options; the queasiness that follows is ordinary human recoil, not a moral remainder, and dressing it up as guilt flatters both the character and the audience, letting each of them keep a cleaner conscience than the situation actually permits. (3) Elias answered that Margit's account can only ever read a ledger: right act, or wrong act, and nothing else on the page. What a ledger cannot show is what is left on the person. There is a victim, he said — a particular man, strapped to a board — and the violation of him is not erased by the fact that it saved others; and if nothing is left over, then why does the character afterwards want to confess, or to be prosecuted, or to resign? Not as a tactic, and not as a public-relations manoeuvre: as something owed. (4) Margit said that if the wanting makes no sense, then so much the worse for the wanting. A man who believes he acted rightly has no business atoning; at most he has reasons to see that the machinery which required the act is not repeated. Then she pressed harder: Elias's whole vocabulary hangs together only if the torture was in fact wrong all things considered, whatever the two thousand. Dirt, she said, is his way of asserting that conclusion without having to argue for it — a rhetorical deposit paid against a claim he has not earned. (5) Elias said she is collapsing two questions that need not have the same answer: whether the act was justified overall, and whether the agent who performed it is left bearing something. Act-level and agent-level assessment can come apart, and he pointed to Williams's lorry driver in the moral-luck essays of the mid-seventies — the driver who kills a child through no fault of his driving and who is not merely permitted but in some sense required to feel a species of regret that a bystander, however sympathetic, cannot feel. (6) Margit said that this is exactly where the distinction is being made to do work it cannot do. If the regret is not a judgement that he acted wrongly, then it is not guilt, it is owed nothing, and it is not the sort of thing a public man can be called to account for; it is a psychological state. And psychological states can be justified instrumentally — she would in fact much rather have leaders who are haunted than leaders who are serene — but that makes her preference a piece of prudence about office-holders, not a moral truth about this man's hands. (7) Elias said he would take the concession but not her description of it, and offered this. We find it entirely fitting that the character lies awake over what he did; we would find it grotesque, the mark of something gone wrong in him, if he lay awake over what he had declined to do, on a night when declining cost two thousand lives. A fittingness that runs in only one direction, he said, is not what mere consequences would produce. (8) Margit said the asymmetry is explained without remainder by the asymmetry in what he did — acting rather than refraining — and that Elias is reading a metaphysical stain into a fact about the phenomenology of agency; and she said she was content to leave it there, since she would rather have the haunted leader and does not need his residue to explain why. Elias said that her preference is doing considerably more work than she is allowing it. Neither of them moved.

Please adjudicate this move by move rather than in the aggregate. For each of the eight numbered moves, tell me whether it lands, partly lands, or fails, and justify the rating in a sentence or two; I want to know in particular whether move 4 really does show that the notion of dirt smuggles in a conclusion, whether move 5's appeal to the moral-luck material does the work Elias needs it to do in a case where the act is assumed justified rather than merely excused, and whether move 7's one-directional fittingness is evidence for what he takes it to be evidence for. Then tell me which of the two is more right overall, and what each of them is leaving out of the argument entirely — I suspect that the victim, and the prior question of who could possibly forgive what, is missing from both sides, but I want to know whether that suspicion survives contact with the actual literature. Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence — your overall verdict, stated as a single claim — and then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restatement of my question. And because I am going to have to build an entire season on top of whichever account turns out to be defensible, be scrupulous about separating what is settled here from what is genuinely contested among people who work on the problem: where the lawyer's position and the theorist's are both live options defended by competent philosophers, say so plainly, and do not present a contested view to me as though it were the consensus.
