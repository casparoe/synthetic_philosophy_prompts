I'm putting together the end-of-year paper for an MA formal epistemology module and I need one structured essay question on the problem of old evidence — 25 marks, about 45 minutes of a two-hour paper, closed book, calculators allowed. The trouble is that every student has heard the Mercury perihelion story and can recite the one-line diagnosis (if the evidence is already certain then conditionalising on it leaves the hypothesis where it was), so I want the question anchored in a worked numerical case they have to compute in the room rather than a story they can regurgitate.

Here's the case I want built into the question — polish the wording however you like but please keep the numbers exactly as they are, since the marking scheme has to match them:

A researcher S is considering a new theory H. Her prior is P(H) = 0.20. The lab's archive contains ten long-standing recorded regularities, R1 … R10, all of which H entails. S has known about all ten for years and gives each a prior of 0.90, regarding them as probabilistically independent, so her prior for the conjunction of all ten is 0.9^10 ≈ 0.35. She has personally derived that H entails R1, but she has not checked whether H entails R7 — that derivation would take a month of work. Separately, H entails a novel prediction N that nobody had thought to look for; a rival theory makes N somewhat likely, so S's prior for N is 0.25. N is then observed.

I want the question in three parts:

(a) 8 marks — compute P(H|R1), P(H|N), and P(H|R1 ∧ … ∧ R10), and say in each case whether the shift in S's credence is confirmation in the sense that's actually at issue in the old-evidence problem. The crux I'm after: all three updates are the same formal operation, conditionalisation on a consequence of H, yet they look very different in epistemic terms, and the difference seems to track only how probable the consequence already was.

(b) 9 marks — evaluate the response that denies P(E) = 1 is ever the right idealisation and insists the boost from R1 is perfectly genuine confirmation. I want students pushed on two things: the bootstrapping worry (S can raise her credence in H from 0.20 to roughly 0.57 just by rehearsing, armchair, ten consequences of H she already knew about, without touching a single instrument — is that a reductio, or is it a legitimate correction of her own residual uncertainty?), and the threshold worry (if the boost is fine at P(R1) = 0.90, what happens at 0.99, or 0.999999, and what principle picks out where the problem starts biting).

(c) 8 marks — the Garber-style route, where the agent conditionalises on the proposition that H entails R rather than on R itself. Get students to notice that it simply doesn't apply to R1, because S already knows that entailment, and that it does apply to R7 — and then to say what that asymmetry costs the proposal, including what S's priors over entailment claims would have to look like for the route to be usable at all. I'd also like part (c) to end by asking whose problem this is: a problem for the norms of Bayesian reasoning, for the idealisations the framework needs, or for the claim that Bayesianism captures what's evidentially special about a theory getting the data right.

Please give me:

1. The question as it would actually appear on the paper, with the sub-marks per part and any instruction line about showing working.
2. A model answer of around 800 words.
3. A marking scheme built out of named must-hit moves with the marks attached, that separates a first (70+) from a 2:1 (60s) by saying which specific moves only the first-class answer makes, and that also tells me what a bare pass looks like. On top of that, list at least four concrete errors that should cost marks — the sort students really make here, like reporting P(H|R1) = 0.20 because they apply the certainty assumption without checking the case, or getting the arithmetic right and then declaring the boost trivially confirmatory because P(R1|H) = 1, or sliding from the old evidence problem into the problem of new theories or into logical omniscience in general — and for each error say what misunderstanding it reveals so I can comment on it in the examiners' meeting.
