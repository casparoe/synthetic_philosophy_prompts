I am finalizing a position paper for a workshop on machine consciousness, and a colleague has urged me to have the underlying commitments audited for coherence before I circulate it. What follows is the complete list of premises the argument rests on, in the order they appear. I would like to know whether they can all be held at once.

1. The access/phenomenal distinction is substantive: for a content to be conscious of, and for that content to be available to the subject for reasoning, verbal report, and the rational guidance of action, are separable conditions, and they can come apart in either direction.

2. The evidence for separability in the "phenomenal but not accessible" direction comes from partial-report experiments of the Sperling type: above-chance forced choices about items a subject did not report indicate more visual phenomenology than the subject can access. I take seriously the standard reply that much of this performance is post-perceptual guessing off a degraded memory trace, but I maintain that a residue of genuine overflow survives that reply.

3. Global workspace theory, on its neuronal reading, is the best-supported account of what makes a state conscious: a content becomes conscious in virtue of being broadcast to a set of specialized processors that can thereafter exploit it flexibly. I treat broadcasting as both necessary and sufficient.

4. Consciousness is graded rather than switched on and off, and there are real partial or borderline cases — light anesthesia, drowsy driving, some nonhuman animals, some artificial systems.

5. What matters morally in a system is whether there is something it is like to be that system. Access-consciousness unaccompanied by phenomenology would have no moral weight whatsoever.

6. I am not an identity theorist about the two. I hold it to be at least metaphysically possible for a system to duplicate every functional and dispositional feature of broadcasting while lacking phenomenology, and this possibility does real work in my argument rather than sitting there as a concession.

7. I nonetheless propose an operational test, and I claim it is decision-relevant for policy: if a candidate system's states become available, in a discrete and all-or-none fashion, to its reporting module, its planning module, and its action-selection module, that constitutes good evidence that the system is conscious of those states. I want to defend this test as an adequate basis for deciding whether a deployed system merits protections.

The paper also contains a case I use to motivate (2) and (7) together. Consider a system with a high-capacity sensory register whose contents are never broadcast, but which are nevertheless readable by a secondary module capable of producing above-chance two-alternative guesses about them. I write that in respect of those archived contents there is something it is like for the system, and I also write that this fact should make no difference to the system's moral standing.

Please audit the set. I am not asking for a defense of any individual claim, for background on the debate, or for a survey of positions I have not listed; and I have no prior about which item I ought to abandon — I would rather find out. Specifically: identify which pairs genuinely pull against one another and which merely appear to once the questions they answer are kept distinct; identify anywhere the verdict I reach on my own case outruns the principles I have stated; rank the tensions by severity instead of presenting them as a flat list; and for each, state the smallest revision that removes it together with what that revision costs me elsewhere in the paper. Finish by naming the single change that would clear the most damage, and say whether the paper's central practical claim can survive that change.
