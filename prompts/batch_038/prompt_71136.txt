I am drafting the design specification for the evidence-gathering policy of an autonomous diagnostic assistant, and I have hit a conceptual snag that I want settled before any further engineering is done on it. The system can either act on what it already knows, or pay first to learn something: the payment may be money for a laboratory assay, latency while a tool call returns, or user goodwill each time it asks a clarifying question, since a user who has fielded six questions answers the seventh less helpfully. Several colleagues keep invoking the classical result — usually credited to Good — that for a Bayesian agent who updates by conditionalisation over a fixed act set, the expected value of free information, assessed before the observation, is never negative; they treat it as a licence to make the system as inquisitive as its budget allows. I do not think the result travels to our setting unchanged, and I would like the requirements stated properly before I write down what the policy must do.

So please write the desiderata: what any adequate principle governing when an agent should buy evidence has to deliver, in order to be usable in a system like ours, with the motivating case given for each requirement. Treat the following as raw material rather than as settled — I expect some of these to be redundant, some to be mis-stated, and some to turn out to be the prejudices of one camp rather than genuine demands on any account. (i) It must state exactly which agents the non-negativity result covers, and say what remains when the assumptions fail: in particular when the agent foresees that a downstream module will over-weight an alarming test result, so that looking makes the eventual action worse. (ii) It must handle observations that are not evidence-neutral but interventions — our assay warms the component whose failure it predicts, so the post-observation credence reflects a world the looking itself helped create. (iii) It must accommodate cases where the information changes the utilities of the outcomes directly, not merely the beliefs about them, as when the news is itself the harm. (iv) It must not be myopic: where a question now consumes goodwill that is worth more later, the account must price the option to ask, and must say whether the principle governs a single look or a whole inquiry policy. (v) It must handle evidence that undermines the agent's confidence in its own updating, and say whether it is bound to respect Reflection or must explain which of that principle's assumptions is failing. (vi) It must respect the weakness of the original claim — non-negativity, not positivity — so that it does not smuggle in a standing obligation to gather all available evidence, or a prohibition on rationally declining a free look. (vii) It must say something about strategic settings, where asking leaks information to the party asked, and must tell me whether demanding that the principle survive there is a real requirement or a decision-analysis habit. (viii) It must distinguish the ex ante verdict from the agent's ex post position, so that regretting a look one was right to take is not counted against the principle.

Having laid the list out, mark which requirements are non-negotiable for a usable policy and which are negotiable, and — this is the part I most want — identify any subset that cannot be jointly satisfied. My own suspicion is that (i), (iv), (viii) and some version of "the verdict must be computable from the agent's current evidence alone" cannot all be had at once, and if you agree, say which must go and what the loss costs us in practice. Close by naming the single requirement that eliminates the most candidate accounts, and say briefly how three of them fare against the full list: a plain expected-value-of-information calculus applied decision by decision, a sequential account that evaluates whole inquiry policies, and a causal rather than evidential treatment of observation. Please write in British English, and do not give me a bibliography or a reading list — the specification is what I need.
