ok so me and three friends run this little rationality reading group at the library on saturdays, and this week we got completely stuck on the Judy Benjamin thing — the one where the recruit in basic training gets told a conditional probability by a sergeant and then everyone argues about what her odds of the company being in the north should be afterwards. Our group split down the middle and by the end we couldnt even agree on what the question was, which was pretty annoying. My theory is that half the arguing is just people reacting to the army framing (nobody in our group has any intuitions about platoons or barracks, so the numbers feel like homework), so I want to rebuild the whole case from scratch in a setting where the odds feel like something a real person would actually have.

Here's what I want you to build. One thought experiment, and the setup should be a single paragraph at most — everyday and realistic, so no cards, no dice, no urns with colored balls, no military, no time travel or weird tech. Seed it something like this if you want, or improve on it: a friend of yours moves to a city you've never visited, the city is cut by a river into an uptown half and a downtown half, each half is cut by a main road into an east side and a west side, so there's four neighborhoods and you start out at 25% on each because you genuinely know nothing. Then somebody who could plausibly have the information — a clerk at the post office, a mutual friend who glimpsed the address on an envelope but wont say more, whoever you think holds up best — tells you a conditional probability about where your friend ended up. The conditional they announce has to be different from what you'd have said that conditional was before you heard it, or nothing at all happens and the case is worthless. The source also has to be someone whose saying it is real evidence rather than noise, because I want the disagreement to be about *how to update*, not about whether to trust the person.

Then the demonstration, because that's the part I actually need for the group. Give me the posterior over all four neighborhoods under each of three ways of taking the announcement, and show the arithmetic, not just the answers: (1) treat the conditional as a material conditional — basically "not-uptown or east-uptown" — and conditionalize on that; (2) hold your probability of the antecedent fixed and just redistribute inside it, the Jeffrey-style move; (3) the newer split-the-difference move, where hearing the conditional is supposed to nudge your probability of the antecedent in the same direction as the shift it makes to your conditional probability, by some fraction η of that shift — pick an η strictly between 0 and 1 and tell me which one and why. All three have to give a different number for the chance your friend is uptown, and they have to fall out of the rules rather than being reverse-engineered to look different. Also check each posterior against the thing the source actually said — does it still respect the announced conditional, or does one of the rules blow up a whole neighborhood to zero probability and thereby overshoot? After that: one line on which single feature of the setup is doing all the work, what happens to the three answers if you delete it, and how a defender of the material-conditional rule would attack my case instead of accepting the verdict it gets. Scenario first so I can paste it in the group chat and let people react before they see any math, keep the notation light, and dont give me a bibliography or a list of stuff to read — just the reasoning, since we wont be able to look any of it up during the session.
