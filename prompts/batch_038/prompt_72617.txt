ok so my roommate and I got into a two hour argument last night about Kavka's toxin puzzle and we got nowhere. He's convinced the whole setup proves you can never genuinely intend something you know you won't follow through on — that the million dollars is unearnable in principle. I kept saying that's overreading it, that people form shaky intentions all the time (every January 1st, basically) and that the interesting question is whether the billionaire's deal is actually impossible or just really hard, and whether it matters which. Neither of us budged. So now I want to settle it by comissioning a document instead of arguing about it more.

Write me the official rules-and-FAQ packet for the billionaire's prize. Standard setup, roughly: a vial of toxin that'll make you painfully ill for a day but won't kill you or leave anything lasting, a million dollars that lands in your account tomorrow morning if at midnight tonight you intend to drink the stuff tomorrow afternoon, no obligation to actually drink it, and you're free to change your mind afterwards. I don't care whether you verify the midnight intention with a brain scanner or a panel of notaries — figuring out what counts as verification is part of what the document has to do. Present it as the billionaire's own legal-and-marketing packet: numbered clauses plus a short FAQ, around 700-850 words total, in that cheerful but airtight voice of an organization whose lawyers have looked at everything and are extremely pleased with themselves. Give him a name and a foundation. The target is the practice of trying to purchase intentions with contracts, and contest legalese as a genre — not any real person.

The humor has to come from what the fine print quietly decides about the philosophy, not from the billionaire being a wacky eccentric. So the clauses should actually take positions, and the positions should be the contested ones: what counts as a verified intention and who adjudicates the borderline cases (does intending-to-intend qualify? a hypnotist? a signed note to yourself? a Ulysses-style self-binding device? someone who just happens to enjoy a day of being mildly ill?); whether the prize is clawed back if the winner drinks for the wrong reason or drinks by accident; a special section for applicants who claim to be "the kind of person who keeps their resolutions" and what documentation that requires; the tax treatment of a million dollars received for a state of mind rather than a service or a performance; the liability waiver covering the sick day; a clause about people who already know they'll change their mind and want to know if they should bother applying; and one clause near the end that inadvertently concedes the entire puzzle — the foundation admitting it has never actually paid out and isn't sure anyone has ever qualified. Keep it deadpan throughout, no winking, no mid-piece author's notes explaining the joke, and write it in American English.

Then after the piece, drop out of character for maybe 150 words and tell me where you took liberties. Specifically: which of your clauses quietly resolve something that's still a live fight among people who work on this (my guess is that the resolute-choice move and the question of whether you can intend what you believe you won't do are both genuinely open, but correct me if that's wrong), and be explicit about what's settled versus what's contested — I don't want the satire smuggling one side of a real debate in as if it were the consensus, and I'd rather you flag it than have my roommate find it first.
