I'm a third-year PhD student in philosophy working on moral responsibility, and I'm drafting a short paper for a graduate workshop on free will and the luck problem. My target is Galen Strawson's Basic Argument, as laid out in his 1994 *Philosophical Studies* paper "The Impossibility of Moral Responsibility": roughly, that true moral responsibility requires being the ultimate source of one's own character, which requires a chain of choices about how one is that can terminate only in something nobody can be, namely self-creation. In our reading group the reflexive response is that the argument begs the question, but my supervisor presses a subtler objection, which I find much more interesting: that the argument may be *inert*. On her view, whatever intuitive pull it has comes entirely from a premise about constitutive luck that hard determinists, luck skeptics, and compatibilists all already accept, so that the distinctively Strawsonian requirement — responsibility for the way one is, cashed out as a regress of self-forming choices — never does any independent explanatory work. I want to test that suspicion with a designed case rather than with more exegesis, and I'd like you to build the case for me.

Here are the specifications. Construct a matched pair of scenarios, each no more than about a hundred and fifty words, involving two agents who perform the same act, foresee the same harm to the same kind of victim, and are matched on age, occupation, awareness of what they are doing, availability of alternatives, and — this is the important one — the strength and character of their occurrent endorsement of the act at the moment of performance. The single difference between them is historical. In the first scenario, the agent deliberately cultivated, over many years, the trait from which the act issues: there were episodes of choice to become that sort of person, made for reasons that themselves issued from a character the agent did not choose. In the second, the agent arrives at a disposition that is, so far as any observer or any test could tell, indistinguishable from the first agent's, but by drift and circumstance — no episode of endorsement, no program of self-cultivation, just accumulation. Keep it realistic: an ordinary professional setting (a department chair, a landlord, a clinic supervisor, a fund manager — your choice), and no manipulation cases, no neurosurgery or lesions, no amnesia or personality-erasure technology, no brainwashing, no demons, no perfect predictors. Do not stipulate the verdicts anywhere in the scenarios; the difference in responsibility, if there is one, has to be generated by the histories you describe rather than asserted by them.

Once the scenarios are in place, do the demonstration. For each case and each of three positions — the Basic Argument; a constitutive-luck skeptic who accepts that nobody is responsible for the way they are but denies that Strawson's regress premise adds anything; and a diachronic compatibilist who grounds responsibility in guidance control plus a history of self-shaping rather than in ultimate origination — say what the verdict is and which premise or principle delivers it. Then give me the payoff: report whether the verdicts actually come apart between the two agents under Strawson's own account, and, if they do not, state precisely which step of the argument has been left idle and whether that vindicates my supervisor's inertness objection or whether Strawson has a reply available. Next, run one variation: add a further layer to the first agent's history such that the program of self-cultivation was itself undertaken under pressure from a standing anxiety the agent never endorsed, and tell me whether the first agent's verdict changes and what that shows about whether looping self-shaping can ever halt the regress as opposed to merely relocating it. Finally, be honest about the design: identify which feature of the case is load-bearing, what happens to the result if that feature is removed, and how a defender of the Basic Argument would attack the construction — in particular, whether it smuggles in a self that stands outside character altogether, or whether it trades on ordinary attributability in a way that simply does not engage the notion of responsibility Strawson says he is arguing against. If you think some pair of my constraints cannot be jointly satisfied — for instance, that occurrent endorsement cannot be held exactly fixed while the histories differ — say so plainly, tell me which constraint to relax, and give the closest workable approximation. I don't need a bibliography; I have the literature. What I need is the case and the proof that it works.
