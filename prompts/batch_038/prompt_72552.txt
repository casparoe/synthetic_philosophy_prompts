I teach a master's-level seminar on evidence, probability, and the philosophy of science, and next week's slot is a single 100-minute session with sixteen students. They have all done an introductory statistics course, can apply Bayes' theorem without hand-holding, and most have met the Monty Hall problem and some version of the Sleeping Beauty puzzle, though nothing anthropic. The room has movable tables, two whiteboards, and a document camera; I have about twenty-five euros for consumables; phones and laptops are banned during the session. The week's reading is on how you should reason about your own place in a larger population. The two positions I want them to run into are, in my own gloss rather than the jargon: first, the view that says your being an observer at all is not itself evidence about how many observers there are, so you should reason as if you were a random sample drawn from whoever exists, and your particular position within that population then tells you things; and second, the rival view that says your very existence *is* evidence, and strongly favors worlds containing more observers, because you were much more likely to arise in a crowded universe than in a sparse one. I want them to feel where these diverge — before they learn anything about their own position, after they learn it, and when someone offers them a way to settle the matter empirically for a price. The last of those is the one I most want them to taste: the second view, pushed to its conclusion, tells you that a costly experiment distinguishing a cosmology with a vast number of observers from one with a modest number is not worth running, because you should already be overwhelmingly confident in the vast one. That conclusion is supposed to feel scandalous in the room, and it should feel scandalous because their own token balances punish them for it, not because I assert it from the front.

Here is the rough shape I have in mind, which you are welcome to improve on or replace. Somewhere behind a screen, one of two "worlds" is selected at random by a procedure the students themselves execute and can later inspect: a small world in which the total population is on the order of a hundred and sixty observers, and a large world in which it is on the order of a hundred times that. Each student then receives a sealed ticket bearing a number representing their position in the population, drawn in a way appropriate to whichever world is actual. There are three betting stations, each settled in tokens that convert at the end into something small and grade-neutral: a bet placed before the tickets are opened, a bet placed after, and a bet on whether to purchase the results of a hypothetical instrument that would directly reveal which world is actual, priced so that it is clearly worth buying on one theory and clearly not on the other. Two problems you must solve explicitly rather than paper over. One: there are sixteen people in the room but the worlds differ by two orders of magnitude in population, so tell me how you want to handle that mismatch and convince me your handling does not quietly load the evidence in one direction. Two: the randomization has to be auditable, because the moment students suspect the facilitator chose the world with an intention in view, the session turns into a puzzle about my psychology rather than about the reasoning; give me a procedure, an auditor role, and a chain of custody for the sealed envelope that survives a hostile student asking afterwards exactly how it was done.

What I need from you: the complete rules and a materials list; a token economy with concrete stakes, odds, and a conversion rate that requires no real money; a minute-by-minute timeline for the hundred minutes including a break; and a debrief that maps each moment of play onto the assumption that generated it, identifies the exact step at which the two views first come apart, and shows where each step could be resisted and what it costs to resist it there. The names of the two assumptions and of the philosopher who refuses the experiment should not appear until the debrief, and there should be no lecture before play begins. Please also predict honestly what will go wrong the first time I run it and how I should handle each failure — I expect at least problems with students pooling information against the rules, with the ticket-holders in the small world realizing what is happening early and tipping off the rest, with tokens feeling worthless so that people gamble wildly, and with someone refusing to bet at all and freezing the round. Finally, tell me plainly which feature of the real problem this activity cannot reproduce and that I will therefore have to supply in words: the absence of any known chance mechanism behind our actual situation, the question of who belongs in the reference class, the fact that participants in a game are volunteers selected for a philosophy class and know it, and the fact that no one gets to play twice. I would rather have a design that admits its limits than a polished one that pretends the game is the argument.
