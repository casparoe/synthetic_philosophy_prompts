I'm in the final weeks of a bioethics seminar. Our last unit was on advance directives, and we spent most of it on the Dworkin line: that a directive written while competent expresses critical interests which survive the loss of competence, so that ignoring it sacrifices the patient who matters to the patient who remains. For the paper I've been permitted to write on a film, provided I engage the arguments rather than recount the plot. I'd like a reading of Christopher Nolan's *Memento* through this debate over whether an earlier self can bind a later self who no longer remembers, and no longer much resembles, the author of the instruction.

Here is the angle I want developed, because I don't think it's the obvious one. The standard objection to precedent autonomy — call it the changed-person objection — says that once the person who wrote the directive is gone there is no one whose autonomy it serves, only a differently-interested survivor who should not be sacrificed to a stranger's paperwork. *Memento* does something the literature almost never does: it compresses the interval between issuing an instruction and acting on it from years to minutes. Leonard's tattoos, annotated photographs, and notes are directives from a self five minutes dead, and each successive Leonard inherits them with no memory of having chosen them. I want you to work out whether the compression matters. If the changed-person objection gets no purchase across twenty minutes, then what is it really objecting to — psychological change, or the later self's loss of access to the reasons behind the choice? And if it does get purchase across twenty minutes, does that collapse the usual framing of the dementia cases, where the interval's length is doing more work than anyone admits?

Second, Leonard is not merely the recipient of his own directives; he is their author in bad faith. He burns evidence, mislabels a photograph, and deliberately sets himself a target he knows to be false on the explicit assumption that his future self will take the record at face value — and Natalie performs the same operation from outside, writing annotations she knows he will later accept as his own settled judgments. Please treat this as a problem distinct from authority: call it the provenance problem. A directive's force seems to depend on the integrity of the channel through which it reaches the self who executes it, and the film shows that channel being corrupted both from within and from without. Does *Memento* imply that precedent autonomy requires something like a secure record — and is that a condition actual directive practice, with its photocopies, family amendments, and decades of drift, could ever meet?

Third, the form. The colour sequences run backwards, so the audience watches effects before reasons, which is roughly the epistemic position of a surrogate acting on a document whose grounds they never witnessed. Tell me whether the structure makes an argument the story does not, or whether I'm over-reading it.

Where the reading strains, say so rather than smoothing it over: Leonard is fully competent between resets, which may make this a case about weakness of will and self-manipulation across time rather than about advance directives at all, and I'd rather hear that argued against me than conceded cheaply. Close by stating what the film contributes to the debate that argument alone cannot supply.

Please describe scenes in your own words — I'm not asking for and don't want verbatim quotations of dialogue, and no bibliography is needed. Keep the whole thing under 900 words, and open with two sentences giving your bottom line on whether *Memento* vindicates or undercuts precedent autonomy.
