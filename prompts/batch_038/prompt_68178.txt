I spent some forty years in nuclear structure physics and have turned to philosophy only since retiring, in part to understand an argument I heard rehearsed endlessly in seminars and never once seen settled. I am writing about the standing dispute between scientific realists and those who hold that our theories need only be empirically adequate, or that talk of their truth is misplaced. My interest is not in who wins. It is in why nobody has won, and in what sort of disagreement this actually turns out to be once one looks at how the two camps argue rather than at what they conclude.

As I understand the positions: the anti-realist presses a historical induction, noting that theories which were enormously successful in their own day — phlogiston, caloric, the luminiferous ether, the humours, the vital force — were later discarded along with the entities they posited, so that predictive success is poor evidence of truth and we should expect the same fate for our own best theories. The realist replies that this mis-describes theory change, since in the paradigm cases the mathematically load-bearing content was retained as a limiting case of its successor and only the superfluous apparatus was shed; and that the reliability of novel prediction would be inexplicable, a standing miracle, unless our theories were at least approximately latching onto something real. Round and round this goes, and I have never seen either party concede so much as a premise.

Two things from my own trade make me suspect that the philosophers' casebook is misleading them, and I would like your judgment on whether they matter.

First, the nucleus was for decades described by two pictures that were presented as rivals and were nothing of the sort: a charged droplet of incompressible quantum fluid, which gave binding energies to well within a percent across the chart of nuclides and made fission intelligible, and a set of independent nucleons moving in orbitals within a mean field, which alone accounted for the magic numbers, for spins and parities, and for magnetic moments. Each was superb exactly where the other was silent. They were eventually absorbed into a single treatment of deformed nuclei with rotational and vibrational spectra, and both survive today as calculational instruments. Neither was refuted. That is not the pattern the induction from discarded theories anticipates, but neither is it the realist's story of a successor recovering a predecessor in a limit.

Second, our everyday workhorse for nucleon scattering employed a complex potential whose imaginary part was frankly absorptive: taken literally, it did not conserve probability, because flux lost into non-elastic channels was simply removed by hand. Every practitioner knew this and none of us was troubled by it. It worked beautifully. Its success cannot be a miracle about truth, since we did not believe it literally true; yet describing it as merely empirically adequate seems to me to under-report how much of its structure we relied upon and reasoned from.

My hypothesis, offered as a hypothesis, is that the debate is stuck because both sides treat two quantities as monolithic and well-defined when they are neither: what counts as one theory, and what counts as its success. Success in my field was always indexed — to a question, an energy scale, and a tolerance. If success is indexed in that way, then so are the notions of what was preserved and what was discarded, and the historical induction silently depends on a coarse-graining operation, a mapping of an old theory's content onto its successor's, that the anti-realist performs without argument and the realist disputes. On this reading the substantive disagreement concerns the identity conditions and grain of theoretical content, not evidence and not the meaning of truth.

So: is that diagnosis right? I would ask you to set out each side's position as its partisans actually state it, in your own words — and please no bibliography, no citations, no recommended reading; I have no means of checking them and would rather you committed to a characterization than hedged with references. Then tell me where the two lines of argument fail to make contact. Evaluate my hypothesis on its merits: is the point about grain a genuine fault line, or a redescription of the familiar realist reply about limiting cases and approximate truth? Does it explain the absence of movement, or merely relocate the stalemate into a quarrel about how to carve up content? Consider also whether my two cases can be absorbed without cost — the anti-realist might say the complex potential was never a candidate for belief in the first place, the realist might say the droplet and orbital pictures were never true rivals — and what each camp would forfeit by absorbing them that way.

Finally, tell me what would count as progress. Could the quantity on which the induction depends be made precise at all: a measure of how much of a theory's content survives in its successor, or a criterion for when two frameworks are rivals rather than complements, definable without choices so arbitrary that they settle the dispute by stipulation? If you think such a formalization is impossible in principle, say why, and say whether the impasse then signals an ill-posed question or merely a hard one. And give me your honest view of one further suspicion of mine: that both camps are arguing over a distinction between belief-worthy and merely useful theory that working physicists never in fact operate with, and that this is either an important datum for the diagnosis or a prejudice of my trade.
