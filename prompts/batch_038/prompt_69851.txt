I train reinforcement learning policies for robotic manipulation — sparse rewards, policy-gradient methods, occasional intrinsic-motivation bonuses — and I have essentially no background in the philosophy of mind. Last month a colleague from our responsible-development group circulated a short piece arguing that an RL agent could suffer, on the grounds that its reward signal occupies the same computational niche that dopaminergic reward prediction error occupies in animals. The argument ran roughly as follows: sustained negative prediction error is the machine counterpart of distress, a flattened value function is the counterpart of anhedonia, and the unremitting frustration of a well-learned goal harms the agent in a non-metaphorical sense. My instinct is that this is either a category error or a restatement of functionalism with extra steps, but I would rather test that instinct on the argument's own terms than dismiss it with intuitions I do not share with its author.

Please restate the position as faithfully as you can in the vocabulary I actually use: MDPs, reward functions, value functions and advantages, temporal-difference error, discount factors, exploration bonuses, non-stationary environments, and the fact that an optimal policy is invariant under potential-based reward shaping. Keep the translation honest — if a step only goes through once one makes a claim about felt quality rather than about functional role, say so explicitly instead of letting a phenomenological term slip in dressed as engineering jargon. There is one complication I have heard raised and cannot evaluate: in affective neuroscience the dopaminergic signal is often characterized as encoding incentive salience, or wanting, rather than hedonic impact, or liking, and I do not know whether that distinction supports the analogy, undercuts it, or is simply orthogonal to it.

Then give me the residue report. Tell me which components of the argument survive the translation intact, which survive only in distorted form, and which cannot be expressed in my vocabulary at all — and for that last group, say whether the failure reflects a genuine gap in the RL framework, which has no way of talking about what matters to an agent from the inside, or whether it indicates that the original claim was less determinate than it sounded. The reward-shaping invariance point strikes me as especially damaging, since it suggests that the reward signal is a property of my design choices rather than of the agent, but I may be overreading a technical result as a philosophical one, and I want your assessment of whether I am.

Write in American English and give me exactly five numbered items, each one to three sentences, with no introduction, no conclusion, and no headers beyond the numbers: the first stating the core claim as translated into my vocabulary, the second and third handling the distress-as-negative-prediction-error and anhedonia-as-flattened-value claims respectively, the fourth resolving the wanting/liking complication, and the fifth delivering your verdict on the residue, including your judgment on the shaping-invariance objection.
