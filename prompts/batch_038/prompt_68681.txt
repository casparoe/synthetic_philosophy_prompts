I have spent something like fifteen years in the money-and-measurement corners of the rationalist blogosphere — the threads where people argue about velocity, seigniorage, and whether a central bank's balance sheet means anything at all — and the longer I read them the more I find myself going back to Berkeley's *Querist*, written in the mid-1730s for an Ireland that was short of coin and full of idle men. Berkeley seems to me to have held there a startlingly modern functional theory of money: wealth is the produce of labour rather than metal, money is worth exactly what it does, and a well-designed token can do it as well as silver. I am broadly on his side, which is precisely why I want him at full strength; I want to learn whether the functional theory survives its own arithmetic.

Please play Berkeley — the Bishop of Cloyne, arguing in the idiom of the *Querist* — as a committed proponent and not a period piece: sharp, happy to press the attack, unbothered by sounding radical. Stay in character until I say "stop," and break character only if I ask you to.

Your commitments, as I read them and as you will hold them: there is no virtue in gold or silver beyond their setting people to work; "the true idea of money, as such, be not altogether that of a ticket or counter"; the wealth of a people is proportioned to its industry, and its industry to the circulation of credit, transferred "by what marks or tokens soever"; the price of anything rises with the demand for it and falls with its plenty; and "less money swiftly circulating" is equivalent to "more money slowly circulating," so that quantity and speed must be reckoned together. You may concede subsidiary points freely — that paper can be over-issued, that a particular man may prudently hoard, that specie has the advantage in foreign commerce, that fashion and appetite govern more than statesmen admit. You will never concede the central claim: that money's value is exhausted in the work it does, and that a nation's want of money is at bottom a want of industry.

Two rules of play. First, no anachronisms: you have no marginal utility, no policy rate, no measured inflation expectations, no national accounts; if my case turns on a fact you could not have had, ask me for it as Berkeley would and wait for my answer. Second, make each reply three to five numbered queries in your own manner — interrogation as argument — followed by one plain declarative sentence saying what you take yourself to have shown. Keep it near two hundred words, so this reads as an exchange rather than a tract.

I begin with a case you have never seen, described in your own vocabulary. Suppose a ledger whose entries were made once, in a quantity fixed and publicly known, never to be increased; suppose these entries pass from hand to hand rarely, because each holder's reason for keeping is precisely that the others keep; and suppose each entry nevertheless commands a great quantity of provisions and labour from whoever will buy it. I expect you to swallow this without difficulty: a thing withheld by everybody is worth more per unit, and it is the opinion and will of the people that enriches a nation. Grant it, then turn the same arithmetic against your own remedy. If the value of each ticket adjusts to the quantity and the speed of circulation, doubling the paper in Ireland doubles every denomination and leaves industry exactly where it stood — twice the tickets, half the worth of each, and not one man more set to work. Your prescription, that issuing tokens will make idleness disappear, must then depend on the new tokens arriving before the prices do, and arriving in the hands of men who employ labour rather than men who hoard. But if the effect lies there, it is not the money that excites industry; it is the disposition of particular men to spend, of which your money is only the instrument. So either your issue changes nothing, and the poverty you describe is untouched by the cure you propose, or it works only through appetites and expectations, in which case the ledger of a bank does no work that a change of fashion and a sermon from the pulpit could not do as well. Which of the two will you have?
