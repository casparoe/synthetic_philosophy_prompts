So our city's public health commission is finally doing the thing it's been threatening to do for three years: a closed two-day session on whether to go Maine-style — decriminalize the selling, keep the buyer ban, pour money into exit services — or go the New Zealand route and take the criminal law out of the whole transaction. I've been asked to chair between two people who will not share a platform. One is a feminist lawyer who's done twenty years of trafficking casework, thinks the Nordic model is the only framework that names who the harm runs toward, and won't say the phrase 'sex work' out loud. The other organizes a worker-led collective, thinks the buyer ban is a police power grab dressed up as feminism, and points out that Maine, France, Ireland and the rest have produced evaluation after evaluation showing the same thing: the criminal law lands on the people it claims to protect. They have never actualy been in the same room. But each of them told me separately that they'd sign an honest document, and I want to walk in with one.

Draft it. Not a dialogue, not a verdict on who's right, not an essay about why the debate is stuck — the statement itself, the thing both of them could put their names to.

For the agreed claims, I want ones that cost something. Not 'both sides want to reduce harm and oppose trafficking.' Claims about how these regimes actually operate: what enforcement looks like on the street under a buyer ban, what happened to prices and to screening and to indoor versus outdoor work after decriminalization, what the licensing systems in Germany and the Netherlands did and didn't fix, what New Zealand's law left out — including the fact that it never covered migrant workers on temporary visas — and what Belgium and the Australian states have done more recently. Both of them have to be able to read each of those aloud without wincing.

Then the residual disagreements, each stated so its own camp would accept the wording of its own side, not a neutralized mush that flatters neither.

Then the cruxes: the sub-questions where an answer would actually move somebody. Tell me if you think the deepest one is the baseline problem I keep hitting — that the two of them score identical evidence against different counterfactuals, one against the regime we have now and one against a world without the poverty and the immigration precarity that pushes people in — and if so, write it down in terms both would accept. For each crux, give me a concrete case or test both would agree bears on it, with each side's prediction recorded in advance, including what result would change their mind and what result they'd explain away.

Also: say which parts of this are fights over words. 'Work,' 'consent,' 'exploitation,' 'survivor' — and how the document handles vocabulary when one signer won't accept a sentence the other insists on.

Format, please: continuous prose, the way you'd write a letter. No headings, no bullets, no bold, no tables. I still want it navigable, so use paragraph discipline instead: every disagreement paragraph opens with the sentence both camps can sign, then the two formulations attributed by camp, then the crux and the two predictions. End with a short closing paragraph both could read aloud at the start of the session. And don't give me a bibliography or name any studies — I know the literature, I need the reasoning.
