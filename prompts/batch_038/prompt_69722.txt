So our consciousness reading group turned into a two-person war last Thursday and it's since moved to email, which is worse. It's between a postdoc who's friendly to global workspace stuff and a fourth-year student who isn't. I'm the bystander who got cc'd. Here's the exchange as close to faithfully as I can manage.

The pilot they were fighting about is ours. A shape flashes for about 30ms, then a pattern mask. Subjects first guess which of four shapes they saw, then separately press a button saying whether they experienced seeing anything at all, then rate confidence 1 to 6. On roughly 15% of trials people guessed right (72% accuracy on those trials) while pressing "saw nothing" and rating confidence 1 or 2 — and on those very trials the EEG showed the late, widespread fronto-parietal deflection that workspace people treat as the ignition signature.

(1) The postdoc said this is a win for the theory, not an embarrassment: ignition happened, so the content was globally available, and global availability is what it consists in for the system to be conscious of the shape. The subject pressing "saw nothing" is just a metacognitive failure on thier part — the same kind of thing as someone being confidently wrong about why they picked what they picked. Nothing is left over.

(2) She added that the student kept collapsing two different theories. Baars's version is an architectural claim — a broadcast from a workspace to specialist processors — and doesn't by itself predict any EEG component at all. The neural version adds the ignition claim and the late signature. The pilot only bears on the second one.

(3) The student said that's a retreat that buys safety with content: whenever the data misbehave you fall back on the architectural version, which is silent about the data, and whenever they behave you invoke the neural version. She also said the neural version absorbs every possible outcome — report plus ignition confirms it, report without ignition means the recording missed it, ignition without report means the report is broken — so it isn't testable in principle.

(4) The postdoc said that's a tu quoque, and it lands harder on the student. If the student holds that there's phenomenal character over and above access, then facing this data she can either say the subject had a faint experience they couldn't report — which makes report unreliable, exactly the move she objected to in (3) — or say there was no experience and the guessing was blindsight relabeled, which then owes an account of why the ignition signature showed up.

(5) The student said the two positions aren't symmetric because hers takes a risk. Her prediction: put actual money on it, let subjects wager on their own guess, and wagering will dissociate from the confidence rating — they'll bet like they know while insisting they don't. She said if that comes out false she'll drop the view.

(6) The postdoc said that's her prediction too. On the criterion-shift story, the low confidence comes from the demand of the yes/no experience button in a task that feels impossible; remove that demand and betting should climb in step with accuracy. So both views are on the hook for the same experiment, there's no asymmetry, and what the student is really doing is treating report as evidence when it favors her and as noise when it doesn't.

(7) The student said fine, but notice what just happened: every prediction on the table — button presses, bets, discriminations, EEG traces — is a prediction about what the system does with the information. Whichever of them wins, the argument has been conducted entirely inside access. So the question of whether there's anything it's like gets settled by ruling out the only kind of evidence that could bear on it.

(8) The postdoc said that's the point, not a complaint. Everything the student can cite as evidence for the extra ingredient is itself an access fact. So the extra ingredient isn't unfalsifiable, it's inert: there's no measurement, actual or conceivable, whose outcome it changes. And two views that agree on every possible observation are one view in two vocabularies.

One thing neither of them mentioned, which I only noticed afterward going through the data: the discrepant trials werent spread across the sample. Almost all of them came from two of the eleven participants, and those same two also gave themselves the lowest confidence on trials where they were accurate and did say they saw the shape. Everyone else's confidence tracked accuracy pretty normally.

Can you adjudicate this move by move? For each of (1) through (8), rate it — clearly wrong, partly right, clearly right — and say why in a sentence or two. I'm especially interested in (3) and (4): is the unfalsifiability charge real, and is the tu quoque actually even? Then tell me whether the thing I noticed at the end should change the score on any of the moves, and which. And finally, is there something both of them are missing — a third reading of the data, or a distinction they're both running together — that would have cut the argument short?
