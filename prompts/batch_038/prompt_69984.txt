I am a graduate student in a seminar on the philosophy of punishment that has just spent two weeks on an unexpected detour through atonement theory. The detour was prompted by David Lewis's paper *Do We Believe in Penal Substitution?*, which several of us found far more interesting than the seminar's assigned reading on deterrence and desert. Our instructor's framing question for the unit is whether the coherence of the doctrine matters at all to those who hold it, and my own reaction after two readings has been that Lewis gives away so much in the course of defending the doctrine that the defense may not be worth having. My supervisor disagrees; he thinks the paper is really a piece of legal philosophy wearing theological clothing, and that its interest lies in what it says about vicarious liability rather than about the cross. One of my classmates has taken the opposite lesson, namely that if Lewis is right we ought to be far more relaxed about letting people serve one another's sentences.

So that you can work without the text in front of you, here is the structure of the argument as I understand it. The traditional objection to penal substitution is retributivist in shape: punishment is essentially the infliction of a hardship on the guilty for their offense, guilt is not transferable, and so what a substitute undergoes is not punishment at all but merely a costly event that happens to resemble one. Lewis resists the premise by pointing to ordinary legal practice. Fines are penalties, and yet nothing in the practice turns on who actually hands over the money; a friend, a relative, an insurer, or an employer may discharge it, and the law is indifferent. Vicarious liability runs in the same direction: a principal answers for an agent, a firm absorbs the penalty for an employee's conduct, a community can be made to bear costs generated by its members. If penalties of this sort are already transferable in our secular practice, then the claim that a penalty must fall on the guilty looks less like a conceptual truth about punishment than like a contested normative principle we have quietly abandoned. Lewis is also willing to concede that what the substitute bears may not be punishment in the strict sense; the substitution can be legitimate even where the hardship is a stand-in for the penalty rather than the penalty itself. His conclusion, as I read it, is that the doctrine is coherent but not cost-free: accepting it requires surrendering a principle about guilt and desert that we thought we held, and the surrender cannot be confined to the theological case, since whatever makes substitution acceptable before God would seem to make it acceptable before a court.

I would like you to write the response paper I have been circling for a week, arguing for the following thesis: Lewis's argument succeeds only by weakening the notion of punishment to the point where it can no longer do the work the doctrine needs, so that the paper is best read not as a vindication of penal substitution but as an inadvertent reductio of it. The core of the argument, as I see it, is a dilemma. Either the substitute bears something that genuinely satisfies the offender's desert, in which case Lewis owes an account of how desert-annulling condemnation attaches to a person other than the offender, and his appeal to fines does not supply one, since a fine's transferability depends on its fungibility and on the fact that what a fine does is impose a cost rather than express a judgment about a particular agent. Or the substitute merely bears a heavy cost that the community agrees to accept in place of the penalty, in which case penal substitution has collapsed into something like an extremely expensive ransom or compensation scheme, which is precisely what the retributivist objector always said the doctrine really was. Please develop that dilemma rather than merely stating it, and please engage the two strongest replies available to Lewis. The first is the reply that fines and imprisonment are disanalogous in a way that helps rather than hurts him, because the relevant point is only that a penalty can be borne by another when the community authorizes the substitution, and authorization is exactly what the divine case supplies. The second is the reply that the doctrine never needed the word punishment to be doing strict retributive work, and that all it requires is that the substitute voluntarily undergo the divinely appointed consequence of the offense in the offender's place. Say explicitly what, on your account, is left of the doctrine once your argument goes through, and whether what is left is worth calling penal substitution. I have the paper in front of me, so do not trouble yourself with quotation, citation, or bibliography, and do not spend more than a paragraph or so reconstructing Lewis's own view before pressing the objection. Aim for roughly twelve to fifteen hundred words, signpost the stages of the argument, and treat Lewis as a philosopher who saw the difficulty clearly rather than as an apologist who missed it.
