The question:

"Explain what environmental historians mean when they call pathogens, volcanoes, or climate fluctuations historical actors, and how that claim differs from environmental determinism. Use McNeill's account of epidemic depopulation after 1492 as your example. Finally, say what is specifically at stake — historiographically or politically — if the two claims get collapsed into each other."

The answer:

"McNeill's Plagues and Peoples treats infectious disease as a force that has shaped history at least as much as war or politics. His clearest case is the Americas after 1492: pathogens like smallpox and measles, against which Indigenous populations had little prior exposure or immunity, killed huge proportions of the population, which is what made conquest possible for a few hundred Spaniards. So germs plainly have historical agency — they did things no human planned, and their effects changed what humans could then do.

The key point is that agency and determinism are not the same thing. Environmental determinism is the claim that the environment matters a great deal for how societies turn out, that geography and climate set the range of possibilities. Nobody serious denies that today. What McNeill adds is that germs are not just part of the setting, they are participants: they respond to what we do. When we crowd into cities, open trade routes, or overuse antibiotics, the microbes adapt, and the result is not what anybody intended. That two-way responsiveness is what makes them actors rather than backdrop. Braudel saw this before McNeill did — the longue durée is exactly the argument that climate and geography act in history, only slowly — which is why McNeill belongs in the same tradition.

The danger of determinism, then, is not that it gives nature too much weight but that it forgets the feedback. A determinist says the climate caused the famine; McNeill says the famine, the trade routes and the pathogen made each other. Keep the loop open and calling a bacterium an actor isn't a category mistake, it's just the accurate description. What should be assessed is whether the causal loop is represented, not whether the actor has a mind."
