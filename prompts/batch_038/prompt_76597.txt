I lead a small platform team, and last year we built an internal allocation tool that ranks proposed engineering projects by a crude expected-value score: a probability-of-success estimate multiplied by an estimated benefit, both entered by the proposing team. Nothing philosophical about it — it was meant to replace a shared spreadsheet. Two things have since gone wrong in ways I cannot debug on my own. First, teams have learned to game the benefit field: one proposal claims a one-in-10^12 chance of preventing a class of outage that would affect 10^18 user-sessions, and because the tool simply multiplies the two numbers, that proposal now outranks a 0.9-probability project worth 10^6. Second, both fields are IEEE 754 double-precision floats, so probabilities below roughly 5 × 10^-324 underflow to exactly zero and products above roughly 1.8 × 10^308 overflow to infinity; the ranking therefore depends on representation in a way that looks arbitrary from the outside but is invisible in the interface. A colleague who reads a great deal of effective-altruism material insists the tool is behaving correctly and that the problem is my instinct. On his view, once you accept expected value as the criterion, declining to fund the one-in-10^12 project is irrational, and any probability floor I might impose is an unprincipled fudge dressed up as prudence; he treats this conclusion — which he calls fanaticism — as something to embrace rather than as a reductio. I have no philosophy training, and I got through my one stochastic processes course by memorising formulas, so I genuinely cannot tell whether he is right and my discomfort is squeamishness about small numbers, or whether there is a substantive objection he is papering over.

Here is what I would like from you. Take this proposition: expected-value fanaticism is true, in the sense that whenever an agent chooses between options with well-defined probability and utility distributions, the rational option is the one with the higher expected value — even when the entire advantage comes from outcomes whose probability is smaller than anything the agent could ever have calibrated, and even when imposing a probability floor would reverse the ranking. Give me your actual credence in that proposition as a number, and show me the arithmetic by which you arrived at it. Name the sub-questions the credence depends on — for example, whether such probabilities are precise enough to be real numbers rather than intervals or orders of magnitude; whether utilities can be unbounded without generating St Petersburg-type breakdowns in the very calculation being appealed to; whether aggregating across persons and across time in the total fashion the proposition assumes is correct; whether a decision rule counts as rational if an adversary can always defeat it by naming a larger prize; and whether the representation problem I described is merely an implementation artefact or a symptom of something wrong with the criterion itself. Assign each sub-question a rough probability with a single sentence of justification, and then combine them into the headline figure. Finally, tell me which one sub-question the headline number is most sensitive to — where a modest revision of your estimate would move it furthest, and roughly by how much.

Allocate the items as follows: item 1, your restatement of the proposition in the sharpest form you are willing to attach a number to, including any restriction you think is needed to make it well-posed; items 2 to 5, four sub-questions, each with your probability for it and one sentence of justification; item 6, the combined credence together with the combination rule you used; item 7, the sensitivity analysis plus the single argument or piece of evidence that would most change your mind.
