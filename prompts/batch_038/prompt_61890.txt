I have just taken a seat on a state sentencing review commission. We do not hear individual appeals; we issue reasoned advisory opinions that the legislature and the parole board treat as guidance, and over roughly two decades those opinions have hardened into something like a body of precedent. Before my first vote I would like a bench memo telling me what principle the commission is in fact committed to, because I have come to suspect that its published opinions are consistent with more than one principle, and that this quarter's case falls squarely in the gap between them.

The opinions to date, in summary:

1. Armed robbery by a nineteen-year-old acting under sustained coercion by an older associate. The commission recommended a suspended sentence and intensive community supervision in place of the custodial term sought, on the stated ground that the response should be calibrated to what the offender could reasonably have been expected to do in the circumstances he actually faced, and that a term matching that of an undistressed offender would misdescribe his conduct.

2. Impulsive violence caused by a diagnosed frontal-lobe tumour, excised after the offence, which the commission accepted as having rendered the impulse irresistible. It held that the offender is not a proper object of blame, and that he may nonetheless be held for as long as he presents a danger to others, under conditions no more restrictive than safety requires, with treatment offered but not coerced.

3. A serious assault committed twenty years earlier by a man now in advanced dementia. The commission recommended suspension of the remainder of the sentence and transfer to care, but declined to vacate the finding of guilt, reasoning that the finding records what he did and that its accuracy is unaffected by his present inability to engage with it.

4. A large fiduciary fraud by an offender presenting no physical danger, where an occupational bar and full disgorgement would reliably prevent repetition. The commission refused a custodial term, holding that where a less restrictive measure reliably prevents the harm, custody cannot be justified by the gravity of the past conduct alone.

5. A persistent offender for whom a well-validated instrument projected a long period of dangerousness, but whose record showed three episodes of sincere and temporarily successful reform, each ending when he was returned to the same housing and employment conditions. The commission set the recommended period of incapacitation below the instrument's projection, on the ground that a portion of the projected risk was attributable to failures for which the state rather than the offender bore responsibility.

6. An offender assessed as no longer dangerous who had never acknowledged any wrongdoing and continued to describe the victim as having invited the attack. The commission made completion of a structured restorative process a condition of release, describing that process as necessary both for the community's confidence and for the offender's own understanding of what he had done.

7. An offender who petitioned for reduction on the basis of sustained, self-initiated rehabilitation in custody: literacy teaching to other prisoners and a change of disposition verified over nine years. The commission granted the reduction, writing that the sentence should reflect the work the offender had done on himself and not merely the risk he continued to present.

The case now before us. The offender was convicted of a grave violent offence and has served eleven years of a term for which the commission's own proportionality guidance indicates fifteen. Three independent assessments, using instruments we have previously accepted, converge on negligible risk of reoffending. He has no cognitive impairment; he understands exactly what he did and why it was wrong; and our evaluator reports that his acknowledgment of the harm is complete, uncoerced, and accompanied by restitution the victim's family has asked to have recognised but not continued. At the time of the offence he was, on every account before us, fully reasons-responsive: he deliberated, weighed alternatives, and chose. He asks to be released. The prosecution asks that he serve the remaining four years, on two grounds — that releasing him now would communicate that the gravity of the offence has been discounted, and that nothing in his conduct at the time reduced what was owed. The family has stated that it does not object to his release but does object to any characterisation of the offence as fully answered for.

What I want from the memo:

(a) Two or three candidate principles, each stated precisely enough to be applied to a novel case without further interpretation, that are jointly consistent with all seven opinions. For each candidate, identify where it strains: which opinion it accommodates only awkwardly, and what gloss the commission would have to place on that opinion for the fit to hold.

(b) For each candidate principle, what it implies about the case now before us — release, release under conditions, or continued detention — and, where it implies detention, how the length of the remainder is determined rather than merely permitted.

(c) A recommended ruling, together with the earlier opinion that is the closest analog and an account of why it is closer than the alternatives. I expect opinions 3 and 6 to pull in opposite directions here; say which controls and why.

(d) A candid statement of what the commission takes on by ruling either way. If we release, are we committed to the proposition that nothing beyond the prevention of harm can ever justify an imposition on an offender? If we detain, are we committed to a backward-looking element that opinions 2, 4 and 5 appear to have disclaimed? Note in each case which of our prior opinions would become unstable, and whether any would have to be quietly re-read.

(e) One further hypothetical case, not on our docket, that would force a choice between the candidate principles — a case in which they genuinely diverge rather than differ in emphasis — and an explanation of why it discriminates. Explain also what the commission should do if it prefers never to face that case, and whether declining to face it is itself a substantive position.

(f) A separate short section on opinion 7. Our reasons there either credited the offender with something he himself did or merely registered that his risk had fallen. Those are different commitments, and I do not think the commission noticed which it was making. Say which reading of opinion 7 coheres better with the rest of the record, and what turns on that reading for the case now before us.

I am aware that there is a standing philosophical dispute between those who hold that a thoroughly naturalised account of agency leaves behind a reduced but genuine form of desert, and those who hold that it leaves none, and that our opinions are usually read as belonging to the second camp. Please do not attempt to settle that dispute in the abstract. What I need is which camp our own record puts us in, whether the record permits us to remain uncommitted, and what it would cost us to state a commitment explicitly in the next opinion we publish.

No citations, no references to the secondary literature, and no invented cases or scholarship; where the memo needs to invoke a position, describe the position and its reasoning directly. The only language you should put in quotation marks is that of the commission itself, as reported above — in particular the word "alone" in opinion 4 and the phrase "the work the offender had done on himself" in opinion 7, both of which I take to be doing more work than the drafters intended. Aim for roughly 1,500 words, organised under the headings above.
