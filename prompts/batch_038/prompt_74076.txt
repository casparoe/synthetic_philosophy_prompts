Forty years in a physical chemistry lab, retired three years now, and I've been reading philosophy on my own since — badly, I suspect. Right now I'm working through the Hart–Devlin argument over whether the law may enforce morals: the Wolfenden business, Devlin's lecture claiming a society has the right to punish immorality because a shared moral code is what holds the society together, and Hart pushing back.

I'm stuck on a conceptual point, not a historical one, so please explain the ideas rather than telling me who said what and when.

In physics we're trained to keep apart two claims that sound identical in ordinary speech. One is constitutive: take away the lattice and it isn't a crystal any more — nothing has to happen, the identity claim does all the work. The other is causal: remove the catalyst and, as an empirical matter, the reaction stalls. Devlin looks to me like he slides between exactly those two. When he says a society just *is* its shared morality, that's constitutive. When he says unpunished immorality will bring the whole thing down, that's a causal prediction, and one that looks testable and largely false.

So:

1. Is that constitutive/causal split actually the fault line here, or am I imposing a physicist's categories on an argument that runs on different ones? If it is the fault line, spell out what each of the two theses would have to say for Devlin's conclusion to follow from it — the law may criminalize conduct that is merely immoral and harmless to anyone — and where each version gives out. I'm particularly interested in whether the constitutive version is even in the running, or whether it can only license preserving *a* morality rather than *this* one.

2. Related: is the disintegration claim a threshold claim or a gradual one? A threshold says a society tolerates some amount of dissent from its code and then abruptly loses coherence; gradual erosion says it just frays a bit at a time. Those are different hypotheses with quite different legal consequences, and I can't tell which one Devlin actually needs.

3. Hart's counter is often glossed as: if your reason for enforcing morality is to prevent collapse, then you're not a legal moralist at all, you're a harm-principle person with a shaky empirical premise, since collapse is itself a harm. Please explain the conceptual difference between punishing conduct because its wrongness is what justifies the coercion, and punishing it because of further damage it does to something else we care about — and say how much disagreement survives once that line is drawn properly.

4. Where does Dworkin's objection sit? My memory is that he's attacking something different: whose morality gets counted, and whether disgust at a practice is any evidence about it. Is that just Hart in different clothes, or a seperate move?

5. What I most want to understand: what would a present-day legal moralist — someone who genuinely thinks the wrongness of an act is itself a reason to criminalize it — say differently from Devlin? The two get lumped together in my reading and I suspect they're structurally different positions.

One last thing, and please take it seriously rather than as boilerplate. I'm trying to work out how much of this is still live and how much is museum. Separate, as best you can, what's settled among people who actually work on this from what's genuinely contested, and don't hand me a contested view dressed up as the consensus. My hunch is that nearly everyone thinks Devlin lost the argument as he made it, but that the underlying idea — that a political community may protect things it values for non-instrumental reasons — is not remotely dead. Tell me if that hunch is wrong or too glib, and flag which parts of your answer are consensus, which are one side of a live disagreement, and which are you extrapolating.
