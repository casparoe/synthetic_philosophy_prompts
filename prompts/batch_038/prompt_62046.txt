So I'm the newest member of the naming committee at a small atrocity-documentation outfit — six of us, we vote on which words actually go into the annual report, and most years it's uneventful. Our record over the last decade, roughly: the Maravik file, where interior ministry orders to "clear" a religious minority's towns were recovered and about a third of the group died — we called it genocide, no argument. The Okessa lowlands, where there was no state plan at all, just settler militias picking off scattered members of an indigenous group over thirty years for farmland, with the state fully aware and doing nothing — we called it genocide anyway, over two members' objections. The Sarn schools file, where a state removed most of a minority's children to majority-language boarding schools, banned the language in public life and split up families, with almost nobody killed — we called it genocide, on the strength of the Convention's clause about forcibly transferring children plus Lemkin's much broader idea that a coordinated attack on a group's institutions and economic life is the thing itself, not a lesser version of it. The Vess purge, where a regime wiped out an organized political opposition — teachers, union people, party cadres, tens of thousands of them — and we declined, saying the protected groups in the treaty are national, ethnic, racial and religious, and a political movement isn't one of them. The Dala steppe famine, hundreds of thousands dead and heavily concentrated in one ethnic group, but the paperwork showed the state cared about grain quotas and not about the group — we declined, called it a mass atrocity. And the Corvin massacre, a single clearly ethnically-motivated killing of about two thousand people out of a group of three hundred thousand — we declined, on the grounds that the part destroyed wasn't substantial enough to touch the group's survival.

Thursday we have to rule on the Kirvan valley file and I think I'm the swing vote. A state fighting a separatist insurgency has relocated something like forty percent of the villages of an ethnic group into fenced "protected hamlets," shut their religious schools, barred their language in courts, and demolished maybe thirty shrines and a monastery. Aid convoys have been blocked for months and the documented deaths so far are around eleven or twelve percent of the group's population, almost all of it children and old people from disease and hunger — there have been very few direct executions. Two ministers have said on tape that the group "won't be here in a generation," and an army radio broadcast told villagers to forget their tongue. But here's the wrinkle: households that registered as loyal and supplied militia recruits were exempted from relocation, and their shrines are untouched. The state insists it's fighting armed groups, the diaspora is large and thriving, and the group's total numbers outside the valley are stable. Our resident former prosecutor keeps reading out Article II of the Convention — acts "committed with intent to destroy, in whole or in part, a national, ethnical, racial or religious group, as such," including "deliberately inflicting on the group conditions of life calculated to bring about its physical destruction in whole or in part" — and says the tape plus the blockade settles it. Our historian says the loyalty exemptions prove the selection criterion was allegiance, not identity, so it's Vess all over again. Worth knowing: a chunk of our funding comes from a government that's friendly with the state in the valley, which is not supposed to enter the room but does.

Can you tell me what we should decide, and give me something I can actually say on Thursday? Specifically: which of our six precedents is the closest analog, what should the report call this — genocide, crimes against humanity, ethnic cleansing, something hedged — and what are the candidate rules our track record is even compatible with? I suspect at least two of them fit every past ruling we've made and then come apart right here, so I'd like you to name those rules, say what each one predicts for Kirvan, and tell me which you'd bet on and why. Then be blunt about what principle we'd be committing ourselves to with either verdict — like, if we go with the loyalty-exemption reasoning, does that quietly undercut Okessa, and if we go structural does Vess become unlivable? Also, one extra fact or one hypothetical future file that would let me tell the competing rules apart before we've locked anything in. Please write the whole thing in American English, and don't bother with a source list or footnotes, I just need the reasoning tight enough that two very stubborn people can't pick it apart.
