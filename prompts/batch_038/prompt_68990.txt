I run a small evening reading group on blame and responsibility and next week's session keeps collapsing into everyone agreeing, because the two things we read both argue that hypocrisy does something real to your standing. So instead of a third paper I want to hand people a dialogue that stages the disagreement properly. Can you write it? Here's the setup I've cooked up — feel free to adjust details but keep the bones.

It's about 9pm in the parking lot of a community college, right after a committee meeting. The committee has voted to formally censure an instructor, Dale, for padding his own publication record with a graduate student's data and then trashing the student when she raised it. Renee, who chairs the committee, wrote the censure language and wants to sign it under her own name and read it aloud at the faculty meeting. Omar, also on the committee and also her friend of twenty years, objects — not to the censure, but to Renee being its face. He knows something almost nobody else does: eleven years ago, at another institution, Renee did a version of the same thing, got away with it, and never made it right with the person involved. So he thinks she's forfeited the standing to be the one who delivers this; she thinks thats a load of nonsense, that the truth of the charge is what matters and her own past is a separate problem to be dealt with separately. Write it as an actual argument, not two alternating speeches — each of them should be pressing on the move the other just made. Neither should come out looking stupid.

For balance, here's the ammunition each of them should get. Renee's side: if the messenger's sins can veto the message, you hand every wrongdoer a get-out-of-blame card, and the tu quoque has been a named fallacy for a long time for good reasons — so she should push Omar on whether he's really saying the censure becomes *inapt*, or just saying he'd rather someone else signed it. She should also hit him with the case where the dirty-hands person is the only one whose word will actually stop the harm (imagine Dale only ever respected Renee, or only she has the institutional clout), and with the self-blame problem: Renee does blame herself about eleven years ago and thinks that self-blame is exactly right, so how can standing be something you lose if you still plainly have it over yourself? Omar's side: blame isn't a report, it's a demand addressed to someone in the name of a standard you're invoking, and you can't invoke a standard you're currently violating without hollowing out the demand — so he should push Renee on the secret-hypocrisy dilemma (if nobody ever finds out, does the status of her blame change? if yes, standing starts looking like a kind of magic that operates at a distance from anyone's knowledge; if no, then standing was never about her authority but about how an audience receives the speech). He should also make her say whether standing can be earned back, and if it can, whether the thing doing the work in earning it back is just repentance and restitution — in which case what he's been calling lost standing is really unpaid debt, and Dale gets a complaint that has to be weighed rather than a veto. You can use or ignore this wrinkle: the grad student whose data was padded has said she doesn't care one way or the other who signs, which Omar reads as evidence that the standing business is entirely between Renee and Dale and has nothing to do with the victim.

I'd like it to end without a winner but with a located crux — the moment they both notice that what they're actually fighting about is what blame is (a demand made second-personally by someone who has to meet its terms, versus a true representation of a wrong that anybody can voice), not Renee's past at all. Along the way it should also surface whether hypocrisy forfeits standing outright, suspends it, or just generates a genuine counter-complaint. Somewhere around 900–1200 words, some heat but they're old friends and they're both tired, and please let them interrupt each other and pick at each other's phrasing rather than delivering tidy paragraphs.
