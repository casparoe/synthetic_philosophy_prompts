I teach a non-majors survey on the history of the physical sciences, and every year the same thing comes up in discussion section and I never have a good answer for it. We look at cases where a claim begins life as a genuinely surprising empirical finding — contested, supported by experiments, defended against people who thought it absurd — and then, a generation or two later, denying it stops sounding like a substantive disagreement and starts sounding like misuse of a word. Water is H₂O; whales are mammals rather than fish; the cladistic point that the animals we call fish do not form a single natural group, so that a strict biologist can say there is no such thing as a fish. What strikes me is how these claims get defended once they settle. When a student produces an apparent counterexample — a clear, drinkable, lake-filling liquid that turns out to have a different composition, or some newly catalogued body out past Neptune that looks planet-like — the response is never to treat that as evidence against the claim. The response is to reassign the case: then it isn't water, then it isn't a planet. The claim itself has become the instrument we use to sort the cases, so nothing that turns up can count against it. And yet the discovery story stays in the textbook. The evidence is still recited, the controversy is still narrated, and the claim is still presented as something the world forced on us rather than something we decided. That combination is what I can't name: practically immune to revision, officially still empirical.

It's obviously adjacent to the August 2006 decision by the astronomical union to adopt a definition of planet that demoted Pluto, but I want to keep those two apart, and I don't know how. In the Pluto case people reasonably complained that a committee changed the meaning of a word by vote, that the discovery of a ninth planet hadn't been shown to be wrong so much as re-labelled. Nobody says anything like that about water. There the narrowing of the term feels like the discovery finally being understood rather than being tampered with. My students' instinct is to say that both are just the no-true-Scotsman move — counterexample appears, term gets tightened, claim survives unscathed — and I want to know whether that is actually right, or whether the folk name for the fallacy is obscuring a real distinction between a legitimate tightening and a dodge. If there's a legitimate version, what makes it legitimate, given that the mechanical structure of the response looks identical in both: the predicate gets narrowed at exactly the point where the recalcitrant case appears?

So: is there an established concept, or a small cluster of them, for this migration of a claim from the empirical side of the ledger to the definitional side without anyone announcing that a redefinition has occurred? I'd like to know what the nearest analyses are, how each of them differs from the thing I've described, and which one you'd actually reach for — and if the answer is that nothing established covers it, then coin something, with a definition sharp enough that I could tell my students what would and wouldn't count as a case. Please treat the carving as the deliverable rather than the label: I want the boundary conditions, an account of why the discovery narrative can survive the acquisition of immunity, and a workable test for separating the legitimate narrowing from the Scotsman-style one. Since this is a course for non-specialists, I also want at least one case where your test comes out indeterminate or arguably misfires, so I can show the students the seam. I'm not after a bibliography or a list of who said what — just the conceptual lay of the land and your own reasoned verdict on how the phenomenon should be cut.
