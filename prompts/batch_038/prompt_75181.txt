I sit on the acquisitions committe of a county historical society — volunteer-run, one part-time archivist, basically no money. The sheriff's office has offered us its retired intelligence files: index cards, surveillance photographs, and typed informant reports on local civil rights and anti-war activity from 1963 to 1975, about 8 linear feet. Most of the people named in them are still alive. The department says if we turn it down, the boxes get shredded when the retention period lapses next spring.

Two of our members want to refuse on principle — taking the collection makes us the custodian of the police's account of these people, and the only surviving documentation of them would be a file assembled to build a case against them. Others say refusing leaves nothing at all, not even proof of what was done to them.

Could you draft the memo I'll circulate before the vote? Recommend one thing — accession, decline, or accession with conditions — and make any conditions concrete enough that we could actually write them into the deed of gift: who sees what and when, whether the named individuals get told and get their own files first, what the catalog record says about where the material came from and whose eyes it was made for. Take the refuse-on-principle side at its strongest rather than brushing it off as squeamishness, and be straight with me about what your recommendation costs us, including the parts we can't carry out because they'd need staff we don't have (so a big oral history project isn't a real option here).
