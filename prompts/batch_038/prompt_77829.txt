So my gran went into a care home near Wakefield about four months back — vascular dementia, and she's now pretty deep into it. Its got me thinking about something at the home that I can't quite get my head round, and I'd rather someone thought it through with me properly than fobbed me off with a platitude.

The home has a rule the staff follow religiously: never contradict a resident. If gran asks where her husband is (he died in 2009), they say he's popped out, he'll be back for tea. When she got it into her head that her sister rang every Tuesday, they'd walk her to the quiet room and mime putting a call through. They've now got a VR suite as well, where they've rebuilt the street she grew up on out of old photos and my mum's memories of it, and the carer who goes in with her is briefed to stay in character the whole time and never break the illusion. Everyone is delighted with it. The way they measure how well it's going is a mood-and-engagement score — basically how smiley and settled she seemed during and afterwards.

But elsewhere in the same handbook there's the opposite rule: when a resident dies, you tell the other residents plainly, no softening it. One woman on gran's corridor has been told her husband is dead forty-odd times. She asks every morning, she's told straight, she breaks down, it's absolutly gut-wrenching, and by lunchtime she's forgotten and asks again tomorrow. The staff are adamant that's the right call, because you don't lie to people.

So what picture of what makes a life go well is holding all that up? It looks to me like one building is running two theories at once. The first says only how things feel from the inside counts and whether it's true is beside the point — my flatmate says it's that old philosophy thought experiment about plugging into a machine. The second says truth is worth something in itself even when it costs a person real distress, over and over.

Can you work out what the going-along-with-it practice actually commits them to, and which views it quietly rules out? Does it strictly require hedonism, or could a desire-satisfaction account or a list-of-good-things account license it too — maybe the interesting move is separating what gran wants now from what she'd want if she were well enough to ask? Is the mood score the real tell, i.e. has measuring success that way already settled the philosophical question before anyone argued about it? And is the death rule genuinely inconsistent with the rest, or is there a story on which both come out of one theory — something about the earlier self's wishes binding the later one, say? Tell me where the practice is actually underdetermined, so that more than one theory would license it and the staff wouldn't notice the difference. Then tell me what would have to change on the ground — handbook, training, the VR suite, the scoring, the family meetings — if they swapped in the rival theory instead. I'm the one who ends up in those meetings, so keep it concrete about the practice rather than floating off into abstractions.
