You're a decision theorist who's run Newcomb-style seminars for years — think of this as a design job, not an exposition, and think carefully before you start writing.

Everyone knows Death in Damascus: the instability lands on the evidential side, because your own choice is evidence about where Death said he'd be, so conditionalizing on fleeing undercuts fleeing and vice versa. I want the mirror image, built to spec.

Design a realistic decision problem where:

- the causal decision theorist is the one who can't settle. Forming a settled intention to do X flips the recommendation to Y, and forming a settled intention to Y flips it back to X. A cycle, not a one-off wobble.
- evidential decision theory converges on one answer and stays there no matter how much the agent reflects on their own intention.
- there's no predictor of any kind: no demon, no simulation, no supercomputer, no copy of the agent, nothing that forecasts the choice. The correlation between act and state has to come from a common cause already in place before the decision — something fixed and unknown (a trait, a disposition, a past event) that the agent's own inclination is evidence about.
- the setting and stakes are ordinary: a job, a deal, a loan, a hiring decision. Non-medical. No genes, no diagnoses, no psychopaths, no buttons.
- you give explicit numbers: the states, the payoffs, the prior credence in each state, and the credences conditional on intending each act. Check the arithmetic acutally produces the flip in both directions before you commit to it.

Do the brainstorming off the page — draft two or three candidate setups, run the numbers, throw the losers away. What comes back to me is exactly five numbered items, no introduction and no conclusion:

1. the scenario, one short paragraph
2. EDT's verdict, and why it doesn't budge when the agent reflects on what they intend to do
3. CDT's verdict under each hypothesis about what the agent is about to do, showing the flip both ways with the numbers
4. which single feature of the case is doing the work, and what happens to the cycle if you remove it — say, by handing the agent a cheap, reliable piece of evidence about the state before they choose
5. the most respectable line a defender of CDT has for saying ratifiabilty just isn't the right test of a theory, and whether taking that line concedes too much elsewhere

Keep each item to a sentence or two (1 and 3 can run longer). If it turns out you can't make the numbers work under all these constraints at once, say so in item 3 and tell me which constraint I'd have to drop rather than fudging the arithmetic.
