In American criminal trials the judge instructs the jury that it must take the law from the court as the court states it, even if the jurors think that law unjust; prospective jurors who announce during selection that they would refuse to apply a law they regard as immoral are struck for cause, and in some jurisdictions seated jurors discovered to be bent on nullifying are dismissed from the panel. Yet the same system makes an acquittal final and unreviewable — the judge cannot direct a verdict of guilt and the prosecution cannot appeal — and justifies the jury's very existence by saying that it carries the conscience of the community into the courtroom.

What account of moral testimony does the first half of this practice presuppose? It appears to require that a juror may in good conscience accept the legal verdict, and with it at least part of the moral verdict, on the authority of an expert whose reasoning she is not equipped to evaluate. Which theories of permissible deference license that, and which does it rule out? In particular, what would have to be true for the familiar unease about pure moral deference to dissolve in this setting rather than condemn it?

Then assess the two halves against each other: do the unreviewable acquittal and the community-conscience rationale presuppose the same view of testimony, or the opposite one, and can the institution live with both? Distinguish what the practice strictly requires from what it merely fits well with — could these rules be defended without any thesis at all about moral deference? Finally, say what would have to change institutionally if we adopted the rival view, that a judgment about the moral gravity of a particular case cannot properly be taken on authority.
