I am working through Charles Sanders Peirce's 1878 essay "How to Make Our Ideas Clear," and I have reached a point where one sentence in it seems to undo the work of another. I need close reading help rather than background. Here are three excerpts, given as they are printed in The Essential Peirce, volume 1 (editions vary somewhat in wording from the 1878 original). Excerpt B comes later in the essay than A and C.

Excerpt A, the maxim:

"Consider what effects, which might conceivably have practical bearings, we conceive the object of our conception to have. Then, our conception of these effects is the whole of our conception of the object."

Excerpt B, from the later discussion of reality:

"The opinion which is fated to be ultimately agreed to by all who investigate, is what we mean by the truth, and the object represented in this opinion is the real. That is the way I would explain reality."

Excerpt C:

"Our idea of anything is our idea of its sensible effects; and if we fancy that we have any other we deceive ourselves, and mistake a mere sensation accompanying the thought for a part of the thought itself."

My difficulty is this. I am reading Peirce with Rorty's antirepresentationalism in mind — roughly, the view that the radical pragmatist move is to stop treating beliefs as inner pictures that succeed insofar as they copy a ready-made world, and to treat them instead as dispositions to act that succeed insofar as they let us get along. On that framing, excerpt A is the antirepresentationalist engine: if the whole content of a conception of an object is a conception of effects, there is no remainder behind the effects for a belief to match. But excerpt B appears to reintroduce exactly the picture A abolishes. It speaks of an opinion that represents an object, and it identifies the real with that represented object. So B looks like a representationalist sentence embedded in an antirepresentationalist argument, and C makes the tension sharper rather than softer.

My working hypothesis is that "represented" in B is vestigial — a leftover of the copying idiom Peirce is in the middle of abandoning — and that all the philosophical work in B is done by "fated," which would make the real a matter of what inquiry eventually settles on rather than of something standing behind inquiry and constraining it. If that is right, Peirce is no representationalist here, and B's talk of an object is only a way of gesturing at the total course of experience on which inquiry would converge.

I can already see the objection to my hypothesis: fate implies constraint, and constraint implies something that does the constraining. If the final opinion is fated rather than merely expected, something outside the process must be forcing the convergence, and the natural candidate is a world already there — which is to say, an object that the opinion represents after all. If that objection goes through, my hypothesis is empty and B is doing straightforward representational work.

Please take the following in order.

1. Gloss each excerpt sentence by sentence in plain contemporary prose. In particular, say what Peirce must mean by "practical bearings," by "sensible effects," by "fated," and by "the object represented in this opinion."
2. Determine whether B defines reality or only supplies a test for recognizing it, and explain why that difference decides whether B is representationalist.
3. Given A and C, is there anything left over for the opinion in B to represent? Lay out what "object" could mean in B consistently with A and C, and what it would have to mean for B to be a copying claim in the ordinary sense.
4. State the objection I sketched in its strongest form, then say whether my hypothesis survives it. If it does not, say what the hypothesis would have to be revised into in order to keep B and A compatible.
5. Assess, separately, whether a Peircean can hold at once that the real is what the final opinion represents and that the real is independent of what any finite number of investigators happen to think, or whether holding both collapses into a kind of idealism. Identify the hinge on which that turns.
