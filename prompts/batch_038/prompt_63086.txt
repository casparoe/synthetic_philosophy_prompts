I sit on the standards committee of a nonprofit that runs behavior-change campaigns (benefits take-up, energy conservation), and we screen our own messaging for manipulation before it ships. Our record so far:

1. Approved: an accurate one-off reminder that a real enrollment deadline was three days away.
2. Approved: replacing population statistics with the story of a single named beneficiary, on the stated ground that narrative gets people to attend to information they would otherwise skip.
3. Rejected: a variant of that same story that added a photograph and the line "picture your own child here," after recipients reported distress.
4. Rejected: one-click enrollment with the consent box pre-ticked, even though the opt-out was described in the form's first line and the program was free and beneficial.
5. Approved 4–3: a message saying that most households on the recipient's own block had already cut their energy use, which was true of their block; the dissent argued it traded on conformity.
6. Approved with conditions: asking people at sign-up to write down when and where they would act, which reliably improves follow-through on an intention they already have.

The new proposal is a benefits take-up campaign: calm, accurate copy laying out the reasons to enroll, chosen because testing showed them to be the most motivating, which omits the two true facts that most often lead eligible people to decline — a paperwork burden and a small offset against another benefit. Nothing in it is false, the omission is disclosed nowhere, and including those facts cut enrollment by a third, while our follow-up data show that the people who declined were worse off on the measure we care about.

Predict our verdict. Candidate rules that might fit the record include a mechanism rule (does the message engage the audience's capacity to respond to reasons), a publicity rule (could the technique be avowed to the audience without defeating itself), an informational-completeness rule, a rule keyed to whether the campaign serves ends the audience themselves endorse, and a welfare-without-falsehood rule; say which of these our six rulings actually discriminate between, and whether any of them leaves the record positively inconsistent rather than merely underdetermined.

Answer as exactly five numbered items, one or two sentences each, with no introduction and no conclusion: (1) the rules that fit the record and which the record discriminates between; (2) what each surviving rule implies for the new case, and which precedent is the closest analog; (3) your predicted verdict and the reason you would give the committee; (4) one further proposal that would tell the surviving rules apart; (5) what is settled versus what is genuinely contested among people who work on manipulation and persuasion — for instance, whether manipulation is fixed by the means used, the intention, or the effect on autonomy, and whether truthful but selective framing counts as manipulation at all. In that last item, do not present a contested position as the consensus.
