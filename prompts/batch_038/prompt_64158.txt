Fourth-year in political theory, mostly distributive justice with one foot in social choice, and I think I've just built an argument against the view I actually hold. Background: I'm a prioritarian, and I reckon I have a clean answer to the leveling-down objection — blinding the sighted makes things more equal but better in no way, because on my view equality isn't valuable in itself; what's valuable is benefits, weighted by how badly off the person getting them is. My advisor wasn't impressed and ran this at me instead:

(1) If a principle's verdicts about outcomes change when you change something that makes no difference to how well off anyone is, it isn't tracking anything real.
(2) Well-being is a quantity, and quantities get represented by numbers on a scale; nothing in the facts about people's lives fixes where the zero of that scale sits.
(3) Prioritarian verdicts depend on the zero. Take priority weight = square root of well-being, and compare A = (Ann 1, Bob 100) with B = (Ann 36, Bob 49): sqrt gives 11 vs. 13, so B wins. Now add 400 to everyone's number in both outcomes — a mere re-zeroing, nobodys life has changed. A′ = (Ann 401, Bob 500) scores about 42.4 and B′ = (Ann 436, Bob 449) about 42.1. A′ wins.
(4) So by (1), prioritarianism isn't tracking anything real.
(5) And notice that (1) and (2) don't mention priority anywhere. They apply to anything that says something about how much well-being people have — utilitarianism, egalitarianism, person-affecting views, even just the claim that saving five is better than saving one. All of them are claims about quantities whose numerical expression is up to us.
(6) So none of those track anything real either.
(7) But (6) is flatly absurd; my advisor and I both think Ann at 100 is better off than Bob at 1, and that that's a fact.

Which step is bad? My gut says (5), that the generalization is doing something (3) doesn't license — but I can't say what, and I'm scared that the only way I can block (5) is by giving up (1), which is what did the work in (4). So: tell me the step you'd cut, why it looked innocent going in, and what I owe if I'd rather keep (1). Also — is there a principled zero a prioritarian can point at? I keep reaching for the level at which a life stops being worth living, and I want to know whether naming that answers the objection or just relocates it to the shape of the weighting function. Please commit: take a position and defend it rather than laying out the menu and leaving me to pick. American English, thanks.
