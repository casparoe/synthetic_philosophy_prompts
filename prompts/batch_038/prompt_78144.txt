I am preparing a one-page flowchart for a final-year metaphysics seminar, and the difficulty I want it to solve is this: asked about persistence, my students announce that they are or are not four-dimensionalists before they have thought about a single case, and cannot say what they would have to give up in order to change their mind. I want an instrument that sorts them by what they say about cases instead.

Produce a decision procedure that begins from a commitment everyone in the room already shares — that some things persist in the ordinary sense, so that the lectern at the front of the room today is the very lectern that stood there when the course began — and that ends, for each complete path of answers, at one named position on the endurantism/perdurantism dispute.

Structural requirements:

- Between seven and nine questions, each with exactly two or three options, each option a single short sentence.
- Every question must be about a case, or about a claim a person can assess without knowing the technical vocabulary. The words "temporal part", "wholly present", "stage", "worm", "multi-location", "endurant", "perdurant", "exdurant" and "four-dimensional" must not appear in any question or any answer option; they may appear only in the leaf labels and in the diagnosis.
- State the routing explicitly: every option should say which question comes next, or which leaf the path terminates at.
- Give me a photocopiable version of the flowchart with the key stripped out, followed immediately by a key listing every complete answer sequence and the leaf it yields, so that I can check the map by hand.

The leaves. The tree must be able to terminate at each of the following, which I have described by content rather than by name — supply the standard name for each, and say so if you think the standard name misleads:

1. Objects exist entire at each moment of their history and have no parts spread out in time; change of intrinsic character is handled by relativising properties to times, or by treating them as relations to times.
2. Objects exist entire at each moment, but "entire at" is to be read as exact location at many regions, including regions that are not connected to one another.
3. Objects can go out of existence and later come back into existence as numerically the same thing, provided the right continuity is restored.
4. Objects are aggregates of time-slices, and the aggregate has no sharper boundary than the region it fills; composition over regions of spacetime is unrestricted.
5. Ordinary object terms pick out momentary things, and what looks like persistence is a relation between momentary things that falls short of identity.
6. The dispute is merely verbal: "there is" admits of more than one equally good meaning, and the two sides are using different ones.
7. There is no fact, sharp or vague, about when composition begins; the right response to the borderline questions is not to refine the theory but to reject the demand for a principled answer.

If any of these turns out not to be reachable as a distinct leaf — because your questions cannot separate it from another, or because you think it collapses into one — say so plainly rather than quietly dropping it. Leaf 3 may be a trap: tell me whether it is a genuine position or a repackaging of 4 or 5.

Cases you must use. Some are familiar; (d) and (e) are mine and, so far as I know, are not in the literature, so treat them on their merits rather than by association:

(a) The straight-then-bent poker, and whether a poker that is straight on Monday and bent on Tuesday is structurally like a poker that is straight at its upper end and bent at its lower end at one moment.

(b) A perfectly homogeneous iron sphere in otherwise empty space, spinning at a constant rate, alongside an exactly similar sphere at rest. Ask whether a complete description of what exists at a single instant, however detailed, could tell these two situations apart — and let the answer turn, if it does, on whether velocity counts as an intrinsic property.

(c) A time traveller who steps out of a machine and shakes hands with their younger self. Ask how many people are in the room at the moment of the handshake, and whether the gripping hand and the gripped hand belong to one thing or to two.

(d) A bronze figure is cast; over twenty years every one of its atoms is quietly replaced by an identical one, the displaced atoms being collected in a basement store. On the last day of the replacement the conservator reassembles the stored atoms into a second figure, qualitatively indistinguishable from the first, and stands it in the original's place on the plinth. Ask what is on the plinth now, and whether the figure the sculptor signed twenty years ago is in the room, is only in the basement's history, is in both, or is in neither.

(e) The same lecture theatre, described once as containing the lectern that has been in it since the course began, and once as containing an uninterrupted series of momentary things each exactly like the lectern and each caused by its predecessor in the ordinary way. Ask whether these two descriptions differ in what they say exists, or only in how they carve up the same thing. I suspect (e) is the most leading of the five and may defeat the purpose of the exercise; if you agree, say so and give me a replacement that does the same work without telegraphing the answer.

Diagnosis. After the key, add a section that does three things: lists any combination of answers your questions permit which no leaf occupies, and says for each whether the gap exists because the position is incoherent, because nobody finds it attractive, or because nobody has thought of it; identifies any pair of leaves your questions cannot pull apart, and says what further question would separate them, or why none could; and gives a verdict on whether the instrument sorts people by their commitments or merely by the words they were taught.

Close with a note on your own confidence. State how sure you are that each question discriminates as claimed — the whole exercise fails if a node routes people by how they have been trained to talk rather than by what they believe — and single out the one node you are least sure of, explaining what evidence or counterexample would change your mind about it.
