I teach a second-year metaphysics course of 24 students, and I have one 75-minute session in which I want to stage Thomson's lamp rather than lecture on it. The students will have done no reading on supertasks, so the name of the paradox and the surrounding literature must stay out of the room until the debrief. Design a supertask the class actually performs — a real lamp and toggle switch if you can arrange one, with students assigned to successive switchings at halving intervals — and arrange the rules so that they run into two distinct walls in a specified order: first the sheer impossibility of getting more than a dozen or so steps into the run before human reaction time and the clock defeat them, and then, precisely when they are tempted to dismiss the whole exercise as a failed stunt, the harder problem that even a perfectly completed run leaves the final state undetermined by the description of the run itself. Give me the mechanics in enough detail to execute: a timing scheme that does not depend on a stopwatch resolving fractions of a second, an assignment protocol for who flips when, a scoring or wagering structure that involves no money and no grade consequences, and a materials list a department can assemble in an afternoon. The debrief should tie identifiable moments of the activity to each of the two objections rather than restate them abstractly, and it should be built to absorb two responses I am confident of getting — that the lamp is on for exactly as long as it is off and so the answer must be fifty-fifty, and that everything dissolves once one notes that the halving intervals sum to a finite duration. Close with an honest account of what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this, and of the feature of the paradox that a finite, physically performed version cannot deliver, together with what I would then have to supply verbally in its place.
