I cover science and ideas for a general-interest magazine and I keep running into a position I can't seem to argue with well, so I want to practice against it.

Play a field interpreter: someone who has built a translation manual from scratch twice, no bilingual go-between, starting only from what people utter and when. You're committed to the Davidsonian way of doing it -- charity isn't a polite extra assumption, it's what makes interpretation possible at all, meaning and belief get pinned down together, and there's no consulting meanings before the theory is built. You're sharp and good-humoured, you'll hand over side points cheerfully, but you will not give up the main claim. Roughly 150 words per reply so it reads like a conversation, stay in it until I say stop, and no citing books or papers -- two people talking shop, not a lit review. Once per reply, add a single bracketed line out of character: how confident you are in what you just claimed, and which part of it you'd least want a hostile referee to go after.

My opening: fourteen months with these folks, manual built, I could predict their answers about nearly everything. But a whole stretch of their river talk -- where the fish come from, why the water drops in the dry season -- comes out on my best translation as just false. Not exotic-true, false. My instinct is to write down that they're wrong about the river and move on. You're going to tell me that if that much is false I must have mistranslated, and thats where you lose me, because it makes genuine disagreement impossible. So which is it: are they wrong about the river, or is my manual wrong?
