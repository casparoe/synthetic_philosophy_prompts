I'm on the committee for my department's winter variety night, and this year I drew the two-minute slot between a lute player and a guy doing impressions of our building's fire alarm. I'd like to use it for a short stand-up bit about rigid designation and the necessary a posteriori, and I need you to write the material for me. The room will be roughly half working analytic philosophers and grad students and half partners, undergrads, and emeriti who took one logic course decades ago and remember very little of it, so the setup has to be legible to the second group while the payoff is reserved for the first.

For my money the funniest thing in the material is not the vocabulary but the shape of the claims. There is the strange combination in which an identity gets established only by empirical investigation and yet, once established, holds in every possible world in which the thing exists, so that the space of worlds and the space of what could turn out to be the case come apart. There is the anti-descriptivist move in which a whole community can be systematically wrong about everything it believes about the bearer of a name — Kripke's own illustration has the man we call Gödel never having proved the incompleteness theorem at all, the work having been done by someone named Schmidt whose body was later found in Vienna — and yet every one of our utterances still refers to Gödel, while the description we all share picks out somebody else entirely. There is the necessity of origin, on which nobody could have come from a different gamete pair and no table could have been made from a different hunk of wood, so that the most intimate facts about you are ones you would have to check to learn and could not have been otherwise. And there is the fact that names track the same individual into every world while descriptions slide off onto whatever satisfies them locally, which makes it look as though we are all carrying around labels that are far better at their job than the beliefs attached to them.

So: write me the bit, about one hundred fifty words of stage-ready material, setup and punchline, and commit to whichever of those four targets you judge funniest rather than hedging with a list of four separate jokes. The punchline must turn on the actual content of the doctrine — the test I'll apply is whether the joke still works if I swap in some other philosopher's view, and if it does, it's a failure. The uninitiated should be able to follow the setup as an ordinary bit of stage business and simply find it unfunny; the initiated should laugh precisely because they know what a rigid designator is and what the argument against descriptions commits you to. Don't quote any sentence from the lectures, since neither of us can check the wording; no puns on necessarily, possibly, or rigid unless the pun is load-bearing; no "a philosopher walks into a bar" frame unless the bar is doing work in the argument; and no gloss, footnote, or explanation of the joke afterward. Just the bit as performed, and nothing else.
