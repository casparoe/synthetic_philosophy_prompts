I'm about two thirds through a novel whose protagonist, Ines, has pain asymbolia after a stroke knocks out a region around her insula — she'll tell you, pleasantly, that the needle going through her thumbnail is "sharp, thumb-side, about a seven," and then ask if you want tea. I've spent the better part of a year reading around it: case reports, some neurology, and a lot of philosophy of mind that kept sliding sideways into arguments about what makes pain bad in the first place. Out of that I built an argument I wanted to be the spine of the book — roughly, that the badness of pain is entirely a matter of how much of it you mind, which is also the thing that makes Ines frightening rather than merely curious. Except when I wrote it out properly it kept running past its target and I haven't been able to stop it. Here it is, cleaned up:

(1) What makes a pain bad isn't the discriminating part — where it is, how intense, what quality it has — but the unpleasantness, the part that grabs you and makes you want it to stop.
(2) In people like Ines the discriminating part is fully intact and the minding part is missing: they can call a stimulus painful and then joke about it, or shrug, or leave the hand where it is.
(3) So in them you have a pain with the bad-making component switched off, which means such pains are not bad, or at any rate are very much less bad than the identical nociception would be in you or me.
(4) So unpleasantness is the whole of pain's badness, and it's seperate from the rest of pain — switchable on its own, in principle and in practice.
(5) So if you gave someone being tortured a pill that switched off the aversiveness while leaving every other aspect of the pain exactly as it was, you'd have stopped harming them. The screaming would stop and nothing bad would be left over.
(6) And the move generalises: a grief you don't mind isn't bad, a humiliation you've been rendered indifferent to isn't bad, a life full of quietly appalling injury that the sufferer doesn't object to isn't bad.
(7) But (6) can't be right — it implies the well-drugged contented victim is no worse off than an unharmed person, and that the only thing wrong with cruelty is that people happen to mind it.
(8) Therefore (1) is false, and unpleasantness isn't what makes pain bad.

I want to keep (1). Honestly I do — it's the sentence Ines is built out of, and it's also just what pain feels like from the inside, so giving it up feels like giving up the only evidence I have. My beta reader, who is a palliative care nurse and has no patience for any of this, says the whole thing is nonsense from (2) onward because her asymbolic-ish patients still flinch; my old tutor, who read it over email, said it's basically fine and that I should just accept (6) and stop flinching at it myself, which is not advice I can use. I can't tell whether the rot sets in near the top or near the bottom, and I don't want you to guess which of those two people I'm hoping you'll side with. So: tell me where it goes wrong — or, if it doesn't, tell me that plainly and say what accepting (6) actually commits me to. Name the step you'd kill, explain why that step looked so innocent going in (I want to know what I fell for, not just that I fell for it), and then price the alternatives: what I lose if I give up (1) instead, what I lose if I keep (1) but block the slide from the case of Ines to the case of the pill, and whether pain turns out to be special here or whether (6) really is just the honest version of (1). Two typos in there are mine, one is deliberate, and if you can't tell which is which then neither can I.
