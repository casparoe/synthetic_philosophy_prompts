I am a novelist, and this year I hold a visiting fellowship in a philosophy department, where I am co-teaching one seminar in their final-year paper on religious epistemology. Twelve students. They have read the lectures Wittgenstein gave on religious belief in the late 1930s, and they have read the posthumous remarks on certainty in which some propositions stand fast for us and serve as the scaffolding of enquiry rather than as its results. They have read no secondary literature, and the final paper is closed book, three hours, two questions from three. The department has asked me to set one of those questions, and I want to set it on a passage from my own novel-in-progress, which the students will be handed cold in the exam room and will have never seen before. The passage is my prose and exists nowhere but in the manuscript; I am reproducing it here exactly as it will be printed.

The scene: a hospital car park at three in the morning, four hours after their father's death. Iris is not a believer. Theo has attended Mass most Sundays of his adult life and has not been inside a church since the diagnosis.

IRIS: Say it plainly, then. You believe you'll see him again.

THEO: Not the way I believe the road is icy. There's nothing I'm weighing. But don't turn it round on me either — I'm not saying I'm less sure. I'd rather not say how sure I am. The question doesn't fit.

IRIS: Then it isn't a belief. It's a comfort you've grown used to.

THEO: A comfort I could put down — that's exactly the difference. If it were a comfort, I'd have put it down by March. What you've got wrong is that you think we're looking at the same thing and seeing it differently. We aren't. You say he's gone. I say he isn't. One of us is wrong, and that isn't a difference of temperament. That's a contradiction.

IRIS: You've just told me you aren't weighing evidence. On what grounds, then? What do you have that I don't?

THEO: Nothing. And you have nothing against it. You've never met anyone who came back, and neither have I. Where what we've seen is concerned, we're level.

IRIS: Then why haven't you been to church since March? If you believed it — really believed it — you'd be the one comforting me. You'd be almost glad.

THEO: I'd be exactly as I am. That's what you keep missing. It wouldn't show. My life wouldn't look different from yours, and it doesn't, and I'm not embarrassed about that.

What I want from you is the complete assessment package built on this passage, and I want it built so that it cannot be answered by reciting a summary of Wittgenstein's position. My own reading is that Theo says something close to Wittgenstein in the first speech, and then undermines it in the sixth, where he appeals to a parity of evidence after having disowned evidence as the wrong currency; and that the final speech raises a separate and harder problem, because it denies the very mark by which Wittgenstein distinguishes religious belief from a mere opinion about the future. I want the paper to test whether the students can find that out for themselves rather than be told it.

Please give me:

1. The question exactly as it will be printed, above the passage, with its rubric: seventy-five minutes, twenty-five marks, closed book. It must direct candidates to the fact that Theo's claims pull against one another without identifying which claims, and it must ask them to say what Wittgenstein's account can and cannot do for him.

2. A model answer of about nine hundred words, written in the voice of the strongest candidate in the class. It may name the lectures and the remarks on certainty but must not quote either, since quotation cannot be load-bearing in a closed-book paper, and it must not appeal to commentators.

3. A marking scheme out of twenty-five, divided into named moves with the marks each carries and a one-line note on how candidates typically botch it. Labels should earn nothing: I do not want marks awarded for producing terms like language game, form of life, fideism or non-cognitivism unless the answer does work with them.

4. A description of the two fluent second-class answers I expect most of the class to write, and, for each, the particular thing it does that should hold it to a B.

5. A short paragraph advising me whether the passage is fair. Is there a defensible reading on which Theo is consistent from start to finish, and how should the marking scheme treat a candidate who finds it and argues it properly?
