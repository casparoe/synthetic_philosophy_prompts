I'm about two-thirds through a novel whose narrator is a biographer, and I've hit a wall that's more conceptual than it is plot. Here's the book: she's been hired by an estate to write the authorized life of a dead poet, she keeps getting handed boxes of letters that make him look worse, and the estate keeps suggesting she "contextualize." Fine, that's the engine. But what I can't get straight is what genre she thinks she's working in, because I've realised I don't know either. I was at a dinner last week where somebody described a celebrity memoir as hagiography and everyone nodded, and it struck me that nobody at that table could have said what they actually meant by the word. So: compare Plutarch's way of writing a life in the Parallel Lives with the way Christian hagiography does it — the saints' lives tradition, from the desert-father stuff onward through the big medieval collections — as two rival theories of how a reader is supposed to morally profit from reading about a real person. I don't want a survey of either one and I don't want a history of biography. I want the comparison to hinge on one specific thing: what each tradition asks the reader to *do* with the subject's flaws, and what each one therefore assumes about the reader's self.

Some seeding, because this is my own half-baked scheme and I want you to pressure-test it rather than hand it back to me polished. I've been telling myself there are three stances a life can put its reader in. The Plutarchan one is curatorial — the reader shops in the life, takes the courage and leaves the drinking behind, and uses the whole thing as a mirror he keeps adjusting. The hagiographic one is devotional — the life isn't a menu, it's a summons; the saint is less a person than a window, the reader's job is conversion, so the bad material gets transmuted (the persecutor becomes the apostle, the vanity becomes the occasion for humility) rather than weighed. And the modern authorized biography, the one my narrator is being paid for, is forensic — the reader sits in judgement, and the estate's entire strategy is verdict management. Three questions. First: is that a real three-way distinction, or am I collapsing two of them, and where does Plutarch resist being called curatorial — he's hardly neutral about vice, he moralizes constantly, and he does something strange with the defects of men he clearly admires, plus he seems to think the tiny throwaway incidents tell you more than the battles. Second: does hagiography really flatten character, or does it just run a different theory of what character is — grace working on a will — which makes the same details legible in another way? Third, and the one I actually need: if the forensic stance has won, what exactly got lost, and could my narrator's genre be a fourth thing rather than a degraded third?

Lead with a single sentence saying whether you think my three-way scheme holds, then argue. No quotes from either tradition — I can't verify anything and I'd much rather you paraphrase than approximate a line. No bibliography, no "further reading," no dates unless you're certain of them. Hard cap 800 words, and please put your answer to the third question in a closing paragraph of no more than five sentences, since thats the bit I want to be able to reread with the manuscript open.
