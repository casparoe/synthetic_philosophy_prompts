**DRAFT: The Sufficiency Illusion — Overdetermination, Causal Crowding, and the Metric Behind Historical Disagreement**

**§1. The received picture.** Since Mackie, philosophers have tended to describe causes as insufficient but non-redundant elements within conditions that are themselves unnecessary but sufficient. On its face this fits history unusually well, because historians characteristically offer complexes rather than single events: an alliance system already rigidified by reciprocal mobilisation assumptions, timetables that could not be halted once started, a doctrine that prized the offensive, a fiscal state with nowhere left to borrow, and then an assassination in Sarajevo. The picture also generates the familiar worry. If a sufficient condition for a general European war in 1914 could have been assembled from many different subsets of that complex, then no single member was necessary, and the binary counterfactual test — would the effect have occurred without the candidate cause? — seems to answer, for nearly every candidate, that it would. Historians refuse that verdict, and I think they are right to. My aim is to explain why the test misfires and then to extract a less comfortable consequence from the explanation.

**§2. Three regimes.** The literature tends to file several distinct situations together under the heading of overdetermination. I want to pull them apart.

*(i) Symmetric overdetermination.* Two conditions actually obtain, each independently sufficient for the effect. The binary test says neither is a cause; the usual repair is to count both. My contention is that this configuration is rare to the point of absence in real historical explanation, and that its prominence in the literature is an artefact of how easy it is to construct in a sentence.

*(ii) Masked dependence.* Two apparently independent sufficient conditions that turn out to rest on a shared enabling condition, so that the plurality is illusory: there is one cause wearing two descriptions. In the 1914 case, the alliance system and the mobilisation timetables are plausibly redescriptions of a single institutional fact about how European general staffs had come to reason.

*(iii) Causal crowding.* My main interest. No factor is individually sufficient. Instead, the space of factor-combinations contains many distinct sufficient subsets, and the outcome is reachable along a dense family of pathways; excising any one pathway leaves others intact. Crowding is a property of the causal structure of the system, not of our ignorance about it, and it is the default condition for outcomes that are themselves defined at the level of institutions, wars, prices, and revolutions rather than at the level of events.

**§3. Consequence for the counterfactual.** Under crowding, the binary question carries almost no information. It answers *yes* for nearly every candidate not because the candidates were causally inert but because the question is insensitive to the structure it is being asked about. The natural repair is to stop scoring dependence as a truth-value and score it as a magnitude: the fragility of a factor is the proportion of nearby interventions that remove or alter it which also remove the outcome. A factor can then be causally substantial — high fragility — while failing every binary necessity test, and this is exactly the situation historians find themselves describing when they say that a factor mattered without saying that it made the difference.

**§4. Where the metric lives.** Fragility is a measure, and a measure requires a metric: an account of what counts as a nearby intervention, of how far the rest of the world is permitted to rearrange itself when one factor is held out. Structuralist historiography, on my account, holds the social and institutional background comparatively fixed and varies decisions and persons within it; the resulting fragility scores for individual choices come out low, and the structural factors come out high. Agency-centred historiography does the reverse, and its scores invert. The two schools are therefore not disagreeing about what happened. They are disagreeing about the metric, and a metric disagreement is not the kind of thing that evidence about 1914 can settle.

**§5. The strong claim.** I want to go further, and this is where I expect to be attacked. My claim is that there is no fact of the matter about which metric is correct simpliciter, because the metric is fixed by the explanatory interest that generated the question, and interests are not truth-apt. Fragility remains objective *given* a metric, so the position is interest-relative without being subjectivist. But where the metric is not pinned down by the question — and I claim this is precisely the class of controversies that have gone unresolved for a century — the controversy cannot converge even in principle. More archival material cannot settle it, because the disputants are computing different quantities and reporting them as though they were the same one.

**§6. Two corollaries.** First, the great-person debate is a metric dispute misreported as an empirical one; both sides are right about their own scores and wrong to think those scores are commensurable. Second, counterfactual history is not the frivolous genre its reputation suggests: on my account it is the estimation procedure for fragility, and its quality varies with how honestly it exposes its metric. Most published counterfactual history is bad for a specifiable reason — it varies too little, holding nearly everything fixed except the single factor whose fragility it then reports as if the metric had been chosen for it.

**§7. Worries I already have.** (a) I have a vague memory that Lewis, in his later work on influence, replaced binary dependence with something like a pattern of dependence across ranges of alterations of cause and effect, and that Woodward has written about insensitive causation and about the depth of an explanation as a matter of its stability under further interventions. If so, my fragility score may be a repackaging rather than a proposal, and the honest move may be to demote it and rest the paper's weight on §4–§5. (b) Crowding may be trivially satisfied by every macro-outcome, in which case it explains nothing. (c) My characterisation of historiographical camps in §4 may be a caricature of a discipline that is far less bimodal than I imply.
