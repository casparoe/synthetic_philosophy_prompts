I cover science and ideas for a weekly magazine, and last month I sat in on a two-day workshop on flavour perception, chiefly to report on how industrial sensory panels are trained. At the drinks on the first evening, two participants — a philosopher of mind I shall call Hale and a sensory neuroscientist I shall call Okonjo — fell into a sustained argument about Dennett's 'Quining Qualia', and in particular about the pair of coffee tasters the paper turns on. I know the paper only loosely, so rather than trust my recollection I have written up their exchange from my notes as even-handedly as I can. I could not tell who was ahead, and I would like someone to settle it.

As I understand the case they were arguing over: two tasters who once agreed that their coffee was superb now both report that it is not what it was, but they describe the change in opposite ways. One says the coffee tastes different to him now, that his palate has altered; the other says the coffee tastes precisely as it always did and that what has changed is his response to it. Dennett's point, as Hale gave it, is that nothing further could decide between those two descriptions.

Here is the exchange, move by move.

(1) Hale: the tasters' disagreement is unresolvable in principle, and that is what sinks qualia. Neither man can be right about his own experience in the way the concept demands, because there is no procedure, first-personal or otherwise, by which one description would be correct and the other mistaken. A term that can never be correctly applied to anything is not a term for a property; it is a noise we make.

(2) Okonjo: that is a non sequitur. Unreliable access does not entail that there is nothing being accessed — people misdescribe their own motives constantly without anyone concluding that motives are unreal. And in this instance the question is straightforwardly empirical. On my own panels, trained tasters' ratings of a chemically fixed sample drift over a decade; when you hold the sample constant you can usually establish whether the drift is peripheral, a loss of sensitivity at the receptors, or affective, a change in how the subject feels about a flavour he still detects perfectly well. Dennett's pair is unresolvable only because he left the chemistry out of the story.

(3) Hale: but notice what you have just granted. If the question is settled by measuring the coffee and the nose, then the answer concerns the world and the body, not the experience. Once every observable fact of that kind is written down — what the sample contains, what his receptors do, how he discriminates, what he says, what he chooses — I want to know what is left over that could be the quale. Your method does not rescue the posit; it replaces it.

(4) Okonjo: it replaces nothing. Measurement tells you which physical change occurred; it cannot tell you what that change was like, and 'what it was like' is not merely a summary of the measurements. Moreover, the distinction you want to dissolve is one my subjects use competently every day: they separate 'it tastes different to me now' from 'it tastes the same but I no longer like it', and which of the two they offer predicts their subsequent choices better than their raw intensity ratings do. A distinction that carries predictive weight is a real distinction, whatever you say about the vocabulary.

(5) Hale: then let us test the weight. Swap the two tasters' descriptions and nothing else changes — no behaviour, no discrimination performance, no physiology, no ranking of samples. If two formulations can be exchanged across every possible state of affairs, they are formulations of one state of affairs, and the choice between them is a choice of words. Your predictive success belongs to the fact that a subject has made one of two reports, which is public; it does not show that the report corresponds to two different private properties.

(6) Okonjo: interchangeability is exactly what privacy predicts. If what it is like to be one of these men is available only to him, then no external inventory of facts could ever compel one description over the other; that third parties cannot adjudicate is a consequence of the view, not a refutation of it. You are converting an epistemic limit into a semantic discovery, and that conversion is the whole argument.

Some way into this, Okonjo invented a third taster, whom she called Tam, and each of them immediately claimed him. Tam reports that the coffee tastes exactly as it always did, that his palate has not changed, that he likes it as much as he ever did — and yet he no longer drinks it, and cannot say why. Hale said Tam destroys Okonjo's case: everything she would measure is stipulated unchanged, so the only variable left is the report itself, which shows the report tracking nothing at all. Okonjo said Tam establishes hers: a man can retain an experience entire and lose the behaviour that supposedly expressed it, and that is precisely the separation between experience and disposition that Hale wants to collapse.

Three things I should like.

First, go through moves (1) to (6) and rate each — clearly mistaken, partly right, or clearly right — with a sentence or two of justification for the rating. I want to know who is correct about which specific point, not simply who had the better evening.

Second, adjudicate Tam separately. Does the case favour either of them, or neither? And is each of their readings of Tam consistent with what they said earlier: can Hale use it, given (5), and can Okonjo, given (2)?

Third, tell me what neither of them raised that would have altered the terms of the argument. I am especially interested in whether some position on phenomenal character survives the whole exchange intact, and in whether the dispute was ever about a single agreed thing — 'qualia' may not have meant the same to both of them. If you judge one of them to have been right overall, say what the other would have to concede, and at what cost, to make that so.
