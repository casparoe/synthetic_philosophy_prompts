hey — i cover science and ideas for a general-interest magazine and I'm toying with a piece whose conceit is that the most interesting monetary thinker of the 18th century is an Irish bishop. Concretely, Berkeley's Querist, published in parts in the mid-1730s, argues a run of things I want to take seriously rather than as a curiosity: that money isn't wealth but a token or ticket that records and transfers power over other people's labour; that a money stock is useful only to the extent it gets idle industry moving, so its right size depends on how much is actually being exchanged and how much slack there is in the economy; that gold and silver have no special claim beyond being one convenient ticket material; and that a state can therefore issue paper (Berkeley floated something like a national bank issuing notes secured on land) to set an under-employed economy going.

I want to test that bundle against Zimbabwe. Facts I'm treating as given: the Reserve Bank financed the governments deficits and various quasi-fiscal programmes by printing, denominations ran up into the trillions, inflation peaked somewhere around 79 billion percent month-on-month in late 2008, then in February 2009 the Zimbabwe dollar was effectively abandoned for a multicurrency basket (US dollar, rand, pula, sterling), and small-change shortages later pushed the authorities back into bond coins and bond notes.

So: run Berkeley's theory over teh episode properly. Which of his claims does real explanatory work, where does the ticket model bite hardest, and where does it go silent or actively mislead — does a token theory have any resources at all for why a note stays legal tender long after nobody accepts it, and does his whole money-stirs-up-industry framing presuppose idle capacity Zimbabwe simply didn't have? Then turn it around: what do the 2009 dollarization and the small-change mess afterwards say about whether his positive proposal — a domestic paper issue run for industry rather than for the treasury — survives the case at all?

Two ground rules. Don't cite query numbers or pretend to quote him; paraphrase, and flag whatever you're unsure of. And tell me plainly where my summary is too friendly or anachronistic, because the piece only works if the theory is recognisably Berkeley's and not just quantity theory in a wig.
