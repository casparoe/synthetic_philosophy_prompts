I'm the physics teacher at a fairly small independent school — ninety-odd kids in the year group — and I'm leaving at the end of this month after nineteen years, so I've been handed the closing slot at the June assembly. At the last faculty meeting we voted 6–5 to scrap valedictorian and salutatorian and replace them with a bunch of categories the students nominate and vote on themselves, plus a sentence said aloud about every single leaver. I argued for it, I won, sort of, and now I have to stand in the sports hall and explain it to the people who lost. In the room: about two hundred parents (a solid third of them hostile — one wrote to the head saying we were punishing excellence in order to spare fragile children, which is not nothing, and I don't want to pretend it is), the ninety leavers including the two girls who both know perfectly well they'd have been top two, and the faculty, half of whom voted the other way. There's also a moral philosophy lecturer in the second row whose son is graduating, and he'd enjoy catching me out.

Here's my problem, and it's a real one, not a rhetorical device. I'm a convinced prioritarian. I don't think equality is worth anything in itself. I think what matters is how well people's lives go, with benefits counting for more the worse off the person is who gets them, in absolute terms rather than by comparison with anyone else. And I take Parfit's leveling-down objection completely seriously: a change that makes some people worse off and nobody better off is not an improvement in any respect, even if the result is more equal. Fine. So what did I just vote for? Scrapping the valedictorian looks like a textbook level-down — you take a distinctive good away from the kid at the top and, on the face of it, you hand nobody anything. So my speech cannot rest on fairness, or on the idea that nobody ought to be ranked above anybody else, because I don't believe that and I'd be lying to a room full of children. What I actually believe, and what I think is true, is that the ranking was making a lot of the kids in the middle and bottom genuinely worse off — the anxiety, the grade-grubbing, the boy who dropped out of the hard science classes in year eleven specifically because he'd worked out he couldn't come top and decided not to play — and that the loss the change imposes lands mostly on people who are already doing well. That's a prioritarian argument, not an egalitarian one, and its a harder thing to say out loud than it looks. The wrinkle that keeps me up is that one of the two girls who'd have been top two is on a bursary and has had a genuinely rough few years at home, and for her the rank was not a hollow prize, it was one of the few good things that happened to her. So the speech can't quietly assume the loss falls only on the comfortable, because it doesn't, and she'll be sitting right there.

So: write the speech, four to five minutes, around 650–700 words, to be read aloud. Three things it must not assert, and I'd like you to keep them straight in your head because the whole point is that the speech has to do its work without them: (A) that equality or the absence of rank is good in itself; (B) that ranking people is wrong or unfair regardless of what it does to them; (C) that nobody is really made worse off by the change, or that the prize the top two lost wasn't worth having. It also has to concede, honestly and in terms a parent will hear, that if abolishing the ranking had made nobody better off then we'd have been wrong to do it — i.e. that the case is contingent, not a matter of principle. And the words "prioritarian," "leveling down," "well-being" and "Parfit" should not appear anywhere in the speech; the structure should be recognisable to the philosopher in row two without any of the vocabulary being spoken. It should land as an affectionate teacher saying goodbye, not as a policy defence — it's still a school assembly and people have driven two hours to be there. Then, after the speech, put one final line beginning **Closest call:** and give the letter of the claim (A, B or C) that the speech comes nearest to asserting, followed by \answer{A}, \answer{B} or \answer{C} and a single sentence on why it doesn't actually cross the line.
