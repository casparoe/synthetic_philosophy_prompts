I serve as one of five voting members on the grants panel of a county arts council in a largely rural county of about ninety thousand people. Each year we distribute roughly forty thousand dollars in project grants, and this cycle two applications have survived the staff's eligibility screening and come before us as finalists. We can fund only one of them at the level requested; the program officer tells us that splitting the money would leave both projects under-capitalized and neither viable. Two panel members — both of whom teach studio art and music at the community college — regard the first application as the only serious work of art before us. Two others regard the second as the reason the council exists. The chair has asked each of us to circulate a short written opinion before Thursday's meeting, and I would like help drafting mine.

The first application is from a composer and pianist proposing a cycle of pieces for prepared piano, to be performed once in the courthouse rotunda. The music is deliberately dissonant and structurally opaque. Her statement says the work transmits the feeling of being unable to make sense of her brother's death, and that the listener's own bewilderment is not an obstacle to the piece but its content. She asks for eighteen thousand dollars, most of it for paid rehearsal hours with two local teenagers and a free public performance. The second application is from a community theater group proposing a plain, story-driven play about a farm family losing its land, written in local idiom, to be performed five times across three towns including the grange hall and the high school auditorium, with a cast drawn from those towns. They ask for sixteen thousand dollars.

I come to this without any academic training in aesthetics. Over the winter I read Tolstoy's late treatise on art on my own, and it has shaped how I think about the case, so I want the memo to test that influence rather than assume it. As I understand him — and this is my paraphrase, not his wording, and I would rather you not attempt to supply his wording for me — a work is art to the degree that it hands a feeling from one person to another by means of outward signs; its worth is measured by how strongly and reliably it infects; and three things govern that infection, namely the singularity of the feeling transmitted, the clarity with which it is transmitted, and the sincerity of the person transmitting it. He holds further that the best art transmits feelings that unite people and is followable by anyone, and he turns this standard against much of what the cultivated classes of his day admired — Shakespeare, the late Beethoven, Wagner — and against his own earlier novels.

Three questions are where I am actually stuck, and I want the memo to answer them rather than gesture at them. First, the composer's defense looks as though it may turn the clarity condition against itself: if the feeling being transmitted is confusion, then faithful transmission means reliably producing confusion in the audience, so the very opacity Tolstoy condemns becomes the mark of success. Either his clarity condition can be satisfied by transmitting confusion, in which case it licenses the difficulty he denounced, or it means something closer to the feeling arriving without effort, in which case it seems to smuggle in a preference for a certain kind of audience. Take a side, and say what follows for this application. Second, whether the unity condition does any real work here: the play certainly unites the people in the room, but it may do so by confirming resentments they already hold, and I do not know whether a theory like his can tell the difference between those two things. Third, whether the panel should adopt a standing rule for future cycles — score every application twice, once by people who make art and once by county residents who have never attended an arts event, and rank applications by the lower of the two scores rather than the average — or whether that converts a felt measure into a tally and distorts the theory it is meant to serve.

What I need back is a memo I could hand to the other four members and the chair: a specific allocation recommendation rather than a comparison; a sentence or two the chair could read aloud on Thursday to explain the decision to the room; what the unsuccessful applicant should be told, including any conditions or reduced-scale alternative the panel could defensibly offer; the reasoning tied to the questions above; and an honest, unstrawmanned statement of the strongest consideration on the losing side, since whichever way this goes two of my colleagues will disagree. Please also tell me plainly where my paraphrase of the theory is most likely to be leading me astray, given that I am working from memory of a difficult book and have no way of checking myself against it.
