I co-host a monthly podcast that grew out of a graduate reading group on the philosophy of computation. Our listeners are a mixed crowd — philosophy graduate students, a few people who work in complexity theory, and generalists who have heard of the P versus NP problem but would not be able to define NP. Next month's guest is a philosopher I would rather not name here, who defends a cluster of claims that I find both striking and difficult to get a handle on. As I understand her position: the gap between verifying a proposed solution and finding one is not a fact about the limitations of human cognition but an objective structural feature of the world, so that P versus NP belongs to epistemology and metaphysics rather than being a merely technical open problem; the known barrier results — relativization, the Razborov–Rudich natural proofs argument, and the algebrization barrier — are on her account themselves philosophical results, because they show that any proof separating P from NP must fail to be natural in the technical sense of combining constructivity with largeness, and she reads that as supporting a kind of non-constructive realism on which the answer, whatever it turns out to be, cannot be exhibited by a proof that generalizes uniformly across functions; and she is explicit that the older Lucas–Penrose style arguments from incompleteness are bankrupt, so that intractability, not undecidability, is where the interesting limits lie. I may have some of this wrong, and I want the question set to leave room for her to correct my description rather than to argue past it.

Please write me nine to eleven questions for a forty-five-minute conversation, arranged so that the arc builds rather than proceeding topic by topic. I want at least one question on each of the following, and I would like you to tell me which of them you consider load-bearing if the slot runs short: the move from a worst-case, asymptotic, idealized setting to claims about how actual verification and actual discovery differ; the fact that what cryptography and her structural reading both seem to want is average-case hardness, which does not follow from P not equal to NP alone; the proof-search consequence, going back to Gödel's 1956 letter to von Neumann, that if P equals NP one could find any proof whose length is bounded in advance in time polynomial in that bound — and whether her claim that mathematical creativity would become cheap under P equals NP survives the point that one never knows the length of the shortest proof ahead of time, so the useful polynomial is unavailable; whether she equivocates between natural in the technical sense and natural in the ordinary philosophical sense of explanatory and non-ad-hoc when she treats the natural proofs barrier as a metaphysical result; whether the barrier results support quietism about the question rather than her realism; what remains of the idea that computation tells us something about the limits of mechanism once the Gödelian route is abandoned; and, since hardness claims are always claims about growth in some input parameter, which quantity is supposed to be the input in her philosophical applications. She is also very good at deflecting any performance-based objection with a remark to the effect that she is making a claim about the structure of the world and not a prediction about how well anyone does on a test, so I would like a run of questions, spaced through the second half, that makes that deflection progressively harder to deploy, ending with one that asks directly what observation or argument would count against the structural reading. Note also that her view is asymmetric in a way I want pressed: P equals NP would overturn her picture, while P not equal to NP would confirm what nearly everyone already believes.

For each question, give me the question itself, then a follow-up keyed to the answer she is most likely to give — state the anticipated answer in a clause before the follow-up, so I can see what you are preparing me for — and then one sentence saying what the exchange is meant to test. Add, where a question depends on a technical point she may contest or on my possibly having misdescribed her, a short fallback formulation that keeps the question standing if she rejects the premise. Please write each question so that a listener with no background in complexity theory can follow it without a footnote, which will probably mean building any needed gloss into the question itself. No generic opening material, no requests for her to restate the view for the audience, and nothing about her intellectual biography.
