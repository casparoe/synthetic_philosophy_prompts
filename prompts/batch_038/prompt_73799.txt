Right, so — two of us have been going round in circles for about a year on Nietzsche's eternal recurrence and its gotten nowhere, so we've agreed to write the document we should have written at the start. One of us is a cosmological/literalist reader: Nietzsche took the doctrine to be possibly true, the notebook argument from finite force in infinite time is a real argument he thought had something going for it, and Zarathustra's task is to actually believe the thing and survive believing it. The other is a test reader: the doctrine is a device, whether it's true is beside the point, what matters is your response to the thought, and the animals chirping their refrain in The Convalescent are the standing warning about what happens when you hold it as a mere proposition you've accepted.

Write the joint statement the two of us could both sign after a good-faith adversarial collaboration. What I want in it:

1. A numbered section of claims both sides accept — including claims about the material (the demon aphorism at the end of Book Four of The Gay Science, the dwarf and the gateway marked Moment and the shepherd with the snake in On the Vision and the Riddle, the animals' song, the notebook argument) and, importantly, claims about what each reading has to explain and where each is on the back foot. No slanted agreements.

2. A disagreements table: the issue, the cosmological reader's own wording of their position, the test reader's own wording of theirs, and a column saying whether that disagreement is substantive or verbal.

3. Cruxes — the sub-questions such that settling one would actually move somebody. Here's my hypothesis about the main one; treat it as a proposal to be tested, not a conclusion. I suspect both readings quietly assume different answers about what I'll call recurrence fidelity: whether what comes back is numerically the same down to the last trivial detail, or merely qualitatively indistinguishable. The test reader seems to need maximal fidelity — the demon's question only gets its grip if it's *this* pain, this spider, this particular moonlight between the trees that returns, not something roughly like it. But the cosmological argument seems to deliver minimal fidelity: finite force, infinite time, so every possible combination of forces must already have occurred infinitely often, and 'combination' is a coarse-grained notion that can't plausibly pin down particulars. (The Stoics apparently had precisely this fight, and it's instructive: Chrysippus is reported as holding that the numerically identical individuals come back, while Origen reports the Stoics as saying it'll be someone indistinguishable from Socrates marrying someone indistinguishable from Xanthippe.) So the crux is whether there's a high-fidelity version of the doctrine that the cosmological argument actually supports, and whether the test still bites at low fidelity. For this one, write down each side's prediction in advance about a near-miss case: you're told the cycle repeats but with small variations nobody in it could ever detect. Does the response change, and if so, on what grounds?

4. Two further cruxes I want covered explicitly. First, whether the response depends on conviction: consider someone fully persuaded the cosmology is true who feels nothing, and someone certain it's false who is nevertheless brought up short by the question. Which of the two does each camp say has failed, and failed at what? Second, whether the doctrine is self-regarding at all — Zarathustra's nausea isn't about his own suffering returning, it's about the smallest and pettiest human beings returning, which suggests what has to be affirmed isn't 'my life' but the whole lot, including everything one despises. Say whether that asymmetry favours one reading over the other and why.

5. For every crux, name a piece of evidence or a case both sides would accept as bearing on it — textual or constructed, I don't mind which — with each side's prediction recorded before anyone gets to see how it lands.

And if you conclude that some stretch of this dispute is merely verbal, say which stretch, say why, and say what the two of us ought to have been arguing about instead.

British English throughout please. No bibliography and no invented references or translations — I'll add the citations myself. Where you refer to a passage, paraphrase it rather than quoting verbatim unless you're completely certain of the wording.
