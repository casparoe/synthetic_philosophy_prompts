ok so I've been going round in circles on Frankfurt cases and the principle of alternative possibilities, and I don't want the survey version — I want you to actually argue for a position. No reading lists or citations, I can't look anything up anyway, I just want the argument itself.

Here's the set-up to work with. Standard Frankfurt case: Jones is going to shoot Smith, Black will intervene if he detects the prior sign that Jones is about to choose otherwise, in the event Jones shoots on his own so Black never acts, and Jones supposedly lacks any alternative possibility but is still responsible. That's the action version.

Now the omission version. Suppose Jones is a lifeguard who can either rescue the swimmer or let her drown, and Black wants to make it true that Jones cannot rescue her. The trouble — and this is the bit I want you to press hard on — is that there isn't one thing Jones would have to do in order to rescue her. He could dive from the chair, wade out, throw the ring, shout to the other guard, radio for the boat. So Black's trigger can't be a single prior sign, it has to be a disjunction: intervene if any of these routes starts up. Call that a disjunctive-trigger case. And call the claim that such a case can be built while still leaving Jones omitting on his own the Disjunctive Parity Thesis, DPT — the thesis that whatever Frankfurt cases do to PAP for actions, they can equally do to PAP for omissions.

Argue that DPT is false. The shape I want is a dilemma. Either Black's disjunction is fine-grained enough to catch every rescue route, in which case his monitering is so continuously entangled with Jones's deliberation that Jones is no longer omitting on his own and the case has quietly collapsed into an ordinary deterministic one, which is no use to Frankfurt's project at all; or the disjunction is coarse, in which case some rescue route escapes the trigger and Jones keeps a genuine alternative possibility. Either horn, you don't get a Frankfurt-style defeat of the omission principle. Then I want you to be honest about the best replies available to Black — that the trigger can be coarse so long as the response is fast, that Black only has to block the routes Jones is actually disposed to take, that the disjunction can be indexed to Jones's own intention-formation rather than to outward behaviour, that a few escaping routes are fine because they're not robust alternatives — and say why none of them rescue DPT.

Finally, draw the conclusion you think really follows rather than the one that sounds best. Does the failure of DPT leave an asymmetry, where PAP survives for omissions but dies for actions? Or is the more conservative moral the right one, that Frankfurt cases were never doing the work people assumed they were doing, so nothing survives on either side? Pick one and defend it, and tell me which premise in your dilemma a Frankfurtian should attack first if they want to fight back.

British English please. And end with a line beginning "Verdict:" giving the single-sentence thesis you've argued for — something a referee could accept or reject in one go.
