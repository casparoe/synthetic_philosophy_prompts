I write a science-and-ideas column and I'm pitching a piece on Flowers for Algernon as a story about the unreliability of self-report. Take the role of a philosopher of mind who also does some literary criticism, and think it through before you land anywhere — sketch the candidate readings first, then commit to one.

Here's a distinction I've been kicking around and want pressure-tested. Call it access failure when the state is there but can't be reported, and transcription failure when a report does get produced but simply doesn't track the state. Which of those is pre-op Charlie, cheerfully recording in his progress reports that the guys at the bakery are his pals? And after the operation, does he gain introspective access he never had, or does he just acquire a better theory of himself? The fact that he ends up predicting his own regression as a piece of psychology — he names it the Algernon-Gordon Effect — reads to me like self-knowledge by inference rather then by looking inward. Is that a third thing my distinction misses?

Two more bits I want you to handle: the childhood memories that surface once he's smart, which the book seems to treat as accurate recoveries of what was always there — does the plot need that, and how does it sit with what memory researchers actually think? And the way late-book Charlie talks about the old Charlie as somebody else who's still in there, waiting to get his life back.

Then tell me where Keyes resists the introspection-skeptic reading — I don't want the novel flattened into a case study — and what it shows about self-knowledge that a journal argument couldn't. Throughout, mark clearly what's settled among people who work on this versus what's genuinely contested; don't hand me a live dispute dressed up as consensus.
