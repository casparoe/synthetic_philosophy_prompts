Oregon's Death with Dignity Act was approved by ballot initiative in 1994, held in abeyance by an injunction until late 1997, then defended against a federal attempt to nullify it through controlled-substances law — an attempt the U.S. Supreme Court rejected in 2006 — and has been generating an annual statistical report ever since. Assume I know the statute's architecture: a competent adult, a terminal diagnosis with a prognosis of six months or less, confirmation by two physicians, a waiting period, and a prescription the patient must ingest without assistance. Assume I also know the most striking thing in the state's own reporting, namely that the concerns patients most frequently cite are the loss of autonomy, the declining ability to engage in the activities that made life enjoyable, and the loss of dignity, all of them well ahead of inadequate pain control.

Read that record through Zhuangzi's views on death instead of through the familiar autonomy-versus-sanctity-of-life frame. I am not asking whether Zhuangzi would approve of the law; I am asking what his conceptual apparatus can and cannot represent about it.

Begin by sorting the resources that might bear on the case. There is the account of his wife's death, where the drumming on the tub and the singing follow an acknowledged first flush of perfectly ordinary grief; the treatment of life and death as a transformation of qi; the suggestion that hating death is like a child who has wandered off and forgotten the road home; the dream conversation with the skull that refuses to be restored to a body, a family, and a set of obligations; the deathbed instruction that heaven and earth serve as his coffin and that it makes no difference whether he is eaten by birds or by ants; and, elsewhere in the text, the thought that one's body and life are not possessions at all but things on loan from heaven and earth. Say what each of these actually contributes to an assessment of the Oregon patient, and be equally clear about which contribute nothing.

Then press the central difficulty. Hastening one's own death looks like a paradigmatic case of the human overriding the heavenly, and Zhuangzi is hostile to that sort of overriding; yet the text's portraits of cultivated competence — the cook, the swimmer who goes down with the whirlpool and comes up with the current, the carver who fasts his mind before shaping the bell stand — are often taken to show that wu wei is trained responsiveness rather than passivity, which would make dying well a skill that could in principle include arranging its timing. Argue whether that reading can carry the weight or whether it quietly imports a conception of the self as author of its own life that the text denies. And say what, if anything, Zhuangzi's framework makes of the other parties — the prescribing physician, the state, the surviving family — or whether it has no slot for institutional actors whatsoever.

Finally, run the analysis in the other direction. What does the Oregon evidence do to his position? If the people seeking prescriptions are mostly not fleeing unmanageable pain but fleeing the loss of self-direction, does that vindicate his diagnosis of the human predicament, or does it show that the predicament he diagnoses is precisely the one his therapy leaves untouched? Is the fact that acceptance must be surrounded by waiting periods, second opinions, and annual statistics evidence that acceptance cannot be legislated, or evidence that it can only ever be legislated? Close by marking where the theory falls silent rather than filling the silence with plausible-sounding Zhuangism.
