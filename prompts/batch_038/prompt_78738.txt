So here's the situation, and I want to turn it into a joke rather than an argument, because arguments with this guy just make Thanksgiving weird. My brother-in-law Dean has a theory — the specifics dont matter much, but roughly it's that a former business partner of his has been "directing energy at him" for years and it explains everything that goes wrong in his life. And the thing is, it has never once been wrong, not in his telling. If something bad happens, that's confirmation. If nothing bad happens, the energy was "deflected" or "absorbed" by something else. If someone else has the same run of bad luck, that's evidence the energy is "wider than we thought." If you ask him for a mechanism, he says you can't measure this stuff with the instruments that exist, which is itself a reason to believe it. If you ask him for a prediction, he gives you one that's flexible enough to survive contact with the calendar. I know perfectly well this is the shape of a theory that no observation could possibly embarrass, and I also know that his individual moves are the exact same moves a normal, respectable theory makes when a prediction goes sideways — blame the auxiliary assumption, question the measurement, dispute whether the observation was really what you think it was. That's what makes it funny rather than just annoying.

Dean's birthday dinner is in three weeks and I've been volunteered to say a few words. Half the table is his family, none of whom have read a word of philosophy of science and would not sit still for it; the other half is my sister and two of her friends from grad school who will absolutely catch anything I bury in there. So: write me a joke I can tell out loud at that dinner. Short — setup and punchline, tellable in under a minute, no essay bolted on. It has to work on two levels simultaneously: on the surface it's just a joke about a stubborn relative and his bad-energy theory, and it should still land for someone who's never heard of Popper; underneath, the real punchline should turn on the machinery of the demarcation problem — that a theory can always be saved by adjusting something in its protective belt, that no single observation ever speaks for itself, and (this is the part I actually care about) that at some point the joke flips onto me, because I catch myself doing the same thing from the other direction: nothing Dean could produce would count against my view, since I'd just file it as another rescue. If the biggest laugh is the critic realizing his debunking stance is at least as immune to refutation as the crank's theory, I'll be thrilled.

A few things to avoid. Don't make Dean a cartoon flat-earther or a punchable idiot — the joke gets worse and less true if his reasoning is just stupid, because it isn't; it's structurally normal. No "walks into a bar" framing, no joke whose payoff is merely the word "falsifiable" dropped like a magic spell, and no gag that only works if you already know which philosopher said what. Don't name Popper, Duhem, Lakatos, or anyone else inside the joke itself — the grad-school half doesn't need the signposting, and the rest of the table will glaze over. And please don't append an explanation of the philosophy afterward; if the thing needs a footnote to be funny it's failed. One last thing: keep the content of his theory vague enough that I could swap in horoscopes, a startup's revenue projections, or a friend's theory about why his knee hurts, because I intend to reuse this for years.
