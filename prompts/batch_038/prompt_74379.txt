I work on measurement systems for a living — service-level objectives, dashboards, the quiet politics of which number gets put on a page and which one gets dropped — and I have no philosophy training at all. My city's analytics group has been asked to stand up a resident wellbeing dashboard, and the first thing everyone reached for as a reference implementation was the World Happiness Report, mostly because it produces a clean ranked list of about 140 countries each year. So I went and read how the list is actually built. As far as I can tell: the ranking uses exactly one survey item, administered to roughly a thousand people per country per year, pooled across three years and reduced to a national average. The item, as the report reproduces it, is: "Please imagine a ladder with steps numbered from 0 at the bottom to 10 at the top. The top of the ladder represents the best possible life for you and the bottom of the ladder represents the worst possible life for you. On which step of the ladder would you say you personally feel you stand at this time?" The report defends the choice on the grounds that "rather than constructing an index from multiple metrics, the Cantril Ladder empowers people to make their own judgements about what matters most, regardless of their culture and background," and it separately argues that life evaluations are preferred to reports of emotion because they "capture the quality of life in a more complete and stable way than emotional reports based on daily experiences." Meanwhile the survey does collect yesterday-affect items (whether you smiled, worried, enjoyed yourself), and it does compute six variables — income per capita, social support, healthy life expectancy, freedom to make life choices, generosity, and perceptions of corruption — but those six enter only as an explanation of the variation in ladder scores against a benchmark "Dystopia" country plus a residual. They do not contribute to a country's score. I want to understand what picture of welfare this whole arrangement assumes, in the way I'd want to understand what a scoring rule assumes before I shipped it.

Concretely: what does the practice only make sense under, and which rival accounts of what makes a life go well does it rule out or simply render invisible? Three spots puzzle me. First, the endpoints of the ladder are explicitly personal — the top is the best possible life *for you* — yet the published artifact is a comparison of averages across countries whose respondents presumably set those endpoints very differently. What has to be true about interpersonal and cross-cultural comparability for that move to be legitimate, and is that a claim about the world or a decision? Second, the summary statistic is a bare mean with no distribution attached, so a country whose average improves because its least happy residents emigrate, or because the sample composition shifts, registers as progress. What view of aggregation does that presuppose, and which views — ones that care about the worst off, or about equality of happiness, or about the number of happy lives rather than the average quality of them — does the format structurally exclude? Third, the affect items are collected and then demoted to supporting material. Is there a coherent theory on which reflective evaluation is the real thing and daily experience is a channel, or is that just an artifact of which measure happens to vary more between countries?

What I'd like back is a reconstruction, not a survey. Please distinguish what this design strictly presupposes from what it merely sits comfortably with, and be explicit about where the practice is philosophically underdetermined — where two or three quite different theories of wellbeing would license the same ladder, the same mean, and the same ranked list. Then push on two things. One: my team is drafting an alternative that swaps the mean for a low percentile, replaces the self-anchored endpoints with reference anchors fixed within each country, and weights yesterday-affect items into the headline number; tell me what each of those three substitutions would commit us to, whether the combination is coherent, and whether it dissolves the problems above or just relocates them. Two: several governments treat a top-ten finish as an objective in its own right. Does steering toward a self-reported ladder contradict anything the instrument presupposes — is a number like this only meaningful on the assumption that nobody is optimizing it? Say what would have to change in both the instrument and the reporting practice if a country genuinely set out to maximize the score. Finally, since I lack the vocabulary, name the positions you end up attributing to the practice in plain terms as you go. I'm not after references or a reading list; I just want the reasoning.
