In three weeks I present a paper at a political theory department seminar, and the composition of the room matters rather more than usual. There will be four or five liberal-egalitarians working in a broadly Rawlsian idiom, two committed deliberative democrats of a Habermasian persuasion, one colleague who defends epistemic democracy through Condorcet-style aggregation of independent judgements, and two empirical political scientists who design and evaluate citizen assemblies. There is no pragmatist in the building; the department's reflex is to read Dewey as a sentimentalist about community who never quite explained what a community is. My thesis is that the unit of democratic legitimacy is neither the vote nor the consensus a well-run deliberation happens to produce, but the experiment: a policy adopted as a provisional hypothesis about what a public's shared difficulties require, judged by the consequences it actually generates, and revised or abandoned accordingly. The argument I want to press has three strands. First, a public is constituted not by shared identity, shared values or shared ends, but by the indirect consequences of transactions that its members suffer together — which is why the public is so often unrecognisable to itself and why its discovery is a matter of inquiry rather than of census. Second, legitimacy therefore attaches to whether an arrangement for making decisions can detect and correct its own errors, not to whether it conforms to a procedure specified in advance of the evidence. Third, this yields a non-sceptical answer to the charge that we cannot cooperate because we disagree about the good: experiments produce evidence about consequences that people who disagree about ends can nonetheless share, so inquiry does some of the work that consensus cannot. I should mention that I trained as a historian of education before turning to political theory, which is why I keep saying schools and laboratories when I mean legislatures and agencies; you may disregard that tic entirely.

Prepare me for the question period rather than defending the paper for me. I want exactly five objections, in a numbered list, ordered by how much damage each would do to the argument as I have sketched it — and specifically the objections this room would raise, in its own vocabulary, rather than the ones a fellow pragmatist, a journalist or a graduate student in my own tradition would put. Each entry should run to a sentence or two at most: the objection phrased as its raiser would phrase it, the reply I could honestly deliver in the two minutes I would have, and a short note on where that reply is softest or on what I ought to concede before making it. Make one of the five, flagged as such within the entry, the question I am hoping nobody asks — the one that goes after the weakest joint in my own argument rather than at a familiar target such as majority tyranny or the risk of technocracy, which I can handle in my sleep. Give me no introduction, no closing remarks and no general advice about pacing, slides or manner: the five numbered entries and nothing else.
