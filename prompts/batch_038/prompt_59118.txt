I work at a policy institute that runs adversarial collaborations: we pair two people who have been publicly disagreeing, give them nine months and a modest stipend, and require as the deliverable a single document both will sign rather than two essays and a moderator. This year's pair spent the year on a sentencing-reform bill now before our legislature. The bill would abolish mandatory minimums; it would have an independent commission publish, for each offense grade, a range of months said to be proportional to the gravity of the wrong and the offender's culpability; and it would require judges to choose a point inside that range by reference to a validated risk-and-needs instrument, the local availability of treatment and supervision, and fiscal cost, with any departure above the published ceiling subject to commission review. One member of the pair is a philosopher who holds that punishment is permissible only where it is deserved, that desert fixes a ceiling no quantity of social benefit can raise, and that it also fixes a floor below which a sentence may not sink merely because nothing useful would come of imposing it — though he stresses that the floor is far less determinate than the ceiling, and that what victims are owed can sometimes be discharged through acknowledgment and enforceable reparation rather than hard treatment. The other is a legally trained criminologist who holds that the only thing that can justify an institution that deliberately inflicts suffering is the balance of consequences it produces; she treats proportionality as a superb summary device for keeping such a system credible and preventing it from destroying the cooperative base it depends on, and is therefore willing in practice to accept sentences longer than a bare deterrence calculation would yield, while denying that any sentence is unjust in principle if imposing it is best on the whole.

They have finished their sessions, they agree on the bill's text, and what remains is the document. Please draft it in their joint voice. It should contain: (1) a numbered section of claims both will sign — about the bill, about what punishment does and does not accomplish, about where the evidence is strong and where it is thin — cutting anything a person with no view on the subject could equally sign; (2) a disagreements table with columns for the question at issue, each side's position stated as that side would state it and would accept as accurate, and what would have to be true for each of them to give way; (3) the cruxes, meaning the sub-questions whose settlement would actually move at least one of them, and for each, the case, argument, or kind of datum both accept as bearing on it, together with each side's prediction written down now, in advance, including what each expects the other to do if the datum comes in unfavourably; (4) a short section identifying which of the year's disputes turned out to be merely verbal — we suspect this includes the recurring slide between asking what justifies having the institution at all and asking who may be punished and how much — explaining why, and saying what is genuinely left over once that confusion is dissolved; and (5) a closing recommendation the legislature can act on, or, if they cannot agree on one, a jointly signed statement of precisely what its decision turns on.

Two exhibit cases should be worked through inside the document, with each side's verdict recorded and the divergence made explicit rather than papered over. First: a man who at nineteen drove the getaway car in an armed robbery during which a shopkeeper was pistol-whipped and permanently lost the sight of one eye; he pleaded guilty at twenty; the commission's range for that grade is eight to fourteen years; he has served thirteen, has an unblemished record inside, a completed apprenticeship, a standing job offer and two young children; and the jurisdiction's instrument places him in the lowest risk decile for his age band, his file returning to the commission this month for a recommendation. Second: a woman in her fifties who has run the same modest invoice-and-benefits fraud six times, each episode costing roughly forty thousand dollars spread thinly across many victims, each conviction drawing a sentence inside the twelve-to-thirty-month range for that grade; she is articulate, has no history of violence, and scores in the highest decile for continued fraud — and the legislature wants to know whether the commission may recommend a departure above the ceiling on the ground of expected aggregate harm. A further question they fought over and should resolve or quarantine in the cruxes section: if the legislature cut every sentence in the code by twenty percent to fund treatment and supervision, leaving all the ratios between offenses untouched, would either of them have a principled objection, and does proportionality constrain only the ordering of sentences or their absolute magnitude?

Two house rules. No bibliographies, no citations, no named studies, and no invented statistics: where a crux turns on evidence, describe the kind of study or observation that would bear on it and state each side's expectation qualitatively. And every formulation in the disagreements table has to survive the test that the party it describes would be willing to have it read aloud in their absence.
