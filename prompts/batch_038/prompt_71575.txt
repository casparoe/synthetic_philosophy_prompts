I'm two years into a novel whose main character is a middle-aged reserve policeman in occupied Poland in 1942 — Hamburg, working class, family man, drafted into a unit that ends up doing ghetto roundups and shootings — and the single decision I can't make is how much interiority to give him. Does he have beliefs, hatreds, a self-image he's defending, or is he basically hollow, filled up by the situation and the men standing next to him? A friend of mine who writes long magazine features sent me a summary of the Browning/Goldhagen fight she'd pulled together from a couple of pieces, and I've been leaning on it hard, probably harder than I should. Here it is more or less in her words:

"Browning studied a Hamburg police battalion — about 500 reservists, too old for the front, mostly working-class family men with no Nazi commitments — who were ordered to shoot Jews at a small town called Józefów, and their commander offered anyone who didn't want to take part the chance to step out. Almost none did. That's the whole point of the title: these were ordinary men, and Browning is basically running Arendt's banality of evil down to the rank and file, showing that evil needs no conviction, only orders and the fear of what your mates will think of you. Refusal meant being shipped off, so the men had no real choice, and Browning's account is situational all the way down — nothing about their characters does any explanatory work. Goldhagen's response, which grew out of a review he wrote of Browning, was that this misses the specifically German thing: centuries of 'eliminationist antisemitism' had made Germans uniquely disposed to want the Jews dead, so the Holocaust was more or less inevitable once the Nazis got power, and the perpetrators often took pleasure in it. The two also disagree about what actually happened — Goldhagen thinks Browning got the facts wrong — and the debate was settled in Browning's favour, since experimental psychology's fundamental attribution error tells us people systematically overestimate character and underestimate situations, which is exactly Goldhagen's mistake. Browning never moved an inch afterwards."

Could you go through this claim by claim and tell me what to trust? Sort it into: what a historian of the Holocaust would accept as stated, what's a fair simplification of something more complicated, and what's just wrong or backwards — I especially want to know about the bits where the summary makes Browning sound like a thoroughgoing situationist, and about whether he and Goldhagen were really arguing about the same events or about how to explain them, because I got the impression they were working from a lot of the same postwar testimony. And where Browning himself later softened or shifted, say so. Then tell me what survives of her conclusion once the errors are fixed, because that's what I actually need: if I strip out the stuff that's wrong, does the corrected version still license me to write this man as someone with no inner moral life of his own, or is that a licence the summary only seemed to give me? I'm not after a bibliography or page numbers — please don't guess at those, and don't put anything in quotation marks unless you're sure of the exact wording. But do tell me how confident you are in each correction, roughly, and then single out the one point in your whole answer you're least sure about and say why it's shaky. I'd rather know where the ground is soft before I build a book on it.
