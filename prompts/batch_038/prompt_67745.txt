I work in the quality function of a contract analytical laboratory, and for some years I have been looking for a name for a pattern in our own records that I can describe precisely but have never seen discussed anywhere.

The pattern is this. We hold a settled result — say, that a certain class of contaminant is effectively absent from a given product line — established long ago by careful work and treated as background ever since. Over the years it has been contradicted by fresh measurements again and again: an out-of-specification reading here, an anomalous chromatogram there. Each contradiction triggered an investigation, and each investigation found a genuine, specific, checkable local defect — a degraded reference standard, a sample swapped in transit, a dilution factor mis-keyed into the data system, a column past its service life. Every one of those explanations was itself confirmed: the standard was assayed and found to be off, the keying error was reproduced, the column's performance was measured. Nothing was waved away. From the inside, our file reads as a laboratory that takes every anomaly seriously, spends real money on it, and gets it right every time.

And yet in twelve years the settled claim has never once been revised. Someone reading that file from outside would see thirty challenges and thirty dismissals, and would surely conclude that the claim has been made immune to evidence. Someone reading it from inside — myself included — would say that each dismissal was earned on its merits, and that it would be absurd to treat a run of individually verified instrument failures as though the run itself amounted to a refutation.

What I want a name for is not a fallacy, because as far as I can tell no fallacy has been committed at any point in the sequence. It is the end state: a belief rendered empirically unreachable not by being defended with excuses but by being defended correctly, over and over. The immunity is real, and every instance of it was deserved.

Two nearby things I have considered and rejected as too coarse. The first is the general habit of blaming the apparatus rather than the theory. That describes our behaviour, but it is neutral on the question that actually interests me, which is what a long accumulation of true blames does to the standing of the belief. The second is the well-known paradox to the effect that if you know something, you thereby know that any later evidence against it would have to be misleading, so that it is rational to dismiss such evidence in advance. That paradox concerns a knower licensing herself to ignore evidence before seeing it. In our case nobody does anything of the kind: each piece of counter-evidence is accepted provisionally, investigated at cost, and only then set aside.

So: is there an established concept for a belief made unfalsifiable by a sustained run of legitimate, verified defeaters? If there is, I should like to know how it is marked off from ordinary ad hoc rescue, from the point that a prediction is never tested in isolation, and from the inertia that attaches to entrenched beliefs generally. If there is not, please coin a term and define it properly — the conditions under which it applies; the conditions under which a similar-looking case does not qualify (a laboratory that investigates honestly but is wrong half the time, or one whose investigations are real but chronically under-resourced, or one that has simply stopped seeing anomalies at all); and what an outside observer is entitled to infer from a long run of dismissals each of which was individually correct. I should also like you to say whether the run is itself evidence of anything: does thirty-for-thirty tell us something about the belief, something about the measurement process, or nothing beyond the sum of the individual cases? If the answer differs depending on whether the anomalies were generated independently of one another or share a common cause upstream, please say so explicitly.

I am after the carving rather than the label; a bare name would not help me, and if you think the phenomenon dissolves on inspection I would rather be shown where it dissolves than reassured.

Please write in British English. At the end, tell me what would change your mind: which argument, case or piece of evidence would lead you to a different verdict on whether the immunity is genuine, on whether it is epistemically objectionable, or on the term you settle on — and point to where in your own reasoning you think you are most likely to have gone wrong.
