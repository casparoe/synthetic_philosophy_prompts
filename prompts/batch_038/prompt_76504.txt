Cause prioritization in the effective-altruist tradition ranks candidate causes on three criteria — importance, tractability, and neglectedness — and then combines them, sometimes by multiplication, into a single score that determines where funding and careers go. The third criterion functions as a discount on the first two: a problem that would absorb resources at an excellent rate per dollar, but on which a great many people are already working, is ranked below a problem with a modest rate of return on which almost nobody is working. Cause reviews therefore ask a distinctively marginal question — what would change if this field received ten times its current resources — and answer it with a returns curve on which each additional dollar buys less progress than the last. Neglectedness is itself scored in a mixed currency: philanthropic dollars, numbers of researchers, government attention, and press coverage are all folded into one estimate of how crowded a space already is.

What theory of value does this ranking practice presuppose? I am especially interested in the work that the neglectedness term is doing, and in whether the practice needs the theory it seems to assume or merely happens to fit one well.

Please address the following:

1. Reconstruct the implicit theory. In particular, what does penalizing crowdedness assume about the information carried by everyone else's allocation of altruistic resources — that it tracks nothing about value, that it tracks something but is outweighed, or that it is not even the kind of thing that could be evidence? Is that an empirical bet about the market for doing good, or a conceptual commitment about what makes a contribution valuable?

2. Say which rival theories the practice quietly rules out. Consider views on which joining a large, publicly recognized common effort has non-instrumental worth — as solidarity, as fairness in bearing a shared burden, as a matter of what one owes to a collective undertaking — and views on which doing good is partly a joint activity rather than a solo optimization against a fixed background of other people's choices.

3. Test the practice for internal consistency. The framework reasons about one's own marginal dollar while holding everyone else's dollars fixed. What happens when the framework is adopted widely: does the neglectedness criterion undercut itself, and if so, does that reveal a coordination problem that the scoring language cannot express? Also address the multiplicative combination, if you think it matters — what is being claimed by an aggregation on which a cause crowded to the maximum extent scores zero no matter how important or tractable it is?

4. Distinguish what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely sits comfortably beside. Mark clearly where several incompatible theories of value would license exactly the same ranking, so that the scoring tells us nothing.

5. Describe what the practice would have to look like if the presupposed view were replaced — for instance, if others' attention counted as evidence, or if contributing to shared efforts had value in itself that had to be scored rather than penalized.

Finally, be candid about the limits of your reconstruction. Flag any point where you are reading one organization's stated framework as though it described how prioritization is actually carried out, or where you are imputing a commitment that practitioners would deny holding. Close by telling me what would change your mind: which argument or consideration would lead you to a different verdict about what this practice commits its users to, and at which step your reasoning is most likely to fail.
