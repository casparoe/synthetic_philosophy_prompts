You are a specialist in the philosophy of psychiatry and social ontology. Please work carefully rather than quickly here, and begin with a diagnosis of the shape of the problem — what would have to be true for my verdicts to hang together at all — before you offer anything systematic.

I teach a graduate seminar on classification in psychiatry, and I have been collecting my own considered verdicts about which classifications loop, in Hacking's sense: which ones change the people they classify in a way that in turn changes the classification. I want to know what principle, if any, they add up to.

- Multiple personality disorder: loops, and loops quickly — the more the category was described and diagnosed, the more elaborate the presentations became.
- Homosexuality as a psychiatric diagnosis: looped, and the loop ran through the classified, who organized against the category and eventually had it withdrawn.
- Autism: loops in both directions at once, and I suspect this is a difference in kind from the first two, not of degree — the category is now partly authored by the people inside it.
- A pediatric category such as failure to thrive, applied to infants: loops, even though the classified cannot possibly have encountered the label. The loop runs through the parents and the clinic.
- A purely administrative underwriting class — an actuarial risk band that no insured person is ever told about and that surfaces in no clinical encounter: does not loop, though it plainly changes what happens to people.
- Breed-specific legislation about dangerous dogs: loops. The classification changes the dogs, via breeders, owners, and shelters.
- A hazard designation applied to a neighborhood — high fire risk, say — which alters how it is built, insured, and patrolled: loops, but I am inclined to say this merely rhymes with the psychiatric cases rather than being one of them.
- A diagnostic category introduced by an epidemiological survey into a community with no prior word for it: loops immediately, before anyone in that community reports having heard of it.
- Feigning symptoms to obtain a diagnosis and the resources attached to it: loops, but parasitically; I would not want a theory that makes this the central case.
- The category of the teenager: loops so fast that it seems to outrun its own extension.

Two clusters I cannot reconcile. The infant case and the underwriting case look identical from the inside — in neither does the classified party know the label — yet I want to say the first loops and the second does not. Likewise, the dogs and the neighborhood both loop only through human intermediaries, but I want to keep them on different sides of a line.

What I am after: a principle, or a small ordered set of competing principles, precise enough to be applied mechanically to a new case; each clause tied to the verdict that forces it; the verdicts the principle delivers on cases I have not listed, including at least one you construct that would be decisive between the candidates; and a frank statement of which of my verdicts the simplest adequate principle must overturn, and what is lost by paying the complexity required to save them all. Please also say explicitly what the principle is a principle about — the extension of the category, the properties of the individuals falling under it, or the practices of those who apply it — since I suspect I have been sliding between these.
