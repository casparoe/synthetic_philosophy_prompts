**Trust Is Not Reliability**

The extended mind thesis, as Clark and Chalmers set it out, turns on a thought experiment. Otto has a memory impairment and carries a notebook in which he writes down what he takes to be true; Inga has an intact biological memory. Both learn of an exhibition of modern art at a museum and both form the intention to go and see it — Inga by recalling where the museum is, Otto by consulting his notebook. The parity principle says that if a process occurring outside the head would be accepted as cognitive were it occurring inside the head, it ought to be accepted as cognitive outside the head too; on that basis the notebook is part of Otto's memory. The authors add that the external resource must be available when needed, easily retrieved, generally trusted, and that its contents must have been endorsed at the time of writing.

My claim is that the last of these conditions — trust — is load-bearing in a way the argument cannot support, because trust is being used in two incompatible senses. In one sense, to trust a source is to have good inductive grounds for thinking it delivers truths: a statistical relation. In the other, to trust a source is to take up a normative stance towards it — to treat its deliverances as reasons one need not check, and to hold it to account when it fails. A calculator is reliable in the first sense but is not trusted in the second; checking a calculator is precisely the refusal to trust it. Only the second sense can do the work the argument needs, because the first is available for the notebook only on pain of circularity: the notebook is reliable in so far as Otto was accurate when he wrote in it, and whether he was accurate is just what a theory of extended memory is supposed to explain.

But once trust is normative in that way, the notebook stops looking like a memory store and starts looking like a witness. To take the notebook's say-so as a reason one need not check is to accept testimony. The peculiarity in Otto's case is that the testimony is his own: the notebook contains Otto's earlier assertions to himself. And believing what one earlier told oneself is not remembering. If I read notes I wrote a year ago and cannot recall the occasion on which I wrote them, I have not remembered anything; I have believed my past self. On the reading that makes trust normative, then, the notebook case describes extended testimony rather than extended memory.

This yields a dilemma. Either trust is read statistically, in which case the notebook is reliable only because Otto's earlier internal states were accurate, and the external store is parasitic on internal cognition rather than an extension of it; or trust is read normatively, in which case Otto stands to the notebook in a relation of dependence on a source of testimony, and the correct description of the case is social rather than individual. Neither horn delivers what the parity principle was meant to deliver: a genuinely cognitive process that happens to lie outside the skull.

Two objections I anticipate but do not resolve. First, a defender might say Otto enjoys something like quasi-memory — content retained from an earlier experience without the ordinary causal chain running through the brain. But if the chain is allowed to run through a written record, quasi-memory seems to lose whatever made it a distinct notion, and I cannot tell whether that is fatal to my dilemma or merely a restatement of it. Second, someone might say that the coupling objection — that Otto is merely causally coupled to the notebook, not partly constituted by it — already disposes of the case and makes my argument redundant. My reply is that this objection tells us the notebook is not part of the process but does not tell us what relation Otto does stand in to it, and the testimony reading is offered as that missing positive account; if so, my argument supplements rather than competes with it.
