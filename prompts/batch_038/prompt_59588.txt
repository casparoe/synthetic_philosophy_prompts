I've talked myself into a fairly strong view about pain and I want someone to genuinely try to break it rather than explain it back to me.

The view: pain is bad entirely in virtue of how it feels. Not because it warns you about tissue damage, not because it gets you to protect the injured part, not because of what it reveals about your situation — those are correlates and downstream effects, not the badness itself. Take away the felt unpleasantness and you've taken away the harm; leave the unpleasantness in and no amount of reassurance about how useful or informative the pain is makes it not-bad.

What got me here is pain asymbolia, as I understand it: patients, typically after insular damage, who will tell you the stimulus hurts and can localise it, call it sharp or burning, even rate its intensity, but show no withdrawal, no distress, no guarding, and no avoidance afterwards — some apparently joke about it. To me that looks like a clean dissociation: the sensory-discriminative side survives, the badness is gone. Which is supposed to show that badness is a separate ingredient sitting on top of the sensation, not built into sensing anything at all.

So — what would count against this? Specifically I want:

- the empirical exposure of leaning on asymbolia at all. My worry (possibly overblown) is that I'm building a general claim about the nature of pain on a handful of mid-century case reports with inconsistent lesion pictures and no clean modern replication, and that the "they feel it but don't mind it" reading rests on trusting self-reports from patients whose introspective reporting is exactly what's under suspicion. What specific findings about asymbolic patients would actually undercut me — say, evidence that they do protect and avoid in some contexts, or that their intensity ratings drift around?
- the cases the view struggles to describe. Pain in masochism and in sex; the cingulotomy patients who are said to still register the pain as pain but to have stopped minding it; the "hurts but I want more of it" of hard training; opioid or hypnotic analgesia where discrimination seems to survive. Which of these are real threats and which can I absorb without straining?
- whether the view risks anything at all. I notice I can always retreat to "then the unpleasantness just wasn't there, whatever they said." Is that insulating the position into unfalsifiability, and if so is that fatal here or just normal for claims about phenomenal character? Be honest about which.
- a thought experiment I invented, tell me if it does what I think: suppose you produced, in an intact volunteer, a perfect mimic of the asymbolic profile — full discrimination of location, quality and intensity, total indifference, no after-the-fact avoidance — while demonstrating that their affective systems are otherwise fine (they're still disgusted by smells, still wince at disgusting images, still crave and resent normally). Does that vindicate my view, or has my view quietly been redefined so that "unpleasantness" just means "whatever makes you mind"?
- and what a committed defender of the felt-quality view would themselves concede counts against it, versus what they'd wave off.

Give me the bottom line up front — is this view well insulated, badly exposed, or somewhere in between, and where's the softest spot — then a markdown table ranking the defeaters from most to least damaging with exactly these columns:

| defeater | kind (empirical / conceptual / report-based) | what would have to be true for it to bite | severity (fatal / serious / awkward / cosmetic) | would defenders concede it? | best available reply |

Then close with two or three sentences on the single thing that would most change the overall picture. No citations or reading lists please, I'm offline and just want the reasoning — I care much more about getting the distinction between fatal and merely awkward right than about coverage.
