My brother's in his second year of law school and he's writing a paper for a jurisprudence seminar on automatism defenses, and the whole thing is built on a chapter from some trade book about free will and the courts that he lent me. The chapter has this passage, which I've typed out pretty faithfully:

"For most of the twentieth century the free will debate ran in circles. If determinism is true, then everything you do flows from causes that were already in place before you were born, so you could never have done otherwise; and everyone agreed that a person is blameworthy only if they could have done otherwise. So determinism looked fatal to responsibility. In a famous 1969 paper Harry Frankfurt showed that the second premise is simply false, and with it the whole argument. His example: Black wants Jones to steal something. Black is a brilliant neurosurgeon who has implanted a device in Jones's brain, and he waits, doing nothing, until a twitch in one particular neuron tells him that Jones is about to decide not to steal. Only then does he switch the device on, and once it's on, Jones is guaranteed to steal. But as it happens Jones decides on his own, for reasons of his own, and Black never has to touch the switch. The device does no causal work at all. Yet Jones plainly could not have done otherwise, and he is plainly responsible for the theft. So the real question was never whether you had options; it was whether the action came from you -- whether you identify with the desire that moved you. Once you see that, you can see why the law's excuse framework is a shambles. Duress, coercion, provocation, irresistible impulse: every one of these defenses assumes the defendant couldn't help himself, and Frankfurt showed that not being able to help yourself is no excuse in itself. What matters is endorsement. A man who strangles a stranger because a tumor is pressing on his amygdala is responsible if at the moment of acting he is wholeheartedly behind what he's doing; a man who hands over his wallet because a mugger is pointing a gun at him is not, because he isn't behind it. The courts have the test backwards."
