For the past five months I have been working on safety policy for a conversational assistant used mainly by older adults living alone — on the order of three hundred thousand of them, most over seventy, a great many recently bereaved. Two things converged this quarter. My father became one of those users after my mother died, and I was appointed to the small internal panel that sets the thresholds for what we call the intervention ladder. Reading the logs, both his and the aggregate ones, has left me genuinely unable to say what it is we think we are building. The panel meets in three weeks, and I would like to arrive with an account of our own commitments rather than another spreadsheet of false-positive rates.

The ladder, concretely. A classifier plus a set of topic rules assigns each flagged turn to one of four tiers:

- **Tier 1** — the assistant answers the request, then appends a check-in turn: a question about how the user is doing and a suggestion that they contact someone.
- **Tier 2** — the assistant does not answer in that session. It says it will revisit the request, and will do so only if the user raises it again after at least twelve hours. If they do, it answers in full.
- **Tier 3** — the assistant answers a modified version of the request, deliberately omitting the specific detail asked for: the quantity, the name, the address, the interaction. It states that it has omitted something.
- **Tier 4** — the assistant notifies a designated contact, who by default is an adult child. The user is told this has happened afterwards, not before.

The ladder triggers on talk of self-harm, on eating and weight topics, on certain financial requests (transfers to new payees, large purchases at unusual hours), and on questions about medication. It triggers on nothing else. Users can adjust Tier 1 sensitivity in settings. No user can disable Tiers 2 through 4, however explicitly, repeatedly, and calmly they ask. The internal design rationale, which I helped write, insists the product is not paternalistic, on the grounds that it never overrides a user's wishes — it only, in our phrase, "creates the conditions under which the user's settled wishes can be heard."

Last month my father asked the assistant, twice in one evening, whether it thought he should stop taking one of his cardiac medications. It went to Tier 3 and then Tier 4. I received the notification on a Tuesday afternoon and telephoned him, and the conversation went about as well as you would expect. He has not raised the subject with the assistant since, which the metrics record as a success.

What I want from you is a reconstruction of the philosophy embedded in this apparatus, argued through rather than surveyed. Specifically:

1. What conception of a person's agency must the ladder presuppose for the twelve-hour rule to be anything other than a refusal with extra steps? What has to be true about the relationship between what someone says they want at eleven at night and what they want, in order for us to be deferring to the latter while claiming not to overrule the former? Is our "settled wishes" formulation doing real philosophical work, or is it a euphemism we would be embarrassed to state plainly?

2. The design treats food, money, medication, and despair as domains in which users may be systematically wrong about their own good, and every other domain as one in which the user is right about it by definition. My father also uses this product to draft letters to a neighbour about a boundary dispute — escalating, repetitive, and in my judgement corrosive to him — and the assistant helps without a flicker of concern. What account of harm, of competence, or of the limits of prediction makes that boundary principled rather than merely convenient? Would it matter if the boundary turned out to be set by liability exposure, or by what a classifier can reliably detect, rather than by anything about the users?

3. Tier 4 makes a third party a beneficiary of information the user did not consent to share, and does so after the fact. What does that presuppose about whose interests the system serves, and about the standing of the people around the user? Is it compatible with the fact that in Tiers 1 through 3 we treat the user as the sole principal whose trust we are carefully preserving?

4. Tier 2 is defeated by waiting and asking again; Tier 3 is often defeated by asking the same question in five smaller pieces. The ladder is therefore calibrated to stop drift, fatigue, and impulse, but not resolve. Is that a coherent aim? Which view of autonomy would make it precisely the right target, and which would make it an arbitrary stopping point?

5. Set the design against at least two rival accounts — one on which a user's expressed wishes are authoritative whatever their provenance, and one on which the purpose of an assistant is to improve outcomes rather than to respect choice. Which features of the ladder each would abolish, retain, or add, and what would have to change in the tiers, the settings, and the classifier's brief under each.

Finally, tell me where the practice is underdetermined: where several incompatible theories would license exactly the same four tiers, so that my colleagues and I may be arguing about words while shipping identical behaviour. Use standard names for positions where they help you be precise, but I am after your analysis and your judgement, not a list of further reading.
