For most of my career I have sat on the committees that write numeric cutoffs into rules: the value of a marker above which a condition is diagnosed, the asset figure below which an institution is exempt from a reporting requirement, the permitted deviation in a manufactured part, the concentration at which a substance counts as a contaminant. Two features of that work have come to seem connected to me, and I have not found a term that covers their combination.

The first feature is that the arbitrariness of the number is never in dispute, and is never in dispute openly. Nobody on these committees thinks there is a relevant difference between the case that falls one unit below the line and the case that falls one unit above it. The number is picked out of a stretch of the continuum where nothing changes, and it is picked for reasons of cost, administrative convenience, and caution. Committee members say this to each other's faces, and the minutes record it.

The second feature is that the number, once it has been in force for a while, becomes nearly impossible to move — and the resistance does not scale with the size of the proposed change. A body will spend years on a proposal to shift a long-standing threshold by one unit, in either direction, while conceding throughout that the unit makes no difference to anyone's underlying situation. What has changed is not what anyone believes about the continuum. What has changed is how much is now hanging off the figure: eligibility rules keyed to it, registries, reimbursement categories, inspection gauges and supplier contracts machined to it, forms with the number printed on them, longitudinal studies whose cohorts were defined by it, and the self-descriptions of the people who find themselves on one side of it. Each of those was indexed to the exact value, so the value has acquired something like a constituency.

There is also an asymmetry I have never seen explained. A newly adopted threshold can sometimes be moved a long way almost without argument, on the grounds that nobody has adjusted to it yet. An old threshold resists a one-unit move more stubbornly than a new one resists a ten-unit move. It is the age of the boundary, not the distance it would travel, that predicts how expensive revision will be.

I would like to know whether this configuration has an established name, in philosophy or in any of the fields that study rules, measurement, and classification. I am not looking for a bare label, and I want to head off three answers that are near this but are not it. I am not describing the general fact that conventions persist because departing from them is costly for everyone: the point here is that the cost is generated by the exact value rather than by the existence of some shared value, so that the convention becomes more rigid the more precisely it is followed. I am not describing the gradual widening or narrowing of a term's extension over time; the extension here does not drift, it is fixed and then fortified. And I am not describing the familiar worry that a sequence of individually negligible steps can carry you somewhere you would never go in one leap — although it strikes me that this runs in reverse in my cases, because the negligible steps have already been taken and it is their accumulated residue that now blocks any revision at all.

So: if one or more established concepts fit, tell me which, and for each, what it explains here and where it runs out, and how the phenomenon divides up among them if it does. If nothing fits, coin a term and define it, and give me its edges rather than a slogan. In particular I want to know what would look like this but not be it — where it shades into ordinary conservatism about rules, and whether the open acknowledgment of arbitrariness is essential to the thing or incidental to it. My instinct is that it is essential, that a threshold everyone believes to be principled behaves quite differently when it is challenged, but I may be wrong about that and I would like the question taken up directly.

Finally, tell me whether any of this has philosophical weight or whether it is only institutional mechanics. We have a boundary in a continuum that everyone agrees is not marked out by the world, and yet over decades it accumulates much of the practical character of a boundary that is. Does a history of use supply something that the continuum itself does not, such that after twenty years the question of which side a given case falls on has a better answer than it had on the day the number was chosen? Or does the accumulation change everything about how the rule operates and nothing about what it means? I would rather be told plainly that it has no bearing than be assured that it does.
