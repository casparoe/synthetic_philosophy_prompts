I write about science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and I am preparing a long piece on how societies assign blame for catastrophes, with the outbreak of the First World War as my central case. A friend sent me an essay from a weekend review section that purports to settle the German historiographical quarrel of the early 1960s, and I want to know how much of it I can build on before I import it into my own argument by paraphrase. The relevant passage runs as follows: Fischer's case rested on documents rather than rhetoric. In September 1914 the German government adopted the Septemberprogramm, an official statement of war aims calling for the subordination of Belgium, the annexation of Luxembourg and the Briey iron basin, a German-dominated Mitteleuropa, and a contiguous colonial empire across central Africa. Because the programme was already in hand when the war began, it shows what the German leadership intended from the outset; and the intention reaches further back still, to the crown council of December 1912, at which Wilhelm II and his generals resolved to force a war within two years, before Russian rearmament made victory impossible. Fischer's achievement was to demonstrate that Article 231 of the Treaty of Versailles, the so-called war guilt clause, was not victors' propaganda but a sober finding, reached in 1919 and confirmed from the German archives in 1961. His critics were chiefly defenders of national honour, and the debate has since been settled in his favour: no serious historian now denies that Germany planned and willed the war, however much they quibble about how much of Europe it hoped to take.

My instinct is that the first half is broadly sound and the closing sentences are smug, but I cannot say where exactly the summary departs from the record, and I would rather not repeat its errors in a different register. So please go through the passage claim by claim and tell me which parts a historian working on this controversy would accept, which are defensible simplifications for a general reader, and which are misrepresentations. Three points in particular I cannot resolve on my own. The first is the status of the Septemberprogramm: was it adopted government policy, or something weaker, and does the essay's inference, from a memorandum composed in September 1914 to what the German leadership wanted in July 1914, commit an error about the direction in which such a document can carry evidence? The second is the December 1912 meeting: what is actually known about who was present, what was said, and whether anything was decided, and is the essay reporting a settled fact or silently taking one side in an argument it never flags? The third is Article 231: is it fair to describe that clause as a verdict of moral guilt, and does anything true in the summary support the leap from a finding about German war aims to a vindication of the treaty's language about responsibility?

Beyond the sentence-level audit I would like your judgment on two larger questions the essay slides past. Does evidence about what a belligerent wanted once fighting was under way license conclusions about what caused the fighting to start, or are those two enquiries separable in principle, such that a historian could accept everything Fischer showed about aims and still reject his account of the July Crisis? And when we say that a nation is responsible for a war, what is the actual object of the ascription: a government, a general staff, a chancellor and his advisers, a milieu of pressure groups and mobilised opinion, or some composite of these, and does the essay's usage depend on running those candidates together? I also want to know whether its claim that the debate is settled survives scrutiny, and where a German critic of the 1960s and a sceptic writing today would press the thesis differently, since those are two distinct kinds of objection and my piece should not conflate them.

Finally, if two or three sentences of the passage could be rewritten so that it becomes fair without becoming bland, give me your version of them, and tell me plainly whether I may cite this essay's characterisation of the controversy as an example of how the argument is retold in public, or whether it is the sort of thing I should avoid repeating at all. Please answer in continuous prose only, with no headings, no bullet points, no bold and no tables, the way you would write a letter; when you work through the passage claim by claim, do it in ordinary paragraphs, naming the clause you are addressing in the sentence rather than setting it apart as an item.
