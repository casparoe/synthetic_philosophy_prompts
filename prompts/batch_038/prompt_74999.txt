A moral philosopher who defends a contemporary version of Taurek's claim that the numbers should not count will take questions from the floor at the end of her departmental lecture, and I am chairing that session. The audience is a mixed one of faculty and graduate students, so the questions may be technical, but they must be ones she can begin to answer aloud within a minute or two. Her version of the position is not the standard one, and the questions need to engage it on its own terms. In outline, she holds the following four claims:

1. Losses cannot be summed across persons, so five deaths do not constitute a single greater loss than one death, and there is consequently nothing that a rescuer fails to minimize by declining to save the five.
2. What each claimant is owed is not survival but an equal chance; hence, where randomization is possible, a fifty-fifty lottery between the one and the five is the only permissible way of deciding.
3. The requirement to randomize binds individual rescuers but not institutions. A hospital, ministry, or disaster agency may allocate to the greater number outright, because the duty to give equal chances has no institutional analogue and an institution is not itself a person who owes anything.
4. Saving the five is in any case merely permissible rather than required, and the widespread intuition that it is obviously the right thing to do is explained by the fact that we nearly always act through institutional arrangements rather than as lone rescuers.

Please draft nine or ten questions for the question-and-answer period, each paired with exactly one follow-up. The follow-up should be keyed to the answer she is most likely to give and should press the position she will be tempted to retreat into, rather than restating the original question in other words. No opening pleasantries, and no question that invites her to summarize the view again.

The set must include at least one question on each of the following topics; please label each question with the topic it addresses:

- Whether claim 1 is a substantive thesis about the world or merely a recommendation to abandon an aggregative vocabulary, and what would distinguish those two readings of it in practice.
- The challenge that a fifty-fifty lottery is itself a way of discounting the five, since it converts five certain survivals into five half-chances, and that any view taking each claimant's prospects seriously from the outset would end up counting the numbers.
- Whether claim 3 survives the observation that institutions are nothing over and above individuals acting jointly, and whether the division of labor she proposes amounts to conceding that the numbers count in precisely those cases of practical consequence.
- Cases of extreme disparity in stakes — a single death against an enormous number of very minor harms — and whether she can decline to count the numbers there without relying on a threshold of relevant similarity that performs the very aggregative work she has disowned.
- The charge that a view which refuses to aggregate cannot give stable verdicts across a chain of cases differing only slightly in the number or magnitude of the harms at stake.
- How she can explain, without appealing to the greater loss, why we think the rescuer who saves the five has done better rather than merely done something permitted.
- What particular case or line of argument she would accept as refuting her position rather than as something she could merely resist.

Finally, order the questions so that the sequence builds. Begin with ones she can answer comfortably, so that the later ones can turn her own earlier concessions against her; where a question depends on something she will have conceded earlier in the sequence, note that dependency in brackets so I know the ordering matters.
