I TA a discussion section for an undergrad jurisprudence class — 24 students, 80 minutes, week 5, statutory interpretation, textualism vs purposivism. They've done the reading and can define both -isms cold on a quiz, but in section they keep collapsing the whole dispute into "should judges be activist or not," and last year no amount of me talking moved them. This year I want them to run into the problem instead of being told about it.

I've already half-invented the fixture and I want the activity built around it. The town of Kessler passes, 4-3: "No person shall operate a sound amplification device within 300 feet of an occupied dwelling between the hours of 10:00 p.m. and 7:00 a.m." I also have a fake committee minute where the sponsor explains the bill was really about one guy who played recorded rooster crowing out his window at 4:40 every morning, plus two versions of the ordinance — one with a "Findings and purpose" preamble bolted on front ("to protect the sleep and quiet of residents at night") and one with no preamble at all. Giving different groups different pieces of that kit is part of the point, so build it in.

Design the whole thing: roles, a ladder of about six cases to apply the ordinance to (flavor I want — a white noise machine in a nursery window, somebody with a megaphone at 2am warning the street about a gas leak, a kid on speakerphone in a driveway with a relative overseas — you build the rest and order them so the last one breaks whatever principle they'd settled on), who rules on what and in what sequence, a points scheme with stakes they actually care about that doesn't need real money (I can convert points into participation credit), the handout text I need to print, and a minute-by-minute run of the 80 minutes.

The one thing I want the rules to produce rather than my mouth: the same student turns out to be a hard-assed textualist when they're writing rules for other people to apply, and a purposivist the second somebody else's rules are being applied to them. My guess is that falls out if the drafting round comes after they've adjudicated, but tell me if I'm wrong and how to force the reversal if the class doesnt stumble into it on its own.

Then a debrief that maps specific moments from the run onto the actual disagreement — I want them to see that "what the words mean" and "what the drafters expected would happen under these words" came apart in at least two of the cases, and to notice whether the purpose they invoked was found in the materials or smuggled in — plus a blunt list of what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it (24 people, half of them eyeing the clock, and at least one group will absolutely try to game the points), and an honest note on which feature of the real debate the game just cannot reproduce and I'll have to supply myself. Please don't build it around "no vehicles in the park," theyve all seen that one.
