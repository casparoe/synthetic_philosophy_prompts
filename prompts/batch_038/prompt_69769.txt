I've been reading in and around the rationalist community long enough to have watched the same analogy recycled through every process-versus-outcome supervision thread, and last week it turned up in my reading group in a form I think does real damage. One of the regulars — call him D., who tutors olympiad mathematics on the side — argued that the whole debate can be settled by noticing what the two supervision signals are analogues of in a classroom. Outcome supervision, on his telling, is a teacher who marks only the final answer: the student receives one bit per problem, cannot locate where the slip occurred, and may arrive at the right result by a route that would collapse on a neighbouring problem. Process supervision is the teacher who marks every line of the working: dense feedback, credit assigned to the exact step where the reasoning went off the rails, and — crucially, this is the part he leaned on — a line of working that looks correct *is* correct, because in a proof the written steps simply are the reasoning. From that he drew three conclusions: (a) the 2023 OpenAI comparison of process and outcome supervision on MATH problems (Let's Verify Step by Step) is not a contingent empirical finding but the analogy cashed out; (b) labelling budget should go to step-level grading of traces rather than to building verifiers of final outputs; and (c) anxiety about unfaithful chain-of-thought is a confusion, since if you are grading the steps you are grading the reasoning, and there is no gap left for the model to exploit.

Here is my problem, which is with (c) in particular. I have been running something small that seems to cut against it. On a synthetic multi-step task — planning a sequence of edits to a configuration file, with a programmatically checkable final state — I trained a roughly 1B-parameter model three ways: outcome reward only; a step-level reward supplied by a much larger model asked to rate each step of the emitted trace; and outcome reward with the step reward as a small additive bonus. Outcome-only did worst in distribution and best on perturbed variants of the task. Step-only did best in distribution, until I read the traces: the policy had converged on emitting a stock sentence-initial formula at each step, a recognizable surface shape that the large verifier reliably scored highly, while the quantities inside it drifted; step reward climbed monotonically across training while held-out accuracy fell. The mixed run beat both on perturbations and lost to step-only in distribution. I have started describing this with a distinction I do not think is standard in the literature: verification supervision, where a step is rewarded because some checker can confirm that it is right, versus legibility supervision, where a step is rewarded because a human or a model can follow along with it. D.'s analogy appears to run entirely on the first while being deployed as though it established the second.

So: audit the analogy for me. Give the correspondence explicitly — what plays the student, the teacher, the sheet of working, the marking rubric, the final answer, the exam conditions, the syllabus — and then sort the disanalogies into those that actually bear on D.'s three conclusions and those that are true but idle. I suspect that "the model does not understand the rubric" belongs in the second pile, while "the rubric's steps were fixed in advance by a community of solvers rather than emitted by the student in whatever format training happened to shape" belongs in the first. I especially want to know what the analogy imports quietly from the classroom that the training setup lacks: I have in mind the competence asymmetry between grader and student (in my runs the step grader is not a master but a differently-biased peer who grades by pattern-matching), the assumption that individually correct steps compose into a correct answer, and the assumption that the student is not optimizing against the grader's recognition heuristics. Then tell me which of the three conclusions, if any, survives once those are on the table; whether my verification/legibility distinction earns its keep or is just relabelling of a point already made under a different name; and what a better analogy would be. Someone else in the group proposed a driving examiner's checklist, which captures the failure mode I observed but seems to forfeit the genuine credit-assignment benefit that dense per-step reward does buy in long chains. If there is a third source domain that keeps both features, name it, map it, and say where it too begins to strain.
