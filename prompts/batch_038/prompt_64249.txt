Been going back and forth on the EV/fanaticism stuff for like twelve years now — the mugging threads, the hinge-of-history arguments, the whole "is it acceptable to trade a dust speck in an eye for a googolplex of happy lives" genre — and I've finally assembled an argument that I find convincing and also completely cannot live with. Help me find the bad step, or tell me which premise to give up and what I'm paying for it.

Setup, so we're not arguing about sci-fi: (i) I'm an expected-value maximizer about total wellbeing, no weird utility functions; (ii) the future is finite but potentially enormous — say up to 10^16 lives of decent quality, so the best realistic case is worth V_max units and present-day outcomes are worth vastly less; (iii) I'm a fairly ordinary person with a moderately useful skillset, not a billionaire and not a lead researcher anywhere.

The argument:

1. I should take the option with the highest expected value, where expected value means the probability-weighted sum of outcome values, and I'm assuming that sum is well-defined for the options I'm comparing.
2. Any option that shifts the probability of a huge-future scenario by some nonzero amount δ contributes δ · (V_max − V_near) to its expected value.
3. As long as δ > 0, that term beats any bounded quantity once the future is big enough. I'm deliberately not invoking infinite futures or infinite value here — just the finite ones people actually take seriously in these debates — so the usual "infinities make everything undefined" escape doesn't apply.
4. My ordinary options change only bounded amounts of value: the career that helps identifiable people, the donation to the global-health charity with good trial data, the weekend volunteering. Call that at most ~10^6 units, with δ ≈ 0, since nothing I do there moves the probability of civilization having 10^16 good lives by any amount I could estimate.
5. So for any ordinary option O and any tail-affecting option A: EV(A) − EV(O) ≥ δ_A · (V_max − 10^6), which is astronomically positive even if δ_A is 10^-20.
6. A gap that large doesn't just mean A is better, it means O is irrelevant — the ratio of O's contribution to A's goes to zero, so any regret about O is a rounding error in the ledger.
7. Therefore I should put essentially all my effort into maximizing δ and treat my job, my giving, my relationships and the specific people I could help this year as morally negligible.
8. And since more exotic interventions have more room for large δ, this pushes me toward the speculative longshots, while never letting me be accused of neglecting anyone — because on the math, nobody in the present is really there to neglect.

I don't believe 7 or 8 and I don't want to. I don't want to give up 1 either: I've read too many risk-aversion-is-incoherence arguments to just say "I'm risk averse about big stakes," and I know the calibration-theorem results, though I also know those are usually deployed the other way, to say small-stakes caution commits you to big-stakes fanaticism — which is exactly where I'm stuck. If I have to choose I'd much rather give up 3 or 4 than 7, but 4 feels like the empirical claim I'm least sure of, and 5 and 6 just look like arithmetic to me.

So: which step is broken? A few things I want you to actually engage with rather than gesture at. If your answer is that my δ isn't a real 10^-20 but a made-up number with error bars wider than itself, then tell me how anyone is supposed to be a coherent EV maximizer under model uncertainty, because that move feels like it dissolves expected-value reasoning everywhere and not just here. If your answer is that the expectation isn't well-defined, tell me which of my options gets to stay well-defined and which doesn't, and why the ordinary ones escape. If you think the slide is 6→7 — that "bigger EV" doesn't license "everything else is irrelevant" — then explain what decision procedure replaces it, because I genuinely don't see how you keep 1 and get anything other than 7. And if your answer is that δ shrinks as V grows, so the two can't be varied independently, say why that's a fact about the world rather than a fact about my overconfidence.
