I am drafting the decision rules for a five-member panel that awards small conservation grants, and before the charter goes to the trustees I would like a consistency check on the package of commitments I have written into it.

The panel's agenda has four questions. N: the proposed method is genuinely novel rather than an incremental replication of work the trust has already funded. B: the project has demonstrated benefit for the communities it affects. R: the applicant falls under the trust's legacy provision, which reserves a second route to funding for first-time applicants from regions the trust has historically underfunded. F: the grant is awarded. The charter states that F holds if and only if either both N and B hold, or R holds. Members vote on each of the four questions separately and are expected to vote sincerely.

My commitments are these.

(a) Admissibility. Each member's own judgment set is consistent and complete with respect to the four questions, and no profile of member judgments is ruled out in advance as impossible, or as too improbable to legislate for.

(b) Collective rationality. Whatever the panel records must itself be consistent and complete, and must respect the charter's biconditional linking F to N, B, and R.

(c) Anonymity. Every member's vote carries exactly the same weight: no casting vote for the chair, no seniority weighting, no rotating tie-breaker.

(d) Uniform treatment of questions. The verdict recorded on each question is determined solely by the votes cast on that question, and one and the same counting standard applies to all four questions.

(e) I am confident that (a) through (d) can all be kept. My reason is that the impossibility results in this literature are said to bite only when an agenda's questions are logically interlinked, and ours is not: N, B, and R can each be settled without reference to the others, and the legacy route was designed to be independent of the merit route. I take that separability to put us outside the reach of the theorems.

(f) The operating procedure written into the charter is premise priority: the panel votes N, then B, then R, and F is derived from those tallies rather than put to a direct vote.

(g) Responsiveness on the bottom line. Whenever a majority of members hold the same view about F, the verdict recorded on F must be that view.

(h) One deliberative voice. The panel must publish the reasoning behind its verdict, and that reasoning must be reconstructible as a line of argument at least one member actually endorses, so that the panel can be held to its reasons and not merely to its outcomes.

Please audit this package. Assume I already know the discursive dilemma and the general shape of the impossibility results; I want the audit, not a tutorial. Rank the genuine tensions by severity rather than listing them flat, and for each say which commitment has to give, what the cheapest revision is, and what that revision costs me in practice. Where two commitments only look as though they conflict, say so and name the distinction that dissolves the appearance — I would rather not weaken a commitment that is doing no harm. Two things I cannot work out myself: whether (e) really does shield (a) through (d), and whether (f) and (g) can both survive once the legacy route is on the agenda alongside (h).

Close by telling me what would change your verdict: which consideration would lead you to rank these tensions differently or reverse one of your calls, and where you think your own reasoning is most likely to have gone wrong, including whether you have read the charter's biconditional the way I intend it. Keep the entire response under 500 words, with the ranked tensions as a numbered list.
