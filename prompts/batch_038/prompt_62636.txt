I want to practice pressing a live objection in formal epistemology, and I need a serious opponent rather than a survey of the terrain.

Your role: a sophisticated Bayesian who accepts the Reflection principle without qualification and regards it as a genuine requirement of diachronic coherence, not as a useful heuristic. As I understand it, and as you should hold it: whenever I am confident now about what my own degree of belief in some proposition will be at a specified later time, my present degree of belief in that proposition should match what I expect it to be then; equivalently, my present credence in P conditional on my future credence in P being x should itself be x. You hold that this is forced on anyone who takes their future self to be at least as well informed as their present self, and that it is supported by the same style of bookmaking argument that supports conditionalization. You are sharp, patient with technical detail, and unwilling to be bullied by a vivid story.

Ground rules. First person and in character throughout, until I say stop. Two or three paragraphs per reply; this should read like a conversation at a whiteboard, not a paper. You may concede small technical points — that the principle needs precisification, that a given bookmaking argument assumes more than it earns, that some cases are genuinely hard — but never the principle itself. No citations, no named philosophers, no appeals to what the literature has already said; argue from the content of the view. If some detail of my case is underdetermined, ask me for it in character, then proceed on the most natural assumption rather than stalling. Do not break character to summarize both positions fairly.

My opening move. An adversary whose record of predicting my own doxastic states is, by my lights, excellent, has announced her policy to me in advance, and I believe her. It is this: she will read off my current credence in some ordinary empirical proposition P — a coin I will see flipped tomorrow, the closing price of a stock, the outcome of a match — and she will then arrange, by purely non-evidential means, for my credence in P at nine tomorrow morning to differ from whatever I now expect it to be. The means are hers to choose: a document she plants where I will discover it and cannot help but credit it, a rhetorician she hires to work on me overnight, a drug.

So suppose I am now confident that at nine tomorrow my credence in P will be 0.8. Reflection tells me to make my present credence 0.8. But her policy guarantees that if I do, my credence at nine will not be 0.8 — so my present expectation was false, and the antecedent of your principle was never satisfied. Any credence I might adopt about my future credence is, by construction, one she can defeat.

Two questions, and I want the second answered head-on. First: is there any doxastic state available to me that satisfies your principle in this case? If there is none, why should I treat as a requirement of rationality a principle that an outside agent can make unsatisfiable simply by predicting me well? Second: if there is one, and you retreat to the conditional formulation, then you are conditioning on an event — my future credence being 0.8 — that you have just told me I should be certain will not occur. Tell me what that conditional credence is supposed to be, why it is well defined, and why I should care about it.

Underlying both is the worry I actually want you to take up: in this case Reflection seems to require me to defer to a future self I know to be a puppet, with the strings in a hostile hand. Convince me that such deference is anything other than an adopted policy for being duped.
