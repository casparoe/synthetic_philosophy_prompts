In a forecasting forum I read, someone defending Malthus against the usual dismissal insisted that he was never wrong, merely early: the checks he described kept operating, they simply migrated from famine and pestilence to the preventive check of restraint and delayed marriage that looms larger in the 1803 revision, and the demographic transition was the theory vindicating itself on schedule. Another user replied that a theory which absorbs two centuries of failed predictions by re-describing them as confirmations has stopped being a theory. I can sense that both are gesturing at something real, but I can't articulate the disagreement.

Treat "right but early" as a defence of a long-run prediction and lay out, as a menu, the distinct claims it might carry — mechanism identified but parameters off; mechanism genuine yet suspended by a discontinuity the theory had no way to anticipate; prediction still pending because no deadline has yet been reached — and for each say whether it is a platitude, substantive and defensible, substantive and false, or vacuous in that no observation could ever count against it. State what a theory must have committed to in advance for "early" to be a legitimate update rather than an ad hoc rescue, then apply the menu to Malthus against the actual record: population and food output both grew enormously, per-capita living standards rose instead of collapsing to subsistence, and yet some historians hold that his mechanism did bind before the Industrial Revolution. Which reading, if any, survives that record?

Close by saying which of your own verdicts you hold least firmly, and what consideration would change it.
