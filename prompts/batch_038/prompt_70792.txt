So — I volunteer at a tiny community radio station and I host a 45-minute show that's basically "me talking to whatever academic is in town." Next month my guest is a philosopher who's made her name arguing that we're much worse at knowing our own current conscious experience than the tradition assumes, and that "privileged access" is basically a fossil from a folk theory we should have buried. I've read a decent chunk of her work and I genuinely like it, but she's also extremely good on the mic, and if I just ask her to walk through the view I'll get a slick 30-minute summary and nothing else. I do NOT want a reading list or suggestions of what to read beforehand — I've done that part — and nothing along the lines of what drew her to the topic. What I want is a set of questions that are keyed to the actual content of the position and that make her standard escapes expensive to use.

For context, here are the three moves she keeps making, and I'd like the questions built so that each move stops working at some point:

1. When you bring up confabulation results — that old study where people pick from a row of near-identical stockings, overwhelmingly favor one end of the row, and then explain their choice by fabric quality and deny that position had anything to do with it; or the arousal-misattribution stuff — she says those are all retrospective reports about the past, and her claim is only about present experience, so they're beside the point.
2. When you suggest introspection might just be reliable-but-noisy, she says reliability only means anything relative to a comparison class, and that vision is unreliable in bad light too, so she's holding introspection to exactly the same standard we hold the senses to and nobody finds that scandalous.
3. When you point out that her own evidence for unreliability comes from what people say about their experience, she says the reports aren't being scored against other reports but against third-person measures, so there's no circularity.

Her positive view is also stranger than "people are just wrong a lot," and I want at least one question that lives there: she thinks a good many of the contested cases have no determinate answer to begin with — there's no fact about whether the periphery of your visual field right now has this or that specific color — so the subject who reports one isn't making an error, they're reporting into a gap. Beyond that, please make sure the set covers: (a) the self-undermining worry, i.e. how the unreliability thesis avoids sawing off the branch it's sitting on; (b) the difference between "you were wrong" and "there was nothing to be right or wrong about," and how a subject or an experimenter is supposed to tell those apart from the inside; (c) the trained-sampling response — people like Hurlburt think that if you drill subjects for weeks in how to report their own experience carefully, the reports get markedly better, which raises the question of whether what's left is still introspection or has turned into an acquired, theory-laden skill, and whether her pessimism isn't really just a claim about untrained reporters; and (d) the downstream stakes — what happens to blaming someone for the irritation they sincerely say they didn't notice, and to any therapy that runs on client self-report.

Here's the shape I need, in this order:

- **Her escape hatches** — about 120 words. Three or four bullets naming each deflection and giving me the one sentence that closes it off, so I can recognize it live when she does it.
- **The questions** — about 600 words, eight numbered items. Each is the question itself in two or three sentences, followed by a one-sentence follow-up under a "if she says…" label that's keyed to the answer she's most likely to give. I'd rather have eight sharp ones than twelve soft ones.
- **If I only get one** — about 60 words. Which single question I should spend the clock on if the interview is running long, and why that one.
- **How sure are you** — about 80 words. Tell me how confident you are that these are the pressure points the debate actually turns on rather than ones you've invented for symmetry, flag anywhere you think I've mis-stated her view or the experiments I mentioned, and single out the one question you're least sure is either fair to ask or possible to answer.
