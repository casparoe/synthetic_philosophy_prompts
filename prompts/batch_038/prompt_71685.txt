I'm preparing a session for an interdisciplinary seminar that pairs philosophy-of-science readings with novels; next week the pairing is the technical literature on truthlikeness with Umberto Eco's *The Name of the Rose*. The abbey generates three competing accounts of the deaths. Bernard Gui's: a heretic cellarer, aided by a girl charged with witchcraft, is to blame. Jorge's: the deaths follow the seven trumpets of Revelation and therefore belong to a divine plan — a correspondence he himself helped impose. William's: Adelmo killed himself, a stolen book's poisoned pages did for Venantius and Berengar, Malachi crushed Severinus at another monk's instigation and then touched the book himself, and the whole sequence turns on a hidden room in the library. William's account is nearest the truth, yet Jorge's has an impressive yield of true consequences wrapped around a false explanation, and Bernard Gui's is false and still the one the abbey acts on.

I'd like a reading that presses the technical question rather than the thematic one: does this ranking survive the well-known collapse of Popper's content-and-consequence definition — on which no false claim comes out more truthlike than another — and does a likeness-based account rank the three any differently, or differently only once a similarity metric has been chosen? Does Jorge's case expose something the likeness approach papers over, or only that truthlikeness is not the sole cognitive value at stake when a community decides what to believe? Please also be honest about where the lens strains: William's own late doubts about signs, and the fact that a false apocalyptic schema did real investigative work for him, suggest Eco may care less about closeness to the truth than about the impossibility of knowing from inside an inquiry that one has achieved it. End by saying what the novel can express about all this that the toy numerical examples of the literature cannot.
