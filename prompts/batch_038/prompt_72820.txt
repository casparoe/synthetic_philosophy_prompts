I cover science and ideas for a UK magazine — maybe a third of my time is psychology and linguistics — and I get pitched some version of "your language shapes how you think" about twice a month. Colour words, counting, direction, gendered nouns, tense, evidentials, whatever. Some of it is real, most of it is a single underpowered study with a press release doing a lot of the heavy lifting, and its not always obvious which, but I still have to make a call fast: is there a story here, and if so what kind (a finding, a controversy, a debunk, nothing)?

Right now my triage is basically vibes plus one rule I invented myself, which is: if the effect survives a verbal interference task — you know, people doing a concurrent language task or repeating nonsense while they perform the experiment — then it isn't really language, it's something deeper, and that's the interesting version. A linguist friend read that over and said it was backwards, or at least that I'd collapsed two very different claims into one. I'd like you to settle that as part of the job.

So: build me a procedure I can actually run. Flowchart in words, ideally — the questions in order, what counts as a pass on each one, where it terminates in "no story", where it terminates in "story, but you have to write it as unresolved", and where it hands the decision over to a human because the question isn't methodological at all. Ground it in whatever the linguistic relativity literature can genuinely support: the difference between language constraining what people can think and language changing what they habitually do inside a task; why a memory effect is not the same evidence as a perceptual one; when a contrast between two groups is linguistic rather than cultural (or just socioeconomic, or about who got tested where); which classes of result the field treats as reasonably robust and which it treats as one-study wonders. No references or reading lists please — I can't check them and I don't want invented ones. Describe the paradigms and the arguments instead.

Constraints so you can calibrate it properly: I get about twenty minutes with a pitch, often with only the press release or an unreviewed preprint, and no realistic chance of emailing the authors before deadline. Output is 800 words for readers who've never met a linguist, and house style requires at least one named researcher who disagrees with the paper. I can't run experiments myself, and I can't read a descriptive grammar of an endangered language to check whether the linguistic analysis in the paper is right.

Also be straight with me about which of your steps are judgement calls in a procedural costume, and about the failure modes — the false negatives especially, because a checklist that lets me kill every interesting result feels safe but isn't.

Then run it on three pitches, showing me the questions being answered rather than just the verdict:

1. A preprint, not peer reviewed yet. Speakers of a language that forces you to mark how you came to know something (saw it, heard it, inferred it) are better than English speakers at remembering the source of a witnessed event — who did it versus who they were merely told did it. Twenty-four participants per group, memory test delivered verbally after a delay. The comparison group is described as "English speakers", but reading further they turn out to be bilinguals who also speak the evidential language, recruited at the same university. Press release line: this language gives its speakers a better memory for the truth.

2. A press release about a brain-imaging study. Two language groups, seventeen people each, doing a non-verbal navigation task in the scanner; one group shows more activation in a parietal region; no difference in accuracy or reaction time. The release says their language "rewires the brain's GPS". The two languages differ in how they describe space, one of them favouring absolute compass-style directions.

3. The one my editor is already excited about, so brace yourself to ruin my week: a large cross-national study using decades of survey data and something like fifty languages, claiming that speakers of languages without a grammatical future tense measurably do more of some prudential thing — save more, smoke less, express more climate concern, whatever it turns out to be. Famous economist author, lots of coverage over the years, and a live news hook this month because of a policy row it supposedly speaks to.

Give me a verdict on each, plus what the piece would actually be if there is one — including for number three if the honest answer is that the story is the criticism rather than the result, or that there's no story at all.

Last thing, and it matters: tell me where this procedure is most likely to hand me a confident wrong answer, which of your thresholds you'd defend least if a decent methodologist pushed on it, and what specific evidence or argument would make you rewrite a step rather than just hedge it. Don't pad that bit — I'd rather have one sharp worry than a general disclaimer.
