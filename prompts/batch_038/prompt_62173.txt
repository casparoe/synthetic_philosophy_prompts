so I'm sitting in on a grad seminar about death this term — I'm the one in the room with no philosophy background, and also apparently the one who keeps drawing timelines on the whiteboard. Last week was the Epicurean stuff: the idea that death can't be a misfortune for the person who dies, because whenever death is there the subject isn't, so there's no one to be badly off. And the discussion kept getting sucked into the symmetry point, i.e. Lucretius's observation that the stretch of time before you were born is a mirror image of the stretch after you die, and yet we're indifferent about one and horrified by the other. Every time I pushed on it people reached for one of three exits: the past can't be changed so it can't be an object of any of our attitudes; our cares only reach forward because desires about the future are the kind of thing we can have; or, when they got really pushed, the non-identity line — you couldn't have been born earlier and still been you, so there's no same-person comparison to make. I want to find out whether the symmetry worry can actually be made to bite on a real case instead of a slogan, and I want it as an engineered scenario rather than an essay.

Here's what I want you to build: a thought experiment in which the pre-natal stretch is a genuine deprivation for one specific individual, in the same currency and roughly the same magnitude as an early death would be. So if a deprivation theorist is happy saying that dying at 30 costs a person the good years from 30 to 60, they should have to say of this individual that starting to exist in 2001 rather than 1985 cost them the good years from 1985 to 2001 — same goods, just at the other end. Constraints: keep it realistic, I'd rather the mechanism be something that genuinely happens in fertility clinics than anything exotic, so no time travel, no body-swapping, no adult cryonics; the scenario itself should be one tight paragraph and the analysis after it can run as long as it needs; the two versions of the case have to feature someone who is numerically the same person, and you need to tell me which account of personal identity you're leaning on to secure that and whether the case still works if that account is wrong; and please dont smuggle in any posthumous-harm material — slandered reputation, projects thwarted from beyond the grave, grieving family — because that's a different debate and I want the symmetry question isolated.

Say what the Epicurean says about the case and what the deprivation theorist says and why, and where exactly the symmetry argument gets its leverage once the non-identity escape is closed off. And if it's relevant, note whether your case makes the "when exactly is death bad, given there's no moment at which the person is worse off" issue better or worse for each side — but that's secondary, don't let it eat the main deliverable.
