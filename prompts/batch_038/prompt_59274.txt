I have worked as a neuro-ICU nurse for twelve years, and since the spring I have been seconded to my hospital's organ donation program as a liaison: I am now present for cases of controlled donation after circulatory death, I explain the sequence of events to families, and I sit on the ethics committee that reviews our protocols. The sequence has become entirely routine to me, which is exactly why I can no longer say what it means. A patient with a devastating, unsurvivable brain injury is not brain dead. Life-sustaining treatment is withdrawn — in our protocol, in the operating room itself, or in a room immediately adjacent to it — with the family present. When the heart stops, we wait. Our stand-off period is five minutes, during which nobody touches the patient and no attempt at resuscitation is made. At the end of that interval the surgeon declares death, a time is written down, and procurement begins at once: the body is still warm, the organs are still perfused, and heparin was given and vascular lines were placed before the arrest, for reasons that had nothing to do with the patient's comfort and everything to do with how well those organs will work in someone else. Meanwhile, in the next bay, a different patient — brain dead after a brainstem hemorrhage — is being kept on a ventilator, warm, with a beating heart, digesting feed, healing his incisions, mounting fevers against infection, and legally dead; he will remain legally dead for two more days while his family flies in.

Two features of this package trouble me, and I don't think they are the standard objections. First, the statute my state adopted provides that death is the irreversible cessation of circulatory and respiratory functions, or the irreversible cessation of all functions of the entire brain including the brainstem. But in the first kind of case nothing has been shown about the brain at all; we declare death because the circulation has stopped and because we are not going to restart it. In the second kind of case nothing has ceased about circulation; a machine is performing it, and we declare death on the strength of the brain. Each criterion appears to be invoked precisely where the other criterion's conditions are still being satisfied. When I raised this with one of our transplant surgeons, he said something I have not been able to shake: the requirement, he told me, is not irreversibility but permanence. Irreversible means the function could not be restored by any means; permanent means only that it will not be restored, because no one is going to try. On that reading the five minutes is not evidence about the body at all — it is evidence about us. Death in that moment becomes partly a fact about a decision we have made not to intervene, which is not what I thought a biological event could be, and I want to know what theory of death makes that move available rather than absurd. Second, our practice explicitly refuses the criterion that would make it most obviously coherent. Nobody here treats a patient in a persistent vegetative state as dead; an anencephalic newborn, with no cortex and an intact brainstem, is a patient owed treatment for as long as she lasts. And yet the words I use with the families of brain-dead patients are entirely about the person: that he is gone, that there is nobody there anymore, that this is not him.

So: reconstruct the theory of death this whole package presupposes. Which view makes it hang together — that death is the loss of the organism's capacity to function as an integrated whole, that it is the loss of the capacity for consciousness, that it is the loss of a soul or of psychic unity, that it is not an event but a process we cut at a point for administrative convenience, that it is a change in social and legal standing rather than in biology — and which of these does the practice strictly require as opposed to merely sitting comfortably with? Please keep those two things rigorously separate, because I suspect the practice is far more underdetermined than our committee assumes and that several mutually incompatible theories would license every step of it; I want to know exactly which steps are licensed by which. Then tell me what the practice rules out: which respectable accounts of death are simply unavailable to a hospital that does what we do, and whether the permanent/irreversible distinction smuggles in a commitment the committee never voted on — a conception of death as partly constituted by human intention, or a conception of the dying patient as already a donor. I would especially like to know whether pre-arrest anticoagulation and pre-arrest cannulation presuppose an intermediate status that is neither alive nor dead, and if so, what that status is, what it permits, and whether the double-effect reasoning we use to defend it depends on that status already being in place. Finally, set out what would have to change if the presupposition were replaced: the operating theatre schedule, the wording on the death certificate, the consent conversation, and my own sentences to a family, under a higher-brain criterion on the one hand, and under a scheme that abandons the requirement that the donor be dead and relies instead on authorization from the patient or the family on the other. I am not asking for references, a reading list, or the history of the controversy — I want the implicit theory laid out, tested against the parts of the practice that seem to contradict one another, and a clear map of where our practice is philosophically optional and where it is genuinely committed.
