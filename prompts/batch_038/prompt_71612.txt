So, context: I'm a software engineer, zero formal philosophy background, and for about six months I've been on a small team building what we internally call the "intent layer" of a consumer AI assistant. The idea is that the assistant shouldn't just do what you tell it in the moment — it's supposed to act on what you'd endorse if you actually had the time and clarity to think it through. Fine in a slide deck, absolute nightmare to spec. Our design doc now has five candidate implementations and I'm pretty sure they're not even answering the same question:

- **A ("the editor")** — only ever *removes* distortions: fatigue, being drunk, doomscrolling at 2am, misreading the UI, an impulse you'd disown ten minutes later. It never adds a value you haven't at least gestured at yourself.
- **B ("the better-informed twin")** — simulate this user fully informed about consequences, with their cognitive biases switched off and their appreciation of the options made vivid, then ask what that twin wants and go with it.
- **C ("the stranger")** — ask what a calm, impartial, extremely well-informed person who knows everything *about* this user's situation but doesn't share their psychology would want for them. Doesn't have to resemble you.
- **D ("the council")** — run a bunch of idealized versions, check whether they agree; if they do, act on the agreement, if they don't, fall back to whatever the actual user said.
- **E ("the extrapolator")** — aim at where the user's values are *heading* rather than where they are, including reflecting on their reflecting, so it can endorse things they'd only arrive at after a long stretch of further thought.

One of my coworkers kept name-dropping "coherent extrapolated volition" in the review and I genuinely cannot tell whether that's E or something else entirely, which is part of the problem. We're six engineers and a PM and we all think we agree on "respect the user's considered judgment" while shipping wildly different products.

Here's what I want from you. Act as a professional philosopher who has spent years teaching metaethics and value theory to people who build software, and think carefully about this before you start writing. First, in a few paragraphs of visible brainstorming, lay out the fork questions that actually separate these positions — the dimensions along which the logical space is carved. I care about things like: whether idealization is subtractive (strip out error) or additive (import values you'd acquire); whether the idealized agent has to still be *you*, psychologically continuous with you, or can be a well-informed stranger; whether idealized deliberators should be expected to converge on the same answers or whether genuine divergence between two equally clear-headed versions of the same person is a live possibility; whether the user's dissent *right now* gets a veto; whether the target is what I'd want-on-reflection today versus what I'd want my future self to want; and whether the procedure recurses. Then turn that into the deliverable: a "which position are you actually holding?" quiz, nine questions, each one a concrete case from our product with two or three answer options, plus a scoring key mapping answer patterns onto positions.

Some requirements, because I've been burned by generic output. No question may just ask whether you think real desires or ideal ones matter, or anything else that lets someone answer by picking the label they like — every question has to be a scenario someone could answer without knowing where it leads. Use at least: a user who soberly told the assistant "never let me do X again" and who, six months later, calm and fully informed, wants X; a case where the better-informed twin wants something the actual user would find repellent and would be furious about; two equally informed, equally calm idealized versions of one person disagreeing with each other; a user who says "just do what I asked, I don't care what the smart version of me thinks"; and one case where *every* position gives the same answer, which I want included deliberately as a calibration/trust question — say so in the key. Each leaf of the taxonomy gets the name of the position it corresponds to, or "unnamed" if nobody seriously defends it, plus one sentence on its most awkward commitment. Build the leaves out of the combinations of answers first and then locate the actual positions in that space, including our five options A–E and anything nearby they don't cover. Mark the unoccupied combinations and diagnose each one: is nobody there because the view is incoherent, because it's unattractive, or because it just hasn't been explored? And if there's any pair of leaves your quiz can't really separate — I'm sure D and E are candidates — flag that explicitly rather than pretending it works. Don't give me a bibliography or a reading list, half the team is on a locked-down network and we can't look anything up anyway; I'd rather you describe the views accurately than hand me references we'll never open.
