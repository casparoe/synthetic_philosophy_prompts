**Q3 (20 marks).** In "What the Tortoise Said to Achilles" (*Mind*, 1895), Carroll has the Tortoise accept A, B, C and D while still refusing to accept Z. Explain what the Tortoise is demanding and what the episode shows about the relation between premises and rules of inference. Then say whether you think the difficulty can be got round, and argue for your answer.

Rubric we were given:
- 6 marks for reconstructing what generates the series (why adding C doesn't do the job, and why adding D doesn't either)
- 6 marks for stating the point about rules of inference vs. premises
- 8 marks for the student's own proposal — argued rather than asserted, and actually engaging with what the Tortoise is refusing to do

**Student answer:**

[1] Carroll sets it up with a bit of Euclid. A is the general principle, that things equal to the same are equal to each other; B applies it to the case at hand, that the two sides of this triangle are things equal to the same; Z is the conclusion, that the two sides of this triangle are equal to each other. The Tortoise says he wants to be considered a reader who accepts A and B but not the hypothetical C, and asks Achilles to force him logically to accept Z. Achilles writes C into the notebook. The Tortoise accepts it, then points out that going from A, B and C to Z is itself a step, so if he failed to see that step he could accept all three and still withhold Z — which gives us D. And D gives us E, and so on. The notebook fills up and Z never arrives.

[2] What's going wrong is a category mistake on Achilles's side. He assumes the Tortoise is missing information, so he keeps supplying information. But a rule of inference isn't a further item of information: it isn't the sort of thing that is true or false and can be entered on a list of what you accept. It's a licence to move. So no list of acceptances can be turned into a move however long you make it, because the thing that would do the turning is always the very thing the list leaves out. Each new hypothetical just joins the pile of things accepted, and a pile is not the kind of thing that compels.

[3] That's why the dialogue looks to me like the old trilemma about justification wearing a tortoise shell. Any reason offered for Z has to rest on something else, and that on something else again, and you have three bad options: stop at an arbitrary premise, come back round to where you started, or go on for ever. Achilles goes on for ever. The regress is vicious rather than merely infinite, because none of the entries he adds is any better justified than Z was — so at the end of an endless notebook he's exactly where he started.

[4] Can it be got round? I think it can, and the way round is to notice that Achilles is writing in the wrong format. In a sequent presentation the turnstile isn't a sentence at all; you can't write A, A→B ⊢ B *inside* the notebook, because what's on either side of the turnstile isn't a claim, and the licensing is done by the rules of the system, which sit outside it. There is no proposition the Tortoise could be asked to accept that would amount to accepting the rule, and that's precisely why his request is malformed — he's asking for an object of the wrong type. Seperate the rule language from the object language properly and the question he keeps asking has no formulation, so there's nothing left to refuse. The dialogue is a joke that depends on the notebook, not a paradox about deduction. Any system that can tell its rules from its premises is immune to the Tortoise.

[5] My guess at Carroll's own moral is that the last resort has to be something like a trained eye. At some point you just see that Z follows, and that seeing isn't another belief you could write down but a capacity, which is why no amount of argument can produce it in someone who lacks it. The Tortoise isn't being difficult, he's simply missing the capacity, and Achilles's mistake is expecting argument to supply what only practice can. That's also why the piece reads as funny rather than alarming: Carroll is showing us the limit of making logic fully explicit, not the collapse of logic.
