I am preparing a paper on the semantics of slurs for journal submission, and before I write it up I would like a rigorous audit of whether the position I have converged on is internally tenable. I would much rather find the fractures myself than have a referee find them. The view is best given as a set of commitments, each of which I hold independently and none of which I would have predicted would strain the others.

(C1) A slur and its neutral counterpart are truth-conditionally equivalent. "Alf is a Boche" is true in precisely the circumstances in which "Alf is German" is true. Nothing contributed by the slur makes any difference to whether the sentence is true.

(C2) The difference between the two sentences is nevertheless semantic rather than pragmatic. It is encoded by convention in the lexical item, not inferred: it is not recoverable from general conversational principles, from the speaker's evident intentions, or from what the audience expects. A hearer who does not know the word cannot work the derogation out.

(C3) The derogatory component is not at-issue content. The sentence is about whatever its neutral counterpart is about, and a bare denial ("that's false") targets the neutral counterpart's content, not the derogation. There is, strictly speaking, no such thing as contradicting a slur.

(C4) The derogatory component is nonetheless projective and non-displaceable. It survives embedding under negation, in the antecedent of a conditional, in questions, and in attitude reports; and wherever it surfaces it surfaces as an attitude of the person whose mouth it comes out of, rather than being shiftable onto someone else or modulated by further features of the context of utterance.

(C5) Reclamation is genuine and not merely apparent. When members of the target group use the word among themselves, the derogation is absent from what is said — not suspended, quoted, pretended, or ironized — and those uses are fully correct uses of the word, on a par with any other, differing only in who is licensed to perform them.

(C6) Meaning is fixed by the standing social practice rather than by any individual. What the word means depends on the norms governing its use in a community: who may deploy it, against whom, and to what end. Users can violate those norms and can be mistaken about them, but no individual use, however deviant, alters the meaning; only the practice as a whole can do that, and the practice as a whole changes slowly, if at all.

(C7) The wrongness of a slur-use is explained by the meaning of the word alone. I want no appeal to the word's history, to the speaker's attitudes, to the likelihood of causing offence, or to any social expectation in order to explain why uttering the slur rather than its neutral counterpart is objectionable. Whatever does the explaining must be located in the semantics.

Please treat these as a package and tell me where it breaks. In particular:

1. Which of these commitments are genuinely in tension with one another, and which pairs merely look as though they are? I expect that at least one apparent conflict dissolves under inspection, and I want to be told why it dissolves rather than having it flagged along with the real problems.

2. Rank the genuine tensions by severity — where severity means how much of the rest of the view collapses if the tension is left unresolved — instead of presenting them as an undifferentiated list.

3. For each genuine tension, state the cheapest revision that removes it, and then state what that revision costs: which of my remaining commitments it strains, and which familiar phenomena the revised view thereafter struggles to explain.

4. Identify the single change I could make that would resolve the most, and tell me whether making it would leave me with something still recognizably a semantic account of slurs rather than a pragmatic or sociolinguistic one. That is the part of the project I am least willing to surrender, so I need to know in advance whether it survives.

Where a tension turns out to depend on a commitment doing unnoticed work — a claim that looks incidental to the view but is in fact load-bearing for several others at once — say so explicitly and say which commitments would go down with it. Reason from the commitments as I have stated them; I am not asking for a survey of the existing literature on slurs, nor for an identification of which published view mine most resembles, except insofar as noting that a given tension has a known escape route bears on how severe you judge it to be.
