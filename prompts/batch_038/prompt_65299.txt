So my roommate (history grad student, hence annoying) and I have been going back and fourth about the "for want of a nail" business — you know, the Cleopatra's nose intuition, if her nose had been different the map of Europe looks different — and I want an actual number from you rather than a both-sides shrug.

Here's the proposition, stated as tightly as I can manage:

P: Take the world as it stood in 1900. Now take a counterfactual that is identical in every respect except one trivial local perturbation — say, a mid-level clerk in one chancery misfiles one document, so a diplomatic exchange that historically took three days takes nine. Let it run forward. In the year 2000, the counterfactual world still matches ours on every macro feature a general historian would list first: same rough roster of great powers, same dominant political vocabulary, same position on the technological curve, and no war that didn't happen here happens there.

I want your credence in P. A number.

But the number is the boring part, what I actually want is the decomposition. Split it into at least these four pieces and put a rough probability on each:

1. Does the system even have the right shape? Chaos in the technical sense — sensitivity to initial conditions — is not the same thing as macro-level unpredictability. A gas is microscopically chaotic and macroscopically lawlike, because there are on the order of 10^23 degrees of freedom and the coarse-grained variables average out. The three-body problem is chaotic at every scale, because there are so few. Which regime is a century of human politics in? How many effective degrees of freedom does it have once you coarse-grain down to "great power configuration" and "dominant ideology"? Give me your probability that history is in the averaging regime rather than the few-body regime.

2. Conditional on the few-body regime, how much of the sensitivity is actually load-bearing on individual agents? I mean the substitutability question: if the clerk misfiles and some ambitious minister never gets his moment, does the structural slot just get filled by the next person in line who does roughly the same thing, or does the specific body in the chair change the trajectory? Probability that structural roles are strongly substitutable.

3. Conditional on 1 and 2 both going the way the nail wins, what's the probability that the divergence actually compounds to macro scale inside a hundred years instead of getting damped? My guess is that institutions, alliance systems, and technological bottlenecks act like attractors that pull nearby trajectories back toward each other, but I can't tell if that's a real mechanism or just a comforting metaphor.

4. And the ugly one: how much of P is simply badly defined? "Same rough roster of great powers," "same position on the technological curve" — I waved my hands at both. Give me the probability that P is determinate enough to have a truth value at all, rather than sliding around depending on how you draw the macro description. And if you think it slides, restate it in the form you'd actually be willing to assign a number to, and give the number for that version instead.

Then combine them — or tell me why you shouldn't, because I assume the terms aren't independent and I suspect 1 and 3 are correlated in a direction I can't figure out. Give me the final number, say which single term it's most sensitive to, and say what kind of evidence or argument would move that term by more than ten points. No citations, no reading list, I don't want a literature review — I want your reasoning, including the parts where you're making it up.

Last thing. I'm at about 0.35 and he's at about 0.8. He says I'm smuggling in a great-man theory of history without noticing; I say he's assuming an averaging result he hasn't earned. Given your decomposition, where does that gap most likely live — which one term — and how far apart would we have to be on it, and it alone, to produce a 0.45 spread at the end? If you think the gap can't be explained by one term, seperate out what combination could.
