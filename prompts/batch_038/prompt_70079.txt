I spent 34 years in computational physical chemistry, mostly running other people's codes and getting paid to know what the answers meant. Retired two years back, and to stop my head going soft I've been reading philosophy — turns out the old argument about testimony, Hume and Reid going at it two and a half centuries ago, is pretty much a description of my job. Anyway. I'm now one of the two "outside" members on the curriculum committee of the engineering school at the university down the road, which is how I ended up with this.

The mess: civil engineering wants to run a pilot in the senior design course where students do the structural analysis and the code checks with an LLM assistant, and the course drops the requirement that they reproduce key calculations by hand. The guy pushing it is a practicing structural engineer who says this is what the work actually looks like now and we'd be training them for a workplace that doesnt exist. Two faculty say the capstone is the last place anybody learns to catch a mistake, and after that it's on them for forty years.

We have data, which is more than these fights usually get. Last year, unofficially, about half the section used the tool. Their reports were better written, their grades a shade higher. But at the end of term we handed everyone ten completed calculations, one of which had a planted error: the assistant had run a strength check on service loads instead of factored loads, so every ratio came out roughly 40 percent low and the member looked perfectly fine when it wasn't. Not an arithmetic slip, nothing a checker would catch — a modeling choice that looks completely reasonable unless you know why it's wrong. Heavy tool users spotted it about 30 percent of the time. Everybody else, about 65.

And here's the part I dont know what to do with. The students leaning on the assistant hardest are disproportionately the ones working thirty hours a week and the ones whose first language isn't English, and they say it's the only tutoring available at eleven at night. Several of them also say it's the first time their reports have read like everybody else's. If we shut the pilot down we're taking away the thing that's helping exactly the students we say were most worried about.

Our program outcome language says graduates have to demonstrate that they can exercise independent professional judgment and check computational results against first principles. Half the committee reads that as killing the pilot on the spot. Half reads it as a description of the pilot.

So: draft the memo I'll circulate before we meet on the 14th. I want a recommendation — approve, reject, or approve with conditions — that fourteen tired people can actually vote on, and if it's conditions, make them things you could put in a syllabus and grade, not aspirations. Take the engineer's case seriously, because I think he's more than half right and I don't want this memo reading like nostalgia for slide rules. And it needs to settle the thing we keep circling: what's the difference, epistemically, between trusting a finite element solver you've validated against closed-form solutions and trusting an assistant that's usually right and confidently wrong sometimes? If the answer is that you can check it, then say whether the capacity to check is something every individual engineer has to carry, or something a team can hold between them — because the answer decides whether the pilot is fine, fine with conditions, or a bad idea. Finally, give me the strongest argument against whatever you're recommending, stated as well as you can. The engineer will make it, and I'd rather have met it on paper first.
