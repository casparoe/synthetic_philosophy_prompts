I write novels, and I've been building a character who's compulsively rational — I keep feeding him variants of the Newcomb problem to see what he'd do, and I've noticed my own verdicts are a shambles. I'd like to know what principle, if any, holds them together.

Regular version, predictor right about nine times out of ten: take only the opaque box. No hesitation.

Same, but the predictors only right 55% of the time: take both boxes.

Same as the first, but the predictor is a copy of my character's own mind running on a laptop: take one box — and I feel surer about it than I do in the first case, which makes no sense to me, since a copy of him ought to be weaker evidence about him than a superintelligence would be.

Now the pair I can't live with. Same as the first case, except they let us watch the box get sealed on camera an hour beforehand, so we know for certain the contents are fixed: take both, and I feel no guilt about it. But the contents were just as fixed in case one.

Transparent variant — I can see the million sitting there in the opaque box: take both. Feels fine.

And the one I keep going back and forth on: the predictor fills the boxes on the basis of what the chooser says in advance that he's going to do. So announcing 'I'm a two-boxer' out loud is itself a move that costs a million. I genuinely don't know whether my character should keep his mouth shut, or whether wanting to keep his mouth shut is just a tell that I have no principle at all.

Is there a single account of choosing under prediction that gets all of these, or nearly all? If not, tell me which verdict I have to surrender and why. State the principle precisely enough that I can see which clause each case is testing — I want the machinery, not a label — and say what it predicts for a case I haven't tried yet: a predictor that's right nine times out of ten in general but wrong about my character every single time, on purpose.
