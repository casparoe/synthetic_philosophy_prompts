I'm putting together a grad seminar on knowledge after Gettier and I have to decide how much time each repair attempt gets. Play the role of a working epistemologist who's spent to long on the tracking/sensitivity literature: think it through carefully, and before you rank anything, brainstorm out loud the failure modes a fix has to clear — the grandmother case, the Kripke-style gambit (if you'd been in a Gettier case you'd have formed the belief the same way, so nothing you know is sensitive), the fact that denying closure leaves you asserting a conjunction that sounds abominable even to the person asserting it, and whether method-relativity is a principled constraint or just a dial you turn until the answers come out right.

Then rank these six from best to worst, each scored out of 10 on a single axis — how likely you think it is to be roughly the correct final theory of knowledge, not how teachable or elegant it is:

1. Nozick's own move: sensitivity, but relativized to the method you actually used.
2. Swapping sensitivity for safety (no nearby false belief).
3. Contextualism about "knows."
4. A probabilistic reconstruction of tracking, where the counterfactual gets cashed out as a high conditional probability of believing p given p.
5. A patch one of my students proposed: evaluate sensitivity only at worlds where you'd use your actual method, and at worlds where the method would switch, require only that you not falsely believe p. She calls it tiered tracking.
6. Deny closure flat out and eat the skepticism.

Two sentences max per option, and be explicit when you think two of these collapse into each other rather than genuinely differing.

One more thing, and I mean it: seperate out for me what's actually settled among people who work on this from what's still a live fight, and don't quietly slide your own favorite into the consensus column. If your ranking depends on a contested premise about what knowledge is for, say so. American English, please.
