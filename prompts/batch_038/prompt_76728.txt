I'm a software engineer, no philosophy training beyond what I've picked up reading things at night, and I've drafted an essay for a blog I don't really have an audience for. I'd like comments on it — properly critical ones, not encouragement. Answer in British English, please.

The context: I work on infrastructure, so I spend a lot of my life in incident reviews. When something falls over at 3am, the norm where I work is the blameless postmortem — you write up what happened and you pointedly do not make it about the person who pushed the bad config. And I've started thinking that narrative history is basically the opposite habit, and that the reasons it gives for being the opposite are not as good as it thinks. Here's the draft:

---

**Nobody caused the outage**

**1.** The reason blameless postmortems exist isn't kindness, it's information. A postmortem that stops at "Dave should have checked" is a low-information document. It ends exactly where a person could have been more careful, which means it never gets to the part about why the system let Dave do that — no rollback path, no alerting on the thing that mattered, a deploy pipeline that made the dangerous action the easy one. Blame is a place where causal enquiry goes to stop.

**2.** A lot of narrative history is written like a blaming postmortem. Wars have causes, and the causes are decisions. Revolutions have causes, and the causes are grievances and then a room full of people. Even when a non-human thing is doing an enormous share of the work — the bacterium, the failed harvest, the volcanic winter — the account gets organised around what people did about it, and the non-human part gets demoted to scenery.

**3.** There's a very good reason to be nervous about demoting the scenery back up. If climate explains everything, then conquest, slavery and genocide get re-described as weather, and the people who did them get to disappear into an environmental backdrop. This isn't a hypothetical worry: environmental explanations of who ends up ruling whom have a horrible track record, and Jared Diamond got hammered for exactly this, in some cases deservedly.

**4.** So my claim is narrower, and it depends on separating three things that get fused together in the word "cause":

- *background conditions* — the climate envelope that makes wheat possible here at all
- *events* — the eruption, the arrival of the pathogen, the three wet summers in a row
- *agents* — things that act for reasons, that could have done otherwise, and that can sensibly be reproached

The determinism worry in (3) is, I think, almost entirely a worry about the third category — specifically about where blame and responsibility land. But you can grant everything about the third category and still insist that Tambora is not scenery. It wasn't a background condition. It was an event with its own timing, its own causal signature, its own duration, that no human being chose and nobody could have negotiated with. Most of the resistance to letting non-human things count as "actors" looks to me like a blame-allocation reflex that has been smuggled from ethics into metaphysics: we're afraid that calling a volcano an actor lets somebody off, so we insist it's a thing.

**5.** Some of what I mean. Laki, in Iceland, erupted along a fissure for about eight months from mid-1783 into 1784, threw a sulphur haze over much of Europe, and killed something like a fifth of Iceland's population through the famine that followed — most of the sheep, half the cattle and horses, dead from fluorine-contaminated grass rather than from lava. Harvests across Europe were bad, the Nile flood was weak, and Egypt went through a famine that took a huge slice of its population the following year. People have tried to draw a line from all that to the mood in France in 1789; I don't want to defend that line strongly, but the modest version is already interesting — the 1780s were grim in ways nobody at Versailles selected.

Tambora, April 1815, the largest eruption in recorded history, tens of thousands dead directly and then many more from what came after. 1816 becomes the Year Without a Summer: frost in New England in June, harvest failure across Europe, food riots, and a party of people kept indoors by the weather on a Swiss lakeside telling each other ghost stories.

And further back, the dust veil of 536 — years when the sun gave no proper light, followed from 541 by the first great plague pandemic in the Mediterranean, and which now gets treated by some people as the beginning of a genuinely colder stretch of a couple of centuries.

**6.** Here's the peril, and I think stating it properly actually helps. The danger isn't that we'll over-rate germs. It's that "the climate did it" is unfalsifiable in the same way "human nature did it" is: it's a residue-soaker, available after the fact to absorb whatever explanation is left over, and unlike a human actor it has no motives you can check it against. Which is why the discipline needs the equivalent of the logs. In engineering, a postmortem claim gets checked against timestamps and traces. In history the logs are ice cores, tree rings, lake and ocean sediment, dated documents, and increasingly ancient DNA. A climate explanation that doesn't line up with the physical record isn't a bold new way of seeing, it's just a story.

**7.** Where I get stuck, honestly. Once you've taken agency away — no intentions, no alternatives, nothing to reproach — is there anything left in "actor" beyond a new label on "cause"? And does the label matter at all, given that the morally loaded work (who starved, who profited, who shipped the grain anyway) is all done by the human layer sitting on top of the physical one?
