I am teaching a graduate seminar unit on Turing's 1950 paper "Computing Machinery and Intelligence," and each year we skip its strange closing digression on extrasensory perception. This year I want to build the unit around that passage instead, paired with Spielberg's *Minority Report*, and I need a reading I can trust in front of a room.

For orientation, and please correct me if I have misremembered any of it, since I am describing the passage rather than quoting it: Turing lists four items under the heading — telepathy, clairvoyance, precognition, psychokinesis — observes that these phenomena seem to contradict our usual scientific picture of the world, and admits that we would dearly like to discredit them. He then concedes that the statistical case for telepathy at least is overwhelming. His response is twofold: much of science remains perfectly workable if one simply sets the phenomena aside, and if telepathy is granted, the conditions of the imitation game must be tightened — competitors placed in distant rooms, doors locked, perhaps the rooms screened — so that the effect is reduced to something negligible. He also likens the contaminated game to an interrogator who is in effect talking to himself while one of the competitors listens in.

The film, briefly: three sedated and non-verbal precogs suspended in a tank produce visions of murders not yet committed; technicians assemble the imagery into a report, which arrives as two names on wooden spheres. One of the system's architects explains that the precogs occasionally disagree, and that the dissenting vision was discarded. When the chief of Precrime receives a sphere bearing his own name, he sets out to disprove the report and ends up inside the scene it describes.

Please give me a reading of the film through Turing's discussion that answers the following:

1. Turing's remedy is spatial — distance, locked doors, screening — and answers a leakage problem between rooms. Precognition is not leakage. Does his fix do any work at all against the one faculty the film is about, and what follows for the claim that the imitation game can be made fair by tightening its conditions?

2. A witness able to foresee the interrogator's next utterance could pass the game without understanding anything. Turing treats ESP as an experimental nuisance rather than as a threat to the sufficiency of his criterion. Is that defensible? Does the film's false positive — a name on a sphere that is not yet a fact — supply the counterexample he misses?

3. The technicians run a working predictive discipline with no theory of the phenomenon, which is exactly Turing's attitude that one gets along very nicely if one forgets about ESP. The film shows that attitude holding for years and then failing catastrophically. What precisely fails, and is the failure epistemic or moral?

4. The precogs never speak; others verbalize their output and dispute its meaning. Read this inversion — a mute witness and interrogators who manufacture the transcript — against Turing's broadly behaviorist assumption that what a subject says is the only evidence available about it. Does the film have a position on whether Agatha thinks, and does it matter to her treatment?

5. Where does the pairing break? The film's real subject is determinism, punishment, and the state's use of foreknowledge; its precogs are human, not machines; and it never asks Turing's question. Tell me plainly which of the above are genuine connections and which are coincidences of vocabulary, and end by saying what the film contributes to the debate about ESP and the test that argument alone would not.

No quotations from either text and no references, please — I want the reading, not the scholarship.
