Here is the dispute as I keep meeting it. The question being argued over is what the alignment problem is even a problem *about*, and I have been sorting the participants into four camps, using my own labels because the field's vocabulary is part of what is contested.

**Camp A, the ends-camp.** The object of alignment is what a system ultimately aims at: its values, in the sense of the ends it would pursue if free to pursue them. On this view two problems are routinely run together — the technical one of getting a system to acquire and stably retain a given set of ends, and the prior normative-political one of deciding whose ends those should be — and recent success at making systems helpful and obedient is not alignment in the relevant sense, because it leaves the question of ends untouched.

**Camp B, the narrow intent-camp.** The object is not ends but what the system is trying to do, indexed to a particular principal and a particular time: does its operative intention match what the person directing it wants done? A system can be intent-aligned while sharing nobody's values, and can hold admirable values while being intent-misaligned in the moment. This camp deliberately brackets whose values should be loaded as a separate question, and argues that a system which reliably does what its principal asks can serve as a collaborator in improving its own alignment rather than having to be gotten right on the first attempt.

**Camp C, the disposition-camp.** Goals and intentions are useful fictions of the intentional stance; what actually exists in a trained system is a profile of dispositions to behave, indexed to situations and to a distribution over them. Alignment is a claim about that profile plus a robustness margin. On this view the inner-versus-outer vocabulary smuggles in a representational picture — a thing in there that *is* the goal — which has no reason to be true of real systems.

**Camp D, the process-camp.** Alignment is a property of a relationship sustained over time — training, oversight, correction, the standing ability to redirect the system — not a property of a system at a time. Asking whether a system "is aligned," absent a process, a principal, and a time index, is a malformed question.

The stall consists in three moves that recur endlessly and never land. Camp A tells Camp B it is solving an easy problem and calling it the hard one. Camp B tells Camp A it is describing a system nobody is going to build and demanding properties no artifact need have. Camps C and D tell everyone else that the entire disagreement presupposes goal-like inner states whose existence is exactly what is in question, and are answered by being accused of changing the subject to something with no stakes. Meanwhile I suspect a great deal of the deadlock is scope-sliding: there is a narrow problem statement in circulation — one agent, one principal, one time, whose-values bracketed — and a broad one, and people answer them alternately without noticing.

To get at the substance, here is a case I constructed. Please engage it as a stipulation first; if you think it is impossible or under-described, say so only after telling me what it shows as given. Two systems, the **Anchor** and the **Chorus**. In the Anchor, a single persistent internal state plays the goal-role across contexts: it is there when nothing is being asked of it, it survives changes of task, and it governs what the system does. In the Chorus, no such persistent state exists; behavior is produced by a coalition of context-sensitive processes that, in the entire domain of situations we can specify, yields exactly the same profile. Stipulate full equivalence on everything we could test: choices, revealed preferences, self-reports about what each system is trying to do, performance on probes designed specifically to expose context-dependence, and even the read-outs of our best interpretability tooling, which I am idealizing as identical. The only difference is the constitutive one about whether anything inside plays the goal-role.
