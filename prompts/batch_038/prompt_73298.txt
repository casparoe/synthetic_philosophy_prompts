Picked up a pop-psych book at the airport with a chapter that takes a swing at virtue ethics, and I dont have the background to tell whether the swing lands. The bit I care about went roughly like this:

'Social psychology has quietly demolished one of the oldest ideas in ethics: that people have characters. Milgram showed that about two-thirds of ordinary Americans were willing to keep pressing a button until a stranger screamed and then went silent, and when he varied the setup obedience ran from almost nobody to over ninety percent depending mainly on where the authority figure stood and who else was in the room. Darley and Batson found that seminary students on their way to give a talk on the Good Samaritan stepped over a man slumped in a doorway if they were running late, but stopped to help if they had time -- what predicted helping was the clock, not their faith or their values. In another study, people who found a dime in a phone booth helped a stranger pick up dropped papers, and people who found nothing mostly walked on. The reason we miss all this is the fundamental attribution error: we invent stable inner traits to explain behavior that is actually produced by the situation. There are no character traits, only local habits tied to narrow contexts, which is why nobody has ever managed to predict real behavior from personality questionnaires -- the corellations come out around .3, which is basically noise. Aristotle built his ethics on a piece of folk psychology that turns out to be false, so virtue ethics is not merely unsupported, it is refuted. The practical upshot: stop trying to become a better person and start designing better situations.'

Parts of this feel right to me -- I do think we overrate character and underrate context -- but I've also known people who are just reliably honest across every situation I've watched them in, and I cant square that with "there are no character traits." So I want an accuracy audit, not a defense of virtue ethics and not a defense of the book. Which sentences would the situationists it's drawing on (Harman, Doris) happily sign, and which would they say overstate their own view? Specifically: is "there are no character traits" what they actually claim, or a slide from something weaker that Doris in particular would reject; what is the .3-correlation line leaving out; are the Milgram and seminary numbers being used in the way those studies license, or is something being read off them that isn't there; and how much weight can a single dime-in-a-phone-booth result bear in the first place? Then tell me whether the last sentence -- the practical advice -- survives once the middle of the paragraph is cleaned up, or whether it depended on the overstatements. Go through it in order and flag anything that's an outright howler, if there is one.
