My friend and I argued until two in the morning about Descartes on animal minds, and every round of it ended on the same piece of furniture: the automaton. The comparison Descartes keeps returning to, in Part V of the Discourse and again in the preface to the French edition of the Principles, is the body as a machine made by God and therefore incomparably better ordered than any of our own contrivances; the hydraulic figures in the royal gardens, moved by water alone; the clock, whose wheels and counterweights observe the laws of their own nature more exactly than a clockmaker could ever have arranged them; and the thought experiment of a machine with the organs and outward shape of a monkey that we could not distinguish from the animal, while the same trick fails with human beings because of language. My friend now presses that analogy into service for a present-day purpose. Since a clock performs everything a clock performs with nothing it is like inside, and since Descartes took beasts to be clocklike in precisely that sense, he thinks Descartes supplies a still-live argument that sophisticated behavioural mimicry is no evidence of mind — and hence that Descartes anticipated the objection we now make to large language models. I am not convinced the analogy will bear that much weight, and I would like it audited rather than recited.

Could you lay out the correspondence explicitly — a short table giving what plays the clock, the weights and springs, the outward performance, and the thing supposedly absent — and then separate the disanalogies that genuinely bear on the AI conclusion from those that are merely rhetorical? Please fold in Descartes' own hedges, above all that the machine argument in the Discourse is doing double duty as a case for human exceptionalism via language rather than as a general thesis about inner life, and that in his later letters to Newcastle and to More he grants beasts the passions and even sensation insofar as it depends on a bodily organ. Close by saying what survives of my friend's argument once the disanalogies are on the table, and whether a better analogy exists for the case he wants to make.
