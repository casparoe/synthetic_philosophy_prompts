So in about three weeks I'm giving an invited talk in a psychiatry department's research seminar — not a philosophy venue, grand-rounds adjacent, forty minutes plus questions. What I'm defending is this: Hacking's looping effects aren't a skeptical threat to psychiatric classification so much as a design constraint, and for a specific set of categories the right institutional response is to treat them as instruments that accumulate what I'm calling loop debt — the growing gap between the population the criteria were originally calibrated on and the population now being handed the label, because in the interim thousands of people have been screened by the label, have learned its vocabulary, and have shaped their self-presentation around it. My concrete proposal is re-anchoring: for these categories, revalidation samples should be drawn from populations that have not yet been run through the current criteria (a birth cohort too young to have been diagnosed, a catchment area or health system where the category isn't in active use, an unselected primary-care sample) and the criteria re-derived from scratch, on a schedule, with the divergence between old and new calibration reported as a maintenance statistic rather than treated as a validity failure. I'll spend maybe three slides on interactive vs indifferent kinds and then move on — nobody in the room has read him and I don't want the prep sheet to lean on getting his exegesis right, so keep it conceptual.

The room: clinical researchers and psychiatric epidemiologists mostly, one of whom runs a large longitudinal cohort and will want to know why I can't just test for measurement invariance across waves and be done with it; two psychometricians who think in terms of symptom networks and latent variable models; a child psychiatrist who does a lot of school-based case-finding and is deeply pragmatic about labels because labels unlock services; a health economist who cares about allocation and will be suspicious of anything that sounds like it undermines the numbers she models with; and one philosopher of medicine who is friendly but will police my terms. Their shared default is that a category is as good as its predictive validity for treatment response, and they are broadly allergic to anything that sounds like "not really real." I already got a preview — I was arguing about this with a friend who does health econ and she said my whole talk reduces to "criteria drift, we know, we handle it statistically," which is exactly the objection I'm most scared of.
