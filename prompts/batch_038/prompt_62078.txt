I teach an upper-level seminar on Adam Smith's Theory of Moral Sentiments — fourteen students, one hundred minutes, week three. They have read Part I and Part II, Section I, and nothing of Part III. I want this session to make Part III feel like the answer to a problem they have just personally had, rather than the next entry on the syllabus. Please design an in-class game that puts the mechanics of Smith's account of sympathy into what the students actually do, so that the conclusions surface during play and are named only in the final ten minutes. The phrase impartial spectator must not be spoken aloud until then.

The rules and the scoring, not anything I say, have to produce each of the following. I have labelled them so that you can tell me which rule delivers which.

(a) The concord bid. An agent in a given incident feels some intensity of a passion that only the agent knows, and separately chooses the intensity to display. Each spectator's approval must be a function of whether the displayed intensity falls within what that spectator can imaginatively share — not of whether the display is truthful. The payoff scheme must make understatement profitable and must charge something for the gap between felt and displayed intensity. Decide whether that charge is visible to the agent during play or only in the accounting afterwards, and defend the choice.

(b) The abatement rule. Smith's spectator never feels what the agent feels, only a weaker copy raised by imagination. Build that asymmetry into the scoring as a structural cap, so that the maximum intensity a spectator can share sits below what the agent reports feeling. Tell me whether you hard-code the offset, derive it from the spectators' own reports, or let it vary with how vividly the incident has been described, and what each option costs you.

(c) The double-sympathy round. Construct one incident in which approval depends on two distinct acts of fellow-feeling: one directed at the person reacting, and one directed at the person on the receiving end, the second being what carries the judgment about whether the act deserved what it got. Build it so the two come apart — the reaction proportionate and easy to share, the person affected unmoved — and so that the group's verdict about desert tracks the second sympathy rather than the first. They should see this in the numbers on the board, not hear it from me.

(d) The hidden-norm round. A subset of spectators holds role cards quietly specifying a local convention about the display of feeling — one group treats conspicuous grief as self-indulgence, another treats its absence as callousness — and nothing else. Aggregate verdicts by seating area, put the two distributions side by side, and only then allow the cards to be revealed. The conclusion I want them to reach unaided is that a verdict produced by pooling the members of this room inherits the composition of this room, and that nothing in the pooling procedure guarantees it answers to anything further.

(e) A proximity round. Two subgroups receive versions of the same incident differing only in vividness and distance, and each must commit a fixed, scarce budget of tokens before the groups compare notes.

Also required: a private ledger in which each agent writes down, before any verdict is revealed, what a spectator who knew everything the agent knows — including the concealed background fact that raises or lowers the fitting intensity — would approve of. The gap between that entry and the pooled verdict is the number I want to close the hour on.

Deliverables: a materials list I can print in advance; a round-by-round timeline in minutes; the scoring rule stated precisely enough that a teaching assistant could run it without asking me anything; a device for keeping spectator reports simultaneous and sealed, since I expect announced verdicts to drift toward one another; how to scale between ten and twenty players; a debrief of five to seven questions in order, each tagged to the moment in play that motivates it; and a mapping table giving, for each rule, the claim in Smith it instantiates and one sentence on where the analogy fails.

Then be candid about three things. What will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and what to do when it does — I expect agents to converge on a safe middle intensity, and I expect some students to hear the spectator's question as whether the reaction is justified rather than whether they can share it. What the incident cards must avoid: they should be ordinary enough that the room's intuitions are shared, with no real identifiable persons and nothing involving sexual violence. And above all, which features of Smith's account the game cannot reproduce at all, so that I know what I must supply by exposition. My own suspicion is that the pleasure Smith thinks mutual sympathy gives to both parties, and the slow formation of a standing inner judge out of years of being watched, are both beyond the reach of a hundred-minute game with tokens. Tell me whether you agree, and what you would put in their place.
