The chair handed me a stack of trade-press books to mine for readings, and one of them — a popular chapter about how modern moral philosophy supposedly lost its nerve — has a section on Susan Wolf's 1982 paper "Moral Saints" that I'd like to assign, because the prose is readable and freshmen will actually finish it. My problem is that I havent touched the ethics literature since grad school, and what I remember of the paper is basically the two-saints arguement plus a strong suspicion that Wolf gets cited constantly by people she'd disagree with. So before it goes on the syllabus I want a careful audit of the section rather than my own skim.

Here's the passage, roughly as it appears: "Susan Wolf's 1982 paper 'Moral Saints' did something few academic philosophy papers manage: it argued that the most morally admirable person imaginable is not the person we should most want to be. A moral saint, in her sense, is someone whose life is as morally good as it could possibly be — someone who at every moment is doing as much good as they can. Wolf's target isn't a rival theory but a shared assumption: utilitarians and Kantians, who disagree about nearly everything else, both treat the moral point of view as if it were the whole of value, so that a life organized around it automatically counts as a life well lived. She then shows that this assumption produces two very different figures. The loving saint is the one most of us picture when we hear the word: a person who loves tennis and Victorian novels and cooking for friends, but who recognizes that morality asks for more, and so gives these things up out of duty — a life of permanent, conscientious inner conflict, which Wolf thinks is genuinely admirable even though it costs him everything he cares about. The rational saint is the stranger creature: he has reasoned his way so thoroughly into the moral point of view that no non-moral wants are left at all, so there is nothing for morality to override and no struggle to admire; he is serene, selfless, and, Wolf thinks, deeply unhappy, because a person who cares about nothing but the welfare of others has nothing to come home to. Her conclusion is that morality has to be demoted from master value to one department of life among others — art, friendship, personal projects — and that these aren't self-indulgence but genuine goods that a moral theory built on the moral point of view has no room for. It's why the paper still gets cited, usually by people who never read past the last page, as the philosophical permission slip: give a sensible share to charity, and then stop feeling guilty about the rest of your life."

Can you go through it claim by claim, and for each one tell me whether it's something Wolf would happily sign, something that's a fair popular simplification that loses nuance but no truth, or something she'd object to — and in the last case, what the objection actually is? I especially want to know whether the two types of saint are being described the way she describes them, since that's the bit I'd be teaching from, and whether the "deeply unhappy" line is doing something illegitimate. Then tell me separately whether the final sentence — the permission-slip reading — follows from anything that survives in the corrected version, or whether it's contradicted by the chapter's own earlier sentences. And finally the practical question: is this something I can assign with a one-paragraph warning, or should it stay off the reading list?
