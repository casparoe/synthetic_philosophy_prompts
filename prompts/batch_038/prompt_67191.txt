So, kind of an odd request but I need this by Thursday and I don't trust anyone else to get it right.

I'm an archaeologist. What I actually work on is the eastern Mediterranean around 1200 BCE — the palace administrations, the scribal bureaucracies, the whole thing going under — and basically my entire professional argument for fifteen years has been that "collapse" is a lazy word. Societies very rarely fail; they usually shed complexity on one axis while carrying straight on along another. The palace storerooms and the redistributive accounting disappear and the villages keep farming the same fields with the same families for another four hundred years. The Maya lowland dynasties stop raising monuments and the cities empty out, and meanwhile Maya people, Maya languages and Maya polities are going strong up north and are still there. Rapa Nui is the case everyone gets wrong: the palm forest going away looks a lot more like rats eating the palm nuts than like a society eating itself, and the actual catastrophe that broke the island was slave raids and epidemic disease long after contact, not a pre-contact suicide. I believe all this. I've said versions of it in print.

Which puts me in a weird spot, because my grandmother's village — Fallowkill, about nine hundred people, upstate — voted in the spring to dissolve itself into the surrounding town. The state threw in a grant to cover the consolidation costs, the last day of the village's legal existence is Saturday, and the town supervisor has asked me, as the person in the family with a PhD and a public voice, to give the remarks at the little ceremony where the seal comes down and goes into a shadow box at the fire hall.

Here is the problem. There are two speeches available and I refuse to give either one.

The first is the mournful one: our world is ending, a way of life dies Saturday, we are witnessing the last day of Fallowkill. I think that sentence is false, and not just pedantically false — false in the exact way I've spent my career saying it's false, and my whole field's evidence says so. The village is a corporation. The corporation is being retired. Almost nothing anybody in that room actually cares about lives inside the corporation.

The second is the state's one, which is worse: nothing is being lost, this is just a more efficient way to deliver water and road services, we should celebrate modernization. That is also false, and it is the sort of thing you can only say if you have never been in the room. Something real is going: the fact that there was a body of neighbors who could get together and decide to put a streetlight on the corner by the creek, and then do it, and be answerable to nobody for it. That capacity is being handed upward to a board that meets twenty miles away. Also my grandmother kept the village's ledger books for thirty-one years as volunteer historian, she wrote the last entries in them herself last month, she voted no, she's eighty-eight, and she will be sitting in the second row in a fire hall with maybe two hundred people in it, and she will catch me in a euphemism from across the room. She has caught other people.

The room: mostly locals, a third of whom campaigned for dissolution because the water district was going to bankrupt them and they're not sentimental about it at all; my family; the town supervisor and two people from the county who want the ceremony to feel like a success; the volunteer fire chief; a couple of my old high school teachers. Nobody in it knows what I do for a living beyond "something with old pottery in Greece."

So: write the speech. Roughly seven minutes, call it nine hundred words, and it needs to be spoken-aloud prose — sentences a person can say in one breath in a room with a fan going.

Hard constraints:
- The word "collapse" does not appear. Neither does "resilience," "adaptive," or anything else from my field's vocabulary. The argument has to be structural, not terminological.
- If you use anything from the places I actually study, one thing only, and it has to arrive as a story about people, not as evidence for a thesis. Nobody should feel lectured at or realize they've been handed an analogy until afterward.
- It has to say, in some plain form, both true things: that the thing ending is much smaller than the mourners think, and that what's ending is a genuine loss and not a bookkeeping change. I want the tension held, not dissolved. Not "on the one hand / on the other hand" — actually held, in a way that doesn't insult either the people who voted yes or the people who voted no.
- The village seal goes in a shadow box. The last line my grandmother wrote in the ledger is a water-main repair on Ketchum Road, $1,240. The July parade, which was run by a committee that is not a village body and has never been on the village payroll, happens in eleven days regardless. Use at least two of those three, and if you use the parade, don't wink at it.
- It should be funny twice and not more, and it has to end on something she can live with, because she's going to be the one walking out of that fire hall last.

Then after the speech, give me four or five lines telling me what claim each of the middle sections is actually making — I want to check that I'm not agreeing to assert something I think is false.
