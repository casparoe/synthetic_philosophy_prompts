I build probabilistic forecasting models for a living — weather-adjacent work, mostly precipitation onset and grid-load exceedance events — and my professional vocabulary is entirely about calibration: reliability diagrams, PIT histograms, the Brier score and the continuous ranked probability score treated as proper scoring rules, the distinction between marginal and conditional calibration, sharpness subject to calibration, prequential backtesting, and the standing assumption that the true data-generating probability of any single event is a latent quantity I never observe directly and can only infer from long-run frequencies within some reference class. A philosopher friend of mine insists that in my daily practice I am already committed to David Lewis's Principal Principle, and last week she wrote it out for me in a form I could half follow: an agent's credence in a proposition, conditional on the hypothesis about what the chance of that proposition is together with whatever admissible evidence the agent has, ought to equal that chance. My working hypothesis, which I want you to test rather than flatter, is that this amounts to nothing more than the claim that a forecaster's reported probabilities should equal the true probabilities of the events they concern — that is, to the definition of perfect calibration — and that the remainder of Lewis's apparatus is metaphysical overhead I could discard without altering a single line of how I evaluate a model. If that is right, say so bluntly; if it is wrong, tell me exactly where my gloss breaks.

So restate the Principal Principle, clause by clause, in the vocabulary I actually use: what chance becomes, what credence becomes, what the conditionalization on a chance hypothesis becomes when I never condition on such a thing but only on features and on past outcomes, what admissibility and inadmissibility become, and what becomes of the principle's status as a constraint an agent is supposed to satisfy prior to and independently of the data that would confirm any particular stochastic model. For each clause, state what translates cleanly, what translates only with a distortion you should flag rather than quietly absorb, and what has no counterpart at all in forecast evaluation. Do not smooth over the rough edges to make the mapping look tidier than it is, and do not import my modeling assumptions and relabel them as Lewis's.

There are three places where I specifically suspect the translation is dishonest, and I would like them handled head-on. First, a level mismatch: the principle governs one agent's degree of belief in one proposition at one time, whereas calibration is an aggregate property of a forecaster's outputs across many events, so it looks possible to be perfectly calibrated in my sense while violating the principle case by case — and conversely, to respect the principle perfectly on every occasion while looking badly calibrated over any finite sample. Tell me whether that possibility is real or an artifact of my gloss. Second, admissibility: the natural translation is that you condition on your full information set, but I have been told the notion does substantive work by ruling certain kinds of information out, including some information bearing on the future, and I cannot see what the analogue of such an exclusion would be in prequential evaluation, where conditioning on everything you know at the time of the forecast is precisely the target. Third, the status of the chance quantity itself: I posit a probability as a parameter of a model and then test it against observed frequencies, whereas the principle appears to require deference to a chance hypothesis as such, prior to those frequencies; whether that deference has any counterpart in my practice, or whether my practice has silently substituted something else for it, is the part I most want clarified. For what it is worth, someone in our reading group argued that the deference ought to run in the opposite direction, with chance defined in terms of what an ideally informed and ideally rational agent would believe, and I cannot tell whether that reversal has any content expressible in my vocabulary or is a purely philosophical option with no operational meaning.

Then give me the residue report, which is the real point of the exercise. List what the philosophy contains that forecast evaluation cannot express, and for each item say whether the absence indicates a defect in my vocabulary — a genuinely missing concept, such as a single-case objective probability that is neither a long-run frequency nor a modeling convenience — or whether it is evidence that the original claim was less substantive than it appeared, a piece of the principle that does no work once you have already assumed a well-specified stochastic model and a scoring rule. I am not asking you to survey the interpretations of probability, to re-teach me proper scoring rules, or to adjudicate whether Lewis was right about the metaphysics; I am asking where the two frameworks actually fail to meet. Begin with your bottom line on my working hypothesis in the first sentence, then give the reasoning and the clause-by-clause translation, with no preamble and no restatement of my question.
