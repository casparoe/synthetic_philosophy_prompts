I run a Saturday philosophy club for about fifteen 16-17 year olds. No philosophy background, but they've just finished six weeks on voting and representation, so they think they already know how democracy works. Next session is 45 minutes, whiteboard only, no handouts, and the topic is the boundary problem: who counts as part of the demos, and whether a democracy can ever settle that question democratically.

Don't explain the boundary problem to me. Give me a plan for how I explain it to them, in order, with the reasons for the order — what depends on what. Specifically:

- What has to be in place before the question is even a question for them. My fear is that if I open with "who should decide X" they'll say "everyone affected" within about ninety seconds, feel clever, and I'll have nothing left to teach. So how do I get them to feel the circularity — you can't ask the people to decide who the people are — before I ever attach a name to it? (I think the name is usually credited to Whelan, but I don't care about the name, I care about the ninety seconds before it.)
- The one case that carries the whole session. I was going to use Brexit, because they'll bring it up whether I want them to or not, but I've talked myself out of it and I want you to tell me whether I'm right: a referendum looks like a paradigm case of a people deciding something, when the actually interesting fact about 2016 is that who got to vote was itself fixed beforehand by an Act of Parliament at Westminster — an already-constituted demos drew the line and then called the drawing democratic. Either defend that reading and tell me how to use Brexit without them turning it into a referendum-rematch shouting match, or argue me out of it. Then give me the case I should build on instead. It has to make the outsider hard to ignore — I keep drifting toward river cases, an upstream country damming or polluting water that flows through three states, or a country whose border policy determines the life chances of people who have never been near it — but it can't be a case where the politics eats the philosophy.
- The misconceptions, ranked by how fast they'll show up, and the exact point in the sequence where I head each off. My list: (1) "everyone affected votes" offered as the solution rather than as the start of the problem; (2) collapsing the boundary of the demos into the internal franchise question — voting age, prisoners, long-term residents who aren't citizens — which I'm fairly sure is a different problem but I can't state the difference crisply enough for a 16 year old; (3) "so let an international court or the UN sort it out," which seems to me to relocate the problem rather than solve it, since that body has its own boundary; (4) the deflationary kid who says "so borders are just wherever the last war stopped, there's no answer" — he's half right and I don't want to squash him; (5) the one who concludes the only consistent view is world government.
- Where Abizadeh's border-coercion idea goes. I want to use it, because "a state is using force against people who had no say in the rules authorising that force" lands hard with this age group. But I don't want to end on "therefore the demos is global," which feels like it proves too much — every state's border policy affects everyone, so the argument dissolves the very thing it was meant to bound. Tell me how to deploy the idea at full strength and then what honest objection to leave them holding.
- One closing question I can ask to tell whether the idea landed or whether they can just parrot it back, plus what a bad answer sounds like, so I can tell the difference between "hasn't got it" and "got it and is being awkward."
- What I should openly refuse to cover at this level and say so out loud. I'm guessing the all-affected vs all-subjected debate in any detail, and secession, but you decide.

No reading list, nothing for them to look up afterwards — I'm not assigning anything.
