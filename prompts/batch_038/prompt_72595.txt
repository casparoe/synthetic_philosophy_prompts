For the better part of a year I have been sitting in a municipal working group that was supposed to produce a joint submission on how our city should handle the sex trade: licensing of indoor premises, outreach funding, police practice around street soliciting. The membership is, deliberately, adversarial — a survivors-led abolitionist organisation, a peer-run sex worker collective, two public health officials, a police liaison, and me as the token policy analyst. What has struck me is not that the group is divided, but that it is divided in an oddly stable way. Every meeting the same jurisdictions come up: Sweden's 1999 ban on the purchase rather than the sale of sex, New Zealand's 2003 decriminalisation and the statutory review that followed it, Germany's 2002 legalization and the registration regime layered on top of it more recently, the Netherlands' lifting of the brothel ban. Each side cites the others' cases as evidence for its own position, and each side's rebuttals are already known to everyone in the room before they are made. Nobody is ignorant, nobody is dishonest, and in twelve months the substantive content of the disagreement has not shifted by a millimetre.

Here is the pattern I cannot name and that I want you to look at. Both camps in that room accept, without argument, the same empirical claim: that every regime ever tried produces a two-tier market — a visible, documented, comparatively protected segment, and a hidden segment, largely migrant and largely undocumented, which absorbs nearly all of the violence, the debt, and the police exposure. Neither side denies the existence of the second tier or disputes much about who is in it. And yet this single agreed fact functions as a decisive argument for opposite conclusions. For the abolitionists it shows that regulation cannot abolish coercion, only manage a residue, so the rational target is demand itself; for the decriminalisers it shows that the residue is manufactured by criminalisation, so the rational target is shrinking the hidden tier by making the work legible and complaint-eligible. My working hypothesis — and I want it tested, not flattered — is that the empirical layer is not where the disagreement actually lives. I suspect two other things are doing the work. One is a conceptual dispute about whether background economic desperation, as opposed to a specific threat from a specific person, can render a transaction involuntary in the morally relevant sense. The other is a claim about the criminal law's attitude-expressing function: that a purchase ban is worth having partly because of what it says, over decades, about bodies as commodities — a claim that neither side in my group knows how to test and that gets smuggled in as though it were a public health finding.

So: diagnose the structure of this impasse rather than adjudicating it. Is the dispute verbal or near-verbal, turning on what "consent," "coercion," and "exploitation" are being made to carry? Are the two camps answering different questions under one heading — one about the permissibility of the practice, one about the expected consequences of a legal regime — with the second quietly subordinated to the first and never separated out? Or is there a real, merely measurement-blocked empirical question underneath, concerning trafficking flows, the elasticity of demand, and displacement between street and indoor work, that cannot be settled in a hidden population with no counterfactual and no reliable baseline? Tell me which of these is doing the most work and why, using what each side actually says in support of its position; then tell me what would count as progress given your diagnosis — a distinction that forces the camps apart, a re-specified empirical question that is answerable in principle, a change of question altogether, or a reason to expect no progress and to treat the stalemate as the correct equilibrium. I am not asking for a survey of the three regimes, for a recommendation about which policy I should back in my own submission, or for citations and further reading, which I already have in bulk.
