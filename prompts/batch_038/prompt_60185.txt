I'm TA-ing a third-year political philosophy course, 90-ish students, and I'm marking the essay section of the final. Question 3 was worth 30 points and read like this:

Q3. Sandel distinguishes two objections to putting human kidneys up for sale. Explain the distinction, and explain why the second is not simply a stronger version of the first. Then: would a regulated market — a single public buyer, a fixed price, medical screening and counselling for sellers — answer the second objection? Give reasons.

Here's one of the answers I've just read:

"Sandel identifies two objections to a kidney market, the coercion objection and the corruption objection. Coercion says the seller doesn't really choose freely, because their alternatives are so bad that the sale is effectively forced on them. Corruption says that turning a body part into a commodity degrades it, treating something that ought to be valued as a gift, or as part of a person, as if it were just a thing to be bought.

The second is more radical because it doesn't depend on how unequal a society happens to be. Coercion only bites under poverty — a wealthy person who sold a kidney to clear a mortgage they didn't strictly need to clear isn't coerced, and the objection falls away. Corruption would still apply to them. Sandel's broader point is that markets don't only allocate goods, they change how we think about the goods, so the corruption objection would survive even a much more equal society.

That is also why regulation can answer the first objection and not the second. A single public buyer at a fixed price, with screening and councelling, removes most of the coercion: no bidding wars, no predatory brokers, no one talked into it by a relative, and the seller knows what they're agreeing to. But a fixed price does nothing about corruption, since the kidney is still being bought, and buying a piece of someone's body at any price degrades their dignity. Though the size of the price does seem to matter to how degrading it feels — a few hundred dollars looks less like treating the person as a commodity than a six-figure sum — which suggests corruption comes in degrees and tracks the price.

Sandel supports the corruption objection empirically, mostly with crowding-out results: Titmuss on blood donation, where paying donors lowered both the supply and the quality of what was donated, and similar findings since. So corruption is an empirical claim about market norms displacing non-market norms, and it stands or falls with that evidence — if those studies failed to replicate, or turned out to be culture-specific, the objection loses its support. He also leans on the fact that people find these markets disgusting, which is basically what Kass means by the wisdom of repugnance, and what the market designer Alvin Roth gets at when he says repugnance is a constraint markets have to work around. Sandel treats the revulsion people feel about kidney sales as evidence that they're wrong."
