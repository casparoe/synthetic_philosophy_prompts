Been rereading Brave New World for like the fourth time, and I've been in the rationalist orbit long enough that I now read every novel as an alignment thought experiment, so sorry about that. I want a reading I haven't seen anyone do.

The lens: tranquilism. The view that what actually matters isn't pleasure, or preference-satisfaction, or flourishing — it's the absence of craving and suffering. Pleasure isn't bad, it's just not the point; a mind that wants nothing is the good outcome. And the version I care about draws a hard line between craving that's been suppressed or engineered away from the outside (drugs, conditioning, a lobotomy, soma, being kept too dumb to want more) and craving that's genuinely gone because the mind saw through what it was doing. From the outside those look identical. On the tranquilist view they arguably aren't the same thing at all. That distinction is what I want the reading to turn on. And no, I don't think tranquilism just collapses into wireheading — partly that's what I want you to check.

So: read the World State as a tranquilist civilization and tell me whether it succeeds or fails on its own terms. Things I want handled:

- the embryos deliberately stunted in the bottle and then sleep-taught to love their caste, so the menial workers are genuinely content. Adaptive preference formation at industrial scale. Does engineered contentment count as absence of craving, or is it a counterfeit that only looks like the real thing?
- the throw-it-away-instead-of-mending conditioning, the feelies, the Solidarity Services. The state doesn't remove desire, it manufactures cheap desire so that satisfaction is guaranteed. Is a craving treadmill running at high frequency and low amplitude better or worse than no treadmill?
- Mustapha Mond, who's the one guy who knows exactly what was traded away. Is his speech actually the strongest argument in the book, and does the novel know it?
- John. I keep wanting him to be the hero and the last third keeps undercutting it — the lighthouse, the whip, the crowd turning up, how it ends. He's maybe the most craving-saturated character in the whole book, guilt and obsession and rage the entire way through. So is Huxley accidentally writing a tranquilist tract, where the guy demanding to be allowed to be miserable, to age, to be sick, to want God is the cautionary tale and the drugged masses are the ones who got it right? Or is there a third position in the book that neither Mond nor John can say out loud, and if so where does it show up?

Also tell me where this reading strains. I'm sure there are places the book just isn't about any of this and forcing it makes a mess, and I'd rather you say so than smooth it over. End with what the novel gives me on this question that a straight philosophical argument about tranquilism wouldn't.
