I am preparing a short comment for a workshop on institutional design, and I have noticed that the debate over futarchy is being conducted almost entirely through two analogies that nobody in the room seems to think need checking. Some setup, so the request is self-contained: in the futarchy proposal associated with Robin Hanson, citizens vote to express what they value — which outcomes count as better — while traders buy and sell contracts on which candidate policies would in fact produce those outcomes, and the policy whose conditional contract trades at the higher expected value is the one adopted. Governance over means is thus outsourced to prices rather than to votes.

One colleague argues against the proposal by saying that a prediction market is simply a casino wearing a civic alibi: gamblers bet for thrill and payout, the house takes a rake, and the events settled are exogenous to the wagers placed on them. If that is what the mechanism is, he thinks futarchy is not merely technically fragile but corrupting in principle — it turns citizens into punters and the state into a bookmaker. He adds, not unreasonably, that the characterization has legal teeth: a federal regulator once barred election contracts on the ground that they constituted gaming, and appellate courts have since disagreed with one another about whether that description is the right one.

A second colleague defends the proposal with the opposite image: a prediction market is a thermometer, or equivalently an opinion poll in which every response is staked. On that reading, manipulation worries are idle — you cannot bribe a thermometer into reading warm, only into being broken — and thin liquidity is noise that aggregation removes. He uses this to wave away the whole objection set at once, including assassination markets, terrorism futures, and wealthy traders cornering the contract on a policy they stand to implement themselves.

What I want is a rigorous audit of both analogies rather than a summary of the literature.

1. For each image, give me the correspondence table: what in the casino, or in the thermometer, plays the role of the contract, the price, the trader, the resolution criterion, the market maker, the electorate, and the adoption rule that makes the price binding on policy. Flag any element of futarchy for which the source domain simply has no counterpart.

2. Identify which features of the source domain each analogy quietly imports that the target lacks, and say which of those imports is doing the real work in the conclusion being drawn from it.

3. Separate the disanalogies that matter from the merely decorative ones. I am especially interested in a structural point neither colleague raises: in a casino the wager is on an event the bettor cannot move, and a thermometer is inert, whereas in futarchy the price is itself an input to the decision that produces the outcome being priced. The instrument is also the actuator. Say whether either analogy can absorb that reflexivity, and whether the image that handles it best is something else entirely — a control system whose sensor drives its own actuator, a jury that is also a party to the case, an auction, a forecasting tournament with performance pay, or something you think fits better. If you propose a replacement analogy, audit it too, at least briefly.

4. Tell me which conclusion survives once the disanalogies are on the table: is futarchy corrupting in principle, or is it an information technology with an engineering problem, or is the truth something this particular analogy contest cannot reach either way? I would rather you said which of the two images you judge less bad than that you declined to rank them.

Finally, close with a note on what would change your verdict: which consideration, counterexample, or reformulation of the mechanism, if I brought it to you, would make you revise the audit, and where you think your own reasoning is most likely to be going wrong.
