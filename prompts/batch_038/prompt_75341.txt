I cover science and ideas for a monthly magazine, and for the past eight months I have been following a pre-registered study at a decision-science lab that presents itself as a controlled descendant of the superrationality experiments Douglas Hofstadter built around his June 1983 column for Scientific American — the piece that culminated in the Luring Lottery, a fixed pot of money divided among whoever entered, later reprinted in Metamagical Themas. Whatever else one says about Hofstadter's version, the set of people eligible to enter it was never closed or enumerated; the new lab's central methodological claim is that it can close the set, publish its size to every member, and thereby make the symmetry that superrationality is supposed to exploit into a matter of common knowledge rather than wishful thinking. I have the full protocol, and last month I sat in on a ninety-minute call between the study's two principals: an economist who has spent her career running one-shot social dilemmas and who regards the entire superrationalist program as a relapse into magical thinking, and a philosopher who defends the symmetry premise and who insists, in effect, that her opponent is describing thirty copies of a single decision problem and then pretending they are thirty unrelated ones. They have now agreed to an adversarial collaboration ahead of the run, and my editor has agreed to publish the resulting document as an exhibit alongside my feature. My problem is that ninety minutes of talk produced nothing either of them would sign, and I need to see what such a document would actually look like.

The protocol has two arms, each with a fresh cohort of thirty participants recruited identically, screened for prior acquaintance with one another, forbidden to communicate, and told the same three things: the rules, the exact size of the pool, and that the other twenty-nine are people just like them who received this briefing at this moment. In Arm A, if exactly one person opts in, that person receives sixty thousand dollars; if nobody opts in or if two or more do, everyone receives nothing; opting in costs nothing. In Arm B, a pot of six thousand dollars is divided equally among those who opt in, and every person who opts in pays a non-refundable fee of four hundred dollars, win or lose; opting out costs nothing and yields nothing. The protocol's own arithmetic note observes that in Arm B an opt-in is profitable only if thirteen or fewer of the other twenty-nine also opt in, so that universal opt-in leaves everyone two hundred dollars in the red, while in Arm A opting in can never leave you worse off than opting out. The lab's statistician adds, drily, that the symmetric opt-in probability maximizing the chance of a single winner in Arm A is not one half, and that even at that optimum the chance of anyone winning is well under forty percent. The registry requires both principals to file numerical predictions for both arms before the data are unsealed.

Draft the joint statement they should produce. It must be a document both could put their names to, so any line one of them would refuse has to be demoted out of the agreed section and into the disagreement section rather than softened into vagueness. I want: first, a numbered list of claims both camps accept — including the payoff structure of each arm, what does and does not dominate in each, what the symmetry premise would have to be true of these particular thirty people in order to license a recommendation, what the briefings do and do not make common knowledge, and the prior question of whether observed opt-in rates can bear on a normative thesis at all. Second, a disagreements table giving, for each point of contention, the issue, the economist's formulation of her own position in words she would accept, the philosopher's formulation of his in words he would accept, and a judgment on whether that particular dispute is substantive or merely a quarrel over the word rational or the word symmetric. Third, the cruxes — the sub-questions such that settling them would move at least one side. Two I already suspect belong there: whether the symmetry premise is a contingent empirical hypothesis about a pool, testable like any other, or a constitutive rule of practical deliberation that no experiment could touch; and whether the philosopher's way out of Arm A — randomizing at some low symmetric rate rather than opting in or out outright — is genuinely superrationality or is really the group-level reasoning of a team choosing a joint strategy, smuggled in. Find the others yourself; I want at least five. For each crux, state the case, comparison, or piece of evidence both agree bears on it, and record each side's prediction in advance as an actual number for each arm, together with the auxiliary psychological assumptions needed to get from a normative recommendation about an idealized reasoner to a forecast about thirty graduate students. Then separate the two layers explicitly wherever the camps are in fact disagreeing about human psychology rather than about decision rules, and finish with two short sections: what each principal would accept as a reason to abandon her or his own position, distinguishing giving up the decision rule from giving up only the auxiliary assumptions; and an accounting of which portions of the argument are verbal, with reasons.

Two constraints on the writing. Assume an intelligent general reader: you may use dominance, credence, and evidential without defining them, and simple arithmetic is welcome, but no formal notation and no jargon that a smart non-specialist would have to stop and parse. And no scholarship — no footnotes, no reading list, no named authorities, and do not put words in quotation marks and attribute them to anyone. I want the reasoning, not the citations. Roughly fourteen hundred words at most.
