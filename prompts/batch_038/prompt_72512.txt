Here's the passage I've been leaning on, roughly: "A lot of people think the new science of interpretability is showing us what neural networks are really thinking about. It isn't, and the reason was pointed out decades ago, when von Neumann remarked that with four parameters you can fit an elephant and with five you can make it wiggle its trunk. These people built the elephant themselves and then announced, with enormous ceremony, that they had found one. Train a model on a dataset of faces and there will be a direction in its activations that tracks faces; train it on code and there will be a direction that tracks indentation. The researchers then run their patching experiments, where they swap activations between two inputs and watch the output, and the swap moves the needle exactly the way anyone who built the machine already knew it would. Nothing has been discovered. Even the field's own papers concede the point: the honest ones admit that single neurons are polysemantic, responding to several unrelated things at once, and that models store far more features than they have dimensions and so pack them on top of each other in superposition. On that picture a representation isn't a thing sitting in the network waiting to be uncovered — it's a decomposition the analyst picks afterwards, out of the astronomically many that fit equally well. Which is why the demos are so theatrical. The famous one where they made a model obsess over the Golden Gate Bridge just amounts to the engineers turning up a dial they had installed. What interpretability actually delivers is a mirror of the experimenter's own expectations, dressed up as an account of the machine's mind."
