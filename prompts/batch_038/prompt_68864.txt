I am helping to compile a casebook on the theory of knowledge in the Nyāya tradition, and the senior editor of my section rules on every case we propose. She works squarely within the Nyāya framework, and she has stated her commitments to us at the outset: there are four pramāṇas and no others — perception, inference, comparison, and testimony; knowledge proper is a veridical apprehension, one that presents an object as qualified by the qualifiers it actually has; error is not the conjuring of something unreal but a misplacement, so that in the shell-silver illusion the silver is real silver, only elsewhere; memory is not itself a means of knowledge, since it re-presents what an earlier apprehension already delivered; testimony carries weight only when the speaker knows the matter as it is and says what he knows; and the postulation of an unobserved fact from an anomaly, together with the apprehension of an absence, are not independent pramāṇas but are, she maintains, reducible to the four.

Her verdicts so far, in the order she gave them:

1. A man walking at dusk sees a coiled rope on the path as a snake and jumps back. Not knowledge. She adds only that nothing unreal was conjured: there are snakes, just not there.
2. The same man, returning in daylight, sees the rope for what it is. Knowledge, by perception.
3. A year later, without any fresh look, he recalls that it was a rope and says so to his son. Not knowledge — the knowing, she remarks, happened at the daylight look.
4. On a hill there is fire, and there is also a hot spring throwing up steam; a traveller takes the steam for smoke, infers fire on the hill, and is right. Not knowledge.
5. A man is stout, and it is agreed that he never eats during the day; from this one concludes that he eats at night. Knowledge, by inference.
6. Looking at a bare floor, one judges that there is no pot on it. Knowledge, by perception; she denies that the absence needs a pramāṇa of its own.
7. A trader is told by a trustworthy forester that a gavaya is a wild animal much like a cow; later, in the forest, he sees such an animal and understands what the word picks out. Knowledge, by comparison.
8. A teacher tells his pupil that the well water is safe to drink. The teacher has been taken in by a forged inspection report, but the water is in fact safe; the pupil believes him, drinks, and comes to no harm. Not knowledge.
9. A man dreams that the low road is flooded, wakes believing it, and the low road is indeed flooded. Not knowledge.

The two cases now before her are these.

Case A. A botanist has a documented anomaly of colour vision: in dim light she systematically takes one particular shade of deep crimson for the particular shade of dull orange that happens to be present. Examining a specimen in a poorly lit herbarium, she judges it to be that dull orange — and it is. Nothing else about her perception is impaired, she is fully attentive, the judgment is new to her rather than a re-presentation of anything she already knew, and she acts on it successfully, filing the specimen under the species that colour indicates, which is the species it turns out to belong to.

Case B. A woman looks at a pot on a table in good light and judges that there is a pot there. A moment later, without having looked away, without any change in the light, and without having learnt anything in between, she looks again and judges, of that very same pot under those very same conditions, that there is a pot there.

Predict her verdict on each case and, wherever she says knowledge, the pramāṇa she will credit it to. Then tell me which rule she is running. Is it a condition that the causal chain be free of defect, in organ, medium, or background circumstance? Is it a condition that the cognition issue from one of the four recognised kinds of process, generously individuated, since she is happy to fold postulation into inference and absence into perception? Is it a condition that the apprehension be new rather than a re-presentation of an earlier one? Is it a condition tied to the cognition's capacity to guide successful activity towards the object as represented? Or is it some conjunction of these, or something I have not thought of?

I want you to identify every candidate rule that is equally consistent with all nine of her verdicts, say what each predicts about Cases A and B, and mark exactly where they come apart. Then commit: give me your best guess at both verdicts, with the reasons for preferring your rule over the alternatives, and tell me specifically how much work verdict 8 — the deceived teacher — is doing, since it seems to me the least obvious of the nine and possibly the most constraining. Note also whether either new case forces a choice she has so far managed to avoid, and if so, what her options are within her own framework.

Finally, name one further case, described in enough detail that I could put it to her verbatim, that would discriminate between the two rules you judge most likely — and explain what each of those two rules would say about it. Please do not give me references, a bibliography, or a reading list; I want the reasoning, and nothing that depends on my looking anything up. Write the answer in British English.
