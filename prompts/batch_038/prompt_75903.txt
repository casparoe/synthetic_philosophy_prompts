I am in the second half of a graduate ethics seminar devoted to population size and the value of numbers, and my term paper is supposed to take a position on whether a larger population is ever better. Before going further into the secondary literature I tried to do the honest thing and write down my own verdicts on a set of cases, treating every comparison as ceteris paribus: same institutions, same distribution among the people involved, no differences other than the ones I state. What follows is what I actually believe, or think I believe. I would like to know what principle, if any, captures the pattern, and where it breaks.

The baseline world, Arcadia, contains ten billion people, all of whom live excellent lives: long, healthy, deeply satisfying, full of achievement and relationship.

1. Arcadia-Plus is Arcadia unchanged, plus a further hundred trillion people living elsewhere who never interact with the first group, each of whose lives is worth living but only barely: drab, short, containing nothing that would be lost to the universe if it had never happened, though each person would say they are glad to have existed. My verdict: Arcadia-Plus is not worse than Arcadia. If pressed, I would say it is very slightly better.

2. Drab World has the same vast population, but everyone, including the descendants of Arcadia's ten billion, is at the drab level. My verdict: much worse than Arcadia, and worse than Arcadia-Plus. I am not prepared to give this one up; the repugnant conclusion strikes me as a reductio of any view that entails it.

3. A world containing a hundred trillion people at the drab level is better than a world containing a single billion people whose lives are dominated by unbearable suffering. Adding the sufferers is far worse than adding nothing at all.

4. Arcadia's government can either spend a fixed sum making each of the ten billion somewhat better off, or spend it bringing two billion new people into existence with good though not excellent lives, leaving the original ten billion exactly where they are. My verdict: the second option is at least as good as the first, and I lean toward its being better.

5. A couple can have a third child who will have a good but unremarkable life. My verdict: permissible, but there is no reason counting in favor of having her. Her prospective good life does not weigh in the decision. Yet if she will exist anyway, improving her prospects is a strong reason, and declining to have a child who would have a miserable life is a strong reason too. I hold all three of those. I also hold that a humanity which chose to become extinct after one generation of unprecedented contentment would have done something tragic, a real loss, even though on the asymmetry I just described nobody is harmed by never existing.

6. A woman is told that conceiving now will produce a child with a mild lifelong impairment, while waiting three months will produce a different, unimpaired child; both lives would be worth living. My verdict: she ought to wait, though I do not think the impaired child is harmed or has a complaint against her, since no one is made worse off. I also think a society-wide policy with the same shape, guaranteeing that the future is populated by impaired rather than unimpaired people, is clearly and seriously bad, whereas the individual choice is only mildly criticizable. Same structure, different weight, and I cannot explain why.

7. My grandmother's death is not compensated by the birth of a stranger who is exactly as happy. Replacement does not make up for loss. But if only one of two possible children can exist, bringing the one with better prospects is better than bringing the other.

8. A state policy explicitly aimed at maximizing population size would be bad, even if a much larger population of people with good lives would be a better world. So I say both that a certain outcome would be better and that we should not pursue it.

My draft principle is this. A world is better than another if and only if neither contains lives that are not worth living, and its weighted total is greater, where each person's contribution is their wellbeing multiplied by a weight that rises with how good their own life is, so that an excellent life counts for a great deal and a drab one for very little. That looks as if it delivers 1, 2, and 3 together: a hundred trillion near-weightless lives cannot catch up to ten billion excellent ones, and suffering lives fail the first condition outright. But I can see the flaw, and I want to know whether it is fatal. A weighted total is still additive, so for any fixed ratio of weights, sufficiently many drab lives will outscore a few excellent ones, and I get the repugnant conclusion back in slow motion. The only repair I can see is to make the weighting non-additive in a strong sense: no finite number of drab lives outweighs a single excellent life. But that seems to forbid trading any improvement for the best-off against any number of new happy people, which contradicts 4, and it makes my rejection of pronatalist policy in 8 look arbitrary rather than principled.

So: is there a principle, or a small set of candidates, that keeps all eight verdicts, or all but one? Please state each candidate precisely enough that I could apply it to a new case myself, tie each of its clauses to the verdict it exists to save, and give its verdicts on all eight. Where a candidate must overturn one of my verdicts, I want the argument that the verdict is wrong, not merely the observation that it offends the theory. Then tell me what each candidate says about three cases I have no settled view on: a final generation of unprecedented excellence followed by voluntary extinction, versus ten thousand further years at a very high but slightly lower level; a fixed resource that can either create a thousand new people with excellent lives or raise a thousand existing drab lives to excellence; and a couple whose individual choice is unobjectionable but which, if everyone made it, would produce the drab future. Finally, say whether verdicts 8 and 6's scale asymmetry are really claims about what is better at all, or whether they are claims about promotion and decision that a purely axiological principle was never meant to carry, since if so, some of my tension may dissolve rather than need solving. I am not looking for a survey or a bibliography; I want the reasoning laid out so I can defend one account in seminar and know exactly what I am giving up to do it.
