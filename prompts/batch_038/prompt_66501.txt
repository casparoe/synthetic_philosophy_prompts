Here's the draft (sections 1–3 written out, section 4 still just notes):

> **1.** The standard picture of Hobbes on geometry goes like this. Philosophy is knowledge of effects acquired from causes, or causes from effects; but we cannot know the causes of natural things, since God is their author, so natural philosophy yields at best conditional knowledge. Geometry and civil philosophy are the exceptions, because there we are ourselves the authors of the objects: we draw the figure, we institute the commonwealth. On the usual reading — call it the stipulation reading — the certainty of geometry comes from the fact that its principles are definitions, and definitions are arbitrary stipulations about names, truth being a property of speech consisting in the joining and separating of names. So geometry is certain because it is cheap: it is certainty about our own vocabulary, and it makes contact with the world only by courtesy.
>
> **2.** I want to argue that the stipulation reading makes a hash of three features of Hobbes's actual practice, and that a better reading is available.
>
> (i) If geometry were the science of stipulated names, then a new notation would be a new stipulation and there would be nothing to object to — indeed it would be an improvement in bookkeeping. Instead Hobbes treats the algebraising of geometry as something close to a fraud, a substitution of marks for reasoning, and he spends decades on it: De Corpore in 1655, Wallis's Elenchus the same year, the Six Lessons the next, and so on until he's nearly eighty. Stipulation predicts indifference. He shows hatred.
>
> (ii) Hobbes insists that the objects of geometry are bodies considered as such, and complains about treating magnitude and number as though they floated free of body. On the stipulation reading it shouldn't matter one bit what the objects are.
>
> (iii) Hobbes wants his definitions to give the generation of the thing defined — you define a circle by the motion of a line carried round a fixed point — and he criticises definitions that fail to do this. Generation is a causal notion, not a semantic one.
>
> So I distinguish arbitrary stipulative definitions from what I'll call **operative definitions**: a definition is operative when it is a recipe for producing the object by motion, and to possess it is to be able to perform the construction. On this reading, "we are the authors of the objects of geometry" is not a loose way of saying "we made up the words." The motion of the generating line literally is the cause of the circle, so the geometer knows the cause in the strict sense, in the way a watchmaker knows the watch — not because he named the parts, but because he made them. Geometry is subordinate to kinematics for a reason.
>
> This has a payoff, and it's the part I like most: operative definitions are not cheap. Only constructions that can actually be performed confer knowledge of a cause, so a construction you cannot complete is not a cause you can know. That explains Hobbes's allergy to infinite quantities in a way the stipulation reading can't — his own diagnosis, in a letter at the time, was that the infinite is a back door for scholasticism, which is fine as far as it goes but doesn't explain why *he* thought so. On my reading it's structural, not merely sectarian. And it explains why squaring the circle mattered to him so much: a quadrature is not for Hobbes the proof that some value exists, it's the exhibition of a construction, i.e. the demonstration that the cause is in our power.
>
> **3.** Objection. Two versions, and I can't decide which is worse. First: if constructions are just physical motions, geometry becomes a branch of physics and inherits physics's contingency — you have traded certainty for contact, exactly the trade Hobbes refuses. Second: Hobbes's explicit account of truth is about the joining of names, and he says a proposition is known to be necessary by resolving it back into its definitions; that machinery looks like it does all the work, with no role left for my constructions. So the operative reading may be the stipulation reading wearing a causal costume borrowed from the polemics — and the polemics are precisely the place where Hobbes is least careful, since he is losing an argument with Wallis in public for thirty years.
>
> My reply is a two-level move: the *necessity* of geometrical truths is semantic, but *scientia* in the strict sense is knowledge of causes, not mere knowledge that truths hold, and only construction supplies the why. I'm honestly not sure this is enough; it may just relocate the problem to the question of why Hobbes would bother having two notions.
>
> **4. (notes)** Push it to politics: is the sovereign's institution an operative definition? Does the commonwealth get defined by generation from the state of nature in the same literal sense? Would this unify De Corpore with De Cive and Leviathan, or stretch the term past breaking point? Also: is the comparison with Vico's later "we know only what we make" slogan worth a paragraph, or is it an anachronism that will annoy the historian of math?
