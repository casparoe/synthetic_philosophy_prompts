The passage:

"Leibniz's law says that two things which share all their properties are really one and the same thing, and it is a cornerstone of logic — if it fails, identity itself comes apart. In 1952 Max Black refuted it. He asked us to picture a universe containing nothing at all except two spheres of chemically pure iron, each a mile across, of the same temperature and colour, and the same distance from each other. Now, no observation, not even in principle, could tell you which sphere was which, and Black's point was that if there is no way of telling two things apart then there really is only one thing there. Defenders have tried to rescue Leibniz by insisting that each sphere has the property of being that very sphere, but this is a verbal trick: it merely repeats that there are two of them without saying what the difference is. So Black showed that two objects can share every property, intrinsic and relational alike — and modern physics has since confirmed it, given that two identical particles can be in exactly the same state and be literally indistinguishable."
