I am co-teaching the third week of an upper-level metaphysics-of-time seminar at a small liberal arts college, and this year the assigned novel is Vonnegut's *Slaughterhouse-Five*, which almost every student has read before and therefore thinks they already understand. The week's technical material is the persistence debate — endurantism against perdurantism, with Sider's stage-theoretic exdurantism as the third option — and the students arrive having made one move that I would like to complicate rather than simply correct. They read Billy's letter to the newspaper, in which he reports that the Tralfamadorians taught him that "when a person dies he only appears to die. He is still very much alive in the past," and that the aliens regard a corpse as a person who is "in bad condition in that particular moment, but... just fine in plenty of other moments," and they conclude that the novel is a four-dimensionalist text, that Vonnegut has dramatized the spacetime worm, and that the case is closed. What I want from you is a piece of criticism, roughly two thousand words, that takes both the novel and the metaphysics seriously and is willing to say where the pairing fails in each direction.

The first thing I need is a careful separation of theses the students routinely fuse. At least three distinct claims are in play: a B-theoretic claim that all times are equally real and that the ordering of moments into a single privileged present is, as Billy puts it, "an illusion... like beads on a string"; an ontological claim that persisting objects, and persons in particular, are composed of temporal parts; and a claim about perception, that a mind can stand outside the worm and survey it whole, which is what the Tralfamadorians are represented as doing and what Billy reports when he describes them seeing an Earthling as a long many-legged creature with an infant's legs at one end and an old person's legs at the other. Does the novel need the second claim at all, or would a B-theoretic endurantist of Mellor's sort accommodate everything Billy says? I think the "in bad condition in that particular moment" formulation is doing real work here and cuts against the students' reading: it looks less like the temporal-parts strategy for the problem of temporary intrinsics than like the time-indexed or relational-property strategy, in which a wholly present object bears a property only relative to a time. Please argue that out rather than asserting it, and then say what the novel loses or gains depending on how it comes out. The second crux is phenomenological. If all moments are permanently and equally real, what is it that moves? Billy does not merely have his life laid out for inspection; he travels, involuntarily, from Dresden to the Ilium airport to the zoo on Tralfamadore, and each arrival is a now for him. That is very close to the moving-spotlight picture — a block ontology with a transitory present attached to a point of view — which is an A-theoretic view, and it sits uneasily beside the fatalism Billy professes. I would like you to press on whether Vonnegut notices the discrepancy or exploits it, and separately on whether the fatalism even needs it: the Tralfamadorians' certainty that the universe will be destroyed by a test pilot pressing a starter button is a claim about determinism and foreknowledge, and it is not obvious that tenselessness by itself delivers any of the necessity Billy attributes to it.

My colleague has a more radical proposal that I would like you to take seriously and then test. Her claim is that the novel is not a worm-theoretic text but a stage-theoretic one: on her reading it never presents a persisting Billy at all, only momentary person-stages juxtaposed across chapters by something like counterpart relations, which is why the narration can put Billy in 1945, 1967 and on Tralfamadore in adjacent paragraphs without there being a fact about which of them is the real Billy. The obvious objection is that anticipation and prudence seem to require a single subject spanning the stages, and Billy anticipates: he foreknows his own assassination in Chicago, he dreads what is coming, he writes letters and books about it. Worse, his visits run backwards as well as forwards, so the counterpart relation cannot be doing its usual work of tracking psychological continuity in the temporal order. Consider whether the novel therefore refutes stage theory, whether it shows stage theory's peculiar advantage — that it alone makes sense of a mind with no single now — or whether my colleague has simply imported a technical vocabulary onto a narrative device that is doing something else entirely. I also want the ethical and affective layer treated with the same rigor. Is the refrain "So it goes," attached mechanically to every death in the book, an enactment of Parfit's Extreme Claim, the reductionist's conclusion that we have no special reason to care about our own future, or is it something cruder — a trauma anaesthetic? Note that Billy's cosmology may not even be his: the pulp science fiction of Kilgore Trout supplies much of the alien imagery, his daughter treats him as a child after the plane crash, and the abduction story is one he tells rather than one the novel guarantees.

Finally, I want the reading to break where it ought to break. Chapter one is stubbornly anti-fatalist: Harrison Starr's remark that one might as well write an anti-glacier book, the narrator's love for Lot's wife turned into a pillar of salt "because it was so human," the announced climax of the execution of poor old Edgar Derby for a teapot in the ruins of a burned city, and the instruction to his sons never to take part in massacres. None of that survives a metaphysics on which nothing could be otherwise, and yet it is the same book in which Billy shrugs at funerals. Tell me whether Vonnegut holds both views, ironizes one of them, or simply fails to notice, and address the formal question too: is the jumbled, non-chronological structure itself a literary four-dimensionalism, a text meant to be apprehended as a completed whole rather than read through — and does the novel's admission of its own failure, along with the necessity of reading it serially, undercut that reading from inside? End with an explicit verdict on what *Slaughterhouse-Five* contributes to the persistence debate that the technical arguments do not. My own hunch, which you should challenge if you disagree, is that it is not an argument at all but a phenomenology: four-dimensionalism presented not as a solution to temporary intrinsics but as a way of living with a death one has already survived.

Likewise, do not quote the novel at length; locate scenes by brief description and paraphrase, using only the very shortest phrases you are certain of, and flag anything you are unsure about as such.
