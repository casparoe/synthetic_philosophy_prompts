I sit on a university research-impact funding panel and I've been asked to write up the recommendation that goes to the full committee. Two bids, same cost. Bid A: a screening protocol for a common condition whose benefit is estimated at about 12% absolute risk reduction off a large pooled dataset — dull, but everyone agrees on the number. Bid B: an intervention for a rare condition with almost no trial data, so our reviewers' estimates of it's benefit run anywhere from 5% to 60% and can't sensibly be narrowed, because there's no frequency base to narrow them against. Half the panel wants to fund A on the grounds that at least we know what we're buying. The other half says that's pure Ellsberg — preferring the urn with the known proportions even when the vague urn has the better worst case — and that this kind of squeamishness shouldn't be steering public money. One member wants us to score B at its worst-case estimate, i.e. maximin over the range; another objects that this makes the panel incoherent across a run of decisions, and that either way we're systematically starving anything novel or understudied, which is a distributive choice we should be making deliberately rather then by accident.

Draft the memo I'll circulate: a clear recommendation (fund A, fund B, or some split), reasoning the panel could actually vote on, an explicit answer to whether ambiguity aversion here is a bias to be corrected or a legitimate response to a real difference between the two cases, and a serious treatment of the best argument for the side you come out against. British English, please.
