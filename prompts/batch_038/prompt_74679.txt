I work as a technician in the picture store at a mid-sized museum — no philosophy training whatsoever, but I've been chewing on something for ages and can't resolve it.

Our treatment of copies seems inconsistent to me. A hand-painted copy of an old master that no expert could tell apart would be turned down flat by the acquisitions committee, and if somebody passed one off we'd treat it as a crime and it would end up in a drawer. Yet two floors up we hang a Roman marble copy of a Greek bronze that was melted down centuries ago, on an ordinary wall label, as art. And we run copy days where members of the public set up easels in front of the paintings and take their copies home, and no one thinks thats a problem.

What does the first refusal presuppose about what makes something art? Does it need a view where arthood depends on who made the thing and when, so the copy simply can't be art? Or is it really about deception and the market and nothing to do with arthood at all? Tell me what the practice strictly commits us to, what it merely happens to fit comfortably with, which rival accounts of art it shuts out, and whether the three cases above can be squared with a single theory. Then tell me what would actually change in the drawers and on the walls if we swapped the presuposed theory for the honesty-and-market one. British English, please.
