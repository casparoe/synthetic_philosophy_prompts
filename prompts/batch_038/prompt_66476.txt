My flatmate has sole control of our household food budget, which means her veto is effectively binding on everything I eat at home, and I would like to be able to predict it rather than negotiate it item by item. Her choices look arbitrary from the outside, but I am now fairly convinced they are generated by some single principle, and I want that principle identified before I bring four new items into the kitchen.

Her record to date, together with the reason she offers on each occasion:

- Supermarket chicken and eggs: refused. Her reason is that the number of birds standing behind one breast is very large and that every one of them was confined.
- Grain-fed feedlot beef: refused. She points out that the animal consumed several kilograms of tilled grain for each kilogram of beef it produced, and she treats the meat as a multiplier on the deaths already incurred in the fields.
- Beef from a neighbour who rotationally grazes cattle on permanent pasture that has never been ploughed and receives no grain: eaten freely and without apology.
- Farmed salmon: refused. The feed is made from wild-caught forage fish, so she describes a farmed salmon as a device for condensing the deaths of a great many small wild animals into one large one.
- Wild sardines and anchovies: eaten.
- Shrimp: refused in both forms, though for what may be different reasons — trawled because of what comes up in the net and what was cleared to make room for the fleet, pond-farmed because of stocking density and water exchange.
- Oysters and mussels: eaten without hesitation, and actively recommended to guests.
- Grains and legumes: eaten, but she will pay a premium and drive further for crops grown without tillage. When I objected that this cannot be about her health or even about soil carbon, she said it is about what the plough and the combine do to mice, voles, and insects in the field. She concedes that no-till yields less per acre and says she accepts that trade.

That is eleven verdicts. The four items I am considering:

1. Cultivated chicken, grown from cells in a bioreactor, so that no bird is ever hatched, confined, or slaughtered — but whose nutrient medium draws its glucose and amino acids from conventionally tilled maize and soy, and whose conversion losses are large enough that a given quantity of the finished product implies more cropland under cultivation than a pasture-raised bird of the same weight.
2. Sea scallops dragged from the seabed, set against rope-grown mussels from the same coast: animals she would score identically for welfare purposes, separated only by the fact that the first is harvested by gear drawn across the bottom.
3. Cricket flour — an enormous number of individuals per kilogram, kept at high density, fed on tilled grain, and killed by gradual freezing.
4. Two plant-based patties of identical protein content, one made from soy grown on prairie broken within the last decade, the other from soy grown on long-established no-till farmland.

What I want from you:

- Name the candidate principles capable of accounting for all eleven existing verdicts. Where a candidate cannot in fact cover them, say precisely which verdict it breaks on and what minimal amendment would be needed to save it, rather than discarding it silently.
- Several of these principles will be families rather than single rules — a weighting of individuals against habitat, a confidence discount applied to less neurally complex animals, a per-calorie denominator against a per-hectare one. For each family, state which parameter her verdicts pin down and which they leave entirely free, since a principle with an unconstrained parameter is not yet a prediction.
- State what each surviving principle predicts for each of the four new items, and mark the points at which the predictions diverge rather than merely the points at which they agree.
- Commit to one best guess about the principle she is actually running, and justify the preference by appeal to the eleven verdicts rather than to the plausibility of the principle in the abstract. If your best guess is underdetermined by the data, say so and specify exactly how much of it is.
- Identify which of the four items would most efficiently discriminate between the leading rivals, or, if none of them would, construct the item that would, and state what her verdict must be under each rival for the test to have bite.
- Finally, assess whether any of the surviving principles is unstable under an assumption she has never stated aloud but may hold: that the lives of wild field animals are on balance worse than nothing. If she holds it, her no-till preference and her grazing-beef acceptance change character, and I want to know whether the eleven verdicts can tell me which way she leans.
