So — slightly embarrassing ask, given that this is my own field, but here goes. Three years ago the county planning commission roped me into facilitating their multi-stakeholder land-use roundtable (the "a professor can make everyone behave" theory of governance), and now I run maybe eight of these a year: a conservation coalition, a ranchers' association, a tribal natural-resources office, two developers, a housing nonprofit. Same cast, different parcel, roughly every six weeks, and every one of them ends with me having to tell the steering committee whether the deal on the table is a real compromise or a bad one. What I've found is that the theory I know cold is useless at 9pm in a commmunity-center basement with twelve tired people and a flip chart. I can tell them about gains from trade under value pluralism — the point that if people disagree about the good, coordinating can leave everyone better off by their own lights without anyone having to convert — and I can name the standard objections: that some things are supposed to be off the table entirely rather than priced, that a compromise between two value systems is often a position neither one actually holds, that side-payments in the wrong currency can be an insult rather than compensation, that a deal between A and B can quietly dump the cost on C, who never got a seat. None of that survives contact with "so do we sign this or not."

What I want is a decision procedure my facilitators can run on a proposed compromise: the questions to ask in order, what counts as a passing answer to each, where the procedure just stops and says no, and where it has to hand the thing back to the parties because its a question about their values and not about the facts. Assume whoever runs it is a grad student or a county staffer with no philosophy training — one page, usable inside a single three-hour session, and it has to yield a recommendation I can put in front of a twelve-member steering committee that will read it in ten minutes and then vote. Tell me which distinctions from the literature genuinely have to survive the simplification and which ones I can safely drop, and be blunt about which steps are judgment calls wearing a procedure's clothes: I'd rather be told the test bottoms out in "does this party sincerely endorse it" than be handed something that pretends there's a fact of the matter underneath.

Then run it on three cases, all near-composites of deals I've actually sat through. (1) The conservation coalition drops its opposition to forty units on the ranch's dry upland parcel; the ranch family puts a permanent easement on six hundred acres of riparian habitat; the housing nonprofit is delighted; the family refuses any cash top-up and insists the easement is a gift to the creek, not a sale. (2) A faith-based group that holds a certain clinic's services to be intrinsically wrong agrees to stop contesting the clinic's lease in the county building, in exchange for the operator funding a counseling program the group itself runs — everyone's lawyers are calling it a win and nobody had to change their mind about anything. (3) The county and a developer settle on a ninety-nine-year arrangement that shifts the stormwater burden downstream to an unincorporated community with no vote, no staff, and nobody at the table. Be straight with me about which of these the procedure kills, and make sure at least one comes out in a way I'll hate: my suspicion is that a procedure genuinely faithful to the gains-from-trade idea is going to endorse something my instincts call a surrender, and I want to find out whether that's true rather than have the thing quietly tuned to reach my preferred verdicts. End with a paragraph on what the procedure can't do — where it will misfire, and what I should tell the committee when it does.
