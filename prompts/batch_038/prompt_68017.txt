I teach a third-year decision theory module (mostly philosophy students, a few economists) and I'm writing the final. I want one essay-style problem on money pumps and diachronic choice that can't be answered by reciting the standard line — you know, "intransitive prefernces let a clever trader drain your wallet, therefore transitivity is a requirement of rationality." Every student can say that sentence and I'm sick of grading it. So I've built a case with numbers in it, and I want the question built around the case rather than around the label.

Act as a decision theorist who has taught this material for years and sets exams designed to separate people who understand an argument from people who've memorised its name. Think carefully and do the analysis yourself before you write anything: start with a short scratch working section where you actually walk the decision tree node by node and check that the outcomes come out the way I've described. If something in my stipulations doesn't hold up, or if a policy is ambiguous in a way I haven't noticed, say so plainly there instead of papering over it.

Here's the case. Keep the numbers exactly as given.

Dana is choosing between three used bikes: X, Y and Z. All-things-considered, comparing bikes only: Y ≻ X, Z ≻ Y, X ≻ Z. There is no uncertainty anywhere, no lotteries, no probabilities. Money matters to Dana separately and monotonically: holding the bike fixed, less spent is better. For each step up the cycle Dana would pay up to but not more than $2, so (Y, −$1) ≻ (X, $0), (Z, −$1) ≻ (Y, $0), and (X, −$1) ≻ (Z, $0).

The sequence. Dana is handed X for free. Then:
- Node 1: offered Y in exchange for whatever Dana currently holds, plus $1.
- Node 2: offered Z in exchange for whatever Dana currently holds, plus $1.
- Node 3: offered X in exchange for whatever Dana currently holds, plus $1.

Three choice policies to define for the students: myopic (compares only the swap on the table right now), sophisticated (reasons backwards through its own future ex-post choices), and resolute (adopts a plan at the outset and follows it regardless of what it prefers later).

The question should have four parts, 25 marks total, answerable in 60 minutes:
(a) the outcome under each of the three policies, with the node-by-node reasoning, and for the resolute policy the student has to say which plan Dana adopts and why;
(b) a diagnosis of a plausible-sounding student answer — "each swap buys Dana a step up they value at $2 for only $1, so Dana nets $1 every time and can't be losing out overall" — saying exactly where that goes wrong, or whether it goes wrong at all;
(c) showing that Dana's ranking of the outcomes reachable from the start is itself cyclic, and what that does to the resolute reply to the pump;
(d) whether the fact that sophisticated Dana isn't pumped shows the money-pump argument is really aimed at myopia rather than at intransitive preference — plus the strongest objection to that reading. This is the crux, its where the marks should be concentrated.

Format, in this order:

1. **Working** (~150 words) — your own node-by-node analysis and any problems you found with my stipulations.
2. **The question** (~300 words) — exactly as students would see it, with the case stated, the three policies defined, and the mark allocation per part.
3. **Model answer** (~900 words) — what a first-class answer says, weighted towards (b)–(d). It must take a position on (d) and defend it, not survey positions.
4. **Marking scheme** (~400 words) — per part, the marks available, the specific moves that have to be present for full credit, and a line on what separates an A from a B answer (e.g. the B student gets the myopic case right and asserts that sophistication "solves" the problem without explaining what it costs).
5. **Where students go wrong** (~150 words) — the four or five most common errors and what each one reveals about what they memorised instead of understood.

No bibliography, no citations, no name-dropping of authors or papers anywhere in the deliverable — the question has to stand alone and I don't want to reward students for remembering who said what. Also don't let the model answer drift into a history of the debate; keep it on this case.
