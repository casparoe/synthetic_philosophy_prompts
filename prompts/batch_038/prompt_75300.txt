I sit on the Community Partnership Review Panel of a mid-sized American city, and in nine days we must decide whether the city will execute a ten-year agreement with a housing coalition that has no legal existence of any kind. Here is the record as it comes to us.

The coalition formed in 2022 to oppose a redevelopment plan covering four neighborhoods. It has no bylaws, no incorporation, no officers, no membership roll, and no bank account. It meets weekly as an assembly in which roughly fourteen organizations — two tenant unions, two congregations, a mutual-aid network, a legal clinic, and several block associations — send delegates, and unaffiliated residents may also speak and, in practice, also block. Its stated decision rule is modified consensus: a proposal carries if no delegate blocks and a large majority assents, with dissenters permitted to stand aside. Its public statements are issued over the coalition's name alone, unsigned, and it consistently describes its own output in the first-person plural.

Two features of the record complicate matters. First, last spring the coalition signed a letter of intent on a related matter; within a month three of the fourteen organizations publicly repudiated it, saying their delegates had exceeded their mandate, and one said it had never authorized its delegate to conclude anything at all. Second, in the current negotiation the city has asked for commitments that run against individuals rather than organizations — that residents will forgo individual administrative challenges to the plan, and that no member will organize opposition to phase two. The coalition's negotiating team says it can deliver these. Two of the fourteen organizations have told staff privately that they cannot speak for anybody's members.

The city attorney's position is that there is no counterparty: an unincorporated assembly cannot be a party to a contract, and even if it could, no one can say who would be bound. Two panel members reply that demanding incorporation is precisely the demand that grassroots groups least able to formalize cannot meet, and that the city would be telling fourteen neighborhoods that only groups that already look like corporations are permitted to make promises.

Please draft the written opinion I will circulate to the panel before the meeting. It should:

1. Open with a clear bottom line — execute as drafted, execute in a modified form, or decline — and, if modified, set out the specific structure you recommend in terms a council attorney could draft from, together with a timeline and what the city should do if the coalition refuses that structure.

2. State explicitly what the city would be relying on in relying on the coalition's commitment: whether the panel should understand itself as dealing with one agent that can form and revise intentions, make a promise, and be betrayed, or with fourteen separate agents plus an unenumerated crowd that happen to coordinate well, and why that distinction has practical consequences in this case rather than serving as metaphysical decoration.

3. Say what would have to be true of the coalition for the first understanding to be available — what you would look for in its decision-making, its capacity to speak and act as one, its ability to bind or discipline its parts, and the standing of delegates — and then assess, feature by feature, which of these the record supports, which it leaves open, and which it contradicts. The repudiated letter of intent is evidence; say what it is evidence of and what else it might be evidence of.

4. Address the commitments that run against individuals. Is there any arrangement under which the coalition could genuinely undertake these, and if not, what should replace them in the agreement?

5. Take the incorporation objection seriously. If your recommendation asks something of the coalition that a formal nonprofit would never be asked for, say so and justify the asymmetry; if it does not, show that it does not.

6. Identify the strongest consideration on the other side of your recommendation — the reason a thoughtful panel member could vote against it without having misunderstood you — and explain why it does not change your conclusion, or say plainly that it does.

Keep it to what the panel can read in ten minutes, and do not pad it with a survey of positions. Assume ordinary American contract principles and cite no authority; where an answer would turn on a jurisdiction-specific rule, say that it does and route it to the city attorney. Wherever your recommendation depends on a contestable view about what groups are and when they act, flag that dependency in a sentence instead of burying it, and note in one line how the recommendation would change if the opposite view were correct.
