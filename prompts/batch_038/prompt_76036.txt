I'm co-teaching an upper-level philosophy of science seminar with a materials chemist this term and our block on classification is coming up — natural kinds, whether the sciences carve nature at its joints, that whole mess. My colleague has the problem sets, I have the two-hour final, and I need one essay question worth 25 marks that students can't just survive by reciting. The trouble is everything I've tried so far collapses into a book report: ask them about water and I get four pages of Twin Earth ending in "so microstructure is the essence," ask them about species and I get the units-of-evolution debate back verbatim, ask them about jade and I get the wikipedia version of the jade example. I'd rather build the question around a made-up case study, concrete enough that they actually have to reason with it instead of pattern-matching to a famous example, and weird enough that whatever they've memorized doesn't cleanly apply.

Here's the case I've been kicking around, feel free to sharpen it but keep the structure — specimens from deep brine pools that cluster tightly on a bunch of properties (absorption spectrum, hardness band, solubility in weak acid, and a catalytic capacity to fix nitrogen at low temperature), except that crystallography turns up at least three distinct lattice types among specimens that are otherwise indistinguishable, and each lattice only forms in brines with a specific ion profile, so when the brine chemistry shifts the same surface cluster reappears on a different structure. Two communities carve it differently: geochemists classify by lattice, field microbiologists classify by the property cluster because the catalytic capacity is what their ecosystem models need. Both classifications support genuinely successful prediction inside their own domain and neither predicts the other's targets very well. The bits I want the question to hang on: the cluster is robust and not an artifact, there's no single homeostatic mechanism holding it together but three independent ones that happen to converge, and the two communities aren't obviously disagreeing about the same thing. What I want students to be forced into is figuring out whether "is this a natural kind?" is even a single question, what a homeostatic-cluster account actually predicts here and whether the multiple-mechanism detail helps or wrecks it, how microstructural essentialism is supposed to cope when the microstructure is disjunct, and how much of the interest-relativity of the microbiologists' classification a realist can absorb before the realism stops costing anything.

So: write the question as it should appear on the paper (scoped so it's doable in about 50 minutes of the two hours, and don't ask them to cite anything or produce a bibliography), then a model answer of roughly 800–900 words, then a marking scheme out of 25 that names the specific moves that seperate an A from a B — not generic stuff like "engages with the literature" but things like "notices that the geochemist and the microbiologist may not be contradicting each other" and "says what the cluster account is committed to about convergence-without-common-cause and why that's a problem rather than a convenience." Also give me the list of mistakes that should cost marks explicitly, with how much each costs, and a couple of sentences on what you expect the modal weak answer to look like so I can calibrate with my colleague, who thinks there should be a determinate right answer here and I'm not convinced. If you think the best version of the question pushes students to defend a claim about what would settle the dispute at all, that's fine — just make that requirement visible in the marking scheme.
