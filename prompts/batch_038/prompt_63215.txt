**Question 3.** Suppose the universe contains infinitely many person-moments, arranged in a time order, and that a world is fully described by the welfare number attached to each person-moment in that order. Consider three worlds:
- **W**: every person-moment has welfare +1
- **X**: every person-moment has welfare +2
- **Y**: the first googol (10^100) person-moments have welfare −10, and every person-moment after that has welfare +2

Background: the overtaking criterion, borrowed from the economics of long-run growth, says that world A is better than world B just in case there's some point in the sequence such that for every later point, the running sum of welfare in A exceeds the running sum in B.

(a) Naive total utilitarianism adds up welfare across all person-moments. Explain why that procedure fails to rank W against X. Is the failure that the two totals come out equal, or something different?
(b) Rank W, X and Y under the overtaking criterion, showing your work. Does overtaking always deliver a verdict for any pair of worlds? If not, describe the kind of case where it fails.
(c) Someone in the club argued that what (b) shows is that in an infinite universe no finite amount of suffering matters at all, so the only thing worth working on practically is making sure the long run happens and is populated by good lives. Assess that argument.
(d) Would switching to average welfare, or to a total measured with a larger infinity (a non-Archimedean value, like a hyperreal), do better than overtaking? Explain.

And here's the answer I need graded:

**(a)** Adding welfare across infinitely many person-moments gives an infinite series, and 1+1+1+… and 2+2+2+… both diverge to infinity. Since infinity equals infinity, the total welfare of W and X is the same number, so total utilitarianism is indifferent between them. That's the problem of infinite ethics in a nutshell: the theory goes quiet (or rather, it says "equally good") exactly where we need it most. It's also bad for practice, because if W and X really are equally good then we're permitted to flip a coin between bringing about a universe where everyone is ecstatic and one where everyone is only barely content, and nobody thinks that's acceptable.

**(b)** Overtaking compares running sums. After n moments X's running total is 2n and W's is n, and 2n > n for every n, so X strictly overtakes W. For Y, the first googol moments are −10 instead of +2, so Y falls behind X by 12 per moment during the prefix and then gains exactly as much as X afterwards, which means Y's running total stays behind X's by exactly 12 × 10^100 forever and the gap never closes. So X strictly overtakes Y. Against W, Y loses 11 per moment during the prefix, a deficit of 11 × 10^100, but after that Y gains 1 more than W per moment, so the deficit is eventually paid off and Y's running total pulls ahead. Y strictly overtakes W. Ranking: X > Y > W.

The striking part is that Y beats W even though Y contains a googol moments of agony and W contains none. Under overtaking any finite stretch of suffering gets washed out by the infinite tail, so the prefix is morally free in the sense that it can't push a world below another world it would otherwise beat.

Overtaking always gives a verdict. The only case it can't handle is when two worlds have exactly equal running totals from some point onward, but then they genuinely are equally good, so nothing is lost. That's a big advantage over summation, which is stuck on every pair of infinite worlds.

**(c)** I think the argument is right and people get squeamish about it for no good reason. Once the universe is infinite, finite amounts of suffering are literally a rounding error, so pouring effort into relieving suffering now is like bailing water out of a boat in an infinite ocean. What matters is that the long run happens at all and that it's full of good lives, since that's the only thing that can move the running totals. One more reason to trust overtaking here: it doesn't depend on the order the moments come in, it only looks at the welfare values themselves, so it's a proper welfarist criterion and not an artifact of how we happen to be arranged in time.

**(d)** Averaging fixes everything more cleanly. The average welfare of W is 1 and of X is 2, both perfectly well-defined finite numbers, so X is better than W and there was never a real paradox, just a bad choice of aggregation function. The average view also agrees with overtaking about Y: Y's average is a tiny bit under 2, so it ranks Y just below X, same order as before. The hyperreal option seems like cheating to me — if you're allowed to invent bigger infinities you can always arrange for whichever world you like to come out ahead, so it doesn't answer the question, it just hides it inside the choice of number system.
