I got stuck writing the 20-year retrospective for the small research nonprofit where I work, and now the whole office is in a fight I apparently have to referee. Our founder made one big dated call back in 2005 about coastal property insurance becoming effectively unobtainable in parts of the Gulf and Atlantic coasts by 2030. It didn't happen on schedule. It's sorta happening now, several years late, through pretty much the mechanism he described in the original piece. Half the staff says "he was right, just early." The other half says that's exactly what a doomer says when the doomsday doesn't show, and if we let it slide in the report we aren't a research org anymore.

The trouble is I don't think either side is being consistent, me included. Here are my honest verdicts and they're a mess:

1. Malthus. The tendency he described is real, and for most of history it looks like it held; globally it stopped holding once yields took off. I want to say: not vindicated, but not "refuted" either, and calling him simply wrong is a catagory error.
2. Wegener. Continents move, announced decades before anybody had a mechanism, and the driving force he offered was junk. I say he was right and the geologists who turned him down were wrong even though they were behaving perfectly reasonably.
3. The Simon–Ehrlich wager. Ehrlich picked five metals — chromium, copper, nickel, tin, tungsten — put $200 of paper on each in 1980 against the same basket in 1990, and mailed Simon a check for $576.07 when all five fell in real terms. People have since pointed out that over other windows, and over longer stretches, Ehrlich's basket would have won. I still say he lost, and losing a dated, priced, agreed-in-advance bet is losing.
4. One of those 1970s "we'll run out of X by 1995" forecasts where the actual story turned out to be that we just used X far more efficiently. Wrong, full stop, and the efficiency explanation is textbook post-hoc rescue.
5. Our founder's insurance call. I want to say right-but-early and I want to say it loudly, and I'm aware how that looks sitting underneath 3 and 4.
6. A colleague's forecast of a specific very-high-death pandemic by a specific year. Year came and went with nothing. Defense offered: the pressure was building, it just hadn't found its vector yet. I say that's a rescue and it scores as a miss. Structurally this is nearly identical to 5 and I can't defend treating them differently except by admitting I liked one guy's reasoning better.
7. A forecast that came true on schedule and that I now think the forecaster deserved no credit for — he'd have been just as confident about three other outcomes, and theres a memo where he hedges roughly all four. I don't want to score that as a hit even though the world did what he said.

What I need is a principle, or a small set of candidates, that I can actually put in the report — something about what had to be pinned down in advance, or how much of the theory had to get stretched afterwards, or what species of mistake was made — that lands on my verdicts in 1–7, or close to them. For each clause of the principle, tell me which case motivated it, because the staff will absolutely ask. Then tell me which of my verdicts the principle can't save and which is cheapest for me to give up; if I have to choose I'd rather lose 2 than lose 5 or 6, and I want to know what that costs.

And run it on the case I've deliberately left undecided, which I suspect breaks whatever you come up with: a forecaster states a date in print, the date passes with nothing, the mechanism later shows up — but it turns out he'd quietly re-dated the call in an internal memo months *before* the original deadline, and nobody outside the org ever saw that memo. Do I score the printed date or the memo date? Does it matter that he wrote the memo when he still had something to lose by writing it, versus a memo that surfaces only after the deadline has already embarrassed him?
