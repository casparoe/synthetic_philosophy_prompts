**Draft: "Weight, Not Psychology: What Keynes's Uncertainty Claim Actually Rests On"**

**§1.** In the *Treatise on Probability*, probability is an objective logical relation between a hypothesis and the evidence actually in hand; some such relations admit no numerical value, and some cannot even be ranked against one another. Where ranking is possible, it runs along two independent dimensions: probability, and weight of argument, the latter increasing with the amount of *relevant* evidence. A thin base can license a high probability while leaving the argument weakly grounded. Weight is not a measure of confidence and is not reducible to probability.

**§2.** The standard reading of Chapter 12 of the *General Theory* treats long-term expectation as governed by animal spirits, spontaneous optimism, and the beauty-contest structure of professional investment. On that reading Keynes is a psychologist of markets: ideally we would compute probabilities, but human beings are constitutionally unable to, so waves of sentiment take their place.

**§3.** My claim is that this inverts the order of explanation. What Chapter 12 says about prospective yields is not that traders fail to compute probabilities, but that the relations required for the computation are absent: the evidence bearing on the twenty-year yield of a coalfield, a railway, or a new process industry is too thin, too heterogeneous, and too exposed to genuinely unprecedented events to support a probability ordering at all. Sentiment is not the cause of the gap; it is what occupies the gap once it is opened. (I also want to tie this to Keynes's broader rejection of the assumption that the world decomposes into independently variable parts whose effects simply add up — I am not sure this belongs here, and say so below.)

**§4.** It follows that convention — proceeding as if the present state of affairs will persist, absent specific reasons to expect a change — is not a poor substitute for knowledge but the correct posture toward a hypothesis of near-zero weight. This is why Keynes can be simultaneously unruffled about convention and appalled at its fragility: the unruffledness and the alarm are one doctrine, not two moods.

**§5.** The cost of the reading, which I want to face rather than bury: if convention answers a permanent evidential deficit rather than an occasional failure of nerve, then the instability Chapter 12 describes is structural, and Keynes loses the ability to treat collapses as episodic breakdowns of a practice that otherwise works. His distinction between enterprise and a well-run speculative system, and the chapter's rhetoric of precariousness as something exceptional, both weaken.

**§6.** Where I am most exposed: if the deficit really is about weight, volatility should track the thickness of the evidence base rather than the fragility of participants, so assets with long homogeneous track records, standardized accounts, and dense disclosure should price more responsively to evidence and less conventionally than assets whose payoffs hinge on novelty. That predicts implausibly little turbulence in mature industries. I read this as an objection to the account, not merely to the prediction, because a weight-based story should leave some footprint.

**§7.** I am not claiming Keynes anticipated anything in contemporary formal epistemology, nor that weight does all the work in the *General Theory* — only that weight, and not a theory of sentiment, is what licenses his claim that we lack knowledge of prospective yields.
