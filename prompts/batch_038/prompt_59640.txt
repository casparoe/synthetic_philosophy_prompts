I sit on the grants committee of a regional foundation here in central Ohio — we distribute roughly $1.4 million a year across three streams, and I came onto the board as a retired credit-union branch manager. None of us has an academic background in any of this, though two of the committee members, myself included, have been reading around the subject on our own for a decade or so, which is why I keep getting handed the memo-writing jobs. Our "Civic Cohesion" stream is meant to fund work that lowers the temperature of local political conflict, and this cycle a single application has the committee deadlocked three to three, with our chair abstaining because her husband works at the applicant institution. We meet on the fourteenth and everything in the stream has to be committed before the fiscal year closes at the end of June, so a decision cannot simply be deferred.

The proposal is a joint bid from a state university psychology department and a small nonprofit that runs facilitated community dialogues: $185,000 over eighteen months. They would administer the Moral Foundations Questionnaire — the instrument from Haidt and his collaborators that scores people on care, fairness, loyalty, authority, and purity — to about twelve hundred residents across two of our most politically polarized counties, then run twenty-four dialogue workshops whose content is tailored to each locality's score profile, and finally publish a set of recommended changes to how foundations like ours write our own mission language. Their justification proceeds in three steps. First, moral disagreement between left and right is largely a difference in which foundations a person is attuned to, not a difference in reasoning ability or sincerity. Second, since liberals and conservatives each lean on different foundations, it is a mistake to treat one side's moral outlook as a deficient version of the other's. Third — and this is the step that has split us — they conclude that our foundation's equity framework, built almost entirely out of care and fairness, is not the neutral baseline we take it to be but one partial moral profile among several, and that we should widen it to give standing to loyalty, authority, and sanctity concerns: they want us to treat attachment to long-standing local institutions and reverence for inherited practice as legitimate community goods rather than as obstacles our grantees are supposed to overcome. Somewhere in the narrative section they write that if a moral concern is widespread, persistent, and found across cultures, it is probably tracking something real about human flourishing, and that a funder which ignores it is not being neutral but is simply siding with one profile. Three of us think that is a genuinely useful corrective and the workshops are the actual intervention anyway, with the survey serving as a needs assessment; the other three have distinct worries. Our former county prosecutor thinks step three is a textbook naturalistic fallacy — that the popularity of an intuition cannot make it right, or we would have to bless whatever happened to be widely approved in any given era — and that the foundation exists precisely to make normative judgments rather than outsource them to survey returns. Our nurse practitioner has read that the questionnaire's clean five-factor structure does not reproduce well in non-American samples, that reanalyses of large datasets suggest a different underlying shape to the instrument, and that people answer moral self-report items with their political identity in mind rather than reporting anything stable. A third skeptic argues that even if the descriptive psychology were flawless it would tell us nothing about who is right, so the money would be better spent on programs that do not require us to endorse any contested moral claim at all. The two members in favor reply that we are punishing an applicant for the way its justification is worded rather than for what it would actually do with the funds, and that the workshops are cheap relative to their reach.

Please draft the written opinion I will circulate to the committee before the fourteenth. I need a clear recommendation — fund as proposed, fund subject to specific conditions you state, or decline — with the reasons set out so that the applicants could actually answer them if they were shown the memo, and I need you to engage the strongest case for the side your recommendation goes against rather than strawmanning it: if you recommend funding with conditions, take the naturalistic-fallacy objection seriously and explain why it does not sink the proposal; if you recommend declining, take seriously the reply that we are objecting to rhetoric rather than to the work. Please also name plainly, for readers with no training, what the philosophical problem with the third step of their argument actually is, whether any version of it survives scrutiny, and whether the measurement worries would matter to the funding decision even if they were fully vindicated. If your recommendation involves conditions, tell us what we should ask the applicants at interview and what revised justification language we could accept. I do not want a bibliography, a reading list, or citations of any kind — we will not look them up and you cannot verify them from here; if you need to refer to a study, describe what it found in plain terms and flag how confident you are that it found that.

Write the memo as prose, between 900 and 1,300 words. After it, give me a JSON object with exactly the following fields, and nothing else: "recommendation" (one of "fund as proposed", "fund with conditions", "decline"); "conditions" (an array of strings, empty if the recommendation is not "fund with conditions"); "decisive_reason" (one sentence giving the single consideration that most drove the recommendation); "philosophical_problem" (two or three sentences, in language a non-specialist could repeat aloud at the meeting, stating what is wrong or questionable about the applicants' third step); "surviving_version" (a description of the strongest defensible version of the applicants' argument, or null if no version survives); "measurement_relevance" (whether the objections to the questionnaire would change the funding decision if fully accepted, and why, in two sentences); "strongest_counterargument" (three or four sentences stating the best case for the position you are recommending against, as its own advocate would state it); "response_to_counterargument" (three or four sentences explaining why it does not win); "interview_questions" (an array of three to five strings, empty if not applicable); "confidence" (one of "low", "moderate", "high"); and "what_would_change_my_mind" (one or two sentences describing the consideration that would most plausibly flip the recommendation).
