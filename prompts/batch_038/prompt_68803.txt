So my advisor and I got into it over a chapter draft and she talked me into a position I now think is indefensible, but I can't find the seam.

Setup: a mid-Holocene village site, decent organic preservation, roughly 40 burials excavated plus a bunch of house floors. Nothing monumental, no storage pits or granaries, house footprints all inside a pretty narrow band, and the grave goods are the same assemblage over and over — a few shell ornaments, some ochre, a tool or two, that's it.

Her argument that this was a genuinely egalitarian community — no heritable rank, no big men, no lineage that owns anything:

1. If a society has entrenched, transmissible inequality, that inequality gets materialized: in what goes into graves, in house size, in monuments, in who's sitting on stored food.
2. At this site none of those channels show any differentiation. Graves are uniform, houses are uniform, no monuments, no storage.
3. So this community had no entrenched inequality.
4. Nothing about that inference is special to this site — it works wherever the recovered material record is uniform.
5. But the record also comes out "uniform" at sites where everything perishible rotted, where the plow got to it, where a flood reworked the deposit, and where we've only opened two percent of the settlement. That describes most of the sites anyone actually works on.
6. So 1 plus ordinary fieldwork implies that most of the human past was egalitarian — which isn't a discovery about prehistory, it's a byproduct of decay and sampling.
7. Therefore 1 is false, and a uniform material record carries no information about social structure.

She wants to patch 1 by restricting it to channels that reliably survive. But if I have to independently establish that the surviving channels are the ones inequality would have used at that particular place, then 2 isn't doing the evidential work anymore and I don't see how 3 comes out the other side. So which premise or step should actually go, and why did it look unobjectionable going in? For whatever you cut, I want the price spelled out — my worry is that the easy fix collapses into "absence of evidence is evidence of absence, except when it isn't," which isn't a rule anybody could apply.

Related thing I keep tripping over: the two camps on burial data. One camp says variability in burial treatment tracks the number of distinct social personae a society recognizes, so a flat mortuary record really is a flat social record. The other says a grave mostly documents how the living wanted to represent the dead, so uniform ritual is compatible with a hierarchy that just didn't get celebrated at funerals. Which camp, if either, is allowed to keep something like 1? And does picking one of them change what I'd have to say about the houses and the missing granaries, or is that a separate inference entirely?

I'd much rather hold onto 1 in some restricted form, becuase if it goes I can't see how anyone gets from pots and postholes to politics at all.
