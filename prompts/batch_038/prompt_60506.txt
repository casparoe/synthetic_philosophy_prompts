I am in the third year of an undergraduate degree in philosophy and logic, and this term I am taking a course on paradoxes and theories of truth. Alongside the lectures, a group of us run an optional Friday evening session that anyone in the department can drop into: roughly eighteen students, mostly second- and third-years, with quite uneven backgrounds. About half have done propositional logic; only three or four have seen any metalogic at all; several are computer science students who have read about the Liar online and are fairly confident they already understand it. I have seventy-five minutes of one of those sessions to fill, and I would like to use it to design a group activity rather than give another talk. What I want is for participants to be placed in a situation where the trouble is generated by the rules and the scoring, and where they fail at the task before anyone tells them what they have just failed at.

The point I want them to arrive at is not the familiar one-sentence puzzle — that has been done to death in lectures — but three things that came up in seminar this week and that I do not think they have genuinely absorbed. First, that there is no consistent way to assign a classical truth value to every sentence once one sentence says of itself that it is not true, so a scoring rule that pays for correct assignments is simply unsatisfiable, and a group will discover that by failing repeatedly rather than by argument. Second, that reaching for a third option — declaring the troublesome sentence neither true nor false — looks like a repair until a strengthened version of the sentence, one that says of itself that it is not true even on the enlarged scheme, walks straight through it; I would like the activity to lure them into thinking they have found the loophole and then close it. Third, that self-reference is not what does the work, because Yablo's construction is an infinite sequence of sentences each saying that all the later ones are false, and no sentence in it refers to itself. I would also like them to get some intuition for revision dynamics of the Gupta and Herzberger kind: assign values, then reassign them in the light of everyone else's previous assignment, and see whether the process settles into a stable pattern or oscillates without end.

So please design the session in full. Give me the rules and the materials — assume only paper, index cards, pens, and a whiteboard, since the room has no projector and no budget. Give me a scoring scheme that creates real incentives without money, a running order with rough timings that fits seventy-five minutes, and a debrief that maps specific moments at the table onto specific semantic distinctions, naming the views only once the group has already experienced the problem. Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this: where a group of eighteen will stall, collude, invent an unanticipated loophole, or refuse the scoring rule altogether, and how I should handle each. And be frank about which feature of the underlying logical difficulty the activity cannot reproduce and that I will simply have to supply verbally — I suspect the infinite case in particular will be a serious problem for a card game with a finite deck, and I would rather know that in advance.

One request about the stance your answer takes. Several of the positions you will inevitably touch — gaps against gluts against a Tarskian hierarchy, whether the revenge problem is fatal to non-classical treatments, whether the fixed-point approach associated with Kripke and the revision approach are rivals or complements — are live disputes among people who work on this seriously. Please separate clearly what is settled or very nearly universal from what is genuinely contested, and do not present one theorist's programme as though it were the default position; where your design quietly presupposes a particular diagnosis of the paradox, say which one. Please write in British English throughout.
