Last month a visiting economist spent the closing minutes of a methodology seminar dismissing long-range climate projections with a single line: the atmosphere is a chaotic system, and in such systems a butterfly flapping its wings in Brazil can set off a tornado in Texas. Pressed on what he took to follow, he said only that a science unable to predict the weather a fortnight ahead cannot be predicting anything about the climate half a century hence, and the remainder of the hour dissolved into cross-talk in which both sides kept using the same words to mean different things.

I would like to reply precisely, and to do that I first need the sentence itself taken apart. Please lay out the distinct propositions this slogan can be read as asserting. Cover at least: the formal property of sensitive dependence on initial conditions; the claim that separation between nearby trajectories grows without bound as time advances; the claim that chaotic dynamics amounts to, or generates, genuine randomness; the claim that a small perturbation is a difference-making cause of some large later event; and the claim that a system exhibiting such sensitivity admits of no reliable long-horizon prediction of any kind, as against no reliable long-horizon prediction of a particular trajectory.

For each reading, state it as a definite proposition rather than a gloss, and classify it as a truism, a substantive but well-supported claim, false, or true only under assumptions the slogan leaves unstated — naming those assumptions in the last case. In each classification, say explicitly how the reading fares against two technical facts that are usually elided when the slogan is deployed: that the interval over which a given initial discrepancy grows to a given magnitude lengthens only logarithmically as the discrepancy shrinks, and that in a system confined to a bounded attractor the separation between trajectories eventually saturates rather than continuing to amplify.

Finally, tell me which of these readings, if any, would license the inference from a limit on weather forecasting to a limit on climate projection, and which would leave that inference entirely untouched; and identify what speakers who repeat the line most often intend by it, together with the distance between that intention and any reading on which the inference holds.
