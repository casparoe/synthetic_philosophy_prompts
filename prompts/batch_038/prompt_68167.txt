I spent thirty-one years as an experimental physicist in reactor instrumentation and control, first at a national laboratory and later running a small group that built the sensor and data-acquisition systems other people's experiments depended on. Since retiring I have been auditing courses in the philosophy of technology and sitting in on a reading group that meets Thursday mornings in our town library; one of the members is a former municipal planner, which is how I came to spend most of the last year inside a controversy I initially assumed was settled. The question is whether artifacts have politics. On one side stands Langdon Winner's well-known 1980 essay, which distinguishes two kinds of case: artifacts whose design or arrangement settles some communal question for decades thereafter — his headline example being the deliberately low-clearance bridges on Robert Moses's Long Island parkways, said to have kept buses, and hence poor and Black beachgoers, off the parks — and artifacts that are inherently political, requiring or at least strongly favouring a particular form of social organisation, as he argues nuclear generation requires centralised, hierarchical, expert-controlled management while solar is compatible with decentralisation. Winner's other famous historical case is the pneumatic moulding machinery introduced at McCormick's works in 1880s Chicago, which he reads as a device for displacing skilled union moulders rather than for improving output. On the other side stands the social-constructionist programme that took shape in the later 1980s, with its vocabulary of relevant social groups, interpretive flexibility and technological frames — the high-wheeler and the safety bicycle being the standard illustration, since what counted as the machine's defining problem, masculine speed and daring versus physical safety, differed between the groups studying it. That camp regards determinism as a straw man, and Hughes's notion of technological momentum was offered as the sane middle: systems accumulate mass in the form of embedded knowledge, physical plant, standards and trained personnel, which constrains later choices without logically fixing them.

What strikes me as odd is that the argument has not been settled by anyone, including by the people who thought they had settled it. Winner himself turned on the constructivists in the early nineties, charging that they had opened the black box and found nothing worth having; and then, in 1999, two separate interventions in the same journal issue attacked his bridge case on empirical grounds, arguing that the story had been garbled in transmission and that the exclusionary reading does not survive the engineering and archival details, with the same treatment extended to the McCormick machinery. And yet the pendulum has swung back again: actor-network theory's insistence that we account for the missing masses of mundane artefacts and that non-humans participate in action, the general talk of material agency, and now the everyday practice of saying flatly that a hiring or policing algorithm is racist — which sounds to me like Winner resurrected under new management. My working hypothesis, offered as a hypothesis, is that the stalemate is largely structural rather than substantive. Almost nobody in this literature actually denies that built structures constrain and enable human conduct; the disagreement seems to be about three different things being run together under one slogan — a question about the causal origins of a design, a question about what a design does once installed, and a question about whether "politics" is something ascribed by a description or something caused by an arrangement of matter. If that is right, the demolition of the bridges may have been beside the conceptual point entirely, and technological momentum may be a verbal compromise rather than a third position.

I would like a diagnosis, not a survey and not a verdict on who won. Take the positions as I have described them and tell me what kind of disagreement this really is: substantive, verbal about the word "politics" or the word "have," a difference in standards of explanation between historians and engineers, two groups answering different questions under a single heading, or a question that is ill-posed from the start. Say plainly whether "artifacts have politics" is even the sort of claim that can be true or false, or whether it is a slogan doing rhetorical work. Then tell me what, if anything, would count as progress. One candidate that appeals to my instincts, and which I would like you to assess rather than accept: an interventionist test — replace the artifact with a functionally equivalent one of different design, or ask what would have happened had the low bridges been built to standard clearance, and see whether the social arrangement persists. Would such counterfactual reasoning be available for historical claims at all, or does the very idea presuppose a determinist picture of causation that the constructivists reject? If you conclude that no progress should be expected, I want the argument for that too. Please do not assemble a bibliography or a list of further reading; I will chase down references myself, and I care about the reasoning rather than the scholarship apparatus.
