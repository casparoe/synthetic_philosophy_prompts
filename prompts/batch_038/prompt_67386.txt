I am closing a graduate seminar on consciousness and would like to end with a poem rather than a summary. Please write a pantoum about a higher-order thought theory of consciousness: the view that a mental state is conscious, and has the particular character it has, in virtue of a suitable thought or representation being directed at it, where suitable means non-inferential and non-observational — the subject does not arrive at the state by reasoning to it or by a kind of inner perception, since if she did, the state would not be conscious in the right way.

The form must be strict, because the form is carrying the argument. Quatrains in which the second and fourth lines of each stanza return as the first and third lines of the next, and a final stanza that takes up the first and third lines of the opening stanza, so that the poem ends by consuming its own beginning. Between five and seven stanzas; rhyme ABAB within each quatrain if you can do it without straining the content, but the recurrence matters more than the rhyme.

I want the recurrence to do three distinct pieces of work, and I want to be able to point to each in discussion:

First, the returning lines should serve as the poem's account of higher-order representation. A line that comes back is not altered by coming back — the words are the same — but its standing is: where it first merely occurred, it now occurs as being about something. The reader should feel, in a returned line, the difference between a state's simply being in a subject and a subject's being aware of it. To make this available, let the first-order content be one ordinary, concrete, determinate thing — a noise in the next room, the cold of a railing, whatever you choose — held constant across the poem so that the returns are unmistakable.

Second, at least one stanza should stage the regress: the thought that confers consciousness must itself go unthought, on pain of an endless ascent. Handle this structurally rather than by announcing it. In any given stanza the line doing the representing should never itself be reported on, so that a reader can identify which line is functioning as the higher-order one while noticing that the poem stays silent about it, exactly as the theory requires.

Third, the closing circle should make the misrepresentation problem audible. The final stanza ought to leave it genuinely undecidable whether the line it takes up was ever uttered earlier, or whether the machinery is now turning over with nothing beneath it — a higher-order representation with no first-order state to answer to it. Do not resolve this: on the view in question the undecidability is the thesis, since what it is like for the subject is fixed by how the state seems rather than by whether it is there. Someone who knows the debate should be able to say which returned line is the suspect one, and why.

If verbatim return makes that undecidability impossible to achieve, take whatever license you need in the last stanza — and follow the poem with a note of two or three sentences saying what you changed and why. That note is the one place where you may discuss your own work; everywhere else the poem must carry the point unaided.

Two further constraints. The vocabulary of the debate — qualia, phenomenology, intentionality, intrinsic, what it is like — should not appear at all; the poem should enact those notions rather than name them. And the theory's answer to the question of why a stone is not conscious should never be stated, though the machinery you build should make it obvious.

Aim for something readable aloud in under two minutes, which works as a poem for a listener who has never met the theory, and which a philosopher of mind would judge correct rather than merely allusive.
