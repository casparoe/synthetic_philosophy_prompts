I'm putting together a case for a paper arguing that the Humean / governing-laws dispute isn't just verbal, and I want a thought experiment built to spec rather than a survey of the literature. Here's what I need.

Give me two worlds, W and W*. Constraints on the pair:

- they have to be exact duplicates in every particular event, object, and property-instantiation from beginning to end. Same planet, same lab, same history, right down to which way each coin landed. No gods, no time travel, no exotic physics, no sci-fi — ordinary stuff only: a kettle, some salt, a ball on a shelf.
- the only difference between them is nomic. Some counterfactual about what would have happened under an intervention comes out true in one and false in the other, where the intervention never actually happens in either world. Something like: in both worlds the ball sits untouched on the shelf for the entire history, and the difference is only about what it would have done had somebody knocked it off. Use that or anything better, but the difference has to be one that would make the *laws* differ, not one that could be explained away by different local conditions.
- the two descriptions have to be concrete enough that a reader who reads only the event histories literally cannot tell which world is which. One paragraph each, written from the inside — no possible-worlds jargon in the descriptions themselves, save that for the analysis.

Then run the check against three views: (a) Lewis-style best-systems Humeanism, (b) the Dretske–Tooley–Armstrong governing account, where the nomic relation between universals is contingent, and (c) dispositional essentialism, where properties are individuated by their powers and the laws come out metaphysically necessary. My prediction — and tell me if I've got it wrong rather than bending the case to fit — is that (a) and (c) both have to deny that W* is a genuine possibility, (b) has to accept it, and (a) and (c) reach that same verdict for diametrically opposed reasons: the Humean because the laws have no independent reality to vary, the essentialist because they have too much.

After the scenario and the check:

- say plainly which feature of the case is doing the work, and whether the case *generates* the disagreement or just stipulates it. I'd much rather hear "you smuggled the verdict in at step X" than a smooth story.
- state the idle-wheel objection — why should anybody care about a difference that no inhabitant of either world could ever detect — and say which of the three views has the hardest time answering it and why.
- give me the smallest tweak you can manage, to the worlds or to the question asked about them, that makes (a) and (c) come apart instead of agreeing, and show all three new verdicts.
- how confident are you in each verdict you've assigned? Flag the single point you're least sure of. And if you think the spec caricatures one of the three views — say, that a dispositional essentialist has a perfectly good way to accept W* after all — say which one and fix the case instead of forcing it.
