I'm a junior in a seminar on probability and its philosophical foundations, and we've just spent two weeks on the doomsday argument. The graduate student leading our section has a standard way of introducing it, and I suspect it is doing considerably more work than he realizes. He says: set the cosmology aside, because this is simply the German tank problem. Allied statisticians estimated total German tank production from the serial numbers on captured vehicles — see a high serial number, infer a large production run; see a low one, infer a small one — and the doomsday argument is the same move with my birth rank playing the role of the serial number and the total number of human beings who will ever live playing the role of total production. On his telling, the analogy settles two disputes at once. First, that the inference is uncontroversial statistics, so anyone who feels something has gone wrong is reacting to the grim conclusion rather than to the reasoning. Second, when a classmate objected that nobody ever drew my birth rank out of an urn — that my position in human history is not the output of any randomizing process at all — he replied that the same objection would sink the tank analysis, since the Allies did not capture their tanks at random either, and that an objection which refutes a piece of statistics everyone agrees worked has refuted itself.

I would like an audit of the analogy rather than a summary of the argument. Specifically:

1. Lay out the correspondence explicitly: what plays the role of the serial number, the population maximum, the sampling procedure, the sample size, the analyst, the moment of observation, and the estimate being produced. Flag where a pairing is forced, and where some item in the source has no partner in the target at all.

2. Separate the disanalogies that actually matter for the two uses above from the cosmetic ones. My own candidates — which I want argued for or against rather than merely listed — are that the tank statisticians were third parties sampling from outside a population that already existed and whose estimates could later be checked against captured records, whereas I am one of the items in the population and my observation is self-locating; the sample size; the fact that the quantity being estimated is partly determined by the future choices of the very people doing the estimating, so the estimate can feed back into what it estimates; and the fact that in the tank case there was an independent, checkable story — a manufacturing practice of assigning ascending numbers to components — explaining why serial numbers carry information about the total. Tell me which of these carries weight and what I have missed.

3. Take the "proves too much" reply head-on. Is the uniformity assumption the tank analysts relied on licensed by something with no analogue in the human case, or licensed in exactly the same way? If my classmate's objection survives that reply, what does it survive as — a complaint about randomness, or a complaint about something else entirely?

4. Tell me whether the analogy fixes the arithmetic. The tank problem is usually attacked two ways, a frequentist estimator and a Bayesian one that requires a prior over the unknown total, and the two coincide only under special conditions; the doomsday version seems to arrive already committed to one such choice. Does the tank framing justify that commitment, or does it conceal it?

5. Finally: does the conclusion the analogy was recruited to support — that this is ordinary statistics with nothing exotically anthropic about it — survive once the disanalogies are on the table? And if the tank comparison is the wrong vehicle, is there a better one, or is the honest move to state the underlying assumption directly and stop analogizing? Please do not send me to the literature; I want your reasoning, not a reading list.
