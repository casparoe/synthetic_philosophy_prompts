For thirty-five years I directed a laboratory in an experimental branch of biology, and for roughly twelve of those I served on review panels for a large public research funder. Only since retiring have I taken up the philosophy of science in earnest, and the more I read, the more one feature of my former working life strikes me as philosophically loaded rather than merely administrative.

The feature is this. Every application the panel considered was scored under headings corresponding roughly to importance, soundness of approach, and the applicant's record, and under all three the discussion returned sooner or later to the same thing: preliminary data. A proposal reporting that the effect it now wished to explain had already been glimpsed — in a pilot, in a related organism, at small scale — was fundable. A proposal whose central prediction nobody had yet observed was described as high-risk and in practice did not survive. Most funders maintain a separate small programme for high-risk work, but it is a rounding error in the budget and is judged by different people under different rules. Continuation of an existing grant, meanwhile, depends on what the previous award produced, and production is measured in papers, and the journals in my field published positive results and very nearly never published a clean failure to reproduce someone else's. Throughout, the agency's public statements insisted that it supports curiosity-driven, investigator-initiated research and that boldness is welcome.

I have been reading about falsification, and about what a hypothesis is supposed to gain from having survived a test it might well have failed, and the mismatch puzzles me. On that way of thinking, the most informative experiment would seem to be precisely the one with no preliminary data behind it — the one whose predicted outcome would be very improbable were the hypothesis false — while the experiment whose result is already all but assured would be worth almost nothing. The funding system appears to rank the two the other way round.

So I should like an account of what theory of evidence, and of what makes research worth doing at all, the preliminary-data requirement presupposes. Take the deflationary reply seriously rather than waving it aside: that preliminary data is a proxy for the applicant's competence and for the practical feasibility of the work, and that a public agency is entitled not to gamble with other people's money. Does that reply dissolve the philosophical question, or merely relocate it, and if so to where? Then distinguish what the practice strictly requires from what it merely sits comfortably with, since I suspect the requirement is compatible with several mutually hostile philosophies of science and would like to know which ones and why. Address also whether the parts of the system I have described — the high-risk carve-out, the productivity condition on continuation, the invisibility of failures in the literature — presuppose the same thing, or whether the whole presupposes contradictory things about what a result is worth. And sketch what a funding system actually built on the value of a risky test would pay for, what it would score, and what it would have to stop rewarding, together with the strongest argument that such a system would in fact be worse than the one we have.

Please answer as exactly five numbered points, in the order of the questions above, each one or two sentences long, with no introduction and no conclusion. Let the fifth point be the one in which you tell me what would change your mind: which consideration, if I pressed it, would lead you to withdraw the verdict you have reached about what the practice presupposes, or where you judge your own reconstruction most likely to have gone wrong.
