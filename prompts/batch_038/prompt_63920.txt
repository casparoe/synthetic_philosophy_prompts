I retired three years ago after thirty-four years in nonlinear dynamics — mostly studying whether small perturbations in a fluid system decay or amplify — and since then I have been reading history and its philosophy with the amateur's enthusiasm and the specialist's irritation at imprecision. Last month I hosted a small dinner at which the conversation turned to whether the Allied victory in Europe in 1945 was, in any interesting sense, inevitable. I contributed only one remark: that I had spent my working life on systems in which tiny disturbances grow exponentially, and that I wondered whether history was such a system. One of my guests, a retired economic historian whom I shall call N, and another, a philosopher who works on the methodology of climate modelling, whom I shall call R, took it from there and did not stop for two hours. I did not intervene again, and I am deliberately not telling you which of them I found the more persuasive, because I do not know. I have reconstructed the exchange as faithfully as my notes allow, and numbered the moves.

(1) N opened with the aggregate-resources argument: by 1943 the Allied coalition commanded several times the industrial output, oil, and available manpower of the Axis, and no sequence of battlefield decisions could convert a disparity of that magnitude into an Axis victory; consequently, counterfactuals of the "what if the invasion of the Soviet Union had never been launched" variety are idle entertainment rather than history. (2) R replied that this is a retrospective conversion fallacy: resources do not become outcomes by themselves, and the aggregates "guaranteed" victory only conditional on a great many contingent decisions having already gone one way — Britain's choice to keep fighting in 1940, American entry into the war, the survival and eastward relocation of Soviet industry through 1941–42. Remove any one of them and the same ledger of steel and tonnage supports a different conclusion. (3) N said this concedes his claim, since he never asserted inevitability from 1939 but only from a date after which the decisive choices had already been made; and a probability claim indexed to a time and a body of evidence is perfectly respectable. R answered that once inevitability is indexed to a time and an evidence base, the claim is nearly immune to refutation, because the index is chosen by the claimant: late enough and anything is inevitable, early enough and nothing is. (4) R then pressed the point I had handed her: if the system amplifies small differences exponentially, trajectories diverge from any perturbed initial condition, and we have no epistemic access to the counterfactual beyond a horizon of a few months — so N's inevitability claim is not merely unfalsifiable but unintelligible. (5) N said that this borrows my own field against itself: chaotic systems routinely possess robust macroscopic properties, the atmosphere is chaotic yet climatology is a science, a double pendulum is chaotic yet its motion is bounded, and one may predict the statistical behaviour of systems whose individual trajectories no one can forecast. Chaos therefore limits the resolution at which a counterfactual can be assessed, not its existence. (6) R said the analogy fails, because an attractor or a climate statistic is defined over an ensemble generated by fixed dynamics, whereas history supplies a single sample of a system whose "laws" — institutions, technologies, beliefs — change during the run; with no ensemble and no stationary dynamics, "robust macro-outcome" has no content. N retorted that R has thereby forfeited the chaos vocabulary as well, since sensitive dependence is a property of a deterministic system with a specified state space, and without a state space it is at best a metaphor enlisted for rhetorical prestige. R conceded the metaphor charge but said her substantive point survives without it: what makes counterfactual reasoning respectable in engineering is a model validated on many comparable systems — we can say whether this bridge would have failed without the retrofit because the physics has been tested on thousands of bridges — and history possesses no such validated model, only narrative.

They eventually asked me to referee, and I would rather an outsider did it. Please adjudicate move by move: for each of the six numbered steps, say whether it is clearly right, partly right, or clearly mistaken, and why, and then say who is more right overall and on what the disagreement ultimately turns. Three further questions I could not settle for myself. First, does N's indexing reply in (3) genuinely answer R's charge of arbitrariness, or is her point that the index does all the work a good one? Second, in the quarrel over chaos in (4) through (6), which of them is using the concept correctly, and does the answer matter — could the original dispute about inevitability be resolved without ever settling it? Third, is R's ensemble objection in (6) fatal to N, or does it prove too much, in that it would seem equally to rule out counterfactual reasoning about singular, unrepeatable events elsewhere, where such reasoning strikes me as entirely legitimate? Finally, tell me what both of them appear to have missed. My own suspicion is that "inevitable" was never pinned down, and that part of the argument concerned the probability of an outcome while another part concerned the probability of that outcome's arriving by a particular route, with each of them sliding between the two — but I cannot tell whether that suspicion is well founded or merely a physicist's fondness for distinctions. I am not looking for references or a survey of who has written on the matter; I want your judgment of these arguments exactly as they were made.
