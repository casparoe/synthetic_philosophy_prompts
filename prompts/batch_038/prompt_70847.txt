I'm pitching a piece that revisits Langdon Winner's "Do Artifacts Have Politics?" for a general ideas desk, and I want to do something a bit cheeky: put his own showcase cases and a handful of more recent ones side by side and actually score them. Can you rank these six on a 0-10 scale for how much politics the artifact itself genuinely carries, then fold them into a tier list (call it "load-bearing," "decorative," "stretch"):

1. the low overpasses on Robert Moses's Long Island parkways
2. the pneumatic molding machines McCormick put into the reaper works after the molders struck
3. the intermodal shipping container
4. the open-plan office
5. the QWERTY keyboard layout
6. a recommendation feed that decides what you see first on a short-video app

For each, one sentence on the number, and also tell me which of Winner's two senses it belongs to — the one where a design settles some dispute inside a community, or the "inherently political" one where the thing demands a certain kind of social order around it — or whether it belongs to neither and I should just cut it from the piece. I especially want you to flag any case where you think the politics really lives in the surrounding arrangements and gets laundered through the object, and tell me how much that matters given that people have disputed whether the Moses bridge story even holds up factually.

Then give me the bottom line: an overall grade out of 10 for the thesis as a whole, and which single item is the strongest evidence in the list and which is the weakest, with reasoning. If you think the whole framing is a trap — that "politics" is doing different work in each case so the scores aren't comparable — say so and score them anyway.
