I'm running a 75-minute seminar section of 24 undergraduates on weakness of will, and I want an activity in which present bias and the question of how to interpret it arise in the room rather than in my lecture. Constraints: no money may change hands, but the stakes must be real enough that students actually care — tokens redeemable for grade-neutral privileges, snacks, time off a task, or the like, at your discretion. Nothing is to be revealed by name until the debrief: students should discover, in their own behavior, both the pull of the immediate option and the fact that they had planned otherwise.

Design the activity, including:

- a payoff scheme and rule structure that induces a substantial fraction of students to defect from a choice they made earlier in the same session, where the defection option is genuinely attractive at the moment of decision and cheap to take;
- an explicit, purchasable commitment device that lets students lock in their earlier choice at a cost, so that the room splits into those who buy it, those who decline it, and those who buy it and later regret having done so;
- a subset of students cast as forecasters who, before the temptation moment arrives, record their predicted defection rate and predicted reasons, so the group generates its own comparison between forecast and outcome;
- sealed or simultaneous recording of choices, to strip out social pressure;
- a minute-by-minute timeline.

Then a debrief that maps specific moments onto the concepts, that uses the forecasters' data to press whether this is a single agent with time-inconsistent preferences or a succession of differently motivated selves, and that meets the objection a clever student will raise: that simply changing one's mind is not weakness of will at all. Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it, and be frank about which feature of genuine akrasia — acting against one's own all-things-considered judgment — a compressed classroom exercise cannot reproduce, and what I must supply by argument instead.
