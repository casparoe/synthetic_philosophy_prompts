I am a doctoral student in political theory, working on the chapter of my dissertation that deals with official apology, and I want to pin down what the Federal Republic's own practice of atonement actually commits it to. The secondary literature treats those commitments as settled and I am not persuaded that they are.

The elements I have in view are these. The Republic does not present itself as a new state that succeeded the Reich but as the same legal subject, reorganised and partly re-founded; on that footing it inherits treaties, citizenship and public debt, and it also treats the crimes as its own. The Bundestag and the Chancellor apologise in the first person plural, a national memorial to the murdered Jews of Europe stands near the seat of government, the anniversary of the liberation of Auschwitz is a national day of remembrance, the crimes are taught in every Land as the nation's own history rather than as the history of a regime that has been superseded, and restitution and pensions are paid to survivors and to their representative organisations and continue to be paid. The criminal law runs the other way. The state cannot be prosecuted, guilt is strictly personal, no living German is said to be guilty of anything, and the only route back to the crimes is the trial of individual, now very old, former guards and clerks as accessories to murder — murder being the one offence in German law that never prescribes. And when Greece and Poland press reparation claims, the continuity that carries the apology is set aside and the government answers that all such matters were legally settled long ago.

My question is what theory of collective agency and of guilt this package presupposes, and whether it is one theory. What has to be true of the nation, as an entity, for the first-person-plural apology and the continuing payments to be more than civic theatre — is a purely forward-looking account sufficient (present-day justice for present-day claimants, remembrance as pedagogy), or does the practice's own self-understanding require a persisting corporate subject capable of bearing something? Please draw the line sharply between what the practice strictly presupposes and what it merely sits comfortably with, since I suspect several rival accounts of collective responsibility license most of it. Tell me whether the presuppositions of the payments and the presuppositions of the memorials are the same ones, or whether the practice is internally plural. Is it coherent for the Republic to speak as a person and pay as a person and decline to be a person exactly where liability is at issue — or does holding that together require a taxonomy of kinds of guilt, of the sort my field reaches for by default, and is that taxonomy doing real conceptual work here or providing cover? Finally, what would have to change in the practice, element by element, if the corporate subject were dropped for a strictly individualist account, and what would have to change if it were instead taken all the way through?
