Setup: I'm in a reading group working through the imprecise-probability literature, the stuff where your doxastic state is a whole set of probability functions instead of one (or equivalently a lower and an upper prevision). The objection we keep running into is that carrying a range instead of a number is just epistemic cowardice — you should be made to pick something, maybe the midpoint. My co-author's counter-move is the market-maker analogy: a market maker quotes a bid and an ask and nobody calls that a mushy belief about value; both numbers do real work, and the pair is exactly the right representation of their position. So an imprecise credence is a bid-ask spread on a proposition, and the "just take the midpoint" objection mistakes a spread for a sloppy represenation of one hidden precise number. And the betting gloss in the reading seems to back him up — the lower prevision is defined as the highest price you'd pay to buy a gamble, the upper as the lowest you'd accept to sell it. Sounds like a quote to me.

Then one of the other people in the group, who used to trade, said the analogy isn't neutral, it's actively working against us. A spread is a symptom of frictions — inventory risk, adverse selection, order-processing cost — and it's supposed to shrink toward zero as those frictions go away; competing market makers get arbed down to a single price. So if believing is like making a market, the analogy proves too much: it makes imprecision a second-best response to costs a believer doesn't actually have, and it makes precision the frictionless ideal you're supposed to be converging to. Which is the opposite of what my co-author wants from it.
