I am a third-year PhD student in philosophy working on social epistemology from a formal angle, and my committee has asked me to circulate a written statement of my position on disagreement before our next meeting, so that they can attack it in a focused way. Rather than let them find the faults at random, I would like a prior audit of the set. Below are the ten commitments I am prepared to defend. I have tried to state them as I actually hold them, not as strawmen, and I do not already know which of them are in conflict — that is what I need from you.

1. There is no common prior. I do not think there is a fact of the matter about a prior that ideally rational agents share; priors encode temperament, conceptual scheme, and interpretive commitment at least as much as they encode evidence, and the common-prior assumption is a modeling convenience rather than a substantive truth about anyone.

2. I accept Aumann's 1976 result without reservation and take it to be exactly right as mathematics: two Bayesians who share a prior, and whose posteriors for a given event are common knowledge, must assign that event the same posterior. I have no quarrel with the theorem, only with how it is used.

3. Fully informed, good-faith, competent disagreement is rationally permissible in the cases I care about most. Two philosophers who know the same arguments, have made no errors, and are equally able can, I think, reasonably settle on different credences in a thesis and remain there indefinitely without either being irrational.

4. The common-knowledge hypothesis is never satisfied in real controversies, and the theorem therefore has no bite in them. What the result tells me is that certain idealized models must be internally consistent, not that actual disagreeing agents are doing anything wrong.

5. Nevertheless, I want to maintain that intransigence after prolonged, open, symmetric exchange is epistemically criticizable. When each party knows that the other knows that they disagree, when nothing relevant is being withheld, and when the exchange has gone on for years without convergence, holding fast looks to me like a failure to respond to one's total evidence — the fact of the disagreement included. I want this to be a substantive normative claim, not a lament about unmet idealizations.

6. In a clear-cut case of peer disagreement I am a conciliationist of the equal-weight sort: absent any independent reason to distrust one party more than the other, the parties should move substantially toward one another, roughly splitting the difference, and the disagreement is itself evidence bearing on which of them is right.

7. Certain asymmetries legitimately license holding fast. If I have worked on an argument for years and my interlocutor met it last week; if I can see that I checked the crucial step and they did not; if the case turns on a distinction they have repeatedly run together — then, I think, extra weight on my own judgment is proper and not merely a rationalization.

8. When I explain real disagreement, I almost always reach for differences in evidence rather than differences in priors: people have read different literatures, had different experiences, weighted different testimony, and would have converged had they started from the same baseline and been shown the same things. This is, for me, the default story about why reasonable people differ.

9. I doubt that the objects of my favorite disputes fit the machinery at all. For questions such as whether a given account of consciousness is correct, there is no antecedently fixed finite partition of a state space and no determinate event whose posterior two people could announce to each other, so the iterative-announcement procedure that provably terminates in agreement in the finite case has nothing to operate on.

10. Methodologically, I count a formal result as philosophically load-bearing only if it constrains what actual agents may permissibly believe. A result that speaks solely to idealized agents and is silent about real ones I regard as decorative.

What I want back is an audit, not a tutorial — please assume I know the theorem, its proof, and the standard conciliationist literature, and do not spend space restating them. Specifically:

- Identify the pairs (or larger clusters) that genuinely pull against one another, and rank them by severity rather than presenting a flat list in which every tension looks equally damaging. For each, say precisely what cannot be held together and why.
- Separately, identify the pairs that only look inconsistent and dissolve on inspection, and state the consideration that dissolves each. I expect some of my commitments to be in merely apparent conflict, and I would rather not have those flagged as serious.
- For each genuine tension, give the cheapest available revision — the smallest change to the set that removes it — and then say what that revision costs elsewhere, including whether it forces me to abandon something in the list I have not identified as problematic.
- Tell me which single change would repair the largest number of tensions at once, and whether the resulting profile is still recognizably mine: I would like to know whether any repair that does real work leaves me holding a view I have argued against.
- Point out anywhere that I am covertly relying on an idealization that I elsewhere reject, since I suspect I am doing this more than once.
- Finally, tell me whether commitment 5 can be given any footing within the formal framework at all, or whether it belongs to an entirely different framework — and if the former, what has to be weakened in commitments 1, 4, or 10 to make room for it.
