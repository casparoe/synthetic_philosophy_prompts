I am preparing a paper on the ethics of advising — specifically, on how a system that knows vastly more than the person it is addressing ought to speak to that person — and for the first meeting of my seminar I have built an argument whose conclusion I cannot accept, though after three drafts I still cannot say where it goes wrong.

The argument requires one stipulation. Call a presentation of propositions bare when it states them with no order chosen for effect, no emphasis, no analogy, no narrative framing, no tone, and in vocabulary the listener already commands. For any communicative act A that conveys a true body of propositions P to a listener L, let the rhetorical surplus of A be the difference between the change A produces in L's confidence in P and the change that the bare presentation of P would produce. A is merely informative just in case its surplus is zero.

The argument runs as follows.

1. A communication is permissible only if its rhetorical surplus is zero — only if everything it contributes to the listener's belief is contributed by the truth of what it says.
2. Any nonzero surplus is produced by features of the presentation that are not features of the content presented: by ordering, emphasis, analogy, narrative, tone, and by the listener's trust in the speaker.
3. Influence on a person's belief that operates through such features bypasses that person's assessment of the evidence.
4. To bypass a person's assessment of the evidence in settling what they believe is to use them rather than to address them, and is therefore impermissible.
5. A system that knows far more than its interlocutor cannot, for most of what matters, offer a bare presentation: the relevant propositions are too numerous to be taken in, too technical to be rendered in the listener's vocabulary, and their bearing on the listener's question is itself something the listener cannot discern unaided.
6. Therefore such a system can be permissible or effective, but not both: whatever it says that changes minds changes them partly by non-evidential means.
7. Nor is silence an escape, since a system installed to advise that says nothing foreseeably leaves its interlocutor relying on worse beliefs than it could have supplied, and is answerable for that.
8. So there is no permissible course open to it: it may not advise, and it may not refrain from advising.

The difficulty is not confined to (8). Holding every premise fixed, the same reasoning indicts a lecturer who saves the striking example for second place, an editor who cuts a paragraph for rhythm, and a translator who prefers the idiomatic rendering to the literal one; each produces a surplus, each therefore bypasses the audience's assessment of the evidence, and I do not believe that any of them does wrong. I would rather abandon (3) or (4) than (1), since the surplus principle is what I take to capture the difference between informing a person and working upon them, but I recognise that preference is not an argument.

Two things I want from you. First, tell me which step to reject — one step if one will bear the weight, otherwise the smallest set that will — and then explain why that step looked unobjectionable when I wrote it, since that is the part I care about most: I have rewritten it three times and it survived each pass. Second, price the rejection. Say what the paper loses if I give the step up, what would have to be substituted for it if anything can be, and whether the notion of rhetorical surplus survives as a serviceable measure or whether the stipulation itself belongs among the suspects. You need not cite anything; I know the neighbourhood and want the analysis rather than the references.

Finally, and please do not let this collapse into a general hedge: state how confident you are in the particular diagnosis you settle on, as distinct from your confidence in the general picture, and single out the one point in your answer about which you are least sure — whether that is the step you nominate for rejection or your claim about what rejecting it costs.
