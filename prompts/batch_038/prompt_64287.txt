I spent thirty-four years in a national measurement laboratory, most of them concerned with how one establishes that an instrument's specification holds not merely where it happens to have been used but across the whole range of conditions over which it might be used. Since retiring I have read political philosophy with some seriousness, and for the past two years I have taught an evening seminar in it through the continuing-education department of our university. Next month I must set and mark the final paper myself. My students are adults, mostly non-specialists — several in public administration, one a registrar, two nurses — who have read Berlin's lecture on the two concepts of liberty and the first two chapters of Pettit's Republicanism. They are capable of careful thought, but they have no appetite for, and no need of, the secondary literature.

I want a single essay question worth 25 marks, answerable in 90 minutes in roughly a thousand words, which cannot be passed by reproducing the taxonomy of negative, positive, and republican liberty. What I have in mind turns on a case of my own construction, which I should like you to sharpen rather than replace.

A municipal housing authority lets flats on rolling tenancies. Under the instrument governing the scheme, the authority reserves to itself the power to terminate any tenancy on forty-eight hours' notice, at its discretion and without stating grounds. A tenants' panel may make representations, but the authority is not bound by them, and the authority may itself amend the instrument. In the stipulated history, this power has not been exercised against anyone in the building in twelve years; the authority's published guidance says it will act only for cause; and no tenant has been inspected, questioned, or refused anything.

Variant A: the tenants know of the power, yet their conduct is indistinguishable, on every measure the authority keeps, from that of tenants holding genuinely secure tenancies — the same rate of complaints lodged, the same attendance at meetings, no evidence of deference or self-censorship, and they report no fear.

Variant B: the power exists but is nowhere published; the tenants do not know of it; their conduct and experience are again indistinguishable from those of secure tenants.

The question I want set must require students to say, of each variant, whether the tenants enjoy liberty on Berlin's negative conception and whether they enjoy it on Pettit's, and then to say what would have to be true for the two answers to come apart in a way that could be detected — or, if they cannot come apart, what that shows about whether the republican conception is a distinct conception at all rather than a redescription. I should like it to press on two further matters as well: whether the absence of any binding constraint on the authority is better described as a deficit of positive liberty than of negative liberty, and whether the two obvious repairs to the case — making the for-cause guidance enforceable, or giving the tenants a vote over the instrument — are repairs of the same kind.

I have a working hypothesis, drawn from my former trade, and I should like it treated as a hypothesis rather than as a premise to be flattered. It is this: negative liberty resembles a reading taken at one point of an operating envelope, whereas non-domination resembles a specification that must hold across the whole envelope, which is why Pettit's insistence on robustness seems to me to do real work and to mark a difference in kind rather than degree. If you think this analogy is a physicist's prejudice that will mislead my students — or that the robustness requirement does no work which a suitably broadened notion of interference could not do — say so plainly, and tell me how the question ought to be framed instead.

Please give me: (i) the question exactly as it should appear on the paper, with the case stated compactly enough for an examination script; (ii) a model answer of about 800 words that commits to a position on the crux and argues for it, rather than balancing the conceptions against one another; (iii) a marking scheme allocating the 25 marks across named moves, with an explicit statement of what separates a first-class answer from a competent pass, and a list of the specific mistakes that should cost marks, described as mistakes real students make; and (iv) a brief note identifying which stipulations of the case are doing the most work and where your own answer is least secure.

Do not pad the model answer with references: my students have two texts and their reasoning, and that is what I am examining. And if you judge that the task cannot be done well as I have posed it — that the case fails to separate the conceptions, or that 25 marks cannot honestly be earned on it at this level — I would much rather be told that directly, together with the smallest change that would fix it, than be handed a polished artifact that quietly fails to discriminate.
