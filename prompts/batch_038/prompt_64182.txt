I am a junior philosophy major and the undergraduate member of the five-person oversight panel for a campus forecasting platform that our economics department and student government operate jointly. The platform trades play-money contracts on questions about university affairs — whether the Board will approve a given capital project by a stated date, whether the provost will announce changes to the general-education requirements before the end of term — and under a standing agreement the provost's office treats closing prices as advisory input to its deliberations. No real money is involved, and the semester's prize goes to whoever holds the largest play-money balance in December, not to whoever turns out to be best calibrated.

The platform's charter this year says the panel 'shall set aside any price it finds to have been corrupted,' but it defines neither corruption nor the grounds for setting a price aside, and it says nothing about whether participants may trade on information they hold by virtue of their campus employment.

Here is the case. A student who works roughly ten hours a week in the provost's front office began, three weeks ago, buying heavily on the contract paying out if the general-education review is postponed past May. Volume on that contract had previously been negligible. The price moved from about eighteen cents to about seventy-four cents over nine days, essentially all of it in his trades, and the other side was held by four long-time participants who tell us they sold because the price was 'too good to pass up' and that they knew perfectly well who was buying. He has not been charged with anything. He says he read nothing confidential, that he formed his view from public signals — committee minutes, a stalled accreditation site visit, an open-budget forum — and that he is entitled to trade on his own reasoning. One of the four who sold to him has now filed the complaint.

The panel must decide three things, and I have been asked to circulate a written recommendation before Thursday's meeting: first, whether to set the seventy-four-cent price aside so that it is not transmitted to the provost's office; second, whether to sanction the trader; third, what rule, if any, to adopt for next semester concerning participants who hold information because of their campus employment.

I am currently taking a seminar in social epistemology whose middle unit concerned markets as knowledge-aggregating institutions, and I would like the memo to engage that material directly rather than treating this as a simple question of fairness. We spent a week on the Milgrom and Stokey no-trade theorem, according to which, given a common prior and common knowledge of rationality, there is no trade rational agents can agree to, and on Aumann's related result that agents with a common prior cannot agree to disagree about something that is common knowledge; we spent a week on Grossman and Stiglitz's argument that prices cannot be fully informationally efficient, since someone must be compensated for the cost of acquiring what the price ends up revealing. I want the memo to say what those results actually imply here: whether the existence of this trade is evidence that the platform is working as designed or evidence that it is not; whether the four sellers' stated reasons are consistent with common knowledge of one another's rationality, or whether their incentives point somewhere else entirely given how the prize is awarded; and whether a play-money platform on which information costs participants nothing to acquire can even find itself in the Grossman and Stiglitz predicament. Please also weigh the argument that a forecasting platform ought to welcome precisely this kind of trader, because his information is the thing we are trying to aggregate, against the worry that the platform is being used as a channel for announcing an administrative decision before the administration has made or disclosed it, and that our own published price may have begun to influence the outcome it was supposed to measure. Finally, address whether it is legitimate to sanction conduct that no rule in force prohibited at the time, since two of my colleagues want to treat the charter's silence as license and one wants to treat it as a prohibition we can enforce retroactively.

Please write the piece as a memo this panel could act on: a clear recommendation on each of the three questions, with a proposed deadline or time frame where one is appropriate; draftable rule language for the third question if you judge a rule warranted; and a closing section stating the strongest case for the outcome you are recommending against and explaining why it nonetheless fails. I expect the trader's own position to be taken seriously rather than strawmanned. Roughly 900 to 1,200 words, and please write in American English.
