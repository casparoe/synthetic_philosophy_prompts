I cover science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and I have been commissioned to write a long piece on how journalists come to know anything at all when nearly everything they report rests on somebody else's word. My editor, a sceptic by temperament, has asked me first to set out my own working epistemology in explicit form so that it can be attacked before it is published. I should like you to conduct that attack, in a disciplined way.

Here is what I believe, or at any rate what I find myself committed to in practice:

1. Testimony is a basic source. When a researcher who is competent and candid tells me that p, I can come to know that p without first possessing any positive reason to think her reliable. The default is trust: defeasible, but not earned.

2. Reliability need not be tracked in order to matter. What makes the resulting belief knowledge is that the source was in fact reliable; I am not required to be able to rule out that it was unreliable, and I do not think sceptical alternatives of that wholly general kind are live for me.

3. My ledger practice is legitimate. I keep a file on recurring sources. When a source asserts p and I subsequently establish p by means that do not depend on that source, I record a hit. Accumulated hits justify upgrading a source from "usable" to "trusted", and the upgrade is itself something I know.

4. Bootstrapping, by contrast, is worthless. The reasoning that runs — this source says that p; p is true (on its own say-so); therefore the source said something true; therefore, after enough repetitions, the source is reliable — cannot raise my warrant one jot. If it could, a horoscope column might be audited into respectability by its own devotee. I regard anyone who reasons that way as guilty of malpractice, not merely of a subtle error.

5. Knowledge is closed under known entailment, and competent deduction from what I know yields knowledge of the conclusion. I am attached to this: so much of my ordinary work consists in drawing out what a result implies, or what a source's own claims commit it to, that I cannot see how I could function without it.

6. Channel capture defeats verification. This is my own coinage, for a case I want to use in the piece. For two years I took a weekly digest summarising new papers in my patch. Each week I read the papers it pointed me to and checked its summaries against them; they matched every time; I concluded that the digest was reliable. But every paper I read was one the digest had selected, so the checking was conducted entirely inside the channel it supplied. My verdict: I did not thereby come to know that the digest was reliable, and what I did was not checking in any sense worth the name.

7. Apparent peer disagreement defeats knowledge. If two sources who strike me as equally credible contradict one another on p, then I do not know p, and I ought to suspend until I find something to break the tie. This is the rule I actually follow and I think it is the right rule.

8. Accountability is not the same as justification. I would not publish a claim I could not attribute to a named, contactable source, and I want my pieces to show their workings so that readers may check them without having to take my word. But I do not think a reader needs to have checked anything in order to know what my article tells them: I think they can know it from me, in the manner described at (1).

Please audit this set rather than lecture me on the surrounding literature. Specifically:

(a) Identify which commitments are genuinely in conflict, and rank those conflicts by severity. I want an ordering, not a list in which every item is equally troubling.

(b) For each genuine conflict, trace it explicitly: which of the numbered commitments, plus what bridging step, generate the problem, and which single commitment must be weakened or abandoned to remove it. Where more than one repair is available, say which is cheapest and what I lose by adopting it.

(c) Identify at least one pair that only looks as though it clashes, and explain why it dissolves. I expect that not everything here which appears to be in tension really is, and I would rather be told which is which than have the whole lot flagged.

(d) For the reasoning condemned at (4), tell me what precisely is wrong with it. Is this a case of warrant failing to transmit through the inference; of the inference being question-begging in some specifiable sense; of its being dialectically useless because it cannot settle a question I am genuinely asking; or of something else? The diagnosis matters practically, since different diagnoses imply different newsroom rules. I want to know whether (4) can be retained as it stands, or must be reformulated — and if reformulated, how.

(e) Finally, tell me whether the set can be made coherent by local repairs, or whether it rests on a conception of trust that cannot survive being written down.

Two housekeeping points. Please write in British English throughout. And please treat all eight commitments as equally expendable in principle: I do not at this stage know which of them I would give up, and I have no wish to receive an audit that flatters the ones I happen to like the sound of.
