I am the operations superintendent for a regional flood-control district. We maintain roughly forty miles of levee, three pump stations, two detention basins, and a set of gated spillways along a river system that drains both dense suburbs and working farmland. About twice a decade we get an event large enough that the system cannot protect everyone, and during such an event the duty engineer makes a run of decisions within a few hours — open the auxiliary spillway or hold it, commit the single sandbagging crew and the pump barge to one reach or another, keep a basin below capacity or fill it — where the choice determines whose property floods and whose does not. Our enabling statute establishes a compensation fund that pays only for damage the district caused; damage the district failed to prevent is borne by the property owner. The internal rule we currently use to apply that line is one sentence long: damage resulting from an affirmative operation of district works is district-caused, and damage resulting from the failure of works to perform as designed is an act of nature.

That rule has stopped being defensible, for two reasons I want the procedure to confront rather than paper over. First, a closed gate is simultaneously an affirmative operation and a failure to open, so the identical decision can be described either way depending on who is writing the incident report. Second, "as designed" conceals choices we made earlier: if we built a structure to a standard we selected, with some awareness of the exposure it left behind, calling its performance a natural event looks like defining our way out of responsibility.

I would like a decision procedure I can put in the operations manual and train to. The constraints are these. Live decisions are made by on-call engineers and technicians, not lawyers or philosophers, in about twenty minutes, at three in the morning, with a radio running and incomplete telemetry. Classification decisions after the fact are made by a five-member board, on written reasons, with counsel present, and any classification that triggers a payment above $250,000 requires board signature. The deliverable should therefore have two tiers: a fast tier the duty engineer can actually run, and a slow adjudication tier that produces the defensible written record — with an explicit statement of which findings the slow tier may overturn and which it may not.

Please address the following in the procedure:

1. Tell me which version of the doing/allowing distinction the fast tier is built on, and why that one rather than the alternatives. The candidates I am aware of: whether the agent initiates a threat that was not coming, versus fails to block one already in motion; whether the agent removes a barrier that was protecting the victim; whether the agent redirects an existing threat away from some people and onto others who would otherwise have been safe; or whether none of these tracks anything morally relevant and the only real question is the balance of harms. For each, say what it yields in a managed river system, where the hydrology is already an artifact of our works and there may be no unmanaged baseline left to appeal to. Then commit to one, or to a short ordered combination, and say what is lost by dropping the others.

2. Each step should state what counts as a sufficient answer, and where the procedure stops with a no rather than proceeding to a harder question.

3. The classification is doing two jobs at once — an ex ante job (should we take this action?) and an ex post job (who pays, and does anyone face an inquiry?). I suspect that coupling them will tempt operators to choose the action that classifies best rather than the one that saves the most. Build in a guard against that distortion, or tell me plainly that the honest design separates the two determinations and how the separation would work.

4. Apply the procedure to three cases. Case A: forecast inflow into the upper basin will exceed capacity within thirty hours; opening the auxiliary spillway now, while the downstream channel still has room, floods sixty farmsteads and cuts a county road that would otherwise stay dry, and prevents an uncontrolled overtopping later that would inundate four thousand homes; waiting risks losing the option altogether. Case B: two reaches will overtop within thirty-six hours, one holding an unincorporated community of about nine hundred people and one holding an industrial park; we have one crew and one barge and time to hold one reach but not both. Case C: a channel we straightened and armoured in the 1970s has been silting for years, dredging it costs four million dollars, and this cycle's capital budget will not fund it; a study we commissioned more than a decade ago found that the reduced capacity raises water levels in an upstream neighbourhood that has since flooded twice. When it floods again, our staff will say we did nothing. I expect your procedure to resist that description, and I want to know what it says instead — but also how it limits the conclusion, because if deferred maintenance items count as acts, then every line in our capital plan is a potential claim against the fund, and I need the procedure to tell me where that expansion stops.

5. Mark the judgment calls as judgment calls. Which steps look procedural but are really value choices? Where must the procedure hand the decision to a named human being rather than resolve it? And what can it not do at all — in particular, whether it can produce a classification that will look fair to the residents whose houses flooded either way.

6. Give me a one-page version of the fast tier suitable for laminating and keeping in the control room.

Use plain language throughout, since the people running this will not have read the philosophical literature, but do not let the plain language smuggle in a substantive claim I have not agreed to: where a term is doing philosophical work, define it. Name the distinctions you are relying on so that I can look them up later, but I do not need a bibliography or citations, and I would rather you flag a step you are unsure about than present it as settled.
