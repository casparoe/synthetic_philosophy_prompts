I have no academic background in this — I left school at sixteen and have spent thirty-odd years in freight logistics — but for the past three years I have been running my own informal versions of the Allais problem on anyone who will sit still for ten minutes: a book group, two workplace chat groups, a chess club, about sixty people in all. I am not asking you to tell me who is right, and I am not asking for a survey of the literature, which I could not look up in any case. I am asking you to write the document I believe the two camps in this debate should have produced jointly long ago: the agreed statement of an adversarial collaboration between a defender of expected utility theory, who holds that the independence axiom is constitutive of choice under uncertainty and that the standard Allais responses are errors to be argued or designed away, and a defender of the Allais intuitions, who holds that independence should be dropped or restricted — through probability weighting, through letting anticipated regret enter the evaluation, or through some account on which certainty carries a weight that ratios cannot capture.

The reason I want it grounded in my own material rather than in the textbook pair is that my version has features the usual formulations lack, and I suspect those features change where the cruxes sit.

I parameterised a family of problems by a single probability p. The undiluted problem: Option S pays $30 for certain; Option R pays $90 with probability p and nothing otherwise. The diluted problem: with probability one half you are given the choice-described draw, and with probability one half you get nothing either way, so Option S' pays $30 with probability one half and Option R' pays $90 with probability p over two. At p = 1/3 the two options have equal expected value in both problems, and the dilution preserves that equality. My results at p = 1/3: roughly four in five took the certain $30 in the undiluted problem, and roughly three in five took R' over S' in the diluted one — an independence violation with expected value exactly matched on both sides, in the same session, five minutes apart. Before choosing, respondents were asked to state the expected value of each option; nearly all computed them correctly and then chose as described. In a second condition I described the identical pair as two machines that would each be run fifty times over a year with the payoffs actually banked, and there the majority went the other way, toward the option expected utility theory endorses; when I then re-asked the single-shot question of those same people, most kept the answer they had just given for the repeated case. Finally, after each choice I offered the chance to pay fifty cents to switch to the other option, and almost everyone declined — including several people whose two answers, taken together, would have made them pumpable had they been asked to choose again.

My own hypothesis, which I want treated as a hypothesis and not as an ally, is that respondents are not evaluating payoffs but the gap between a payoff and what they took themselves to be entitled to expect at the moment of choosing; on that view, dilution destroys the entitlement even though it preserves the ratio, and that is what flips the answer. I want the joint statement to say plainly whether this is a distinct third position or regret theory wearing different clothes, and what observation would tell the two apart.

Please draft the document with the following structure. First, a numbered list of claims both parties can sign — about the formalism, about what the axioms do and do not entail, about what my data would have to show to count as a violation at all, and about the conditions under which each side thinks a choice pattern is genuinely criticisable. Second, a disagreements table, giving for each point the question at issue, the formulation the expected utility defender would sign, the formulation the Allais defender would sign, and a verdict on whether the difference is substantive or verbal. Third, the cruxes: the sub-questions such that settling one would actually move at least one side. I expect these to include whether a person who faces the pair once and cannot be pumped is within the reach of the money-pump argument; whether the single-shot and repeated presentations are two different decision problems or one problem answered by two different people; and whether counting disappointment and relief as part of the description of an outcome rescues independence or concedes the point — and if it rescues it, what criterion each side accepts for when such redescription is legitimate rather than an ad hoc rescue of whatever preference turns up. For each crux, give a test that a person with a spreadsheet and sixty volunteers could actually run, and record each side's prediction in advance, in words they would not later disown. Fourth, a short closing section listing what each party would give up, and under what result.
