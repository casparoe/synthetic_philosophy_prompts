I'm a second-year in a philosophy PhD program and I drew the short straw: next week in our formal epistemology seminar I have to argue that Jeffrey conditionalization is in worse shape than its defenders admit. There are six of us, we meet in a grad student's apartment, and half the room hasn't done probability since high school, so the argument has to be one they can follow on paper without me losing the thread either. The problem is I don't believe the position I've been assigned, and the objections I can think of are the ones the Jeffrey side already has canned answers to. Order-dependence is the obvious one and everyone knows the reply: two different sequences of inputs just amount to two different bodies of total evidence, so it's no surprise the endpoints differ, and anyway there's a dynamic-coherence result supposed to show you can't be Dutch-booked so long as each individual step obeys the rule. Fine. I want to press something else, and I want you to argue it as hard as you can, whether or not you think it works.

The line I want: Jeffrey updates don't accumulate. Under ordinary conditionalization, once you learn E it's locked at probability 1 forever, and every later posterior is just your prior conditional on the whole conjunction of what you've learned, so your final credal state is literally a record of your evidence and that record constrains everything downstream — you can't coherently end up where the record doesn't license. Under JCON there's no analogue. The q-values you set on the basis of an experience leave no trace that binds later updates: a subsequent update on a different partition can walk your credence in the earlier proposition right back, and nothing in your final state says that you ever had the first experience at all. So the Jeffrey agent doesn't have a growing body of evidence, it has a summary that keeps getting rewritten, and that undercuts the things we actually want from an account of learning — a way to say two agents have the same evidence, a way to criticize someone for having downweighted or forgotten an earlier deliverance of experience, and a coherence guarantee over a whole sequence that amounts to more than "each step was fine given that step's inputs."

This isn't purely abstract for me, which is how I ended up with the toy case. My brother has been chasing a diagnosis for a year and I've been doing this by hand after every appointment: D = the rash is drug-induced, M = the marker is present. My prior over the four atoms (D&M, D&¬M, ¬D&M, ¬D&¬M) was 0.40 / 0.10 / 0.10 / 0.40. Bad lighting in the dermatologist's office leaves me with q(D) = 0.8; JCON with rigidity gives 0.64 / 0.16 / 0.04 / 0.16, so P(D) = 0.8 and P(M) = 0.68. Then the lab calls the marker result borderline-leaning-negative and I set q(M) = 0.3. Rigidity fixes P(D|M) = 16/17 ≈ 0.94 and P(D|¬M) = 0.5, and the new joint is ≈ 0.282 / 0.35 / 0.018 / 0.35. So P(D) ≈ 0.63 — my 0.8 is gone, overwritten by an update in which nobody said anything about the rash. Reverse the order and you get the mirror image: q(M) = 0.3 first gives 0.24 / 0.14 / 0.06 / 0.56, then q(D) = 0.8 gives ≈ 0.505 / 0.295 / 0.019 / 0.181, and now P(M) ≈ 0.52 instead of 0.3. In each case the last partition keeps the number I put in and the first one silently drifts. Please check that arithmetic and tell me if I botched it, because a lot rides on it in the presentation.
