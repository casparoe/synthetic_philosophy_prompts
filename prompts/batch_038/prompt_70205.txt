I'm a curator at a small model-organism database — four of us, and I personally sign off on maybe 30 or 40 gene product annotations a week. Our entries feed into a shared ontology, and a lot of downstream analysis treats a blank cell as evidence that a gene product doesn't do the thing, which is not something we ever intended but its now baked into everyone's pipelines. So errors in both directions are expensive and I've stopped being able to wing it.

The real problem is that our internal guidelines say to record what a gene product does, and nobody on the team has ever pinned down what that claim amounts to. Half the time the evidence in front of me is a purified-protein assay showing an activity in a tube; half the time it's comparative genomics plus a dN/dS argument, or a knock-out showing that losing the activity costs the organism something; sometimes it's just a mutation in a patient. I've been reading around the philosophy of biology on function — the selected-effects line versus the causal-role / contribution-to-a-capacity line, and what each side makes of malfunction, promiscuous activities, and freshly recruited traits — and now I want to stop philosophising and get a procedure.

So: write me a decision procedure my team can actually run on an annotation. Requirements:

- ordered tests, each with what counts as passing evidence and what doesn't, runnable in about twenty minutes from the paper(s) in front of us by someone who has never read Wright or Cummins and never will;
- an explicit call at the top on whether our database is a causal-role resource, an etiological resource, or two resources in a trenchcoat — i.e. separate term types or evidence tags for the two kinds of claim — with the argument for whichever you pick and what we give up on the other options;
- the steps that are really a metaphysical commitment wearing a workflow rule as a disguise: flag them, and then tell me what the rule should say anyway so curators don't freeze;
- where the procedure stops and hands the call to a senior curator, and why it can't be mechanised at that point.

Then run it on four cases: a crystallin-type lens protein whose same activity in other tissues is ordinary intermediary metabolism; an antifreeze protein in a polar fish lineage that looks recruited from a digestive-enzyme gene; a duplicate copy retained after a genome duplication where the assay shows the activity and nobody can construct any selection story for it; and a human loss-of-function allele where the wild-type product plainly has the activity in vitro but the tissue we'd want to claim it for is precisely the tissue that's broken.

At least one of those four should come out against what I'd like — I would rather the procedure force me to withdraw a well-loved annotation than find out in two years that we've been making it up all along. Finish with the short paragraph we'd put in front of users saying what our function terms do and don't commit to, and a straight answer to whether the account we pick actually changes any entry, or whether the whole dispute is invisible downstream.
