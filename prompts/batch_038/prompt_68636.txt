**Draft: Locked in to what? Granularity, ensembles, and the contingency debate**

The argument about whether the modern world was inevitable is usually staged as a contest between two camps. On one side are those who insist that history is locked in by early, small events: Paul David's account of how a typewriter keyboard layout became a durable standard, and W. Brian Arthur's work on increasing returns, are the canonical exhibits. On the other side are those who think the underlying forces are strong enough that the outcome would have arrived anyway, whether by way of Britain's coal or somewhere else's, and who treat the accidents as noise around a robust trend. My claim in this chapter is that a large share of this argument is not a disagreement about the world at all, but a disagreement conducted at shifting levels of description, where the shifts are invisible to both parties.

Start with a distinction. For any historical outcome, two questions can be asked. The token question: would this very outcome, with these particulars, have occurred? The type question: would some outcome falling under the same general description have occurred? A process can be maximally contingent in the first sense and strongly convergent in the second, and nothing about the causal machinery forbids the combination. Consider the width of the standard railway gauge. It is a genuine historical accident, propagated by copying from Stephenson's coal-waggon tramways, that this particular width rather than Brunel's broader one became the world's norm; the popular version of the story that traces the measurement back to Roman chariot wheels is textbook folklore, but the accidental character of the number survives the debunking. Meanwhile the claim that some common gauge would have been settled on, within a similar range of engineering trade-offs, is entirely compatible with that. The same holds of the keyboard: locked in as a token, near-certain as a type.

The characteristic failure I want to name is what I shall call the individuation slide: a claim established at one granularity is restated at another without notice, and the plausibility earned at the first is spent at the second. Slides run in both directions and serve both camps. The contingency-writer slides from "this token was an accident" to "industrialisation was an accident", inflating the reach of a local contingency. The convergence-writer slides from "industrialisation would have happened somewhere" to "Britain's particular sequence was inevitable", deflating an accident that demonstrably occurred. Diagnosing a slide requires that the description under which an outcome is counted be stated explicitly, because outcome descriptions are nested and there is no obvious stopping point: the choice of granularity is doing substantial work, and it is almost never argued for.

The second distinction concerns the modal status of these claims. Assertions of inevitability and contingency are implicitly claims about dispersion across an ensemble of replays: how much would outcomes vary, and how much would their timing vary, if the sequence were run again? We have a single draw. Worse, the ensembles over which we reason are not discovered but constructed, and the construction procedure is calibrated on the actual sequence. I shall call this the endogenous ensemble problem: our imagined replays are produced by a historical imagination formed by studying what happened, so an estimate of how things could have gone otherwise is not independent evidence about how things could have gone otherwise. There is a concrete consequence. The counterfactuals people find vivid track the near-misses that the documentary record preserves, and near-misses are over-represented precisely because they were nearly actual and therefore nearly recorded. A near-miss generates an intuition of branching; it does not establish that the branch was live, which requires showing both that an alternative was causally available and that something available could have flipped the switch.

This yields an asymmetry of burden that explains the rhetoric of the debate without vindicating either side. Contingency claims are mechanism-hungry: demonstrating a live branch forces you to name the alternative and the switch. Convergence claims are frequently mechanism-poor, resting on functionalist reasoning that explains why an outcome was useful, adaptive or needed rather than why anything would have produced it. Path-dependence arguments therefore feel more rigorous, and are cited as though they were better evidenced, when mechanism-richness speaks to the actual sequence and not to the dispersion across replays.

There is a synthesis here that I take to be the most important point in the chapter. The mechanism both camps invoke most often supports both conclusions simultaneously, at different granularities. Increasing returns guarantees that a winner emerges, which is type-convergence; and it leaves the identity of the winner to early noise, which is token-contingency. The same feedback process that makes an outcome robust makes its particulars arbitrary. Anyone who cites increasing returns as evidence for lock-in and anyone who cites it as evidence for convergence is right about the mechanism and unclear about the question.

I therefore propose that claims in this debate be subject to a contingency specification: four declarations. (i) The outcome description at a stated granularity, together with a reason for that granularity rather than a coarser or finer one. (ii) The branch point: when, and what would have had to differ. (iii) The mechanism making the branch live: what would have flipped the switch, and whether the flipper was itself available. (iv) The ensemble over which the claim is meant to hold: which set of replays, held fixed in which respects and varied in which. My contention is that most celebrated statements on both sides fail at least two of the four, and that when they are made to comply, many apparent disagreements turn out to be agreements about different items.

Two worries remain, and I do not know how to settle either. First, the specification may render the debate unanswerable rather than answerable: if no one can honestly declare an ensemble, perhaps such claims are expressive only, and my four declarations are a polite way of saying so. Second, granularity may not be a free parameter. Some descriptions may be privileged by the causal structure — a process that produces a winner looks like a natural kind, whereas the identity of the winner may not be — in which case the slide is not always a fallacy, and the framework owes an account of what fixes the correct granularity. That is where the chapter stops.
