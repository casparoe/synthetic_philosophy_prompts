**The question (response paper 2, question 2):**

"Consent is often said to have moral magic: it can turn an act that would otherwise be a serious wrong into a permissible one. State the conditions under which consent has this transformative effect, and then argue whether deception, coercion, and incompetence each defeat consent in the same way or in relevantly different ways. Illustrate with the paid kidney donor and the undisclosed diagnosis cases from week 5."

**The submitted answer:**

Consent does moral work that nothing else does. If I cut you open without asking, that is battery. If I cut you open after you agree to it, the very same movements are a surgery. So consent is not evidence that the act was permissible all along, it is what makes it permissible. That is the moral magic, and it is why the conditions on consent matter so much: they are conditions on the only thing that can do this job.

The conditions are usually given as three. The person has to be competent, they have to have been given the relevant information, and they have to be free from force. My view is that the three do not fail in the same way, although I will argue that by the end they converge.

Deception is the cleanest case. If the surgeon tells the patient the growth is benign when he knows it is not, and she signs, then she has agreed to a different operation from the one she gets. Her consent does not attach to the act that actually occurs, so it cannot transform it. Deception does not weaken consent, it misdirects it. What matters is not how badly the patient is set back by the lie but the fact that her agreement was aimed at something else. This is why deception invalidates consent even when the lie is trivial and the patient loses nothing by it, since in every case of deception the agreement is aimed at the wrong thing.

Coercion starts out looking different. A coerced person does agree to precisely what happens: the tourist hands over exactly the wallet that was demanded, so his consent describes the act correctly. It is tempting to say his consent is fully formed and merely regrettable. That cannot be right, because the account we discussed says coercion is a proposal to make you worse off than you would have been relative to what would otherwise happen, in order to get you to comply. Threats do that and offers do not, which is why the line between them is a line about baselines.

But the kidney case shows the baseline idea is too permissive. The donor's only alternative to selling is defaulting on his family's debts and losing the house. Nobody threatens him — the buyer only offers money — and still he agrees because he has no acceptable option left. If voluntariness is about whether the agent had a real choice, and he had none, then his consent is not voluntary, and the fact that no one worsened his position is beside the point. Once you see this, the difference between the cases starts to collapse. In deception the agent chooses on the basis of a picture of the world that is not true. In coercion the agent chooses with no exit. In both, the choice is not really his own, and so all three defects have the same upshot: there is no consent to the act, hence nothing to transform it.

Incompetence differs only in degree. Competence comes in levels and attaches to the decision rather than to the person: someone may be competent to agree to a blood draw and not to a course of chemotherapy, and a fifteen year old may understand a procedure perfectly well and still not be able to weigh it against her own future. Mill took the same sort of line about contracts to sell yourself into slavery. He thought the law is entitled to refuse to enforce a bargain that destroys your capacity to make bargans at all, because in the end the state is better placed than the individual to see which choices will ruin them. That is the one place where the refusal to hold people to their own agreements really is about protecting them from themselves.

If the three conditions converge like this, the practical conclusion is that we should stop treating them as separate tests and ask one question instead: was this choice the agent's own?
