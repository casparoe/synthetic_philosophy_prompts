I run vendor operations at a mid-size localization company (software strings, help-center articles, some marketing collateral) and I've been trying to write up how our workflow actually works for an internal handbook, and somewhere around the third draft I started finding the whole thing kind of disturbing rather than boring. Here's the setup. We sell three tiers. Tier 1 is a human translator working from the source, reserved for anything medical, legal or financial. Tier 2 is machine translation plus a human post-editor, and the instruction sheet we hand the editor says to fix only what blocks comprehension — don't restyle, don't improve, don't make it prettier, get in and get out — and they're paid per word at maybe a third of the Tier 1 rate with a target handle time tracked in the dashboard. Tier 3 is raw machine output shipped untouched, mostly internal helpdesk and knowledge-base junk, and the stated rationale in the handbook is that nobody really reads that stuff anyway. On top of that we have a quality gate: a model scores the machine output before any bilingual human looks at it and predicts which spans the editor would end up rewriting, and if the predicted bad-span density is under a threshhold the string is auto-approved and goes straight out the door. Anything that does reach a human reviewer gets annotated against an error taxonomy — accuracy, fluency, terminology, style — with severities of minor/major/critical carrying penalty points, and a file passes if points per hundred words stay under a limit. The bit that trips me up: our handbook defines a critical error as one the end user would notice and act on, so a wrong number in a document nobody opens isn't critical, while a perfectly faithful but stiff rendering can rack up major fluency errors. So we simultaneously tell clients that a machine can't be trusted where the content matters and that it's fine where the content doesn't, as if the system's relationship to meaning shifts with the stakes.

My brother's a philosophy grad student and he keeps bringing up Hofstadter on statistical translation — roughly, that a translator has to carry around a mental model of the world the text is about, so a thing trained only on sentence pairs is doing something categorically different, matching forms, and can come out fluent while missing the meaning entirely, and crucially that this kind of failure isn't a pile of local annotatable errors. And I want to know whether our whole apparatus is basically a bet against that picture. So: reconstruct the theory of meaning and understanding our practices presuppose, as explicitly as you can. Does the auto-approve gate assume that a translation being wrong about the world always leaves a detectable mark on the target text alone — that you can judge adequacy without knowing what it was supposed to say? Does the penalty-point scoring assume that semantic failure is local, additive and commensurable, so that a systematically ungrounded output and a merely clumsy one differ only in point totals? Which rival views does the bundle quietly rule out, and does the "critical = the user would notice" clause commit us to meaning being a property of the reader's uptake rather than of fidelity to the source? Then separate what we strictly need from what just happens to fit comfortably — I suspect a Hofstadter-style skeptic could sign off on the exact same tiers, gate and taxonomy and just read them differently, and I want to know exactly where the practice is underdetermined like that and where it isn't. And finally, if we genuinely came to believe the machine never means anything by its output, what would have to change operationally — the tier boundaries, the gate, the error categories, the per-word pricing — not just how we'd talk about it in the sales deck, which currently says the system "learns the meaning of the text," a sentence I've started flinching at.

Some grounding detail in case it matters: the post-editors are all bilingual and do see the source; it's only the auto-approve path where nobody who knows the source language ever looks. We also do an annual audit where a sample of Tier 3 output gets back-translated into the source language by a vendor and eyeballed for gross failures, which everyone treats as a formality because the numbers always come back clean. No need for citations or reading lists here, I couldn't check them anyway — I want the implicit theory reconstructed and argued for, with the underdetermination flagged honestly, plus a short list of the two or three places where I think our practice is straight-up incoherent rather than merely philosophically naive.
