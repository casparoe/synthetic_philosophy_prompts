I am setting the paper for our department's doctoral qualifying examination in metaphysics, and I need one essay question on counterpart theory and transworld identity, to be answered in two hours, closed book, and marked out of 25. The candidates will all have done the standard reading on both sides; what I want to discover is whether they can reason about the semantics rather than rehearse the familiar controversy.

The question must be built around the following case, which should be stated inside the question itself so that nothing beyond it is required. Alice is an actual person. Fix one world, w. In w there are two individuals: V, who closely matches Alice's psychological profile, her character and the shape of her life, but who arose from different gametes; and B, the human organism that developed from precisely the same egg and sperm as Alice, but who, after a prenatal injury, has a psychology and a history utterly unlike hers. Let C1 be the counterpart relation keyed to psychological and biographical continuity, on which V is Alice's closest counterpart in w, and C2 the relation keyed to biological origin, on which B is her closest counterpart in w. Suppose further that the conversational context makes C1 and C2 equally salient: the speakers are in the middle of a dispute about whether Alice is the person or the organism, and neither relation has any claim to priority.

The question should ask candidates to determine whether, in that context, counterpart theory leaves the truth-values of de re modal claims about Alice indeterminate, and whether the partisan of transworld identity is any better placed to secure determinacy. It must require them to take and defend a position rather than survey both. It should not be answerable by reciting the Humphrey objection, and it must force engagement with at least these three points: (a) that the counterpart relation is a parameter of the semantics, so that two relations may assign different truth-values to the same modal sentence without either being incorrect; (b) the difference between a context that makes two counterpart relations salient and a single relation that admits two counterparts within one world, and why only the second raises any worry about counting; and (c) whether the haecceitist has an answer to the parallel question of what makes it the case that a given individual belongs to a given world, or whether that question is simply declined.

I would also like the marking scheme to treat it as a substantive error if a candidate claims that counterpart theory is distinctive in abandoning the inference from 'possibly P and possibly Q' to 'possibly both P and Q'. That inference fails on any possible-worlds treatment of de re modality; the specifically counterpart-theoretic phenomenon is that the two possibility-claims can be witnessed by different individuals within a single world.

Please provide: the question exactly as it would appear on the paper, in 250 to 350 words; a model answer of about 900 words; and a marking scheme out of 25 that names five or six specific moves an A answer must make, states the marks each carries, and notes for each the characteristic way in which a B answer goes wrong. No bibliography or reference list, please — the model answer should stand on its own reasoning.

Finally, tell me where you think the item is weakest. Identify the part of your model answer's verdict that a strong candidate could most plausibly overturn, and say what argument or consideration would change your own mind about whether this case genuinely threatens determinacy at all, as opposed to merely exhibiting a context-sensitivity that everyone already accepts.
