I am in the second term of a philosophy of social science module, and for our reconstruction exercise each of us has to rebuild a contested methodological thesis as a numbered argument and then say where it fails. I chose the position defended in Friedman's 1953 essay on the methodology of positive economics: that a theory is to be judged by the accuracy of its predictions, and that the realism of its assumptions is not a separate criterion. My difficulty is that my reconstruction appears to go through, and it proves considerably more than the position it was meant to capture. I would like to know which step to reject and what the rejection costs.

First the case, since the argument depends on it. Consider two models of how a firm sets output. Model M says the firm continuously equates perceived marginal revenue with perceived marginal cost and revises whenever either moves. Model N says the firm does nothing of the sort: it applies a fixed markup to average cost and revises output once a quarter, in the direction indicated by the change in sales. Over eighteen months of quarterly data from one industry, M and N generate output series that are indistinguishable within measurement error, because demand in that period moved slowly and seasonally, so quarter-end revision tracked continuous revision closely enough. Separately, in a laboratory market run for the module, subjects in the firm role behaved as N describes; they were never given the cost schedule, so the calculation M attributes to them could not have been performed. In the laboratory we could also shift demand abruptly, and under abrupt shifts M and N diverge sharply: N lags by up to a quarter and overshoots.

Now the argument.

1. What we want from a theory is accurate predictions about the phenomena we care about; the assumptions are instruments for generating those predictions.
2. Realism is a property of assumptions rather than of predictions, so facts about realism can bear on the assessment of a theory only through their consequences for predictive accuracy.
3. Accordingly, a fact about a theory's assumptions counts against that theory only if it shows the theory predicting less well somewhere in the range of phenomena we care about.
4. Over the industry data, M and N are predictively indistinguishable.
5. The laboratory result is a fact about the decision procedure, that is, about assumptions; by (3) it counts against M only if it shows M predicting less well.
6. It might be said that it does, since M and N diverge under abrupt demand shifts. But a claim about what would happen under conditions that have not obtained is not tested by any data we possess; the divergence can only be checked when such a shift occurs, at which point both models will be assessed on the new data along with everything else. Until then the divergence is a promise, not evidence.
7. So the laboratory result gives us no reason to prefer N to M, even though it shows M's central assumption to be false of these firms and N's to be roughly true of them.
8. This generalises. For any theory that predicts well over the data we have, no direct evidence that its assumptions are false is a reason to abandon it.
9. Independently, a theory is the more significant the more it abstracts: it explains much by little, and what it leaves out it leaves out falsely. Significance is a merit and it is purchased with unrealistic assumptions; so, other things equal, the more unrealistic a theory's assumptions, the better the theory.
10. Therefore evidence that an assumption is false is never a reason to reject a predictively adequate theory, and the more we learn about how firms actually make their decisions the worse our economics becomes, since what we learn tempts us to replace significant abstractions with realistic clutter.

I cannot present (10). It implies that the laboratory market was worthless; it implies that a model may be carried into precisely the conditions under which we have watched its mechanism fail; and it seems to condemn experimental economics as a discipline. Yet every step looked acceptable to me when I wrote it down, and I do not want to escape by simply declaring (1) false, because without something like (1) I cannot see how two theories are ever to be compared at all.

Which step should go, or which needs restricting, and what do I lose by restricting it? If the answer is that (1) survives only in a weakened form, I would like to be told what that form is and whether it still does the work Friedman wanted from it — in particular, whether it still licenses dismissing the businessman's own account of how he sets prices as evidence about his firm's behaviour. And I would like to know how much of (9) can be kept: I think abstraction really is a theoretical virtue, and I do not want to be pushed into holding that the more detailed model is always the better one.
