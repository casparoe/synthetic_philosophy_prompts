Our hospice was founded by a Buddhist trust in the late 1980s, and I chair its ethics committee. There are seven of us: three clinicians, two lay Buddhists from the sponsoring trust, a solicitor and a fundraiser, the last two being neither Buddhist nor willing to be lectured in Pali. We meet monthly and see ten to fifteen substantial donation offers a year — legacies, foundation grants, corporate gifts, the occasional conditional bequest. Each currently goes up to the board with a one-paragraph recommendation that is really a record of who argued hardest in the room. Two camps have hardened. One holds that the money relieves suffering and that refusing it is a way of buying our own good consciences at our patients' expense; the other holds that we cannot take money made by industries whose harms arrive, in some cases, in our own beds. We have twenty minutes per case, no outside counsel, and offers lapse if we drift.

Please write a decision procedure we could actually run on an offer: the questions in the order they must be asked, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure terminates in an accept or a decline, and where it stops recommending and hands the question to a person or to the board. It must be usable by a member who has read the procedure and nothing else, it must fit inside twenty minutes, and it must generate written reasons the board could publish if the local paper asked.

Ground it in Buddhist ethics rather than in generic charity-governance norms, and be explicit about the fault line that matters here: whether the moral quality of an act lies in the intention behind it and the states of mind it cultivates, or in the balance of suffering and wellbeing it produces. Use what the tradition actually supplies — right livelihood and the trades a lay follower is told to avoid (weapons, living beings, meat, intoxicants, poison); the rule that a monastic may accept meat unless it was seen, heard or suspected to have been killed for the recipient, which is a test of the recipient's knowledge rather than of the producer's act; the same discourse's account of what the recipient's mind must be doing while accepting a gift; giving as something that changes giver and receiver alike; and equanimity as a discipline against the compulsion to relieve every suffering one happens to be able to see. Then tell me which of these distinctions must survive translation into plain English for the solicitor and the fundraiser, which can be dropped, and what is lost by the dropping. Where a consequentialist would simply net off the good the money does against the harm the donor's business causes, say whether your procedure can do anything other than borrow that arithmetic.

Run it on three live offers. First, a £1.4m legacy from the widow of a man who made his fortune in intensive pig and poultry farming; she asks that nothing be named after him and that we make no announcement, but the connection is a matter of local record. Second, £75,000 a year for five years from the charitable foundation of a defence contractor, offered anonymously, which our fundraiser is confident the local paper could trace within a week. Third, £300,000 from a gambling operator's charitable trust to endow our bereavement service — a service the trust already funds at 40 per cent, and a noticeable share of whose caseload consists of families bereaved by suicide after gambling losses. My own instinct is to accept the second and refuse the third. If your procedure reverses either, say so plainly and explain why, rather than bending a step to suit me.

Finally, be honest about the limits. Where will the procedure misfire? How does it handle harms that are diffuse and global rather than traceable to one identifiable producer, and does it thereby favour visible local harms over invisible structural ones? Which steps are judgment calls dressed as procedure? And at exactly which point does it cease to yield a recommendation because what is at stake is not a fact but what kind of institution we intend to be?
