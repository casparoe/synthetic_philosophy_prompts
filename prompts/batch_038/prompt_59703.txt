I'm a PhD student in philosophy of perception assembling a handout for a seminar session that pairs Molyneux's question with the surgical-restoration studies: adults blind from birth who, once operated on, initially cannot match a seen object to a felt one, and then acquire that matching within days. I want to open the session with a poem rather than an abstract.

Please write a poem of twenty to twenty-four lines, in two halves divided by a blank line, in the first-person voice of the man of the case — an adult long able to tell a cube from a globe by hand alone. In the first half, set on the morning his sight is restored, the two sensory vocabularies must be kept strictly disjoint: no term of shape, edge, corner, roundness, or solidity may be predicated of anything he sees, which registers only as brightness, colour, dazzle, and a flat confusion of patches; asked to say by sight alone which is the cube, he must fail, whether by a wrong guess or a refusal. In the second half, some days later, at least two of those shape terms must reappear verbatim, now doing duty for sight as well as for touch. The passage from one half to the other must be carried by a gesture — the hand going out to the object — and not by an argument, a named philosopher, or a stated moral.

Above all, do not let the ending settle the question: the reader should be left unable to tell whether he has found that the two senses share a single world, or has only learned new names for what his fingers already knew.
