I am in the third year of a PhD in political philosophy; my dissertation concerns the epistemics of national well-being measurement — specifically, what a state can learn from an aggregate happiness number that it also has an interest in producing. What I want from you is a case analysis rather than a survey, and I want it to run in both directions: a framework that I supply brought to bear on a real episode, and then the episode brought to bear on the framework, including on the parts of it that I suspect are wrong.

The episode is Bhutan's Gross National Happiness project, from its articulation in the 1970s through the index that now bears the name. Since you cannot look anything up, here are the facts I am treating as established; take them as given rather than reconstructing them. The fourth king, Jigme Singye Wangchuck, advanced GNH in the 1970s as a rival criterion of national progress to GDP, and the 2008 constitution commits the state to promoting the conditions for it. In the late 1980s and early 1990s Bhutan introduced a tightened citizenship regime (a 1985 act with stricter documentation requirements), carried out a census in the southern districts that reclassified many long-resident Nepali speakers as non-nationals, and imposed measures requiring national dress and Dzongkha-language schooling in the service of a single consolidated national identity. Between roughly 1990 and 1993 something on the order of 100,000 Nepali-speaking Lhotshampa — around a sixth of the country's population — left or were made to leave, ending up in camps in eastern Nepal administered by UNHCR, where most stayed for about two decades before large-scale third-country resettlement, principally to the United States. The government's own account describes much of that departure as voluntary emigration, and both the numbers and the characterization are contested; do not spend the answer adjudicating the dispute, but do address what the existence of the dispute does to the case. The GNH Index itself came much later, built by the Centre for Bhutan Studies on an Alkire-Foster-style sufficiency method: nine equally weighted domains, 33 indicators, a person counted as happy if sufficient on at least 66% of the weighted indicators, the index combining the share of happy people with the average sufficiency of the not-yet-happy, four classification bands (unhappy, narrowly happy, extensively happy, deeply happy), decomposable by district and by group, and — this matters — weighting objective indicators considerably more heavily than self-reported ones. The 2010 baseline found roughly four in ten Bhutanese happy; a revised instrument was published in 2023 for the 2022 round. Meanwhile Bhutan, the originator of the entire idea, has consistently sat in the nineties or lower out of roughly 140 countries in the World Happiness Report's Cantril-ladder ranking.

The framework is my own, and I want it used and then broken. It says that a national happiness number can rise by three distinguishable routes. The *circumstances* route: people's lives actually get better. The *standards* route: people's internal comparison standards shift — adaptation, changed reference groups, cultivated contentment, a differently anchored response scale — so the same life earns a higher number. The *constituency* route: the set of people whose lives enter the aggregate changes, without any of them being better off. My contention is that the Goodhart-style worry about happiness policy is nearly always voiced about the standards route alone, that the constituency route is almost invisible in the discussion, and that Bhutan is the case where it runs most starkly. I would add a fourth, instrument-internal channel available to any threshold measure: a *cutoff* route, by which the number improves through marginal movements across the 66% line or through revision of the indicator set, rather than through any proportional change in how anyone's life is going.

My thesis, which I want you to attack rather than defend, is that the standard objection — Bhutan invented happiness-based governance and still ranks badly on the ladder, so either the project failed or the metric is bogus — is a category error, because the ladder and the GNH index are not rival measurements of a single construct: one is a single-item evaluative self-report, the other a multi-domain, sufficiency-based, deliberately objective-weighted instrument, and neither can referee the other.
