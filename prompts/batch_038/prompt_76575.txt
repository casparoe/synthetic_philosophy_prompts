so this is kind of an odd request and feel free to tell me if I'm asking for something weird. I'm a software engineer, zero formal philosophy training (I've read maybe four papers in my life on this stuff), and in three weeks I'm giving a 25 minute talk plus Q&A at a university workshop on language and inference. I got invited because of a production system my team built, and the room is going to be philosophers of language and pragmaticists: at least two experimental people who do reaction-time and eye-tracking work on implicature, one relevance-theory person, one person who works on exhaustification in the grammar, and one who defends a neo-Gricean "presumptive meaning / defaults" style view. Nobody in the room ships software. The one person with real stats chops is a psycholinguist who lives in mixed-effects models.

Here's the thesis I'm defending, stated bluntly because that's how it'll land in the room:

1. The system. It reads inbound support messages and outputs a ranked list of candidate "what this person actually wants", trained on human labels. Example that always gets a laugh: "does anyone else notice the export takes a really long time?" → top-ranked reading: "please make export faster / open a perf bug." No stage of the pipeline computes a context-free literal content, and there is no explicit step where we model the sender's intentions. It's a learned ranking over paraphrase candidates conditioned on context features (account history, whether the described thing is fixable, who they're writing to, prior tickets).

2. Claim A: the maxims are a folk gloss on what a decent inferential model does. Nothing in our pipeline needs the cooperative principle or an intention-attribution module to hit production accuracy, so I want to say that layer is explainatorily idle — a story told after the fact, not a mechanism.

3. Claim B: I ran an internal analysis over roughly 40k labeled messages looking at "some but not all" style readings and indirect-request readings. Whether the enriched reading fires is predicted almost entirely by context features; adding a stage where a default/literal reading is computed first and then revised added nothing measurable. I want to use this as evidence against defaults and against any mandatory first pass at literal content.

4. Claim C: cancellability is a non-event for us. When a later clause walks it back ("just curious, not asking you to fix anything"), the ranking just moves. No special machinery, no re-derivation. So I want to say the cancellability test doesn't carve out a distinct theoretical kind, it's just monotone updating.

5. Claim D, the spicy one: the "what is said" vs "what is implicated" distinction doesn't survive contact with our data, because nothing in a working system ever builds the first term.

What I need is a Q&A prep sheet, not a defense of my view. Specifically:

- The eight to ten objections this room will actually raise, ordered by how much damage each does to my thesis (not by how easy they are to answer), and attributed to which kind of person raises it — the exhaustification person and the experimental pragmatics people will not attack the same soft spot, and the philosopher of language will go somewhere else entirely.
- Each objection phrased the way that person would phrase it, using the technical vocabulary they'd reach for. I need to be able to recognize it when I hear it, including when it comes wrapped in jargon I half-understand.
- For each: a reply I can deliver out loud in about two minutes with no slides, plus an honest note on where that reply is softest or where it quietly assumes something I haven't argued for.
- The two things I should concede in the first five minutes to buy goodwill, and the one reply that absolutely has to land for the talk to not be a trainwreck.
- The question I'm praying nobody asks.
- And separately: which parts of my setup an expert would call a confound in about ten seconds. I already suspect my labeling task measures what annotators will endorse, not what they computed — tell me the others.

One more thing, and it matters a lot to me: I'm going to get quoted by people who know this literature far better than I do. So for each objection, tell me which side of it is genuinely settled and which is a live fight among the experts, and don't dress up one camp's position as the consensus view. If the reply you're handing me is the standard/majority view, say so; if it's what one school says and other schools would reject on sight, say which school. I'd much rather walk in knowing which of my five claims is respectable-but-contested and which is just me being an engineer with a hammer.
