So I'm a third year in political philosophy and this term I somehow ended up running our department's grad student podcast — format is one outside speaker, no slides, forty minutes of unscripted Q&A with me moderating and someone editing out the ums afterwards. Next week's guest is a political philosopher with a book coming out that defends benefit conditionality: job search requirements, sanctions for non-compliance, the whole apparatus. She's not a strawman and she's a good talker, and my problem is that the audience has just done a week of luck egalitarianism and relational egalitarianism so most of them are already hostile, and two of them have separately told me they plan to ask the same question about sanctions pushing people into rough sleeping. I'd rather head that off with something better than let it turn into a pile-on that my supervisor then has to listen to.

The thing is her position is an unusual package and I want the questions built out of that package, not out of the standard critiques, which she obviously has answers to. Roughly, as I read the intro chapter: (i) she thinks the deserving/undeserving framing is a red herring — what deservingness is actually doing in welfare administration is screening under imperfect information about who's willing to contribute, and the moral desert talk is just an ex post gloss on the screen; (ii) the real justification for sanctions is expressive, i.e. what matters is the message a conditional scheme sends to net contributors, because that message is what sustains the solidarity and tax compliance the whole thing rests on; (iii) so the harshness objection supposedly misses, since a sanction isn't a punishment and isn't tracking anyone's blameworthiness — it's a costly signal, and the cost has to land on the claimant because a costless option wouldn't signal anything at all; (iv) she grants that sanctions can be counterproductive in practice but denies that's decisive, because the expressive requirement constrains the design of the scheme, not its outcomes.

Can you write me nine or ten questions for her, and for each one give me (a) the question as I'd actually say it out loud, short enough to be asked in about twenty seconds, (b) one or two sentences on the reply she's most likely to give, and (c) a follow-up keyed to that specific reply so she can't just restate (i)-(iv) at me? Nothing should ask her to explain or restate her own view, and no softballs like what got you interested in this — assume she and the room know the familiar objections (the imprudent-driver style cases, the point about means-testing forcing people into revelations they'd reasonably be ashamed of, the structural-injustice line) by name. The angles I most want hit:

- the open-endedness of costly signalling — if the cost has to be credible to the audience, who fixes the level, and hasn't she just handed the harshness objection back to herself wearing different clothes, since there's no principled upper bound on how much hardship counts as enough signal;
- whether screening and expressivism can really be pulled apart — contributors will read a sanction as a desert judgement, so the moral content she wants to bracket comes back in through the audience's uptake. I want her pushed on uptake specifically, not on intentions;
- the asymmetry with tax non-compliance, given she's making the message to contributors load-bearing;
- claimants with no market-valued capacity at all: what message does the scheme send about them, and does she need an independent needs-based principle for that group — and if she does, is conditionality carrying any justificatory weight, or is it just a tax on the marginal cases;
- whether a sanction that isn't a punishment is distinguishable from punishment in the sense that does any work, i.e. if the state knowingly imposes a serious foreseeable harm on someone for failing to do what it requires, what's actually left of her distinction;
- the self-defeat worry: she should be made to name which empirical claim in her argument is load-bearing and which is dispensable, and say what finding would make her drop the view — that's my closer.

Also — and this matters to me — include one or two questions where you think she genuinely has a good answer and the honest move is to concede it, and mark those, so the session doesn't read as an ambush. For each follow-up, tell me whether it's meant to press a real weakness in the package or just to stop her retreating into a definition, because those need different delivery.
