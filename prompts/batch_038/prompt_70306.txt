I am drafting the centrepiece example for a paper in non-ideal epistemology, and I need a thought experiment built to a tight specification rather than an illustration of a point I have already made.

The thesis the case must serve: the problem of logical omniscience is not a by-product of cognitive limits. In the familiar examples, an agent's failure to believe a consequence of their evidence is explained away by pointing at how long the derivation is, how unfamiliar the concepts are, or how little attention the agent has paid, and the formalism escapes unscathed. I want a case in which all of those excuses have been withdrawn in advance, so that the misdescription belongs to the representation of evidence itself and not to the agent's equipment.

Please construct the case under the following constraints.

1. One scenario, ordinary and realistic — a newsroom, a clinic, an engineering firm, a household — not a logic seminar, and no science-fictional apparatus.
2. The scenario itself should run to no more than about 250 words, and the proposition at issue should be statable in a single untechnical sentence.
3. The agent is not short of time, attention, or processing power, and the derivation is short enough to be written out and checked inside the scenario itself.
4. The agent possesses every concept required and is fluent in the relevant subject matter.
5. The entailment is a single step, one a reader can verify at a glance. No chains.
6. The agent's evidence is consistent, and the difficulty does not arise from inconsistency in what they have been given.
7. No lottery, no preface paradox, no self-locating or indexical twist, and no necessary a posteriori identity that the agent has not yet discovered.

After the scenario, do the following.

- State the agent's evidence in prose, state the consequence, and show in a line or two that it genuinely follows in one step.
- Give the verdict of the classical possible-worlds model of evidence alongside the intuitive verdict, and explain why the gap is not simply a matter of the agent's not having got round to it.
- Take four repair strategies in turn — admitting non-standard worlds at which a logical or mathematical consequence fails; restricting ascriptions by the concepts or propositions the agent is capable of entertaining; dividing the belief state into compartments that are not simultaneously available; and replacing sets of worlds with degrees of belief — and say for each what it makes of this case and what it costs, or predicts wrongly, elsewhere.
- Identify the single feature of the case doing the work, and say what happens when it is removed: does the case collapse back into an ordinary story about limited time or unfamiliar concepts?
- Give the strongest counterattack available to someone who insists that such models are legitimate idealisations, in the manner of frictionless planes, and say whether that reply reaches this case.

Two house rules. The verdicts must be generated by the features you describe, not stipulated: a case that merely asserts that the agent rationally withholds the consequence has failed. And please close with an honest note on which constraint proved hardest to satisfy, and whether you bent any of them. I will supply the references myself, so no bibliography is needed.
