Been arguing with my friend Dev for about four hours now, mostly over the kitchen table, and I've come away wanting to see the thing staged rather than argued. So: write me a short story about the cognitive penetration of perception — specifically about whether what you believe and want can actually get inside the seeing itself, or whether every apparent case is just memory, imagination, judgement or plain response bias happening a moment later and getting misremembered as seeing.

Some context on where the argument ended up, since I want the story to live in the weeds rather than at the level of "our brains are amazing":

Dev is a hardliner in the Firestone and Scholl direction. Her view is that basically every famous demonstration collapses once you look at the method — the hungry-man-sees-a-bigger-logo stuff, the moral-disgust-makes-grey-looker stuff, all of it is either demand characteristics, or the subject imagining rather than perceiving, or a shift in what they're willing to report. I kept reaching for the classics: the anomalous playing cards in the tachistoscope, where people call a red six of spades a six of hearts for ages before the world catches fire; the old colour-matching business with cardboard hearts and diamonds, where the same physical hue gets dialled redder if it's cut into a shape that means red; even memory-colour effects on banana-shaped discs. Dev's answer to all of them was the same and honestly it's a good one: knowledge-resistant illusions. The hollow-face mask doesn't flip even when you know perfectly well it's a mask, so knowing clearly isn't enough to touch the percept — which suggests the whole class of "penetration" effects is downstream.

My best counter, which she wouldn't give me, is the epistemic one: if seeing can be infiltrated by fear, prejudice and prior commitment — the Siegel line — then perception stops being a neutral court of appeal, and the whole reason we trust it is quietly circular. She said that's philosophy, not psychophysics, and that if my worry can't be tested it isn't a worry.

Here's what I'd like you to make out of that. A near-future setting where this has become practical rather than academic: some kind of perception audit that has to be passed before an eyewitness account is admissible, run by a technician whose job is to work out, case by case, whether a witness actually saw what they say they saw or merely ended up believing it. I want the audit to be a real instrument with real controls, and I want you to design it plausibly rather than hand-wave — something like: a forced-choice task with no colour names or object names allowed anywhere in the response, a version run under cognitive load so there's nothing left over for top-down inference, a version run after the subject has been told explicitly that the stimulus is deceptive, and a non-verbal action measure (pointing, reaching, a saccade, a match dialed in by hand) as a check against the verbal report. Make at least one scene turn on those controls disagreeing with each other. Make one scene a replication that fails.

And the twist I actually want, which came out of the argument: the auditor is himself being penetrated. Not by anything dramatic — by the expectation, drilled into him by the job, that witnesses are contaminated, so that he starts seeing contamination in clean accounts. Do it entirely through what he does and how he fills in a form and what he can't stop noticing, never through anyone saying it.
