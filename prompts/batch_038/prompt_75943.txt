My case, in full, so you can judge it. A guy in my conservation program states a principle as though it were a law — call it P: any way of behaving that lets a group of organisms persist longer will, given enough generations, end up more common than any way of behaving that doesn't. I want to break it. So: one valley, one species, sealed — no migration in or out, nobody ever innovates anything, and food is unlimited (stipulate that, it matters). Two variants. Slow lives twenty years and leaves six offspring over a lifetime. Fast lives two years and leaves three offspring over a lifetime — so per head per year Fast compounds at about 1.7 and Slow at about 1.1. Now run it. Read P literally, individual by individual: a Slow one lasts ten times as long as a Fast one, so Slow takes the valley. Read it the way people usually read "does better" — descendants left per lifetime — and Slow wins again, six to three. But actually compound the two populations forward with the food unlimited and the time unlimited and Fast swamps Slow: it's essentially the whole valley inside a few decades. Then start lifting stipulations one at a time. Cap the food and run to equilibrium and Slow holds the valley (Slow converts food better, stipulate that too). Keep the food unlimited but make the weather swing in fifteen-year droughts and Slow holds it again (Slow is still alive when the good years come back). Keep both stipulations and let time run out to infinity and Fast wins. So the same principle applied to the same valley gives you three answers, and which one you get depends entirely on which of my idealisations you remove first, and P itself never says which. My fear is that H grades this as Laplace's demon in a cardigan — that I've only shown what "persist longer" fails to mean — rather than as a result about organisms.
