For the nationalism week of my philosophy of history seminar I have paired Hobsbawm's introduction to *The Invention of Tradition* — a volume whose opening case study is Highland dress — with Anderson on imagined communities, and we are screening *Braveheart*. What I want is a reading that keeps the two frameworks apart rather than treating them as synonyms for the same debunking: Anderson's "imagined" is descriptive, a claim about any community larger than face-to-face acquaintance, whereas Hobsbawm's "invented" picks out ritualized practices that assert continuity with a past they substantially construct.

On that basis, tell me which features of the film are Hobsbawmian — I have in mind the tartans and clan insignia, the blue war paint, and the right of first night, which the film presents as an old prerogative rather than a fresh invention — and which are instead Andersonian, that is, a nationalism that only later became available as a modular form, poured backward into a thirteenth-century setting. Then tell me where the film resists both readings. My suspicion is that the sequence doing the most national work is one neither theory accounts for, and I would like you to identify it and explain why it escapes them; consider also whether a film watched and quoted by a present-day audience is itself a site of tradition-invention rather than a mere depiction of one.

Close with the objection my students will press: if *Braveheart* simply gets medieval Scotland wrong, what distinguishes that from inventing a tradition, and does the distinction carry any weight, or is one merely the other described less sympathetically?
