Ok so, two years into a small project — three of us, four years of money, two philosophers and a historian, no data people and basically no fieldwork budget. Subject: how moral norms that everyone once took for granted just... stop. The duelling / Atlantic slave trade / footbinding kind of case, not the slow-burn expanding-circle kind.

The area seems split between two explanations and I've gotten tired of the split. One says these practices were propped up by beliefs that were simply false — about who counts as fully human, about what the practice was even for — and the practice goes unstable the moment enough people say that out loud. Argument does the work; the revolution is a slow conversion. The other says no, the argument was available for centuries and nothing happened; what changed was the reputational payoff. The thing stopped being honorable and started being ridiculous, and that's what killed it. Honor, not reasons.

My own view — and I can't find it anywhere, so treat it as a hypothesis I'm bringing rather than a summary of the literature — is that both are true of different stretches of the same trajectory, and the real question isn't which explanation wins but what the transition between stretches looks like. Roughly: a norm held up by a false belief is vulnerable but looks stable; a norm held up by honor is stable but brittle; and the move from vulnerable to dead is not the same move as the move from honorable to absurd. I think the two camps argue past each other because they're describing different segments and each thinks it's describing the whole thing.

So: where should three people put four years? I want an ordered agenda with the reasoning attached, not a tour of the debate and definitely not a reading list. For whatever you'd put near the top, tell me what makes it upstream of the others rather than merely interesting; whether it's armchair work or genuinely needs cases, and if cases, what kind would discriminate rather than illustrate; what would count as progress on it as opposed to just having written something publishable about it; and what I'd see over the next couple of years — in our work or anyone else's — that should move it up or down the list.

Then tell me which of the obvious questions we should deliberately skip and why. I suspect at least one of them is a trap where settling it wouldn't change what anybody does. And push back on the two-segment picture wherever you think it's wrong, becuase if it's wrong the project is mis-shaped and I'd rather find that out now than in year three.
