I'm a program officer at a small private foundation that funds whole research programs, not projects — six to eight year commitments in one subfield, with a renewal decision at the end of each cycle. I make about fifteen of these calls a year and my recommendation goes to a five-person board that signs off. Our bylaws say funding continues unless there's a documented case for stopping, which is deliberate: the founders wanted the default to be patience.

The board has started complaining that our renewal memos are just citation counts, grant dollars leveraged, and how famous the PIs are, and honestly they're right. I want to rebuild the memo around something with actual conceptual content, and Lakatos's methodology of scientific research programmes is the candidate I keep coming back to — hard core versus protective belt, the negative and positive heuristics, progressive versus degenerating problemshifts, whether a programme is generating novel facts or just absorbing ones after the fact, his insistence that you can't rationally ditch a programme unless there's a rival on offer, the tenacity point about staying loyal, and the gap between appraising a programme and deciding what to do about it. The trouble is that all of that was written to describe the history of physics, not to be run by a generalist with a doctorate in something else, three hours per file, and a PI who gets to write a rebuttal.

So: write me the runbook. Questions in the order I should ask them, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, what evidence in the file discharges it, where the runbook terminates in a stop-recommending, and where it hands the decision to the board because what's left is a question of values rather than a question about the science. It has to survive translation — tell me which of Lakatos's distinctions you're keeping because the runbook breaks without them, which you're dropping as unusable at this level of access, and where the simplification will systematically misfire. I'd specifically like your handling of: (a) what makes a fact "novel" here, when everything in the file is described by people with a stake in it looking novel; (b) how to tell an auxiliary move that's ad hoc from one that's merely content-preserving, and whether you think there's more than one flavour of ad hocness worth tracking separately; (c) how long a young programme gets before "no results yet" counts against it; (d) what to do when the whole subfield looks degenerating and there is no rival to point to; (e) how to run the prediction test in fields that can't do experiments, where the closest thing to a prediction is a retrodiction of data nobody had gone and collected yet.

Then run it on three real files of ours:

1. Forty-year-old programme in a molecular subfield. Enormous literature, trained essentially everyone currently working in it, and its models fit a huge amount of data better than anything else available. But its last confirmed ahead-of-time prediction was about eighteen years ago. Everything since has been a sequence of auxiliary hypotheses, each one introduced after the anomaly it was designed to handle was already on the table, each individually plausible and locally well-motivated by the positive heuristic, none of them having produced a new successful prediction. The PIs are good people and if we stop, the subfield loses roughly half its capacity for a decade. For what it's worth I want this one to come out as continue.

2. Nine-year-old programme, four groups worldwide, one strange core commitment everyone else in the field treats as a non-starter. Two confirmed predictions that nobody expected and that were registered before the data came in, and one that failed. The incumbent framework in the field works fine, is cheap, and has no anomalies it can't handle — so on the rival question there's nothing to compare it to, it's the incumbent that would need a rival, not this.

3. A programme in a historical/observational science where nothing can be run in a lab, whose recent work has repeatedly specified in advance which archive series or which uncollected measurements it needed, gone and got them, and been right — but where critics say the "specification in advance" was written by people who already knew roughly what they'd find, and where the failed specifications are simply not written up.

Mark every step that's really a judgment call wearing a procedure's clothes, and finish with what the runbook can't do — including whether it can say anything at all about whether the questions a programme is asking are worth asking. No reading list or citations, I don't need them and I can't check them anyway.
