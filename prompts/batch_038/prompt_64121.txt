Every account of bounded rationality I teach runs into the same wall, and I want a diagnosis of the wall rather than another summary of it. Since Simon introduced satisficing, the orthodox response has been that it is no rival to maximizing at all: once search costs, time, and cognitive capacity are entered as constraints on the choice problem, an agent who stops at the first option clearing an aspiration level is simply optimizing subject to those constraints, and Stigler's search theory is offered as the proof of concept. The Simonian rejoinder is that this misses the point, because aspiration levels are not terms in a utility function to begin with — they are set by the agent's history and revised by rule of thumb in response to how often the search has been succeeding, so satisficing names a procedure rather than a constrained version of the substantive maximization it is said to reduce to. Decades on, neither side has budged and the two literatures barely engage.

My working hypothesis is that the dispute is not substantive at all but a disagreement about what a reduction must preserve: whether the explanandum is the choice the agent makes or the process that produces it, and whether re-describing a rule in optimizing vocabulary counts as explaining it. Test that hypothesis against what each side actually claims. Is the stall instead driven by a hidden empirical question — say, whether aspiration levels track the payoff structure of the environment tightly enough for the optimizing description to earn its keep — or by a hidden formal question about whether aspiration-based rules admit exact representation as solutions to some well-posed constrained problem? Then tell me what would count as progress given your diagnosis: a distinction, a representation theorem, a class of environments in which the two descriptions diverge in their predictions, or a reason to expect none.
