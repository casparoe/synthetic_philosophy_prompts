I am a junior, and I debate policy for my school. Last weekend the resolution ran roughly as follows: that the underrepresentation of women in engineering and physics is chiefly a matter of choice rather than of barriers. During the second constructive, the opposing team's speaker closed with a sentence that our entire side seemed unable to answer, and which our own coach repeated approvingly on the bus ride home: "the more equal the country, the wider the gap — so the gap can't be about equality." She had gestured at a figure showing that in countries usually ranked as more gender-equal, young women are, if anything, less likely to end up in the physical sciences and engineering, and she used that figure to cut off an argument I had made about adaptive preferences: the idea that what a person ends up wanting can itself be shaped by the options that were visibly open to her growing up. Her line made my argument sound not just wrong but confused, as though I had failed to notice that freedom of choice had already been granted by the premise. I have thought about it all week and I suspect the sentence is doing at least three different jobs at once, but I cannot yet say which, or which of those jobs it can actually perform.

What I want is a dissection rather than a summary of the literature on the subject. Please take that sentence apart and lay out, as a numbered menu I can bring to practice, the distinct claims it could be making. I would like the premise separated from the inference: on the premise side, what "the more equal the country" would have to mean for the comparison to work at all, given that countries are ranked on composite indices that measure many different things at once, and what "the gap" could be a gap in, since the article we were shown described it partly in terms of students' relative academic strengths — how they do in mathematics compared with how they do in reading — rather than only in terms of how much they say they like science. On the inference side, please separate the readings of "can't be about equality": that the differences are genetic, that they are hormonal, that they are universal across societies, that they are not produced by the norms of the society in question, that they are immune to policy, and that they merely reflect what individuals chose once nothing was in the way. Those are obviously not the same claim, and I do not think most of them follow from the others.

For each reading, tell me whether it is a platitude that nobody disputes, a substantive claim that could be defended, or simply false — and then, which is the part I care about most, tell me whether that reading would have supported what she was using it for, namely ending an argument about whether the gap reflects barriers. I would especially like to know what the sentence is worth if preferences are formed under conditions that are themselves unequal, because that seems to me to be the case in which free choice and social shaping stop being opposites; and I would like to know whether it even matters, for the question of whether the resulting distribution is unjust, whether the gap turns out to be natural. Please argue from the logic of the claim rather than sending me to studies I cannot get past the paywall, and if some reading of her sentence is the one a reasonable person most likely meant, say so explicitly, so that I know what I am actually arguing against next time.
