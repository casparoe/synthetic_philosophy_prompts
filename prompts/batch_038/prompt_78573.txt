You are a professional epistemologist who works on the relationship between knowledge and practical reasoning, and who is used to advising non-specialists. Please think the case through carefully before answering.

I run a small print shop. Twice this year I have had to promise a customer a delivery date on the strength of nothing but my memory of what our paper supplier said about lead times in a conversation weeks earlier.

In March the memory was "twelve days." Getting it wrong would have cost us about a hundred dollars. I quoted the date without checking, and told the customer flatly that I knew it. It came in at twelve days.

In September the memory was again "twelve days," from a conversation that felt every bit as clear. But this was a wedding invitation run on a fixed date: getting it wrong would have cost us the account and something like fifteen thousand dollars. So I emailed the supplier first. They confirmed twelve days. The check cost an hour and changed nothing.

Two things bother me. First, my business partner says I am simply inconsistent: the evidence was identical both times, so either I knew it in March or I did not know it in September either, and the email was anxiety dressed up as diligence. Second, he has support from my own head — within a week of the September job I was quoting dates off memory again with complete confidence, exactly as before. My certainty seems to track what is riding on the answer rather than how good my memory is, and then to bounce back afterwards.

Was there a point in September at which I stopped knowing something I had known in March? Was checking the rational thing to do, or a waste? And what policy should I adopt going forward — verify always, verify never, or keep verifying only when the stakes are high? Give me your verdict on each, not a survey of positions.
