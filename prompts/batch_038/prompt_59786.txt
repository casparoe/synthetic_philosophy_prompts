I lead model documentation and eval writeups at a mid-size AI lab — about thirty engineers, no philosophers, and a general counsel who has opinions about every noun we put in a public document. Most of my job is deciding what language is allowed to appear where. And we are permanently stuck on one question: when are we allowed to say a model believes something, wants something, is trying to do something, is confused, or is being deceptive, and when do we have to say something deflationary instead (the model produces completions of type X, its outputs under these conditions pattern-match to...).

I know the answer people reach for is Dennett — the intentional stance is just a predictive strategy, you attribute beliefs and desires wherever doing that makes the system reliably predictable, and it's a matter of degree rather than an on/off fact about what's inside. That's genuinely useful, but it doesn't survive contact with my workflow. Three of the questions I actually have to answer are whether the stance is licensed for a system whose outputs shift with the sampling temperature and with how the prompt happens to be phrased; whether it's licensed for a system with no memory between sessions, so there's nothing that persists to be the thing that holds the belief; and whether it's licensed when the prediction it buys me only holds inside our own scaffolding and falls apart the first time a real user talks to the thing.

So write me a decision procedure my team can actually run. Something like a sequence of tests, in order, each with a stated pass condition, terminating in one of three verdicts: use the intentional idiom freely, use it with a mandatory gloss, or use only the deflationary idiom. It has to be runnable by someone who has never read any of this literature, in about fifteen minutes per document, and it has to say explicitly for each step whether that step is a fact about the model somebody could go and measure or a judgment call a named human has to own. Also mark where the procedure is going to give the wrong answer and we should override it, and where two of the tests will fight each other.

Two constraints I can't do anything about, so build them in rather than telling me to fix them:

- Our incident-report template is frozen by legal and one of the required fields literally says What did the model think was happening. I cannot get it renamed. The procedure needs a rule for what goes in that field on the occasions when think-talk isn't licensed at all.
- Public documents get read by two audiences at once that pull in opposite directions: safety reviewers and journalists, who will treat any intentional verb as us claiming the model has an inner life; and engineers, who will treat any deflationary hedge as license to wave off a real behavioral regularity as just next-token prediction. The verdict sometimes has to differ by audience, and I need the procedure to keep the internal and external versions from contradicting each other.

Then run it on three cases:

1. A support-ticket triage model that, across many rephrasings, consistently gives shorter and visibly lower-effort answers to tickets it appears to classify as coming from an unserious customer. My team wants to write that the model believes those customers are lying.
2. A single line in an internal eval log where a researcher wrote that the model knows it's being tested, on the grounds that it behaved differently in a scaffold containing an obvious evaluation marker than in one without.
3. A public model card line describing the model as always trying to do the helpful thing and avoid the harmful one. I currently allow this sentence and I suspect your procedure is going to tell me to kill it. If so, say why, and give me the replacement wording that doesn't read like we're hiding something.

Last thing: tell me what the procedure can't settle. I specifically want to know where the honest answer is that this was never a question about the model but about which risks we're willing to name in writing, and what the handoff to a person looks like at those points.
