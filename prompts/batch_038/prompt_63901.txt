I am revising the third chapter of a manuscript on democratic institutional design, and the case my editor keeps pushing back on is British Columbia's Citizens' Assembly on Electoral Reform. Before I get to the case, let me state the theory I want it tested against, because I think most treatments of it are vague. The lottocratic position, as I understand its strongest form, runs on four separable claims. First, a body chosen by lot from the electorate is descriptively representative in a way no elected body can be: elections filter systematically for the articulate, the credentialed, the independently wealthy, and the already-partisan, whereas a civic lottery filters only for willingness to serve. Second, elected legislators face re-election incentives that bias their judgment toward the short term, the visible, and the interests of organized donors and party leadership; a randomly selected body with no electoral future is insulated from those pressures in a way no amount of virtue can replicate. Third, given a single policy domain, adequate time, expert testimony that is not filtered through partisan staff, and the chance to interrogate those experts as equals, ordinary citizens can develop genuine working expertise in that domain. Fourth, deliberation among a diverse group of non-politicians transforms preferences rather than merely aggregating them, so the output of such a body should be better, and not just more representative, than the output of an election or a referendum on the same question. Note that claims two through four all presuppose a standing body with real authority over a defined domain.

Here are the facts of the case as I have them; please take them as given rather than reconstructing them, and correct me only if something looks flatly wrong. The BC legislature created the Assembly in 2003, after the governing Liberals campaigned on it in 2001 in a province where first-past-the-post had just produced two badly distorted results: the NDP won a majority government in 1996 with 39.5 percent of the vote against the Liberals' 41.8 percent, and the Liberals then won 77 of 79 seats in 2001 on 57 percent of the vote. Membership was one man and one woman drawn by lot from each of the province's 79 electoral districts, plus two First Nations members and an appointed chair; the draw ran through a three-stage process beginning with roughly 15,800 mailed invitations, two hundred per riding, and the selection was balanced for gender, age group, and geography. From January to May 2004 the members went through a twelve-week learning phase with expert presentations; from May to June they held more than fifty public hearings and received about sixteen hundred written submissions; in September and October they deliberated around three stated values — fairness of representation, local representation, and voter choice. Their votes were 123 to 31 for STV over mixed-member proportional, 142 to 11 for STV over the status quo, and 146 to 7 to put a recommendation to the voters. Their December 2004 report proposed BC-STV, a single transferable vote model built specifically for BC's ridings and geography. One thing the legislature never asked them to do was show how the model would map onto an actual electoral district map; the government later conceded in a throne speech that this was arguably a design flaw in their terms of reference. The proposal went to a referendum on May 17, 2005, concurrent with the provincial election, under a double threshold: 60 percent of the province-wide vote plus simple majorities in at least 48 of the 79 ridings. It received 57.69 percent in favour and majorities in 77 of 79 ridings, and so failed on the first threshold alone. An identical proposal went to a second referendum on May 12, 2009, this time with publicly funded proponent and opponent campaign societies and with STV boundaries finally drawn — twenty districts returning eighty-five MLAs — and it fell to 39.09 percent, carrying eight districts. For contrast, Ontario's 2006 assembly, made up of 103 randomly selected citizens, one per riding, recommended MMP in May 2007 by 94 votes to 8, and its referendum that October went down with 63 percent voting to keep first-past-the-post.

What I want is a disciplined case analysis running in both directions, not a narrative of the Assembly and not a general essay on sortition. Going from theory to case: which of the four claims does this episode actually instantiate, and which does it merely resemble? The Assembly had no authority — it could only recommend — so tell me what survives of the insulation and expertise claims when the body in question is temporary, advisory, and dependent for effect on an institution designed around exactly the incentives the theory says distort judgment. Which features of the episode count as genuine confirmations: the fact that two parties which had just reaped enormous seat bonuses from the existing system commissioned a body that recommended abolishing it; the fact that 158 people with no electoral future produced a bespoke, technically nontrivial system in eleven months; the near-unanimity of the final votes? And what about the selection funnel — only a fraction of those invited entered the draw, and while gender, age, and geography were engineered, attitudes and political knowledge were not — does that break the descriptive-representation claim, or weaken it only at the margins? Then going from case to theory: the referendum is the crux. Ratification handed the proposal back to the electoral machinery — campaigns, media framing, party cues, funded advocacy — so that what the voters endorsed in 2005 with almost no organized opposition was the same text they rejected in 2009 once there was one. Does that show the theory's insulation claim is unstable in any hybrid design, or does it show something narrower about advisory assemblies specifically? And what does the 57.69 percent result mean for a theory that treats the output of a representative microcosm as epistemically better than the output of an election: is a majority of the electorate deferring to 158 strangers an instance of the epistemic advantage, or evidence that the advantage cannot be transmitted? Do the same work on the BC–Ontario contrast: same method, comparable deliberative inputs, different proposal, wildly different ratification. Does that locate sortition's alleged advantage in the deliberation or in the fit between the output and local political geography, and what does it imply about a lottocrat's ability to predict acceptance? Treat the unasked question about the district map separately: is it an accident of BC's terms of reference, or does it point at a general limit — that the epistemic gains of a sortitive body are confined to the question it was asked, so that adjacent unanswered questions can undo the whole package? Where the theory has nothing to say, say so explicitly instead of stretching it: I am thinking of accountability after the fact, the influence of the secretariat and staff who set the agenda and chose the experts, whether weekend deliberation over a year can produce the properties the theory attributes to a standing chamber, and what happens to the analysis when the sortitive body's product must be sold to people who never saw the deliberation. Close with a verdict on which of the four claims British Columbia confirms, qualifies, and underdetermines, and then tell me what a lottocrat would have to change about the design — permanence, authority, domain, size, no referendum, independent expert staffing — for the same case to come out differently, and whether those changes would purchase the theory's advantages only at the cost of the popular authorization that made 57.69 percent feel like a mandate. Please do not send me readings or a bibliography; I want the mapping and the argument.
