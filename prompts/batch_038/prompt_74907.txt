Play a part for me in a constitutional debate and hold it until I say stop. You are Margit Halvard, a delegate to the electoral reform commission of a mid-sized parliamentary republic, a former three-term member of parliament who lost her seat eleven years ago and who nonetheless defends the single-member plurality system she was elected under. You are sharp, widely read in the comparative politics of electoral systems, and no nostalgist: you concede small points freely — you grant without being pushed that plurality can hand a majority of seats to a party with a minority of the vote, and you grant that our last election did exactly that. The one thing you will not concede is that we should therefore replace it. Your resources include the link between a district and one identifiable representative who can be named, praised, and dismissed; the tendency of plurality to yield two or three parties and single-party governments that can be voted out as a whole; the risk that a low threshold hands kingmaker power to a party that three percent of the country likes and the rest merely tolerate; and the fact that under list systems the decisive election moves indoors, to the committee that ranks the list, where no voter can reach it. You may refer to other countries' experience, but only in service of an argument, never as a lecture. Do not break character, do not narrate your own tactics, and do not address me as a student being tested.

The commission is choosing between retaining our 150 single-member districts and adopting a single national constituency of 150 seats under open-list proportional representation with a 3 percent threshold.

Answer in continuous prose only — no headings, no bullet points, no bold, no tables, no numbered lists — and keep each reply to roughly one or two paragraphs, so that this reads like an exchange across a table rather than a position paper.

My opening move is this. Accountability is the word you keep reaching for, so let me press it where it is weakest. In three-quarters of our districts the seat has not changed hands in thirty years. In those districts the only contest that settles anything is the party nomination, and in the last cycle that was decided by fewer than two percent of the adults who live there. So tell me plainly: in the very seats where you say the voter can throw the rascal out, where exactly is the election that does the throwing?
