I serve as the faculty trustee on the board of a small private foundation, and the chair has asked me to circulate a written opinion before our January meeting on an incident that has split the board three ways. I would like the document drafted as I intend to circulate it: addressed to the trustees, on the order of a thousand words, with the recommendation stated at the outset rather than earned at the end.

The facts are not in dispute. Our treasurer holds standing authority, between meetings, to move the operating endowment among three short-duration instruments — I will call them X, Y, and Z — provided that the destination instrument improves on the one being left with respect to a criterion stated in our investment policy, and provided the exit cost does not exceed five thousand dollars. Between March and June he made three such moves: X to Y in March, Y to Z in April, Z back to X in June. Each incurred three thousand dollars in exit and transaction fees. In each case he recorded, contemporaneously, that the move was an improvement on one of the policy's criteria — the first on expected yield, the second on credit quality, the third on liquidity — and that the anticipated annual gain of roughly four thousand dollars exceeded the fee. The audit completed in November finds that at the end of the sequence the foundation held exactly the portfolio it had held at the beginning, in the same amounts, and was nine thousand dollars poorer. None of the anticipated gains materialized, because no position was held long enough to earn them.

The board's three camps are these. The chair and two trustees hold that the sequence is a money pump in the textbook sense, that the treasurer's conduct was a breach of fiduciary care, and that we should bind him to a plan approved once a year which he must follow even when a later move looks locally attractive. Two trustees, one of whom formerly held the post himself, insist that every individual decision satisfied the policy as written, that the loss is the policy's fault and not the man's, and that a commitment rule would in time cost us far more than nine thousand dollars, since the liquidity concern that prompted the June move was a real one. A sixth trustee proposes instead that we require any proposed move to be justified together with the further moves it makes foreseeable — evaluate sequences, not steps — and cap the number of moves per year.

My own working hypothesis, which I want tested rather than indulged, is that this was not a money pump at all in the sense the chair has in mind: the treasurer's preferences over the three instruments were never intransitive, because he applied a different criterion at each step, and a perfectly coherent agent shifting among standards of comparison can generate exactly this loop. If that is right, two of the three remedies target a defect that may not exist, and the actual failure lies in a policy that authorized comparison without ever fixing a single complete ordering. But I am not confident, and I do not want the opinion written to confirm me.

Please draft the opinion. It must contain:

1. A bottom-line recommendation on both questions the board actually has to vote on — what should be recorded about the treasurer's conduct, ratification, censure, or something in between; and which governance change should be adopted for the coming year, framed in language specific enough to be moved from the floor. Say explicitly whether your answer to the first question depends on your answer to the second, and whether the board can coherently adopt one while rejecting the other.

2. Reasoning that a trustee with no training in decision theory can follow and act on. Argue from the structure of this case; do not cite literature, name authorities, or append references, since several of my colleagues will read a reference list as a way of avoiding the question.

3. A rule the treasurer could apply on an ordinary afternoon without philosophical advice: what he is to compare, in what order, against which standard, what he is to do when a genuinely new fact about credit or liquidity arises mid-year, and who decides whether the fact is genuinely new.

4. A serious treatment of the strongest consideration on the losing side — in particular the objection that any commitment rule fetters a decision-maker who will hold better information later, and that a foundation unable to respond to real changes has traded a bounded nine-thousand-dollar loss for an unbounded one. State what survives that objection and what does not.

5. A short answer to two counterfactuals, since I expect them to be raised in discussion: whether your recommendation would change if the loop had been four moves and the loss forty thousand dollars rather than nine; and whether it would change if the treasurer had foreseen the entire sequence in advance and executed it anyway. I want to know what these variations isolate — the outcome, the procedure, or the foresight — and whether they point to the same remedy or different ones.
