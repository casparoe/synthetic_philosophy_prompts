I'm a software engineer with no philosophy training, so please don't assume any vocabulary I haven't used myself.

I got pulled into the digger-wasp case that Dennett made famous. The wasp paralyzes a cricket and drags it to the mouth of its burrow, goes inside to check the nest, comes back out, and drags the cricket in. If you move the cricket a few inches while she's inside, she locates it, drags it back to the mouth of the burrow — and then goes in to check the nest again. The loop repeats, by some accounts indefinitely. The moral people draw is that her routine is keyed to a trigger rather than to the situation, and that is what rigidity is.

I run on-call at my company, and a colleague argues that our incident runbooks make engineers sphexish: the page fires, the engineer executes step three, even when the metric that fired the alert stopped being a symptom of anything real an hour ago. I wanted to defend the runbooks by showing that his objection proves too much. Here is the argument I wrote. Every step seemed plainly true to me as I wrote it down, and the conclusion is one I cannot accept, because it dissolves the wasp along with everything else — and the wasp is the whole reason the concept interested me.

(1) A behavior is rigid — sphexish — if and only if it is produced by a routine that fires when its trigger condition is met, whether or not the trigger still has anything to do with the goal the routine was there to serve.
(2) To say an agent acted freely is to say its action was responsive to reasons: it would have done otherwise had the reasons been different.
(3) Being responsive to reasons is not a different kind of thing from having routines. It is having routines keyed to evidence about reasons rather than to incidental features, plus routines that revise the first set.
(4) But every routine at every level has a trigger condition, and given a complete specification of the agent's internal state and its inputs, what it does next is determined. Revising routines are routines too, with triggers of their own.
(5) So for any finite agent and any behavior of it, there is some perturbation of its inputs to which it responds in a way that has nothing to do with its goals: find the trigger that the reason-revising machinery is keyed to and pull it, and the machinery runs without tracking anything.
(6) Hence every finite agent is sphexish with respect to some inputs, including the inputs that look most like deliberation.
(7) If rigidity is universal in that sense, then whether an agent looks rigid is a fact about which inputs you happened to test it with, not a fact about the agent — the wasp and the on-call engineer differ only in how quickly someone found the loop.
(8) Therefore no finite agent acts freely, and the rigid/flexible distinction is empty: there are only inputs nobody has found yet.

If I have to give something up, I would much rather give up (7) and (8) than (4) and (2). (4) looks to me like a plain fact about how any implementation works, and (2) is the only reason I care about any of this.
