Six weeks ago I inherited the uncertainty-assessment function at a small public body that advises a regulator on the safety of an ageing industrial fleet. Every deliverable that leaves my office takes the same shape: a single probability, quoted to two decimals, attached to a one-line justification, and consumed downstream by a committee whose minutes record it as a finding of fact. My predecessor's template has never been questioned. Two of my senior analysts have been arguing about it for the better part of a year, and the argument has now reached the point where each doubts that the other's output is an assessment at all rather than a performance.

One is an uncompromising precise Bayesian. A doxastic state is a single finitely additive probability function; coherence is all the representation theorems establish and all that needs establishing; a credal set is either notationally idle or a decision rule wearing the costume of a belief state; and what the other side calls rational ambiguity aversion is more honestly modelled as a maxmin-style preference over acts, which is a fact about the chooser's tastes, not about the evidence. The other defends imprecise credence in the Knight–Keynes line. Evidence has weight as well as direction; Ellsberg-type choices are rational rather than merely stubborn; lower and upper previsions over a set of probability functions capture something a point estimate must either distort or invent; dilation is a theorem of the framework and not a slip, and its appearing to go wrong is a symptom of reading the output as a forecast rather than as a record of what the evidence licenses; and a precise prior launders ignorance into authority.

The dispute became my problem because of a live case. We must estimate the probability that a particular failure mode occurs somewhere in the fleet within ten years, and the estimate governs a retrofit decision of some size. Our evidence is: eleven near-misses across forty years of fleet operation, none of which developed into the failure; a mechanistic model of the failure pathway that is well validated at high stress and extrapolated well below anything observed; and two expert elicitations that disagree materially, one of them from an engineer previously retained by the manufacturer. The committee wants one number by the end of the quarter.

I want the document the two of them should have produced a year ago. Please draft it, in their joint name, with the following parts.

1. A numbered agreed-claims section of at least eight items. These must be non-vacuous: not shared platitudes about Bayes' theorem, but claims a neutral observer would be surprised to learn both sides endorse — for instance about what the formal machinery permits, about which phenomena each side concedes are genuine rather than artefacts, about what a point estimate does once it enters an institutional record, and about the limit behaviour of an interval as evidence accumulates. For each claim, one line saying why each side accepts it.

2. A disagreements table with four columns: the disagreement, stated neutrally; the precise Bayesian's formulation of his own position, in words he would sign without amendment; the imprecise-credence advocate's formulation of hers, likewise; and a judgement of whether the disagreement is substantive, partly verbal, or unresolved. Cover at minimum: whether a body of evidence can license more than one doxastic state; whether ambiguity aversion is a rational preference or an anomaly to be explained away; whether dilation is a bug or a feature; whether weight of evidence is a genuine second dimension of belief or a redescription of imprecision; whether the choice of decision rule under imprecision is itself arbitrary, and if so whether that arbitrariness is worse or better than the arbitrariness in the choice of prior; and the dynamic-coherence charge that ambiguity-averse rules can be exploited by a sequence of individually acceptable choices.

3. The cruxes: the sub-questions such that settling one would actually move at least one of them. For each, state the case, argument, or experiment both would agree bears on it, and record each side's prediction in advance. I suspect one crux is whether there exists any decision problem in which the two frameworks issue in different recommendations and a neutral party can say which recommendation is better; I suspect another is whether the divergence between them survives at all when the evidence base is large and homogeneous, or whether the imprecise position is really about a limit case that never obtains in my office. Test both suspicions rather than adopting them.

4. A worked application to the failure-mode case above. Both parties run the same evidence: what each framework outputs, what each recommends under its own preferred decision rule, and exactly where the outputs diverge. If they converge on the recommendation while diverging on the representation, say so plainly — that would be a significant result and I want it stated as one.

5. A short closing section on falsifiers: what each would count as evidence or argument against their own view, and whether there is anything both agree could actually move them.

No citations, no bibliography, no reading lists, and no named papers — I want formulations, not scholarship. Do not caricature either side, and where the two formulations of the same disagreement turn out to be mutually inconsistent, flag the inconsistency instead of smoothing it over. Finally, if part of this argument is not about the failure mode at all but about what a number owed to a committee is for, say which part, separate that institutional question from the question of which representation is more rational, and state what each representation licenses a committee to do that the other does not.
