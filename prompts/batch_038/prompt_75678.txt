Our working group on synthetic media is drafting a response to a colleague's revival of Dennett's 2023 Atlantic case against chatbots, which runs almost entirely on the analogy with counterfeit currency: because counterfeiting has been treated as a grave crime for as long as money has existed, on the grounds that it corrodes the trust a society runs on, systems that pass for people ought to be banned both in their making and in their passing along, and marked in machine-readable ways of the kind that make photocopiers refuse to reproduce banknotes. One analogy is being made to carry three claims at once — that the injury is to a shared trust infrastructure, that the danger lies in undetectability, and that a marking regime is therefore both necessary and workable. Give me the correspondence table first (currency, issuer, counterfeiter, unwitting passer-on, watermark, detection regime), then sort the disanalogies into those that actually bear on the proposed prohibition and those that are merely picturesque — including whether something capable of performing real work is still a counterfeit in the operative sense, whether a harm to trust dilutes the way a currency does, and whether marking transfers at all when the medium is generated rather than a fixed design. Close by saying whether the ban survives the audit, and if it does not, which analogy would carry what the argument actually needs.
