I am assembling a unit on the ethics of immigration for a political theory course and want to pair John Steinbeck's The Grapes of Wrath with the philosophical literature. Before I commit to that pairing, I need a serious reading of the novel against the standard positions on admission, membership, and redistribution, and I need it to tell me where the pairing earns its place and where it embarrasses me.

The positions I have in mind are these: Walzer's claim in Spheres of Justice that membership is the good a political community distributes, so that control over admission follows from a community's right to shape itself; Carens's Rawlsian counter, that reasoning from behind a veil of ignorance in which one does not know one's citizenship yields a presumption of free movement strong enough to defeat that right; Miller's insistence that the solidarity required for redistribution depends on boundaries a national community controls; Shachar's treatment of birthright citizenship as inherited property, with the accident of birth doing all the distributive work; and, cutting across them, the widely held view that a generous welfare state and open admission are in tension, because benefits attach to members and members are defined by the border. Arendt's account of the person who has lost a polity, and whom nobody is consequently bound to shelter, is the background worry.

The complication is that the Joads are not aliens. Every mechanism of exclusion in the novel operates inside a single citizenship. The family is evicted by a bank that cannot be located in any person, since the tractor operator is a neighbor's son who has his orders and his three dollars a day; they cross a state line where they are inspected by officers, and Ma rides through that inspection beside her mother-in-law's corpse rather than reveal the death and risk being turned around; they are moved on from a squatter camp by a deputy who has no warrant and no charge; they reach a federal camp that is clean, self-governing, and closed to local police, but that has no work and no food to give its residents; they take a job at a peach orchard without knowing they are strikebreakers, and the organizer they find there is killed by men with a pick handle; and the book ends in a flooded barn, where a daughter who has just lost her own child feeds a starving stranger with her body. Two years after publication, the Supreme Court struck down a California statute criminalizing the bringing of a non-resident indigent person into the state, resting on the commerce clause while several justices preferred to ground free interstate movement in national citizenship.

Against that record, I would like answers to four questions. First: does the novel show that the literature has been aiming at the wrong boundary, so that the operative variable is not who may enter but whether the claims of the poor travel with them, or does it simply dramatize a case that the right-to-exclude debate was never meant to handle? Second: Steinbeck offers a sociology of solidarity, tracing the shift from the first person singular to the first person plural as something produced nightly in roadside camps by people who met that morning. Does that constitute an answer to Walzer and Miller, who think shared history and bounded community are prerequisites for mutual obligation, or does the novel also supply its own counter-evidence, in the camp's expulsion rules, the informant, the unwitting scabs, and the vigilantes? Third: what does the federal camp prove about the welfare-state tradeoff, given that it is the one institution in the book that treats the migrants as rights-holders and still cannot feed them? Fourth: where must an honest critic refuse the reading? I have in mind the novel's silence about the Mexican, Filipino, and Asian workers whose labor preceded the migrants' in the same fields, and the ending, which offers private sustenance rather than any institutional remedy. Tell me whether those features make the book unusable for a cosmopolitan argument, or usable only at a cost I should state to my students in advance.

I want the scenes assigned to the claims they carry, a clear verdict on the tradeoff question, and an account of what the novel contributes that argument alone cannot. Please do not supply a bibliography or page references, and do not feel obliged to quote Steinbeck; paraphrase freely and say plainly where the text will not bear the weight I am putting on it.
