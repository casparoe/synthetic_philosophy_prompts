--- DRAFT ---

Everyone in this literature says roughly the same thing about amnesty: it trades justice for peace, it is presumptively illegitimate, and the interesting question is whether the peace it buys is worth the justice it sells. I think that framing is aimed at the wrong feature. What does the moral work isn't whether amnesty is granted but what kind of institutional transaction granting it is.

Call the first kind transactional amnesty. It is individualized: a named person applies in respect of specified acts. It is conditional: the concession is purchased with disclosure, and it can be refused. It is adjudicated: a body hears the applicant, hears victims, and gives reasons. And it leaves a public record behind. The South African Amnesty Committee is the paradigm — under the 1995 Act amnesty ran act by act and applicant by applicant, only for acts connected to a political objective, only on full disclosure of what the applicant knew, decided by a committee made up of judges and lawyers. Over seven thousand applications came in before the deadline and only a small fraction succeeded.

Call the second kind administrative amnesty. It is blanket and automatic: it applies to a class defined by a status or a date, it demands nothing from the beneficiary, nobody decides anything, and no record is produced. Argentina's Punto Final (1986) and Obediencia Debida (1987) are the paradigm — first a statute-of-limitations deadline, then a legal presumption that subordinates had acted under due obedience, both wiped out by Congress in 2003 and declared unconstitutional by the Supreme Court in 2005.

These two are usually lumped together as "impunity." Structurally they are close to opposites. In the first, the state extracts the maximum available truth as the price of the concession and reserves the right not to pay. In the second, the state extracts nothing and just switches the law off.

Rwanda is the case that made me write this. Gacaca granted no amnesty at all, yet it had exactly the purchase structure: confess, plead guilty, get a cut sentence, with the back half converted into community service. So what I thought I was picking out is not amnesty versus no amnesty. It is whether a concession is bought individually with disclosure or handed out wholesale for political convenience.

Now the part my partner thinks is a stretch, and which is the actual reason I'm writing the paper. If the criterion is an individual concession purchased with disclosure, then the DOJ's corporate leniency policy for cartels lands on the transactional side. The first firm to self-report gets non-prosecution, conditional on full disclosure and continued cooperation, decided case by case by prosecutors who can and do refuse; there is even a marker system so firms queue to be first in line. That is structurally close to the Amnesty Committee and very far from Obediencia Debida.

Which leaves me two options, and I want to defend the first. Either (a) transactional/administrative is a real axis but "amnesty" is the wrong name for it — what is being described is a general institution, the purchased concession, of which truth commissions are one species and cartel leniency another, and the reason transitional justice treats amnesty as uniquely tragic is that it has mostly only ever met the worst version of it; or (b) the axis is fake, and I'm pattern-matching on confession-leads-to-immunity while missing that the two settings differ in every respect that actually matters morally — victims present and speaking versus harm so diffuse that nobody represents it, political reconciliation versus deterrence, men with guns versus firms with lawyers.

--- END DRAFT ---
