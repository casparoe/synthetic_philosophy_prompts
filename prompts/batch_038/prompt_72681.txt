My friend and I spent most of last night arguing about the chapter of *Gödel, Escher, Bach* on the location of meaning, and toward the end he wrote out what he takes Hofstadter's position to be. His summary, more or less as he put it:

> "Hofstadter says every message has three parts. The outer message is the physical carrier — the groove in the record, the ink on the page, the sequence of bases in DNA. The inner message is what that carrier means, and the frame message is the sender's intention, the part that tells you how to take what you are being given. The upshot is that meaning cannot reside in the marks themselves, because marks are inert until something reads them; the inner message is produced by the decoder, so what a text means is settled by whoever decodes it. That is also why translation is possible: since the outer message is arbitrary anyway, you can substitute entirely new marks and carry over the same inner message. And the whole scheme dissolves the old anxiety about misreading — if meaning is constituted by the decoder, there is no such thing as getting a text wrong, only getting it differently. The record player illustration proves the point: a record played on a damaged machine comes out as noise, and there is no fact of the matter about which machine gets it right."

I found this persuasive at the time, and I still half believe it, largely because I am already inclined to think that meaning is mostly a matter of who is doing the interpreting. Two things nag at me, though. First, I have a dim memory that the frame-message idea is not Hofstadter's own but borrowed from an anthropologist who was interested in how animals signal that a nip is play-fighting rather than a bite, which would make "the sender's intention" the wrong sort of thing entirely. Second, his last sentence seems to prove too much: if no machine gets it right, I cannot see how he is also entitled to say that a substituted set of marks carries over the *same* inner message, since "the same" appears to require a standard that his own account has just abolished.

Please audit the summary for accuracy. Where a claim is wrong, give the correct version and explain why the difference matters, rather than merely flagging it; I am especially interested in whether the three-part architecture is a fair description of how the frame message relates to the other two. Then tell me whether his conclusions — that meaning is fixed by the decoder, that translation follows from the arbitrariness of the outer message, and that misreading is impossible — survive once the summary is corrected, or whether each depends on the parts that do not. Finally, since both of my nagging doubts may themselves be misrememberings, say plainly whether my recollection about the anthropologist is right, and whether my objection to the last sentence identifies a real tension in his account or only my failure to follow it.
