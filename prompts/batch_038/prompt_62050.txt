I am a junior in high school, and for an independent study in philosophy of mind my teacher has asked each of us to write down our position in advance and then have it taken apart by the rest of the group. My topic is Dennett's four kinds of minds — the Darwinian, Skinnerian, Popperian, and Gregorian creatures. Below is what I actually hold. I would like an audit of it, not an exposition: please do not explain the taxonomy back to me, and please do not tell me which of these I should most want to keep.

1. The tiers are distinguished by what does the selecting. In a Darwinian creature, behavior is shaped only by genetic selection across generations. In a Skinnerian creature, the organism's own behavior is shaped within its lifetime by the consequences that behavior produces. In a Popperian creature, internal models of the world are built and revised, so that candidates for action can be eliminated before they are ever tried out. In a Gregorian creature, the models it thinks with are largely imported from the accumulated external artifacts of its culture — language, writing, notation, and other tools of thought designed by other people.

2. The tiers are strictly cumulative. Nothing can be Gregorian unless it is also Popperian, and nothing Popperian unless it is also Skinnerian, because each higher capacity is built on the machinery of the rung below it. A rung cannot be skipped.

3. Placement on the ladder is a matter of mechanism, not of cleverness or of how impressive the behavior looks from outside. A creature that solves novel problems by fixed means is not promoted on account of the results.

4. The line between the bottom rung and the second is whether an individual organism's behavior can be modified by what happens to it during its own life. If it can be, the organism is at least Skinnerian; if it cannot, it is Darwinian only.

5. Nothing that lacks a nervous system is capable of learning in the sense that matters here. Sponges, plants, fungi, single-celled organisms, and slime molds are Darwinian creatures and nothing more.

6. A present-generation large language model is a Gregorian creature. Almost everything it has to think with came to it from outside, from the artifacts human beings left lying around, and its characteristic activity is operating over those artifacts.

7. The same model is not a Skinnerian creature. During its training the target is the next word of a text that somebody else composed; no consequence of the model's own output ever comes back around to reshape that output. Its behavior is not selected by what it does.

8. The same abstract logic of variation and retention is at work on every rung, only applied to a different substrate each time — genes, then emitted behavior, then internal models, then cultural artifacts.

9. Which rung a creature occupies does not mean that everything it does happens at that rung. Much of my own behavior — reaching for my phone when it buzzes, flinching — appears to be run by lower-tier machinery I simply inherited, and I think it would be a mistake to call those acts Gregorian.

Please go through these and tell me which pairs genuinely pull against one another and which only look as though they do, ranking the real tensions by severity rather than listing them all as equally damaging. For each real tension, say which commitment has to be abandoned or narrowed, what the narrowed version would have to say instead, and what I lose by narrowing it that way. Where a tension could be dissolved by a fact about how these systems are actually built rather than by any philosophical revision, tell me the fact and say whether it really does the work. Finish by identifying the single revision that would clean up the most, and what it would cost me elsewhere in the list.
