I teach a third-year interdisciplinary course on the limits of prediction, run jointly between our philosophy department and the applied mathematics group, and every year the same complaint comes back from the mathematicians: the handout I issue on unpredictability treats chaos as a catch-all, so that anything hard to forecast gets filed under it regardless of what is actually going wrong. This year I want to replace that handout with a proper decision tree, and I should like your help designing it. The tree should take a student who is facing some particular system they cannot forecast, and lead them in no more than six questions to a diagnosis of why the forecast fails. The students have done ordinary differential equations and numerical analysis but no philosophy of science, so the vocabulary has to be theirs rather than ours.

The constraint that matters most is this. Every branch node must be an operation or a scaling question that a modeller could actually carry out, or at least imagine carrying out, with the governing equations and a computer in front of them: what happens to the useful prediction horizon if you halve the error in your initial data; if you double the computing budget; if you improve the model by adding a term you had been neglecting; if you shield the system from its environment; if you publish the forecast to the people whose behaviour it concerns; if you ask the machine to output its own later states. No node may contain the words determinism, deterministic, chaos, chaotic, indeterminism, random, emergent, or epistemic/ontic, and — this is deliberate — the first split must not be the familiar distinction between unpredictability in principle and unpredictability in practice, because working out which of those you are in is precisely what the whole tree is supposed to accomplish. That distinction, if it survives the exercise at all, should emerge as a pattern across the leaves rather than as a question anyone must answer first.

Each leaf should carry the name of the diagnosis it corresponds to if such a name exists, or be marked unnamed if nobody defends it under that description, together with one sentence on its most awkward commitment: the thing a student who lands there will most want to deny. Then place each of the following systems on the tree, stating which leaf it reaches and by which path — rainfall at a particular postcode tomorrow afternoon; the position of a double pendulum's bob after thirty seconds; the arrangement of the planets a hundred million years hence; fully developed turbulence in a wind tunnel; a billiard ball's position after fifty collisions with the cushions; which nucleus in a lump of radioactive material decays first; the closing level of a national stock index in three months; the state of one particular cell, very late in the run, of a one-dimensional cellular automaton with a simple deterministic local rule; an algorithmic trading desk whose own forecasts move the prices it forecasts; and a machine asked to print a complete description of its own state a week from now. Finally, tell me honestly where the instrument breaks down: which pairs of leaves it cannot really separate, which of my ten systems are being forced into a leaf that does not suit them, and which combinations of answers along the branches are logically available but empty — for each empty combination saying whether nothing could occupy it, whether nothing attractive occupies it, or whether nobody has simply thought to. My colleague wants a leaf reading "we have not tried hard enough yet"; I have told her that is an absence of diagnosis rather than a diagnosis, but if you think the tree can be made to justify it as a genuine leaf, say so and defend the claim.
