I'm supervising a dissertation on whether religion makes people more moral, and I've hit a methodological wrinkle I can't find a label for. The standard evidence is self-report: ask people whether they volunteered, gave money, helped a stranger last week. Religious people come out higher. But the disposition to answer a question like that affirmatively — to have examined your own conduct recently, to hold it up as evidence, to think of yourself as the sort of person who helps — is itself something religious practice trains very hard. Confession, testimony, the examen, the whole bussiness of narrating your life as a moral life. So the instrument may be picking up fluency in self-description rather than conduct, and picking it up best in exactly the group that's been coached in it.

Crucially, nobody's lying, so it isn't social desirability bias in the faking sense. And it isn't self-selection either — I'm not claiming decent people disproportionately join churches. I know about common method variance and demand characteristics; tell me why neither covers this, and whether anything does. If there's no established term, coin one and give me its boundaries. The edge I most need pinned down: at what point does a self-conception of that kind stop being an artefact of the questionnaire and start being a genuine cause of better behaviour? I want the concept sharp enough to survive a viva.
