THE QUESTION WE WERE GIVEN:
In "On the Uses and Disadvantages of History for Life" (1874, second of the Untimely Meditations, sometimes translated as "On the Use and Abuse of History for Life"), Nietzsche distinguishes three ways of relating to the past. Identify all three, explain what each one is useful for and what goes wrong when it is practiced in excess, and say which of the three Nietzsche himself favors and why.

THE ANSWER I HAVE TO GRADE (spelling is theirs, they typed it on a phone, please ignore that, only grade the content):

"Nietzsche says history belongs to a living person in three different ways and each one produces a different kind of history. A person who acts and strives needs monumental history — they look back at the great people and the great things that got done to get proof that the great was possible once and so could be possible again, and that gives them models to act on instead of being paralized by how much has already happened. A person who preserves and reveres needs antiquarian history, which is the pious attachment to where you came from, your town and your ancestors and your tradition, and its useful because it makes you feel at home in the world and gives you roots and a reason to keep things going instead of starting from nothing. A person who suffers and needs to be freed needs critical history, where you drag the past in front of a tribunal and interrogate it and finally condemn it, because you cant live otherwise.

Each one goes bad in excess. Monumental history goes bad because the past gets beautified and the real causes and effects get smoothed over until its more myth than history, and also because mediocre people use the great men as a weapon — like we already have our geniuses so we dont need any new ones — which is why an age that only knows monumental history ends up feeling like a bunch of latecomers. Antiquarian history goes bad when the piety dries up and everything starts looking equally old and equally worth saving, so the person just hoards curiosities, cant tell what matters anymore, and turns hostile to anything new only because its new. Critical history is dangerous too because we are all products of earlier generations, so when you condemn the past your partly condemning yourself, and it takes real strength to do it without it just turning into bitterness. He calls the ability to take in the past and still stay yourself plastic power, and thats what decides how much history you can stand.

For the last part, critical history is the one hes really pushing, because its the rigorous one — you dont take the past's word for itself, you test it — so its the mode that turns history into something scientific and objective instead of something that serves your feelings, and the other two are basically traps for people who arent disciplined enough. And the fix for the whole problem is moderation: none of the three is bad in itself, they only get bad in excess, so a healthy person or culture keeps a balanced portion of all three and doesnt drown in any one. History is like food, the right amount keeps you alive and too much kills you."
