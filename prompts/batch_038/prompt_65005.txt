Something has been nagging me about the Nash vs. Kalai–Smorodinsky thing and I want a diagnosis, not a textbook recap of what the two solutions are.

Here's how I see the state of play, tell me if I'm getting it wrong. Everyone agrees on the math: Nash's solution is the unique one satisfying Pareto efficiency, symmetry, invariance to affine transformations, and independence of irrelevant alternatives; KS keeps the first three and swaps IIA for monotonicity, and you read it off geometrically as the furthest point where the line from the disagreement point to the ideal point meets the frontier. Everyone also agrees you can't have both axioms, that IIA sounds compelling in the abstract — why should an option neither bargainer would ever accept change what they settle on? — and perverse in application, since it licenses cases where making strictly more outcomes available leaves one player worse off; and that monotonicity sounds compelling in application but rests on a reference point, the ideal payoff, that nobody can actually get. And its been like this for fifty years. Both get taught, both get used, the lab work never cleanly crowned either one as far as I can remember, and there's no sign of convergence.

My hypothesis, which may be forcing it: the real fault line isn't about which axiom is more intuitive. It's about where bargaining power is supposed to live. Nash says all the power facts are packed into one number per player — the disagreement payoff — plus the local shape of the frontier near the answer; KS says power also depends on the best each player could reach anywhere in the set, which is why enlarging the menu has to help someone. On a straight-line frontier the two land on the same point, equal gains over d, and they only part company once the frontier bends. If my hypothesis is right, the dispute is really about whether your "outside options" means your fallback or your aspiration, and no amount of axiom-shopping settles that, because both readings are internally coherent.

So: what kind of disagreement is this, actually? Substantive and normative, verbal (do the two camps even mean the same thing by a "fair" split), a disagreement about what the enterprise is for — predicting what people do, versus recommending what an arbitrator should do, versus describing what some bargaining procedure would converge to in the limit — or a sign that "which solution is correct" is just ill-posed? Argue from what each side is actually committed to when it defends its axiom, and be concrete about the geometry: I suspect frontier curvature is where the philosophical disagreement bites, and I'd like that either cashed out or refuted.

Then tell me what would count as progress. I don't mean a new characterization theroem, those just add a fifth candidate to the shelf. I mean something that would make a reasonable person say the question got resolved: an experiment that could in principle discriminate between them rather than both; a strategic foundation whose limit behavior differs from the other's; a demonstration that one axiom is redundant with the rest under some extra assumption people would actually accept; a change of question; or an argument that the two are answering different questions and should stop competing. Rank those candidates for me and be honest about whether you expect any of them to actually happen.

For context on why I care: I'm trying to work out what a mediator or an arbitrator should commit to in advance, and whether "maximize the product of gains" versus "aim for proportional gains" is a genuine choice with consequences or basically a notational preference that only shows up in weird cases.
