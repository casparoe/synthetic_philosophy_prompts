I've been chewing on an old story about digger wasps: the wasp paralyzes a cricket, hauls it to the mouth of its burrow, leaves it on the threshold, goes inside to make sure the nest is clear, comes back out, and drags it in. If you nudge the cricket a few inches away while she's underground, she finds it, hauls it back to the threshold — and then goes inside to check the nest again instead of dragging it in. She can repeat that indefinitely. She isn't blind to what happened; she just can't leave the sequence. I've read that someone coined a word for this kind of stuckness and then turned it on human beings, and the standard moral is that we're the ones who notice and change course.

That moral is what bothers me. Noticing and changing course can also be a routine, just a longer one with more moves in it. I'd like a short story, 1,200 to 1,800 words, that takes that worry seriously rather than answering it. A premise you can use or replace: a woman is paid by a firm to ambush employees mid-task with small absurd malfunctions — a form that doesn't exist, a machine that won't behave — and to score them on whether they abandon their script. One man scores perfectly every time, and she slowly realizes he passes because he keeps a ranked list of fallback moves that he deploys in the same order every time.

Ground rules: no robots, no artificial intelligence, no talking wasp — keep the characters human so the story can't dodge the question by handing it to a machine. Never name the concept, let no one deliver a speech about free will, and let the wasp survive at most as an oblique image. Show the man's fallbacks concretely enough that a reader can spot the pattern before she does. The ending should leave it genuinely unclear whether anyone in the story ever steps outside a loop, and should quietly make the reader suspect the same of themselves — while still working as a plain workplace story for someone who has never heard of digger wasps.
