I am drafting the framework section of a paper on whether punishment may be borne by someone other than the offender, and before I engage with any existing account I want to fix the specification. Please write out the desiderata: the conditions an adequate theory of the permissibility of penal substitution must satisfy, each accompanied by the case or consideration that motivates it, and a judgment as to whether it is non-negotiable or a preference belonging to a particular camp.

Start from the puzzle as I want it treated. Lewis's short paper on this topic makes the observation that our rejection of penal substitution is not uniform: where the sentence is a fine, we think it entirely acceptable for a devoted friend to pay it, even though the fine is the punishment and the offender thereby escapes it; where the sentence is imprisonment or death, the identical proposal strikes us as monstrous. Since some fines are more burdensome than some short custodial sentences, and carry comparable public opprobrium, the divergence in our reactions does not track severity in any simple way. It follows that a theory which concludes flatly that substitution is impermissible, or flatly that it is permissible, has not addressed the phenomenon. I take it as a candidate first requirement that the theory explain the shape of the pattern rather than explain it away as confusion on our part; you should say whether you agree.

Two further features of the territory should be accommodated, and I would like the list built so that they are not afterthoughts. First, something appears to survive a successful substitution: after a friend has paid the fine, we still describe the offender as having been punished, and we expect him to feel a distinctive discomfort at having been rescued that he would not feel had he paid it himself. A theory must say what remains with the offender, and whether that remainder is itself a punishment — for if it is, the substitution was only ever partial and the question shifts accordingly. Second, the asymmetry may not be about the burden at all but about who initiates: we tolerate a volunteer stepping into an offender's place, while we find it intolerable for an authority to assign an innocent person to that place, even where the resulting distribution of suffering is identical. Whether initiation or burden-type does the explanatory work is, I suspect, the crux, and the desiderata should force an account to choose.

Here is a provisional list of my own, which you should assess, refine, and augment rather than accept: that the theory explain why substitutability seems to vary with the kind of burden imposed and not only its magnitude; that it respect the intuition that suffering borne by someone who deserves nothing cannot communicate condemnation of the offender, or else explain what it communicates and to whom; that it keep the volunteer's consent, the offender's acceptance, the victim's waiver, and the state's authorization distinct rather than pooling them into a single permission; that it say where discharging an obligation ends and undergoing punishment begins; and that it not license the wealthy to purchase immunity. Some of these may be prejudices of a camp rather than genuine requirements — I expect the fourth is — and I want that diagnosed rather than repeated.

Then: identify any subset of the desiderata you believe to be jointly unsatisfiable, and argue for the incompatibility rather than assert it; state which single requirement eliminates the largest number of otherwise attractive views; and, briefly, indicate how three broad strategies fare against the finished list — treating only the material burden as transferable, treating the substituted suffering as itself expressive, and reconceiving punishment so that the identity of the sufferer never mattered.

No bibliography, literature review, or quotation is wanted; I will supply references. The deliverable is the list and the argument for its shape.
