Our working group has been asked to produce a standard for deciding whether a class of engineered agents — long-running software systems with persistent goals, self-models, and no bodies — has emotions, and we are stuck. Every candidate theory settles the question by fiat: appraisal and functional-component accounts hand emotions to anything with the right control structure, while feeling and embodiment accounts withhold them for lacking something that looks question-begging. I want the specification before the argument. Write the desiderata an account of what emotions are must meet in order to be usable for adjudication of that sort: the phenomena it must accommodate; the hard cases it must get right or explain away — fear that survives the abandonment of the judgment of danger, arousal from one source experienced as attraction to another, the emotion reports of people with high spinal lesions, objectless moods, grief laced with relief, being moved by what one knows is fiction, the emotions of infants and nonhuman animals who have none of the relevant concepts; the distinctions it may not collapse, among them emotion against mood against trait, occurrent against standing, having against displaying, and aptness against mere cause; the empirical and normative constraints it must respect; and, specific to our task, what could serve as evidence for or against an ascription at all. For each item give the case or consideration that makes it a requirement rather than a taste, and mark which are genuine and which belong to one camp's program. Then say which single desideratum does the most excluding of candidates, and whether any subset is jointly unsatisfiable — if the list is itself a result, that is what I most want to see. Two or three sentences at the end on how the feeling, judgment, perception-of-value, and constructionist families fare against it.
