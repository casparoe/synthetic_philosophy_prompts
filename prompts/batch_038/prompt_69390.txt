I am in the third week of a moral psychology course, and we have been working through moral foundations theory in detail — including the questionnaire itself rather than only the textbook summaries. I would like you to treat the instrument and its standard interpretation as a single practice and tell me what theory of morality it presupposes. Three features have been troubling me, and I suspect they are entangled.

First, the questionnaire asks respondents two kinds of question: how relevant various considerations are to their moral judgments — whether someone suffered, whether someone was denied a right or treated unfairly, whether someone showed a lack of respect, whether someone did something disgusting — and whether they agree with a set of general moral statements. Both require a person to step back and articulate the grounds of a disapproval. The same research program, however, argues from dumbfounding vignettes involving incest or the eating of a dog killed by a car that moral judgment is produced by fast intuition, with verbal reasoning largely an after-the-fact justification. What has to be true about the relation between intuition and self-report for a reflective relevance rating to count as a measurement of an intuitive module? Is there a coherent account on which the measurement strategy and the theory that motivates it stand together, or does the first quietly contradict the second?

Second, the criteria offered for admitting something to the list of foundations are of this sort: an immediate emotional response, presence across many cultures, a plausible biological predisposition, and a plausible adaptive function. Nothing in that list concerns harm or fairness. Does it therefore already settle the dispute with critics who hold that authority and purity violations are social conventions that some cultures have moralized rather than moral concerns in themselves? And is the taxonomy exposed to refutation in that direction, given that it has only ever been expanded — an original hierarchy ethic split into loyalty and authority, a liberty foundation added later — and never reduced?

Third, the foundations are said to be universal, yet results are reported as continuous individual differences, and the familiar conclusion is that conservatives draw on all five while liberals draw chiefly on two, glossed as a difference in the breadth of a person's moral sensibility rather than as a disagreement about what morality consists in. That gloss seems to require that a liberal who scores low on sanctity nevertheless possesses the sanctity foundation and merely neglects it — a claim about a latent module, not about a person's values. What rival view does the gloss rule out? And what does the slide from adaptive function to genuine moral standing presuppose about the connection between the two, when the evidence offered for the group-oriented foundations is that they helped groups cohere?

Please reconstruct the implicit theory as a whole; separate what the design strictly presupposes from what merely sits comfortably with it; mark the places where the practice is philosophically underdetermined, such that several rival theories would license the same items and the same scoring; and then say concretely what would have to change — the items, the scoring, or the conclusions drawn from the left-right asymmetry — if that implicit theory were replaced by a harm-based account on which the remaining foundations are derivative. Finally, since the question my course keeps circling is whether this psychology can tell us anything about which side is right: does the instrument's own design settle that question in advance, and if so, in which direction?
