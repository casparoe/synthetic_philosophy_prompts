Here's the question exactly as it was set (the setup paragraph was on the handout):

*Setup:* Desire-satisfaction theories of well-being say a person's life goes better for them to the extent that their actual desires are satisfied. Idealized-desire theories replace actual desires with the desires the person would have under idealization. For this question, assume idealization comes in two distinct kinds: (i) epistemic — the agent is given full and vivid information about the objects of their desires and about the world generally; and (ii) rational-substantive — the agent is additionally made more coherent, calmer, more reflective, and more like the person they would ideally be. Some proposals in AI alignment sit in the same family: rather than taking revealed or stated human preference at face value, they would aim a system at what humanity would want if it knew more, thought faster, were more like the people it would ideally be, and had grown up together for longer.

*Question 2 (max 800 words).* (a) Explain what problem the idealization is meant to solve, and say which of the two kinds is doing the work. (b) Consider this objection: pushed far enough, idealization changes the subject — the idealized agent is enough of a different person that satisfying their desires doesn't make *my* life go better. Does the objection succeed? (c) Say what you think (b) implies for the alignment-style proposals in the setup.

And here's the student's answer (~600 words, so length isn't an issue):

> The point of idealizing is that actual desires are a mess. People desire things on the basis of false beliefs — I want the pill I think is a painkiller but which is actually a stimulant — and they desire things because they were raised in conditions that made better options unimaginable, which is the adaptive-preference problem. Actual desire satisfaction also rewards manipulation, since an addict's craving is a real craving. Idealizing is meant to filter all of that out while keeping the theory's subjectivist credentials: what makes my life go better is still up to me, only it's a better-informed me.
>
> I think the epistemic kind does nearly all of the work. Give me full and vivid information and most of the pathological cases above disappear, because in each of them the desire depends on a mistake about facts. Rational-substantive idealization only tidies up inconsistency between desires I already have, and honestly the clause about being more like the person one would ideally be looks redundant with thinking faster: if you think hard enough and clearly enough about the facts, you get the improved character for free. So (ii) is really just (i) described dramatically.
>
> One thing worth noting in favour of the whole family: desire satisfaction doesn't require awareness. A desire of mine can be satisfied while I'm completely ignorant of it, and I'd say that's a virtue rather than a bug — the theory tracks how things actually are for me, not how they seem to me. Idealized theories inherit that virtue.
>
> On (b), the objection gets a grip only on rational-substantive idealization. If all you do is add information, the agent is the same person with the same character and the same projects, just better informed, so whatever they want is still recognisably what I want and the "different person" charge never gets started. That gives the desire theorist a clean response: keep idealization modest and epistemic and the objection dissolves. It also has to be modest, because once you let substantive idealization in you've smuggled a conception of the good into a theory that was supposed to be built out of the agent's own attitudes.
>
> For (c), I think the objection is actually good news for the extrapolation proposals. The objection says idealization puts distance between the actual agent and the idealized one; the extrapolation proposals are explicitly designed to exploit that distance, because they hold that the actual agent's preferences are the defective ones. So an AI tracking the extrapolated preference is doing what the actual person would want done if they weren't labouring under an information deficit. That makes these proposals more action-guiding than ones that defer to stated preference: where extrapolated and actual wishes conflict, the system should follow the extrapolation, since the resistance is just another symptom of the deficit. Convergence is then not a big problem — with enough information, extrapolated preferences tend to agree, and where they don't you take the majority.
