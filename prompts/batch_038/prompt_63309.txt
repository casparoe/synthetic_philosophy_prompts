**The question** (from the handout; the sheet splits it 40 marks for part a, 60 for part b, total 100):

(a) Explain Robbins's objection to interpersonal comparisons of utility and why he thinks they have no place in positive economics.
(b) Harsanyi claimed that his aggregation theorem answers that objection and vindicates classical utilitarianism. Set out the theorem's assumptions and conclusion, and assess whether it really does answer Robbins.

**The student's answer:**

(a) Robbins's objection is methodological rather than moral. Economics on his picture is a positive science about the allocation of scarce means among competing ends, and its data are the choices people actually make. Cardinal utility is not among those data: I can observe that someone takes A rather than B, but I cannot observe any quantity of satisfaction attaching to either. Within a single person this is not fatal, because the ordinalist reconstruction shows that everything the theory needs can be stated in terms of a ranking, and, once uncertainty enters, in terms of choices among lotteries. The trouble starts across persons. To say that a policy which helps Anna and harms Ben raises total utility, I must place Anna's and Ben's utilities on one scale — at minimum a shared zero and a shared unit. Robbins's point is that no behavioural evidence could settle this, since each person's utility function is recoverable only from that person's own choices, and those choices fix the function only up to a positive affine transformation. So a claim about whose gain is bigger has smuggled in an ethical judgement about how much each person's satisfaction counts. Hence such comparisons are normative rather than scientific, and hence the New Welfare Economics tried to get along without them — Pareto, and the compensation tests, which were meant to license policy conclusions from ordinal information alone.

(b) Harsanyi's aggregation theorem is normally offered as the reply. Setup: a finite set of individuals and a set of prospects, i.e. lotteries over outcomes. Assume (i) each individual's preference over prospects satisfies the von Neumann–Morgenstern axioms and is therefore represented by an expected utility function Ui; (ii) the social preference over the same prospects also satisfies those axioms, so it too is represented by an expected utility function; (iii) weak Pareto — if every individual is indifferent between two prospects, so is the social ranking. The conclusion is that the social utility function is a positive affine transformation of a weighted sum Σ wi Ui with non-negative weights. So the social ranking of prospects can be written in the utilitarian form, up to the weights.

The standard reading of this as an answer to Robbins is that Harsanyi is not claiming to have found behavioural evidence for interpersonal comparisons. He is showing that if you accept assumptions that look modest, you are committed to a social ranking that can be written as a sum, and a sum only makes sense if the Ui share a unit and a zero. Comparability is therefore not an optional extra hypothesis: it is built into the structure of the problem as soon as you grant that the social ranking is itself an expected-utility ranking. That is why the theorem is said to show that Robbins's neutrality is unattainable — you cannot have a Pareto-respecting expected-utility social ranking without already having made the comparisons he wanted to ban.

Note that the invariance result does the work here. Because the conclusion is unchanged when any Ui is replaced by aiUi + bi for ai > 0, nothing in the theorem depends on where each person's zero sits or how their unit is chosen. The comparisons the theorem licenses are thus not the fragile, ethically loaded ones Robbins objected to; they come for free, as a consequence of the axioms rather than as an input to them.

Equal weights need the second, impartial-observer argument. Consider an observer who has an equal chance of being any one of the n individuals, with that individual's preferences, and who is otherwise indifferent among these identities. Such an observer's own expected utility is the average of the individual utility functions, and so, by (i)–(iii), is the social utility function. Classical utilitarianism follows rather than merely a weighted cousin of it. Worth stressing that the equality is not an independent stipulation: it is forced by weak Pareto together with the symmetry of the observer's identification with each person, since any unequal weighting would rank some pair of prospects differently even where every individual is indifferent, contradicting (iii).

Sen and Weymark object that the theorem is about preferences and not about well-being: the social utility function in the conclusion represents a social ranking over prospects, and no axiom links the individual Ui to how well those people's lives are going. On the face of it this is a labelling worry — Harsanyi is entitled to read the Ui as measures of well-being once he has the axioms, and the substantive content is carried by (iii). In any case the objection cannot get off the ground, because the axioms already treat the Ui as interpersonally meaningful, which is precisely what the theorem was supposed to establish.

Overall: the theorem does answer Robbins, though not in the way its admirers claim. It does not refute the claim that interpersonal comparisons involve value judgements — assumption (ii), that there is a coherent social ranking obeying the expected utility axioms, is itself a substantial normative postulate, and the equal-weight result adds another. What it shows is that those judgements cannot be kept out of welfare economics at all. Robbins's programme is not neutral; it is only quieter about its commitments.
