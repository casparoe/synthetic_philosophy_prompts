I cover philosophy of science, cognition, and religion for the ideas desk of a general-interest magazine, and I am commissioning an original piece of short fiction to run opposite a feature I have written on Luis de Molina's doctrine of middle knowledge. The feature sets out the standard machinery: the three logical tiers of divine cognition (what God knows as necessary and merely possible; what any free creature would freely do in any fully specified circumstance; what God knows of the actual world only consequent upon his decree to create it), the asymmetry between strongly actualizing a state of affairs and only weakly actualizing an outcome by arranging circumstances and accepting whatever free agents then do, Plantinga's thought that some agents are such that no assignment of circumstances yields a life containing no wrong act, and the grounding objection pressed by Adams, van Inwagen, and Lewis, namely that a counterfactual of creaturely freedom has no truthmaker and so is not the sort of thing that can be known.

The fiction has to do the work those ideas are doing, without borrowing a word of the vocabulary. Please write it. Take the commission seriously as fiction first: I would rather publish a good story with the philosophy slightly soft than a competent dramatization of a technical literature.

Adopt the combined role of a practising literary short-story writer and a careful analytic philosopher of religion, and think the structure through before you draft. First, in no more than two hundred words, sketch two or three candidate narrative vehicles and commit to one, giving a sentence on why you discarded the others. Then write the story. Do not append an author's note, a glossary, or any explanation of what the story means.

Here is the conceit I want you to build on, which you may modify in detail but not in its two constitutive rules. An institution — a lender's recovery office, a parole forecasting unit, an underwriter, a ministry's placements directorate; your choice, and it should be banal rather than grand — owns an instrument the staff call the Register. For any named person and any circumstance specified completely enough to leave nothing out (the wording of a letter, who is waiting in the corridor, the weather, the hour), the Register reports what that person would choose if placed in exactly that circumstance and left free to do otherwise. The two rules:

1. An entry is not produced by the choice it reports. It is there before anyone is placed in the circumstance, it is there whether or not the circumstance is ever supplied, and nothing the institution does can revise it. The institution can read an entry without ever testing it.
2. Knowing an entry confers no power over the person. The institution cannot make anyone choose anything. It can only decide which circumstance to supply, and then accept the consequence. Everything the institution has ever achieved, it has achieved by placement alone.

The story must honour both rules simultaneously and must not smuggle in a resolution that violates either — no revelation that the Register is really predicting, no reveal that it is really commanding, no collapse into determinism and no collapse into chance. The emotional engine I want is the one the doctrine actually has: perfect knowledge of what anyone would do buys you no leverage at all, only the ability to choose your circumstances well and to know in advance which of your hopes are unavailable.

Four things must be in the story, in whatever order serves.

An officer has spent years assembling a sequence of placements for one particular person, aiming at one outcome the institution urgently needs — and the stakes must be concrete and small enough to feel real, a signature, a returned ledger, a withdrawal of a claim, not the fate of the world. The Register shows that person declining under every circumstance the institution is in a position to supply. The officer must keep working anyway. The story may not resolve by the person changing, and may not resolve by the officer discovering a circumstance nobody had considered; if you can find a third ending that is neither a defeat nor a triumph, take it.

A schism on the staff over what makes an entry true, conducted as an office dispute with consequences for budget and for careers rather than as a seminar. One faction holds that entries are grounded in the sort of person someone is, and can therefore be extrapolated; the other holds that entries are simply true, owe nothing to character, and so license no extrapolation whatever — and if the second faction is right, the institution's forecasts have been lucky rather than knowledgeable, which changes what the officers are entitled to do with them. Let this quarrel be over something mundane, and let it cost somebody.

One scene in which a true entry is disclosed to the person it concerns. Either the disclosure voids the entry, because the circumstance it was indexed to did not include having been shown it, or the disclosure changes nothing at all. Choose one. Whichever you choose, the choice must damage somebody in the room.

The reader must be able to tell that the institution is not omnipotent and never was, without anyone saying so.

Hard constraints. Between 1,200 and 1,500 words, close third person on the officer, with a title. No character may use the words God, free will, counterfactual, omniscience, determinism, fate, or prophecy; no philosopher's name may appear; no character may explain the Register to a colleague who already understands it. At most one flashback. Prose should carry the exposition through procedure, paperwork, and withheld speech rather than through discussion. End inside the consequence — not on a moral, not on a summarising image, not on the officer's realisation. For tone, think of the institutional fictions of Kafka and Lem and the quiet conceptual cruelty of Borges, rather than of parable.

Our contents page requires a one-line entry for every commissioned piece, so end your reply with a final line beginning Logline: followed by a single sentence of no more than twenty words that states the situation without giving away the ending.
