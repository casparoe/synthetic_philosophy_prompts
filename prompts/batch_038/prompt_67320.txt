So I've been running an informal reading group for a few years — mostly ex-LW/SSC types, plus two people who actually work in clinical ethics — and lately we've been reading the advance-directive literature and the alignment literature side by side, because as far as I can tell they're the same problem wearing different clothes. Dworkin's critical-interests move is basically a designer pre-committing against a successor system whose values are going to drift; Dresser's reply is basically "the successor's values are the only ones that are real now"; neither side has read the other's corpus. For our next session I want a diagnostic case rather than an intuition pump, and I'd like you to build it to spec.

Here's the spec. One paragraph, ~200 words, realistic clinical setting, no sci-fi hardware — no uploads, no neural implants, no magic competence-detector. The directive-writer's *understanding* of what dementia is like can't be questioned (hospice nurse, neuropsychologist, whatever you like), she wrote the directive anticipating precisely the stage she's now in, and the document explicitly forecloses the "but she seems happy now" objection: she anticipated that her future self would be contented and said that contentment is not a reason to keep her going. Paraphrase the directive, don't put words in quotation marks. The present self has advanced dementia — contented, no pain, no distress. But, and this is the load-bearing detail, one specific psychological thread survived the damage: she retains a vivid, organizing memory of *writing* the directive and a strong emotional attachment to the physical document, keeps it with her, is calmed by it, can repeat chunks of it. Crucially that attachment is de re and not de dicto — if you actually put the question to her ("should we do what this says, now?") she says no, she wants to go on. So carrying out the directive requires deceiving her, and the deception isnt gratuitous, it's the only feasible route, since asking openly fails.

What I want out of it is four verdicts, two of them backwards from how they're usually deployed: (a) the Dworkin-style critical-interests view says carry it out — fine, expected; (b) a Parfit-style psychological-continuity view *also* says carry it out, which is the reverse of how continuity arguments normally get used in the dementia literature, where the decay of connections is the standard reason to discount the directive; (c) Dresser's current-interests view says don't; (d) any consent-or-respect-for-persons view says don't. For each one give me the reasoning the view itself supplies, not just the label. Then the honest engineering report: name the single feature that makes (b) come out that way — my guess is that it's the *content* of the surviving connection rather than its mere existence, but you tell me — and show me what happens to (b) if you delete that feature and give her generic dementia with no directive-specific memory. Then tell me how the Dresser side attacks the case, and whether I've cheated: a scenario that stipulates its verdicts instead of generating them from the theories is worthless to me, so if you think (b) can't be produced by a continuity view without smuggling in something Dworkinian, say that plainly rather than fudging. Close with a short section in the alignment vocabulary — earlier self as designer, the directive as a shutdown mechanism deliberately built to be non-corrigible, the surviving attachment to the document as a value-lock that outlived the drift — and be specific about where the analogy breaks (the present self isn't really an agent with goals, the shutdown isn't reversible, there's no corrigibility-preserving alternative that gets the designer what she wanted). Two logistics things: American English please, and no bibliography or citations of any kind, I want the whole thing to stand on its own for someone with no access to a library.
