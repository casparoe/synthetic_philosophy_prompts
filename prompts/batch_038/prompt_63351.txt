I spent forty-one years in a biophysics laboratory, mostly on the kinetics of ion channels, and since retiring I have taken up philosophy with the habits of that trade still on me: I want a quantity, an account of the assumptions the quantity rests upon, and a statement of which assumption, if wrong, would take the quantity down with it. What follows is a thought experiment of my own construction, and I should like a number in return. Suppose a procedure that replaces the neurons of a subject's cerebral cortex with synthetic modules, each reproducing its native counterpart's input–output function exactly, including its responses to neuromodulators and its contribution to the local field, so that the whole system remains functionally isomorphic to itself at every instant. The schedule runs at one hundredth of one percent of the population per day, which — I have checked the arithmetic — completes the exchange in roughly twenty-seven years; the subject stays awake and conversant throughout, with a continuous visuomotor tracking task and a sampled verbal report running alongside. Two variants matter to me. In the first, the same modules are installed in a single eight-hour session. In the second, the slow schedule is preserved but the order is changed, so that replacement proceeds in contiguous blocks rather than scattered at random through the tissue, while the replaced units are no longer whole cells but smaller functional subunits whose collective behaviour matches the cells they displace.

Here is what I want probabilities for. C1: at no instant during the twenty-seven-year schedule is the subject's stream of phenomenal consciousness interrupted, and at no instant does one stream cease and a numerically distinct stream begin in its place. C2: the person who completes the schedule is numerically identical with the person who began it. C3: if C1 fails, it fails observably — that is, there is no possible case in which the reports stay fluent, the tracking performance holds, and the comparative judgements the subject makes about her own experience remain untroubled, while the experience behind them nonetheless degrades. Give me your credence in each, and give me the decomposition behind C1 in particular: the probability that conscious states supervene on causal–functional organisation at some grain; conditional on that, the probability that the grain at which they so supervene is coarse enough that the neuronal modules in my schedule count as replacements of the right kind rather than of the wrong kind; the probability that the relevant substrate question is a sharp one rather than a vague one, and if vague, the probability that vagueness should be cashed out as indeterminacy about identity rather than as a hidden determinate fact unfavourable to survival; and any further term you think belongs, including the probability that the whole business is empirical in principle — that a sufficiently clever experiment could settle it — which is the one I most wish to hear argued. I am aware that these terms will not be independent; where the correlations run, say so, because a product of correlated terms is not a probability, and I have no patience for arithmetic that pretends otherwise.

Then three further things, and I would rather have them than have C1 to a third decimal place. State which term the final number is most sensitive to, in the sense that a modest shift in it moves the output most, and say what observation or argument would shift that term in you. Tell me whether your credence in C1 differs between the twenty-seven-year schedule and the eight-hour one, and if it does not, explain what work the gradualness was supposed to be doing — I suspect this is where my own thinking is weakest, since I have always assumed that a slow exchange is safer than a fast one for reasons I cannot state crisply. Finally, my former postdoc, who now works on machine intelligence, puts C1 at about 0.4 and, unlike me, assigns the same value to both schedules; I put it at about 0.9 and think the schedules differ sharply. Using your decomposition, identify the term on which the two of us most likely diverge, and say whether the divergence is one of metaphysical commitment or of how much weight to give an empirical question that neither of us knows how to pose. Do not send me a reading list. I am retired from the literature, and I would far rather you put the reasoning in your own terms than attribute it.
