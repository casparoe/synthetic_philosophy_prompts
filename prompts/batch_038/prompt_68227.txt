I'm a software engineer, no philosophy background, but I've been poking at constructivism/intuitionism and it keeps feeling like it's describing my day job. Here are the cases where I've noticed I have a firm opinion about whether I actually *know* something about a piece of code, and I want to know what principle they add up to.

1. Someone hands me a proof that a configuration satisfying our constraints must exist, but with no way to build one. Doesn't count as knowing. Useless to me.
2. A proof that the flakiness is in either the parser or the scheduler, without saying which. Doesn't count either — and my gut says if you really proved that, you'd be able to tell me which one.
3. Fifty rounds of Miller–Rabin on a 2048-bit candidate, so the chance it's composite is under one in 2^100. I do count that as knowing it's prime, I'd ship on it today.
4. A Lean proof that typechecks end to end but pulls in the classical choice axiom somewhere in the middle. Counts, no hesitation, I don't even read that part.
5. An argument that just exhaustively checks every input up to 2^64, so the claim is airtight in principle. Doesn't count, because nobody is ever going to run it.

The pair I cant reconcile is 3 and 5: one isn't a proof at all and I trust it completely, the other is a proof and I wave it off. Is there a principle that gets all five, or nearly all five? If something has to go, say which verdict and why, and tell me what your principle predicts for a case I didn't list — say, a fuzzer that's been hammering the parser for a week and hasn't produced a crash. Also be straight with me about whether what I've described is actually constructivism or something else wearing its clothes.

Format: exactly five numbered points, a sentence or two each, nothing before or after the list.
