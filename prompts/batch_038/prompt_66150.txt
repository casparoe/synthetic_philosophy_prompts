I've been asked to give a talk in my film society's new philosophy strand and I've picked Disney's Mulan as the vehicle for Confucian role ethics — the Ames and Rosemont version, where persons are centres of roles rather than discrete autonomous individuals, ren is something like consummatory conduct achieved *through* those roles, and there's no self sitting behind the role-play that the roles are supposed to express.

The problem is the film seems built on exactly the picture role ethics denies, and I can't work out whether that's fatal to the pairing or whether the pairing is the thing that exposes it. Here's the tension as I've got it so far:

The matchmaker audition is the whole village performing li as a checklist, Mulan fails at it, and then she sings about her reflection not showing who she is inside. That's the residual-self model — strip away the daughter, the bride-candidate, the soldier, and there's a leftover core that is really her, and the drama is that core finally getting out. On that reading the film is a fable about authenticity against role, which is precisely what a role ethicist ought to call a category error.

But there's a rival reading I want you to take just as seriously. Role ethics says personhood is something you do and achieve, not something you have going in. If that's right, the disguise isn't a mask over a pre-existing self at all: she makes a person out of roles nobody offered her — comrade, captain, the one who saves the capital — and the Emperor's bow is the community ratifying a role that didn't exist before she performed it. Under that reading the film isn't individualist, it just narrates *itself* in individualist language, and the homecoming, where her father pushes the sword and the crest aside to embrace her, is role ethics doing the real work rather than the maybe-I-did-it-for-me beat that comes just before it.

So: which reading is right? Please don't split the difference and say both — commit, and then price what the losing side has to give up. And work through these three:

1. The matchmaker scene as li gone external. Is the film criticising ritual propriety as such, or only a bureaucratised, feelingless version of it? This matters, because role ethics needs li to be the practice that cultivates the feelings, not a costume for feelings that are already there.
2. The deception. She lies about who she is to everyone whose opinion of her constitutes her, and the film punishes her only by having Shang spare her life. Does role ethics have the resources to take that lie seriously, or does it have to let her off because the conduct afterwards was consummatory?
3. The structural worry I know someone in the room will raise: role ethics looks conservative. It makes you out of your roles, so what does it say to someone whose society simply does not offer the role she'd need? Can it ground role-refusal as something seperate from role-failure, or does it have to borrow a liberal self, a natural right, some outside resource, to get her over the training-camp wall?

Then tell me where I'm forcing it. Where does the film actively resist this reading — the comic ancestors, the romance subplot doing work that has nothing to do with either the philosophy or the filial plot, the conventions of the musical form — and where would an honest critic have to admit the pairing is strained? I'd rather know before I stand up in front of sixty people.

End with what the film can do for the debate that a journal article on role ethics can't: something about the phenomenology of wearing a role until it is you, maybe, or about what it feels like from inside a role-set to be told the role-set is all there is. If you think it does nothing, say so and suggest I pick a different film.

British English throughout, and keep it to something I can actually deliver in about 25 minutes rather than a survey of the literature.
