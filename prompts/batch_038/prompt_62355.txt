I am a novelist. In the spring I give the opening evening session of a residential workshop for twelve working fiction writers — people with books out or under contract, none of them trained in philosophy, several actively suspicious of theory, and all of them expecting, by force of professional habit, to be given craft advice. Fifty minutes, a whiteboard, a circle of chairs. No clips, no slides, no handouts. The subject is the paradox of tragedy: why we deliberately seek out art we know in advance will grieve us.

I do not want you to explain the paradox to me. I want a plan for how I should explain it, built to survive this particular room.

First, the dependency structure. Give me the order in which the components must be introduced, and for each transition say what has to be in place already for the next piece to be intelligible. I have read enough of the literature to know the standard families of solution: that the pain is converted into pleasure by the eloquence or form of the work; that the pleasure lies in the audience's control, in the fact that they could stop and do not; that the pain is offset by goods of another kind; that what is enjoyed is one's own response rather than the work itself; that something is purged; and that we value a richer range of experience and are not, in the end, chasing pleasure at all. Do not deliver these to me as a list. Tell me which of them can only be grasped once some assumption has been surrendered, which two will collapse into each other in my students' minds if they arrive in the wrong order, and where the sequence can be compressed once I hit minute forty.

Second, the carrying example. One scene, from a single novel or play or film that this room is overwhelmingly likely to have actually read, on which the whole session hangs. Specify what that scene must have and what it must lack, and justify the specification. My own instinct is that the pleasure taken in it must be undeniable and unembarrassed; that it cannot be attributable to the beauty of the sentences alone, or the first solution wins for free; that the reader must know beforehand what happens, or suspense does the explaining; that nothing usefully applicable may be learned from it; and that no one in it may be spared. Correct that specification if it is wrong or incomplete. I am also tempted to build the session around a death in my own second novel: either talk me out of that, or tell me exactly what would have to be true of the passage for it to serve.

Third, the misconceptions, mapped onto the sequence rather than listed beside it. The room will arrive holding at least these: catharsis understood as a kind of emotional pressure-release; the insistence that what they feel is not sadness but some unnamed third thing; the idea that all of this is harmless because none of it is real; a mild gothic curiosity about death; and the belief that sad art makes us more empathetic and is therefore good for us. Tell me which to name and dismantle early, which one I must let stand until late because the paradox depends on it, and at precisely which point each is headed off. I also need a way to keep the craft reflex from hijacking the evening — the moment someone asks me how to make a reader weep, I have lost the session.

Fourth, the refusal. What I should openly decline to explain at this level, and say so. My candidates are the puzzle of how we can feel anything for people we know do not exist, the scholarly quarrel over what the ancients meant by purgation, and any question about whether the relevant psychology is actually true. Confirm these or substitute better ones, and give me the sentence in which I set each aside.

Fifth, the check. One thing I can put to the room in the final five minutes that will show whether they have grasped the structure of the problem rather than merely enjoyed hearing about it. It must be something a person who wrote down catharsis on the way in would get wrong, and something a person who simply agreed with me all evening would get right for the wrong reason. Tell me what the wrong answer would sound like.

Finally, I want candor about the fragility of all this. Close with a short account of where the plan is most likely to fail: which of the six families you have characterized in a way a specialist would dispute, which of your ordering claims you are least sure of and why, what objection from the room would force you to rebuild the sequence, and what consideration would change your mind about the example. Do not soften it. I would rather be shown the weakest joint in the argument than be handed a plan that looks unassailable and collapses at minute twenty.
