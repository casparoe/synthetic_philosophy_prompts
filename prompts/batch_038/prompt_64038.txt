Bit of an odd one. In mental health law in a handful of places you can write what's called a Ulysses clause into a psychiatric advance directive: somebody with, say, bipolar disorder decides while they're well that when they tip into mania they should be admitted and medicated, and they waive in advance their right to say no. Some versions go further and hand the trigger to a third party — the psychiatrist, or the patient's sister, decides the clause has activated, and the patient can't appeal that call. And there's a variant I've seen floated that I find genuinely wierd: the document states that any attempt by the patient to revoke it once it's live is itself to be taken as evidence that they aren't competent to revoke it. So the paper tells you how to read its own cancellation.

Meanwhile the ordinary default everywhere else in medicine is that if a patient is competent right now, their refusal right now wins, and something they signed two years ago doesn't beat it. And the same legal culture treats a person who acts because they have no reasonable alternative — duress, a threat, the gun in the drawer — as not really acting freely at all.

So: what theory of free action, and of autonomy across time, does the Ulysses clause practice actually presuppose? It looks like it is built to delete the patients alternatives at precisely the moment the treatment happens — there's no live branch left in which they say no and anyone listens — and yet everybody involved treats that as making the treatment more their own doing, not less. Reconstruct what has to be true about freedom for that to add up. I've got a hunch the relevant axis is something like: free because you could have done otherwise, versus free because the action issues from your own reasons-tracking machinery even when the doors are bolted — but tell me if that's the wrong cut, or if there are more than two live options here. Be explicit about which bits the practice cannot survive without, which it merely happens to fit comfortably, and where it's underdetermined so that several rival accounts would licence the very same form. Then: does the revoke-proof clause belong to the same theory as the rest of the practice, or does it quietly change the subject — is the patient binding their future self, or binding the judgement of everyone who later meets them? And finally, what would have to change in the wording of the form and in what happens on the ward if a court decided that being able to do otherwise really is necessary for the treatment to count as the patient's own?

British English please.
