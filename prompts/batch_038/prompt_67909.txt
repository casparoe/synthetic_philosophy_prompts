I am a novelist. My fourth book is set in a provincial Italian city during the influenza epidemic of 1918–19; it invents its protagonist, a municipal sanitary inspector, and it invents the central episode, a night on which the city's death registers are carried out of a flooded archive. Two minor figures are real, documented people, to whom I have given invented words to say. In three weeks I deliver the annual invited lecture in a history department's series on history and literature, with twenty minutes of questions from the floor afterward. The room will hold perhaps forty professional historians: mostly social and cultural historians of nineteenth- and early twentieth-century Europe, one historian of medicine who knows far more than I do about the epidemiology and the municipal records of the period, one legal historian who works in notarial archives, and two or three intellectual historians who have genuinely read the essay I mean to lean on and will resent my treating it as a slogan. There will be no literary scholars and no philosophers present.

Here is the argument I intend to deliver, so that you can aim the objections at my actual position rather than at a convenient reconstruction of it.

First: fidelity to the documentary record is not the governing standard by which a historical novel should be judged. The governing question is which of the three modes of history distinguished in Nietzsche's second Untimely Meditation of 1874 — monumental, antiquarian, critical — the novel is actually doing, and whether what it does serves life in his sense: whether it enlarges the reader's capacity to act, to forget where forgetting is health, and to grow out of a past and incorporate it rather than be flattened by it.

Second: the novel is structurally a monumental-historical instrument. Monumental history on Nietzsche's own account owes no debt of exactness; it compresses, generalizes, idealizes, suppresses contingency, and rounds the past into shapes the present can use, until it verges on invention. The novelist who does this commits no special sin of the fiction-maker, and anachronism and distortion judged inside that mode are not the novel's failure but its operation.

Third: the novel that reproduces the record faithfully and reverently, accumulating period detail because the detail is there, is usually failing in antiquarian mode — preservative, pious, incapable of judgment, and on his diagnosis life-stifling, since it venerates the old for being old and suspects the new for being new.

Fourth: the strongest historical novels do the work of critical history, which is to judge and condemn a past. That mode carries a distinctive burden, because the reader descends from what is being condemned; a novel that indicts its own conditions of possibility must earn the right to do so, and nearly all of them fail to.

What I want is a preparation sheet for the question period, not a defense of my thesis. I would much rather be shown where the position is thin than be reassured about it. Give me the eight or so objections this audience will actually raise, ordered by how much damage each would do if I answered it badly rather than by the order in which they will arrive. For each objection, phrase it as these people would phrase it, in their idiom and with their priorities in view: presentism, anachronism, the archive as an instrument of power and a record of who was permitted to speak, the ethics of putting invented words into the mouths of the named dead, the distinction between Historie and Geschichte if one of the intellectual historians wishes to be exacting about it, and the question of what my standard cannot be told apart from. Then give me a reply I could deliver in two minutes standing at a lectern without notes, and then state plainly where that reply is soft — and whether the softness is fatal or merely embarrassing. Tell me what I ought to concede in the first five minutes of the lecture so that I am not later seen to have evaded it, and identify the single reply that must land if the evening is to count as a success rather than a courteous disaster. Finish with the question I am hoping nobody asks: the one that goes to the deepest trouble in my argument, including the obvious point that a standard licensing me to round off the past for the sake of the present equally licenses someone with worse intentions to do the same, and the further question of whether I have misused a diagnosis of German self-culture in the 1870s by converting it into a criterion of literary judgment. If you think I have overstated anywhere what that essay actually says, mark the place and tell me what a well-read intellectual historian would correct.
