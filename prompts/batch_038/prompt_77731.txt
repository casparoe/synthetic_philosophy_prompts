Next term I'm running an upper-level seminar on fakes, forgeries, and the value of originals, and I want to hand out a diagnostic in week one and then hand back the same thing in week ten to see if anybody moved. So: a "which view of forgery do you actually hold?" quiz. Ten questions, each built out of a concrete case rather than a thesis.

Rules I care about:

- Every case describable in three sentences or less, in plain language, for students who've never read any philosophy of art. No jargon in the stems — no "supervene," no "aesthetic properties," no "autographic." They shouldn't be able to sort themselves by recognizing vocabulary.
- Two or three options each, and the options have to be genuinely different verdicts, not three wordings of one verdict.
- No question may ask anything equivalent to "does the history of a work matter." That's the conclusion, not a datum.

Before you write the questions, sketch the space you're testing. I want two axes up front: whether how good the object is depends only on what you can perceive in it, and whether the work's identity is fixed by the actual history of its making. Give me the four cells, say which are populated and by roughly what kind of view, and be honest about the cells you think are empty or unstable — and diagnose each one: incoherent, merely unattractive, or just unexplored. Then the questions get built out of that grid, not out of a list of famous names.

Leaves I want the key to be able to land someone on:

(a) only the perceptible object matters, so a flawless fake is aesthetically identical and the wrong is entirely about lies and money;
(b) the history is part of the work, so the fake is a different and lesser thing even when you can't tell them apart;
(c) the fake is worse because the forger did the easier thing — the problem was already solved — no matter how the result looks;
(d) the wrong lives entirely in the deception, and there'd be nothing wrong with the copy if it were honestly labeled;
(e) value is partly set by social and market facts, so the fake really does become worth less on exposure, and always was going to;
(f) "forgery" isn't an aesthetic category at all but a legal and moral one, and my seminar question is confused;
(g) the view that in certain art forms there's no such thing as forgery at all. I want the quiz to make them discover which art forms, so don't tip it off in the stem.

For the key itself: rules over answer patterns, not a points total. I want to be able to say "if you went this way on the scan-and-print case and that way on the workshop case, you're in c." Then tell me at least one pair of leaves your ten questions can't actually seperate, and what an eleventh question would have to look like to do it. My hunch is you'll get stuck on b versus c — show me I'm wrong, or show me why it's unavoidable.

Cases I'd like in the mix (swap or add as you see fit, but keep the first and the last): the van Meegeren-style Vermeer bought in good faith by a museum and admired for forty years before the confession; a museum scanner-and-printer replica made molecule-for-molecule identical to a destroyed original where nobody lied about anything; a copy signed with the copyist's own name and hung beside the original under a label reading "after X"; a workshop piece where the master painted only the hands; an artist who remakes her own lost painting from memory twenty years on; and — this is the one I made up for the course, so don't go looking for it — a manuscript in an archive, stylistically flawless, attributed for decades to a major poet, until a curator shows it was written in 1911 by a graduate student who turns out to be a fine poet in her own right and is now getting her own scholarly editions. Ask whether the poem got worse, better, or stayed put, and ask it twice, once before the discovery and once after.

One last thing: a short paragraph at the end on what it would mean if someone's answers came out inconsistent across the grid — whether that's a bug in the instrument or a finding about the concept. And don't send me a reading list, I've got one. I just want the instrument.
