I am preparing the opening session of a graduate seminar on the concept of democracy, and I would like to build the first handout around a single sentence that I heard doing two incompatible jobs within one hour.

The sentence is: "Democracy is more than elections."

At an interdisciplinary workshop on democratic erosion, a comparative politics colleague used it to argue that a country in which the governing party keeps winning genuinely contested elections — having in the interim rewritten the electoral law, packed the constitutional court, and consolidated nearly all private media under friendly ownership — has nonetheless ceased to be a democracy, and that anyone still pointing at the ballot is measuring the wrong thing. Hungary is the obvious instance, and my colleague's point was that the elections are not fake in the old sense; they are simply no longer the load-bearing element of the regime. Ten minutes later, in the same session, a political theorist used the identical sentence to argue the reverse: that since democracy is more than elections, the unhappiness of liberal critics about courts and press freedom is not by itself a loss of democracy, and that pronouncing such a regime non-democratic merely relabels a political disagreement as a conceptual verdict. Neither speaker defined the sentence. My working suspicion is that it cannot be doing the same work in both arguments, and that in at least one of them it is doing no work at all beyond manufacturing an air of consensus.

What I want is a disambiguation of the sentence itself, delivered as a menu I can hand to students rather than as a single favored reading.

First, enumerate the distinct precise theses the sentence could express — formulated so that a thoughtful person could accept one and reject another. I would like at least six, distinguished along the axis that seems to me to matter most, namely what kind of claim each one is: a claim about the meaning of the word, about the function or point of the institution, about the causal conditions under which competitive elections remain competitive over time, about how democracy ought to be operationalized and measured, or about the moral weight of electoral authorization relative to other political goods.

Second, attend to the grammar of "more than," which I suspect is where the ambiguity is concentrated. It can be additive (democracy equals elections plus X), scalar (democracy admits of degrees, and elections are one dimension among several, so a poor score elsewhere can be offset by a good one here), or lexical (X outranks elections, so that no amount of electoral quality can compensate for a deficit in X). These are very different commitments and I want them kept apart rather than folded together.

Third, for each reading, classify it: a platitude no serious person denies, a substantive and defensible thesis, a substantive and false one, or an unfalsifiable gesture as typically deployed. Wherever you judge a reading false or empty, say what would have to be the case for it to be false, and whether anyone actually asserts its negation — I want to know which readings are live positions and which are strawmen that make the slogan look more contentious than it is.

Fourth, for each reading, say which way it cuts in my two colleagues' arguments: does it block the inference from "elections are free and regularly held" to "this is a democracy," does it license the inference from "the executive has degraded the courts and the media" to "this is no longer a democracy," or does it do neither? Please flag the places where a reading silently requires a threshold claim or a weighting claim that the sentence does not supply, since I suspect most of the actual argumentative work in this literature is done by those unstated thresholds rather than by the slogan.

Fifth, tell me which reading each of my two colleagues most plausibly had in mind, whether either is entitled to it, and whether the sentence's rhetorical force in public argument derives precisely from the fact that it sounds like a platitude while being cashed out as a substantive claim.

Sixth, say whether any reading answers the objection that "democratic backsliding" is a partisan label affixed to governments its users simply lost to at the polls — or whether every reading either concedes that objection or quietly assumes its own conclusion.

I am not after a literature review, a bibliography, or citations; I want the readings and your verdicts on them. Please indicate how confident you are in each classification as you go, and close by naming the single point in your analysis you are least sure of, together with what would settle it.
