I run a five-person forecasting cell inside a state public health agency. Our numbers go straight to a deputy commissioner who sometimes acts on them within a day, so they're not academic. About once a week we face the same structural call, and we have no way of making it: do we lock the forecast in now, or do we leave it open for whoever's on call to revise later — 2am, half the team out sick, a press conference scheduled for four hours from now?

We've been burned in both directions. One time we locked and the person on call had the actual lab data in hand and wasn't allowed to move the number. Another time we left it open and the revision was made by someone who'd been awake nineteen hours and had just read a bad preprint, and that's the number that went in the memo.

Someone on my team pointed me at van Fraassen's Reflection principle — roughly, your credence now that your future credence in something will be x should itself be x — and said it's an argument for trusting your future self. I don't think it's that clean, and I suspect the textbook version doesn't even cover our situation, since our "future self" is very often literally a different analyst who wasn't in the room for any of this.

So: write me a procedure I can actually run in fifteen minutes when a lock-or-defer call comes up. Ordered questions, and for each one what counts as a real answer (not "consider how the situation may have changed" — that's not a question, that's a shrug). Where does it come out at lock, where at defer, and where does it have to stop and hand the call to me because it's a values question or a question about my own team that no rule can settle? Keep whatever genuinely survives of Reflection when you make it operational, and say out loud which of your steps are judgment calls wearing a procedural costume. I also want a straight answer on the substitution problem: does the principle have any grip at all when the future credence is going to be held by somebody else, or is that a different animal entirely that you'd be smuggling in under the same name?

Then run it on three cases:

1. We're forecasting whether a respiratory outbreak crosses a reporting threshold within six weeks. The on-call reviser will have two weeks of case data we don't have, and will also be sleep-deprived.
2. Same kind of forecast, but the revision window falls after a legislator has gone public with a position on it — and we know from our own history that publicity moves our people.
3. A junior analyst is asking us to pre-commit right now to not revising their number downward later, because they don't trust themselves to resist softening bad news when the commissioner is in the room.

Give me the answer for each, including whichever one my team is going to hate. And end with what the procedure flat out cannot settle, plus the situations where it will systematically get it wrong — I'd rather know the failure mode in advance than discover it in a memo.
