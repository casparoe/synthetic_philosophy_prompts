So, not a philosophy person, I write backend stuff, but I've gotten myself into a knot that I'm pretty sure is a famous one and I want someone to write me a real essay about it.

Context: we're building a policy engine for an internal platform. Policies are little rules that can check other policies — e.g. "allow this request only if policy X would approve it". Fine. But a policy can also end up referring to itself. Someone registered one that reads, roughly, "deny this request iff this policy approves it", and the evaluator just spins forever. My instinct was to treat it like a stack overflow: detect the cycle at load time and refuse to register the policy. Then a teammate pointed out you can get the same blowup with no findable cycle at all, using a quantifier: "approve this request only if nothing in my namespace approves it". If that policy is the only thing in its namespace, there's no loop for a static checker to catch, it's still broken. His counter-proposal was versioning instead: a policy may only read the verdicts as they stood at the previous deploy, so every reference points backwards in time and there's always a definite answer.

That sent me down a rabbit hole and I ended up reading about the liar paradox, Tarski's undefinability result, Kripke fixed points, and apparently a camp that thinks some contradictions are just straight up true. So here's the essay I want.

Is my "detect the cycle and ban it" fix an actual solution, or is it the levels/hierarchy move wearing a different hat — and does it even survive the quantifier case? Is the versioning idea genuinely different from stratifying into levels, or is it the same thing indexed by time? Then weigh the real options for a self-applicable approval predicate — stratify it, go three-valued and accept gaps, allow true contradictions, or something revision-flavored where the answer settles over rounds — and commit. Tell me which one you'd defend as correct, not just which is least painful to implement, and say why each of the others loses. And answer this: if the engine is contractually required to return allow or deny for every request, with no "unknown" state ever leaking to the caller, does that change your verdict or just hide the problem?

Don't end on a non-committal note please, I want a verdict. And skip the reading list — I can't chase down papers, just argue it out.
