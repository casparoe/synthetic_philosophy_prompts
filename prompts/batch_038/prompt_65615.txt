I've been trying to work out whether moral theories have to accept aggregation — you know the puzzle, one person tortured for fifty years versus a dust speck in the eye for each of an enormous number of people — and I've noticed I keep changing what I'd accept as an answer depending on which side I'm currently sympathetic too. So before I read another argument I want the standard written down.

Purpose, so that "adequate" has a target: I want a rule — call it an aggregation operator — that a consequentialist could bolt onto an otherwise complete theory. It takes a description of who bears which harms and returns a verdict on which of two options is better. So these should be requirements on the rule, not requirements on a whole moral view.

Here's the thing that made me want the list. Every version of the puzzle I've seen idealizes in four ways at once: the tiny harm is exactly the same size for everyone; everyone gets exactly one, once, and never again; the people are otherwise identical, so there's no background of prior advantage or disadvantage to aggregate over; and we know precisely how many people there are and how bad each harm is. Drop any one of those and I honestly don't know what happens to the intuitions, and I suspect that's where the interesting stuff lives.

Cases I want the list built around:

- The same person gets a mild speck every single day for fifty years, versus a million other people each getting one speck once. If the first is clearly worse and the second isn't clearly bad at all, what property of the rule explains the difference — and is that property defensible, or is it just a way of assuming the answer?
- Harms of slightly different sizes, distributed slightly differently, so there's no clean spectrum to walk along. Does the rule still have to spit out a verdict? What if the correct output is "no verdict"?
- The number of people affected is huge but vague: nobody knows whether it's 10^15 or 10^16, and the two options differ in both the number and the per-person badness.
- The specks fall on people who are already much worse off, or much better off, than average.

Deliverable: the properties the rule must have, and separately the ones it must not have, each with the case or consideration behind it. For each, mark whether it's non-negotiable — fail it and you're not a candidate at all — or whether its really a preference of one camp wearing a requirement's clothes. Be aggressive about that second category, because I want the ones that get stated as neutral and quietly settle the debate. I'm thinking of anything that assumes you can reach the verdict by comparing two people at a time, and anything that assumes the output shouldn't shift when you rescale how harms are measured.

Then the part I care about most: is some subset of the list jointly unsatisfiable? If dropping the first idealization makes the better-for-someone style constraints pull against the no-arbitrary-cutoff constraints, show me the tension with a concrete pair of options rather than asserting it exists. And name the single requirement that eliminates the most candidates.

American English please, around 1300 words, and a table is fine for the list as long as the table isn't doing the thinking. Skip references and don't name-drop papers — when you need to point at a camp, describe it (the ones who insist there has to be a cutoff, the ones who deny harms are tradeable across the board, whatever). If there's room at the end, two or three sentences on how the obvious contenders do against the list, but don't turn it into a scorecard.
