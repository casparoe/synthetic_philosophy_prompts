Every autumn the graduate students in our department stage a semi-serious evening that we advertise, with only mild irony, as "Computation and Comedy": two short talks, a wine-and-cheese interlude, and then an open mic at which every contributor is expected to offer material bearing on their own research area. I work on decision theory and my slot comes up next Thursday. I have no desire to recycle the perennial bits — the trolley problem restaged as a family road trip, Newcomb's predictor cast as a passive-aggressive roommate, two prisoners' dilemmas walking into a bar and insisting on paying for each other — because half the room will have heard those at some earlier iteration of the same event. What I want is something constructed out of Douglas Hofstadter's notion of superrationality, and constructed out of the content of the notion rather than out of its name.

For calibration, here is the machinery I would like the joke to engage with. A superrational agent facing a symmetric choice situation does not reason solely about its own move: it reasons that every other superrational agent in the situation will reason exactly as it does and will consequently land on exactly the same strategy, and so it asks which strategy is best on the assumption that everyone plays it. That is why two superrational agents in a one-shot prisoner's dilemma both cooperate without communication, contract, or enforcement, and why critics reply that this is a species of magical thinking, since my choice cannot reach out and cause yours — only our shared reasoning can. It is also where the doctrine becomes funny rather than merely paradoxical, because in any realistic mixed population the agent must first decide who counts as "people who think like me," and the entire edifice topples if that reference class is drawn wrong.

So: write me one joke on that material, setup and punchline, no more than about a hundred and twenty words, deliverable aloud to a room of philosophers who have encountered the idea but never thought hard about it. Please avoid punning on "super," avoid the walk-into-a-bar template, and avoid any premise that depends on the audience recognizing a particular published thought experiment by name; the bit should run entirely on the mechanism sketched above. As a seed, which you may develop, reshape, or discard: the twelve members of a neighborhood potluck committee all turn out to be superrational, and the resulting dinner is a catastrophe of perfect agreement — or, alternatively, a support group whose members cannot stop reasoning about what the other members must be reasoning about. Aim for a punchline that lands harder the more precisely it tracks the logic, so that a specialist laughs a beat behind everyone else but laughs harder. Close with two or three sentences identifying which feature of superrationality the punchline exploits and how the setup primes it, so that I can judge whether the material survives contact with someone who actually works on this.
