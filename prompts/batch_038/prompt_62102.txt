Ok so I run the philosophy society at my sixth form — about 14 of us, mix of Year 12 and 13, and we get a classroom for 45 minutes on a Thursday lunchtime. Half of them only come because their mates come and nobody has done any philosophy before. We've been on Hume's Of Miracles essay (section 10 of the Enquiry) and I did a little talk about it last week and it went down like a lead balloon, everyone just nodded and nobody disagreed with anything, which is honestly the worst outcome.

So I want to run it as a game where they run into the problem themselves, before anyone says Hume's name.

What I want them to actually feel is the collision Hume is describing: that a report can be really good and still not be good enough, because how good it has to be depends on how weird the thing reported is. I want someone to lose points for believing a reliable person about an unbelievable event, and to feel annoyed about it in a way that makes the argument land. Then the second thing — the one I think they'll get instantly — is a round where two teams get equally strong evidence for two things that can't both be true, which is one of Hume's own moves.

Some constraints on the design:

- 45 minutes, 14 people, one room, one whiteboard. Budget is basically zero — paper, pens, a jar of sweets if it helps, no real money.
- No worksheet with a Bayesian calculation on it. I know that version exists and I specifically don't want it. They should not see any numbers about how likely anything is until the very end.
- The reports in the game should be about our school, not about ancient Judea or Lourdes or whatever — I want the base rates to feel real to them, like they genuinely know how often the thing happens.
- They have to commit to a level of belief before anything is revealed, and the reveal has to be something I've fixed in advance and hidden, not something we debate afterwards.
- Someone will just answer "5 out of 10" every time to be safe. The scoring has to make that a losing strategy.

Sections I want, in this order:

1. **The pitch** — about 80 words on what the game is and what it's designed to make them feel.
2. **What you need** — short bullet list, cheap stuff only.
3. **The report cards** — write me the actual cards to print off, around ten of them, plus a line or two on how you balanced them and what mix of ordinary and marvellous reports you went for and why.
4. **Minute by minute** — around 350 words, the running order, including exactly how the betting works and how and when the truth gets revealed.
5. **Scoring** — around 150 words, with the actual point rules written out so I can put them on the board.
6. **The debrief** — around 300 words, mapping specific moments from the game onto specific moves in Hume's argument, including where in the sequence I should say his name.
7. **What'll go wrong** — around 150 words on the predictable failures first time round and what I do about each one.
8. **Where the game is rigged** — around 150 words, being honest about which bits of Hume's case the game quietly builds in for him, and what I have to supply by hand because the activity can't produce it.

Don't give me a menu of options and let me pick — I havn't got time for that. Choose one game, commit to it fully, and give me your reasons for the specific choices (why that scoring rule, why that mix of cards, why reveal in that order). And tell me straight whether you think the obvious alternative — a mock trial where a miracle is prosecuted and defended — is weaker than what you've designed, and why, because everyone suggests that to me and I'm bored of it.

Write the whole thing in British English.
