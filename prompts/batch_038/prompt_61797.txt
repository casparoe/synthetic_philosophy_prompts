I help administer the staking policy for a small betting syndicate — six of us, a shared bankroll, mostly football markets and a couple of prediction markets, nothing exotic, running for about four years. We're rewriting our charter this winter and I've been asked to state our sizing rule in a form the other five could actually apply. The difficulty is that I've discovered I don't have a rule; I have a set of verdicts about particular cases that I'm no longer sure are consistent with one another. I'd like you to do the reflective-equilibrium work on them: tell me what principle, if any, delivers all of my verdicts, or nearly all, and where it forces me to give something up. I'm not after a history of the debate over growth-optimal betting or a list of things to read — I want the principle itself, stated precisely enough to be operational.

Here are the verdicts, in the order I wrote them down.

1. A market we judge to be 60/40 in our favour at even money, recurring weekly with roughly the same edge: stake the log-optimal fraction, about 20% of bankroll. Completely comfortable, no hesitation.
2. That same 60/40 wager offered exactly once, with no reason to expect another like it: I still want to stake 20%, and I think that is the correct answer rather than a failure of nerve. Two members insist the growth-rate justification simply has no grip on a one-off bet, and I can't articulate what replaces it — I only feel strongly that the number shouldn't move.
3. A one-shot offer of a 90% chance to triple the amount staked against a 10% chance of losing it. By the arithmetic I trust, the log-optimal stake is 85% of net worth. I would stake 20% at most, and I do not believe I am being irrational. This is the case I keep returning to.
4. That same 90/10 offer repeated monthly for thirty years: the log-optimal stake is still 85% each month, as far as I can calculate. I find that less believable than the one-shot version, not more, and I want to know whether my disbelief is a confusion or a genuine objection.
5. Bankroll composition. Money that isn't ours — my rent, one member's tuition, both sitting in the same account — I exclude from the bankroll entirely and size off the remainder. Firm verdict. But I also think that our plan to dissolve the fund and spend the proceeds in five years should shrink today's stake, even though nothing about any bet has changed. One of those moves treats the horizon as irrelevant, the other treats it as decisive, and I can't get them to sit together.
6. Two members, identical edge, different means. I think the member with a substantial net worth outside the fund and the member whose entire savings is the fund should stake different fractions of the fund. I also think staking a fixed fraction is the whole point of the approach, and that the fraction has no business knowing how large the number is. Both feel obvious; I suspect they cannot both survive.
7. Confidence about the edge. When I'm sure p = 0.6, I stake the full log-optimal amount; when I think p is somewhere around 0.55 to 0.65, I stake a third of it. I want to insist this second move is not risk aversion at all but something about my uncertainty over the edge, and I'd like the principle to keep those two things apart rather than collapsing the second into the first.
8. Payoffs that cannot be reinvested. I'd take the 60/40 bet for money, but not the structurally identical bet whose winnings are a share of a house we'd live in, or whose loss would cost us the house we live in. A friend says this is loss aversion in a waistcoat; I think it turns on whether the winnings can compound, and I don't know whether there's anything to that.
9. Direction of the horizon. I'd stake more aggressively when the run of bets ahead of us is short and more conservatively when it is long. I have no argument for this whatsoever; it merely feels right, and I half-suspect it is exactly backwards.

Give me the principle, or a small set of candidate principles, each stated precisely and each mapped clause by clause onto the verdicts above — which verdict motivated which clause. Then give me its verdicts on cases I haven't listed, including at least one where it surprises me, and an honest inventory of the verdicts it cannot save, with an argument for why I should drop those rather than buy complexity to preserve them. If the truthful answer is that no single principle fits and the best available is a two-part rule — one part governing what counts as the bankroll, another governing the fraction staked of it — say so and tell me exactly where the seam runs and why it runs there rather than somewhere else. And whatever you propose, state explicitly what it implies about case 3, because that is the one I will have to defend to five other people who think I am leaving money on the table.
