You've been a philosopher of economics for decades and you've taught the socialist calculation debate enough times to know where it usually goes wrong, so I'm coming to you first rather than to the literature. Context: I run a reading group that's split down the middle. Half are CS people who think the debate was settled in 1990 and that a large enough optimization problem plus enough compute is a complete answer to it. The other half think Hayek won on the epistemology and that the CS people haven't understood what the objection actually was. Before we argue any further I want the spec on the table: what does any account of how a complex economy settles on what to produce, with what, and for whom have to *deliver* to count as a serious answer, pro or con.

So: write the desiderata. Think carefully and do a bit of brainstorming before you commit to the list -- jot the candidate requirements, then cut the ones that turn out to be the same requirement wearing a different hat. For each one you keep, give the case or consideration behind it, not just a label. I care about the motivating cases as much as the criteria.

A few things I specifically want you to wrestle with while building the list, because they're where I think the camps quietly beg the question:

- The firm. A big retailer or a platform runs an internal economy the size of a medium country: procurement, allocation of shelf and warehouse capacity, labour scheduling, no meaningful internal prices for most of it, and it works. Any set of requirements has to explain why the same computation is allegedly impossible at national scale but routine at firm scale, and say where exactly the scaling breaks -- or admit it doesn't.
- What the actually-existing planned economies did, not what their ideology said they did. The material balances procedure and the way it degraded in practice, the output-metric perversities everyone knows the anecdotes about, and above all the informal machinery the plan grew to survive itself -- fixers, barter, the second economy. A requirement that has no room for this stuff isn't a requirement about economies.
- Two objections that get conflated and shouldn't be: the *computational* one (the problem is hard, but a well-posed program with enough solve time gets there) and the *epistemic* one (the relevant facts don't exist as facts until somebody acts on them, so there's nothing to feed the solver). Note that the strongest version of the computational reply was developed inside a Soviet planning institution by the people who invented the relevant mathematics, and was largely ignored there -- which is itself data your list should be able to accommodate.
- The real-time feedback alternative, the Chilean one from the early seventies with the ops room and the telex network: what does a requirement list have to say about latency, about the difference between allocating a given bundle and discovering that a new bundle is worth producing at all?
- The formal inheritance from mechanism design: does a candidate account have to be expressible as a mechanism with stated preferences, and what does incentive compatibility do to the requirements -- is it a separate desideratum or a restatement of one you already have?

Then do the sorting work, which is the part I actually want:

1. Mark each requirement as non-negotiable or as a preference of one camp. I suspect several of the things both sides demand are prejudices -- e.g. building ownership of the means of production into the definition of a workable price, on one side, and treating "a central board with perfect information" as the target on the other. Call those out explicitly and say whose prejudice each one is.
2. Require each candidate to state the *modal strength* of its claim -- impossible, impractical, we-don't-yet-know-how, undesirable-but-feasible, or the question-is-malformed -- and tell me which of those is actually load-bearing in the two sides' arguments.
3. Flag any pair or trio of desiderata you think can't be jointly satisfied. If the list itself is a result, that's the most valuable thing you can hand me. My own hunch is that demanding the aggregation of local knowledge into something a center can use is incompatible with demanding that the knowledge be generated by the decentralized trial and error that produces it, but argue it rather than nod at it.
4. Tell me which single requirement does the most to eliminate candidates, and which requirement sounds demanding but turns out to be nearly free to satisfy.

Finally, briefly and only briefly, score the main contenders against your list -- a short paragraph each, no more: Mises, Hayek, the Lange/Lerner market-socialist proposal with parametric prices and trial-and-error adjustment, the mathematical-planning tradition, and the modern "but now we have machine learning" revival. If one of them fails on a requirement the others don't even engage with, say that.

A table is fine for the requirements themselves if it keeps it tight. Two housekeeping things: no bibliography, no reading list, don't name-drop works or invent page references -- I want the reasoning, not the citations. And if you think I've framed the target wrong, that some requirement I've implied is not really a requirement at all, say so up front and build the list you'd defend instead.
