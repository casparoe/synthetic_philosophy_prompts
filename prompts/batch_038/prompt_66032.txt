The episode, with the facts laid out so you don't have to guess at anything: the public Human Genome Project's funders met in Bermuda in 1996 and agreed that assembled sequence over about 1 kb would be released into public databases within 24 hours of generation, a private-ordering norm that cut hard against the usual publish-then-share practice in biology; Celera comes in from the private side in 1998 with a whole-genome shotgun strategy and a subscription-database business model; the two camps jointly announce a draft in 2000 and publish in early 2001, after which Celera's model mostly collapses; and separately Myriad Genetics ends up holding patents on the isolated BRCA1 and BRCA2 sequences and effectively controls diagnostic testing for those mutations until AMP v. The lens I want is the anticommons — Heller and Eisenberg's 1998 Science piece, where the worry is that too many owners can each block the others so a resource gets underused rather than overused. Not the commons. So map it properly first: who the fragment-holders are in the genome case, what exactly is supposed to be getting aggregated, and which of the anticommons's enabling conditions are actually satisfied — I'd like that as a compact table, one row per condition, marked holds / doesn't hold / can't tell, with a line of justification each. Then the part I actually care about, which is my suspicion that the concept does less work here than people assume. My worry: an anticommons needs complementarity — lots of separate rights-holders whose permissions you have to stack up to build anything — whereas what the genome episode mostly gives you is a single gatekeeper sitting on a necessary input and charging a toll. Call it a tollbooth instead of a thicket. If the Myriad position is a tollbooth rather than a thicket, then the paradigm case everybody cites for the anticommons arguably isn't an anticommons case at all, and the concept is getting credit for an explanation that monopoly pricing plus over-broad patent scope could deliver on their own. Tell me whether that objection lands, whether there's a thicket component I'm underweighting (upstream research tools? the European testing labs?), and what the aftermath does to the concept — the follow-up empirical work on whether gene patents really blocked downstream research came out a good deal milder than the 1998 predictions, which raises the question of whether the anticommons is the kind of theory that can't lose: obstruction confirms it, non-obstruction gets explained away by licensing and informal norms.
