I am writing about a decision that has stopped being hypothetical. Three weeks ago I was offered a place in a two-year residential formation program at a religious community — communal life, daily office, spiritual direction, a fixed reading schedule, and very limited contact with outside work or media. I would enter as someone who puts his credence that Christian theism is true at about 0.2. I also confidently predict that if I complete the program, my credence will be around 0.9. Two stipulations about that prediction, because both of the people below kept trading on it: first, I cannot name any particular argument, text, or piece of evidence I expect to encounter there; what I expect is the setting, not a syllogism. Second, I also predict that if I leave the program partway through, my credence will drift back to roughly 0.25 within a year.

I described all this to two people I trust. Daniel did graduate work in philosophy; Nadia is a clinical psychologist. They ended up arguing with each other rather than advising me, and I could not settle it. Here is the exchange as nearly as I can reconstruct it.

1. Daniel: This is a textbook case for van Fraassen's Reflection principle, from Belief and the Will (1984). If you now believe that you will then have credence 0.9, you should have credence 0.9 now. Anything else is diachronic incoherence: you are planning to be convinced by what you presently regard as no reason at all. He added that a bookie who knows both of your states of mind could sell you bets now that your future self would happily rebuy against you, so the combination guarantees you a loss.

2. Nadia: Reflection cannot reach this case, because your 0.9 will not be produced by evidence. It will be produced by isolation, affection for the people around you, the cost of leaving, and the emotional force of ritual. To defer to a self you expect to have been shaped by those things is not coherence but capitulation — you would be licensing your own conversion in advance.

3. Daniel: That proves far too much. Ordinary learning also moves you by causes you cannot evaluate from where you stand: you expect to believe the result of an experiment because you expect to see the data, and the data will be generated by an apparatus you have not inspected. If "my future belief will be caused by something I do not now possess" were grounds for refusing deference, no one could ever rationally expect to learn anything.

4. Nadia: The cases are not parallel. Data are truth-conducive; immersion is a mechanism whose output you can predict without predicting a single new fact. Note the asymmetry: Daniel's account of you cannot distinguish a community that forms true beliefs from one that forms loyal ones, and yet you produce the same number in both. That is precisely why the prediction carries no authority.

5. Daniel: Then you are committed to something stronger and stranger than you want — that you already know, at a distance and without any of the relevant first-hand experience, that two years of sustained attention to these questions will terminate in a false belief. That is not modesty about mechanisms; it is an implicit claim of present infallibility. If it is permitted, nobody could ever rationally plan to change his mind.

6. Nadia: I am not claiming to see the truth from here; I am claiming to see the mechanism, and predicting an error is not the same as knowing the answer. Besides, his Dutch book never gets going. Those bets would be settled by someone who cares about different things than you do. If the program changes what you want, the losses are not ones you are now paying to avoid, and a bookie who can collect only from a man who no longer minds paying has shown nothing.

7. Daniel: That is the "my future self is a different person" escape hatch, and it is question-begging here. You get to say that only if the change is total; you yourself predict the same propositions held in the same vocabulary by a man who will still call himself you. And Reflection does not rest on bets alone — it expresses the idea that you take your future self to be better placed to judge, which you have just conceded.

They went around twice more without progress, and I have to answer the community by the end of the month. Could you adjudicate the exchange move by move — rating each of the seven from clearly mistaken to clearly right, with a short justification for each rating — and then say who is more right overall? Please also tell me whether either of them has stated Reflection accurately, and whether some more careful formulation of the principle decides the case or simply fails to apply to it. Finally, say what both of them are missing, including anything that bears on what I should actually do about the offer, which is the question I asked them in the first place.
