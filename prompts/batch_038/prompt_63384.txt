I restore and calibrate old thermostats and pressure gauges as a hobby, and over the winter I talked myself into an argument I have not been able to get out of. Please write in American English.

Here it is, step by step.

1. An instrument gives me knowledge about a quantity only if I already know, or am at least justified in believing, that the instrument is accurate.
2. The only way to find out whether an instrument is accurate is to compare its readings against readings I already take to be accurate — another instrument, or a standard.
3. So every check on an instrument's accuracy presupposes the accuracy of something else.
4. That chain of presuppositions cannot run backward forever, and nothing in my shop is infallible. So it must either loop — returning to an instrument already credited earlier in the same check — or bottom out in an instrument whose accuracy is simply assumed, with no check at all.
5. If it loops, then somewhere an instrument is being validated by a procedure that already counted it as accurate, which is exactly the circular validation I set out to exclude. If it bottoms out, then part of my knowledge of accuracy rests on an unchecked assumption, which contradicts (1).
6. Therefore I do not know that any of my instruments is accurate.
7. And the reasoning does not depend on anything about me or my shop, so nobody knows that any instrument is accurate — including the ones used to maintain the reference standards everything else traces back to.

(7) strikes me as false, and not only as a philosopher's puzzle: certification works, my three best thermometers agree to within a tenth of a degree, and I would happily rely on them. A friend told me this smells like what epistemologists call bootstrapping, but as far as I can tell my trouble is the regress in (4), not any inference from a source to the source's own reliability. The claim I am least willing to surrender is the practical one — that I really do know my best thermometer is accurate, because I have checked it against two others.

So: which premise should go, and what does giving it up cost? I especially want to know whether there is a principle that forbids the looping case — validating a gauge by means of gauges whose accuracy was itself established using it — while leaving ordinary comparative calibration intact, since that is plainly legitimate. If your answer is that the loop is sometimes permissible after all, say what makes it permissible here and impermissible elsewhere. I cannot find a difference that does not reduce to how many instruments happen to be involved, or to how much I already trust them.
