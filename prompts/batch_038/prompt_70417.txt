Our monitoring group has spent the past year building a mechanistic anomaly detector and has ended up deadlocked over a conceptual question that turns out to govern every downstream design decision. The setup is this: an audited reference model is run over a large bank of prompts, and its internal computation on each is characterized and labeled; a deployment model is then run on matched prompts, and a monitor compares the two, flagging any case in which the deployment model appears to have reached its answer by a different mechanism than the reference did. The working premise is that misaligned or deceptive behavior would recruit different computations than honest behavior even where the surface answers coincide. Two camps in the group disagree about what "different mechanism" could mean, and each insists the disagreement is not merely verbal.

Camp R (role individuation) holds that a mechanism is typed by its intervention-defined function and its position in the model's causal graph: what it takes as input, what it contributes downstream, and what it does when its inputs are perturbed. Two computations answering to the same counterfactual profile are the same mechanism, whatever neurons, layers, or directions in activation space realize them. On this view, a deployment model that reaches its answers by a functionally equivalent but internally reorganized route should not be flagged, whereas a model that reuses the reference's features for a different downstream effect should be.

Camp G (realization individuation) holds that a mechanism is typed by the concrete representational geometry it recruits: which directions in activation space, which components, with roughly which weights. Functional equivalence at the level of input–output counterfactuals is too coarse precisely because misalignment is expected to present as the recruitment of different features — a memorized lookup, an approval-tracking representation, a separately maintained account of what to say — while preserving the same overt function. On this view, same function with different realization is a different mechanism, and must be flagged.

Produce the document this collaboration should have yielded: a joint statement that a representative of Camp R and a representative of Camp G could both put their names to without reservation. It should contain the following, in this order.

1. A numbered section of claims both can sign. I want these to be substantive and non-trivial — for instance, that any such monitor presupposes some equivalence relation on computations; that some candidate relations are too fine-grained to be usable (exact activation match under any perturbation) and others too coarse (agreement on the task's inputs and outputs) ; that fixing the relation fixes the detector's false-positive and false-negative tradeoff in a way that data alone cannot settle; and that the dispute is not about whether to build the monitor at all. Cut anything hortatory, definitional, or true regardless of which camp is right.

2. A disagreements table with four columns: the issue stated neutrally; Camp R's formulation of its own position, in wording a Camp R representative would accept as accurate; Camp G's formulation likewise; and a judgment, with reasons, of whether that particular disagreement is substantive, partly substantive, or an artifact of two uses of the same word.

3. The cruxes: the sub-questions such that settling one would actually move at least one side. I expect at least three, and I expect them to include whether "function" is to be characterized over counterfactuals inside the model's own activation space or over counterfactuals in the task environment; whether mechanisms admit of correct typing at more than one grain size with no single privileged grain; whether the relevant comparison is to the reference model's mechanism specifically or to the class of mechanisms any honest model might use for that task; and whether the equivalence relation must be fixed before deployment or may be revised as evidence accumulates. For each crux, specify a concrete case, controlled comparison, or thought experiment that both sides agree in advance bears on it, and record each side's predicted outcome — recorded before the fact, so that a loss registers as a loss.

4. A short section stating what each side would accept as evidence or argument against its own position, and whether there exists anything both agree would settle the matter rather than merely reframe it.

5. A closing assessment of which parts of the year-long argument were about words rather than about the world, and why. State explicitly whether the familiar analogy to the multiple-realizability debate in the philosophy of mind — mechanism identity as functional role versus identity as physical realization — carries over and does useful work here, or is a trap that misdescribes what a trained network's internals are and should be struck from the joint statement. Argue for your verdict rather than gesturing at it.

The document must be self-contained: define every technical term it uses, and do not cite, name, or recommend any literature, since both signatories are assumed to be reading it offline. Because both parties are signing, nothing may be phrased in a way either would regard as slanted toward the other, and no claim may be softened into agreement by ambiguity. Write it so that a third group could take it as a specification of exactly what remains unsettled and what would settle it.
