I'm a personal injury defense lawyer, about twelve years in, mostly premises liability and trucking wrecks, and for the last year I've been siting in on a moral philosophy seminar at the community college two nights a week because my daughter talked me into it. We just did a unit on duties and what lies beyond them, and I've spent the whole week with this nagging feeling that about half of what they're arguing about I already have names for, just from a different filing cabinet. So I want you to test that feeling rather than reassure me.

Take the supererogation debate -- the claim that there's a real category of acts that are morally good and praiseworthy but not required, the guy who throws himself on the grenade, the stranger who gives a kidney, the person who dedicates their life to something nobody asked them to -- along with the main attacks on it: the paradox that if doing X is better than doing some permissible Y, why on earth isn't X required; the Kant-flavored reply that these are just wide duties with latitude in how and when you discharge them; the consequentialist reply that there's nothing beyond duty because the best available act is always the one you must do; and the suspicion that the whole category is a cultural artifact, a way of leaving room for saints and heroes in systems that don't want to demand sainthood from everyone. Now restate all of that in the vocabulary I actually work in: duty versus no-duty, misfeasance versus nonfeasance, special relationships, voluntary undertakings and the rule that once you start helping you can't abandon it carelessly, the rescue doctrine that lets an injured rescuer go after whoever created the peril, Good Samaritan immunity statutes, standard of care, consideration, gratuitous promises, enforceability, remedies, and the difference between what a court will make you do and what you had good reason to do. As faithful as you can manage -- including the couple of states like Vermont and Minnesota that actually make it a crime to walk past someone in danger, because I suspect that's the sharpest test case for whether a legal system can even house this category.

Then give me the residue report, and be blunt about it. Which claims translate cleanly -- is "beyond the call of duty" just nonfeasance where no duty attaches, full stop? Which ones you had to bend to make fit, and specifically where you caught yourself smuggling my framework's assumptions in and calling it translation, since I know law is binary about duty in a way the philosophers insist morality can't be, and I know we have no vocabulary at all for degrees of praiseworthiness (we have damages, not merit). Which parts have no expression in my terms whatsoever -- I'd guess the praise half dies first, but tell me exactly where and why it dies rather than just agreeing with me. And then the verdict I'm actually after: is that residue a limitation of legal thinking, because we only care about what's enforceable and are therefore structurally blind to the admirable, or is it evidence that the philosophers' category was doing less work than it looks -- is the paradox of supererogation just what happens when you push reasons and requirements, two different scales, through a single word, the way we'd never let anyone push negligence and unreasonableness through one word in a deposition? Don't give me sources or a reading list, I'm not going to chase anything down, I just want the conceptual work done honestly.
