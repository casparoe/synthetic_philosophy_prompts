Got a cheap microscope for my kid last month and fell down a rabbit hole about what actually counts as alive, and now I've written down a list of things I think and I want to know if they can all be true at once. I'm not a scientist, just someone who can't stop thinking about it.

1. Viruses aren't alive. Nothing happens inside them. They're instructions in a box that get unpacked by somebody else's machinery.
2. But a seed that's been sitting in a drawer for ten years is alive, and so is one of those water bears dried out into a little husk. Being alive is about what a thing *could* do, not what it's doing right now.
3. Reproducing can't be part of the definition. My dog was neutered years ago and a mule can't have babies, and noone thinks that makes them not-alive.
4. At bottom though, what life IS is a lineage — stuff that keeps making copies of itself with little errors in them. The individual creature is just a temporary carrier for that.
5. Fire isn't alive, even though it eats fuel, grows and spreads, because it doesn't build anything up or hold onto a shape.
6. To be alive a thing has to be a *thing* — an individual with a boundary you could point at. So a slime mold crawling over a log is one organism, but "the whole biosphere" isn't alive, no matter how nicely it seems to balence itself.

So: which of these actually pull against each other, and which ones only look like they do? Put the real conflicts in order of how bad they are, and for each one tell me what I'd have to give up and what the cheapest repair is — plus which single change would fix the most at once. And fair warning, I genuinely don't know which of these I'm most attached to, so don't just assume I'd sacrifice the virus one to save the rest.
