Take the IAU's Pluto vote in Prague, August 2006 — 424 members of a union of roughly ten thousand raising cards on the closing afternoon, eight days after a committee draft that would have made the count twelve planets was met with an uproar and rewritten. I want it read as a speech act rather than as astronomy.

So: what kind of act was Resolution 5A? A declaration in Searle's sense, where the vote is what makes it true that Pluto isn't a planet? An Austinian verdictive — a finding about what was already the case, like a referee's call or a jury's? Or an exercitive, given that the resolution describes itself as fixing the term for the IAU's own usage, which sounds more like a rule about the union's vocabulary than a claim about the solar system? Then run the felicty conditions: is the IAU the body with an accepted conventional procedure for settling this, were the few hundred people still in the hall the right persons in the right circumstances, does it matter that the definition only speaks of bodies orbiting the Sun when more than a hundred exoplanets were already known, and what did uptake look like, given that textbooks and the public mostly complied while a lot of planetary scientists just kept calling Pluto a planet?

Last thing, and it's the part that bugs me: Searle holds that a status function always carries deontic powers — rights, obligations, entitlements. Nobody's obligations changed when Pluto got demoted. Does that show the vote wasn't really a declaration, or that his account of declarations is too tight, and what would a declaration that creates a status with no deontic payoff even be doing?
