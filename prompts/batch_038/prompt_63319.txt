I have come to think Harsanyi's aggregation theorem carries more weight than its critics concede: if individual preferences over prospects satisfy the expected-utility axioms, if the social preference does too, and if society is indifferent between two prospects whenever every individual is, then the social ranking is representable as a positive weighted sum of individual utilities — and with a suitable symmetry condition, the weights are equal. On that reading the theorem is not one utilitarian argument among others but a schedule of the price of resisting utilitarianism. What would count against it? Rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward. Does continuity do real work once unbounded or infinite utilities are admitted, and what would agents have to look like for it to fail? Does re-scaling any one person's utility function alter the weights, so that the theorem presupposes interpersonal comparability rather than deriving it? Does equal weighting follow from symmetry alone, or from some stronger claim about occupying another person's position? And is holding the population fixed what makes a sum and an average rank prospects alike? Then take up the structural escape route: keeping the Pareto condition but denying that the social ranking is linear in probabilities, so that aggregation happens within each state of the world once the uncertainty has resolved rather than across whole prospects. Is that a genuine rival to the theorem's conclusion, does it collapse back into it, or does it purchase fairness at the cost of dynamic inconsistency? Finally, does the theorem risk anything at all, or is it insulated by being a representation result rather than a moral claim — and where should even a sympathetic reader concede that it falls short of establishing utilitarianism?
