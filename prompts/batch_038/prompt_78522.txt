**The exam question (25 marks, allotted 50 minutes):**

> Explain how eliminative materialism about the propositional attitudes differs from reductive materialism about them. In your answer, say what intertheoretic reduction requires, and say why the eliminativist thinks folk psychology fails to meet that requirement. Finally, state the self-defeat objection to eliminative materialism and explain how an eliminativist can respond to it.

**The answer submitted:**

> Both eliminative materialism and reductive materialism are physicalist views, so both deny that beliefs and desires are non-physical items of the sort Descartes had in mind. They differ in what they think happens to folk psychology.
>
> Reductive materialism identifies mental states with brain states: pain is C-fiber firing, and a belief that p is some neural state or other. On this view the folk term is simply given a new definition in the language of neuroscience, the way "bachelor" is defined as "unmarried man," so nothing ordinary is lost. This is intertheoretic reduction, and it requires that each term of the old theory be matched by some term or structure in the new theory that plays the same causal role — the way temperature was matched with mean molecular kinetic energy, and lightning with electrical discharge. When reduction succeeds, the old theory is not thrown away. It turns out to have been true all along; we simply had not noticed what its terms were really picking out.
>
> Eliminative materialism says folk psychology is not a candidate for reduction, because it is a bad theory. Churchland's argument is that it has been stagnant for thousands of years: it cannot explain mental illness, it cannot explain sleep, it cannot explain how we learn or why we forget, and it has made no progress while the sciences around it have transformed. When a theory fails that badly we do not reduce it, we drop it. Phlogiston was not identified with anything in modern chemistry; caloric fluid was not identified with anything in thermodynamics; the vital force or élan vital of the nineteenth-century biologists was not identified with anything in molecular biology. All three were simply abandoned. So the eliminativist holds that beliefs and desires do not exist, and that this is not a merely verbal point: it is not that "belief" is a sloppy word we had better stop using, but that nothing answers to it.
>
> That is also why Dennett's intentional stance counts as a version of the view. To say of a system that it believes that p is only to say that predicting its behaviour by treating it as a believer works well, so beliefs are useful fictions rather than real components of the world. On this reading, belief sentences are neither true nor false, since there is nothing for them to be about.
>
> The self-defeat objection runs as follows. If eliminative materialism is true, there are no beliefs; so no one believes it; so it could not be asserted or held by anyone; and a thesis that no one could so much as assert cannot be true either. Churchland concedes the point, and concludes that the eliminativist must keep quiet about it until neuroscience supplies a vocabulary in which it can be said. That seems an overreaction: the objection shows only that the view is not yet expressible, not that it is false, and every theory that replaces an old vocabulary starts out in the same difficulty.
