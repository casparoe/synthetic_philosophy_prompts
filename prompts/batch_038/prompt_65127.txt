I'm in a small reading group working through the middle stretch of the *Philosophical Investigations*, and we've deadlocked over how much of the private language argument is actually carried by its two most famous pictures. The member I keep arguing with is a software engineer, and his position is that the whole thing reduces to a point about redundant confirmation: when the diarist of §258 wants to know whether he is applying "S" in the same way again, he is in the position of the man Wittgenstein describes at §265, "as if someone were to buy several copies of the morning paper to assure himself that what it said was true." Extra copies of one source are no check at all, so there is no distinction between getting it right and merely seeming to get it right; therefore, he says, sensation words cannot be naming private items and can only be reporting behavior. He thinks the beetle-in-the-box at §293 is the same claim in costume: whatever is in the box "has no place in the language-game at all," so the sensation drops out of consideration and only the public expression remains.

Could you audit both pictures as arguments rather than as illustrations? For each I'd like an explicit correspondence table — in the newspaper case, what plays the paper, the additional copies, the assurance being sought, and the fact those copies are supposed to confirm; in the beetle case, what plays the box, the beetle, the impossibility of looking into someone else's box, and the word's continuing use in the language of these people — followed by the features of the source situation that each picture quietly imports and the target lacks. Two copies of a newspaper are still copies of something with a determinate content, and their failure looks epistemic rather than constitutive, whereas the diarist's difficulty is supposed to be that there is no such content to be right or wrong about; is that a fatal disanalogy, or is it precisely what the analogy is being recruited to establish? And the beetle picture appears to depend on a prior grammatical claim about construing sensation-language on the model of "object and designation" — does it do any independent work, or does it merely dramatize a conclusion reached elsewhere? Please separate the disanalogies that genuinely threaten his inference from the decorative ones, and say whether the behaviorist reading survives once they are on the table. I'd also like to know what each picture remains good for rhetorically at the points where it fails as proof, and whether a better picture exists for the thought that "whatever is going to seem right to me is right" — one that does not smuggle in the assumption that what the diarist is missing is a second witness.

Finally, a third member pushed back in the spirit of Ayer's old objection: any chain of cross-checks must bottom out somewhere in something simply recognized, so the newspaper simile cannot show that checking memory is worthless — only that it is not self-certifying. Tell me whether that objection lands against the analogy, against the argument, or against neither. And note that our engineer extends his conclusion past human sensation to machine introspection: because a model can only consult its own records about its own states, its reports about its internals are, on his view, exactly the redundant newspaper. I'd like an assessment of whether either picture licenses that extension or whether the analogies simply run out there, together with what would have to be true of such reports for the comparison to amount to more than a pun.
