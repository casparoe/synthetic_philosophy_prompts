I am assembling the opening session of a graduate seminar on the philosophy of aging, and I want the first assignment to be a counterfactual reconstruction that disciplines the students' thinking rather than merely amuses them. The figure is Cicero, and the text is the *Cato Maior de Senectute*, written in 44 BCE in the interval between Caesar's assassination and Cicero's own flight from Rome, addressed to Atticus, and staged as a conversation in which an elderly Cato the Elder answers two much younger interlocutors, Scipio and Laelius. I would like you to carry out the reconstruction I am going to set the students, so that I have a model answer to argue against.

Because I do not want the exercise to turn on anyone's recollection of the dialogue, here are the passages I want it anchored to. All translations below are my own rough renderings, offered only to fix the sense.

Cicero explains his choice of spokesperson in the preface: *Omnem autem sermonem tribuimus non Tithono, ut Aristo Cius, (parum enim esset auctoritatis in fabula), sed M. Catoni seni, quo maiorem auctoritatem haberet oratio* — the whole discourse is given not to Tithonus, as Aristo of Ceos did, because a myth would carry too little authority, but to the aged Cato, so that the argument would carry more.

Cato's first move is a diagnosis of inconsistency: old age is the thing *quam ut adipiscantur omnes optant, eandem accusant adeptam; tanta est stultitiae inconstantia atque perversitas* — which everyone prays to reach and then accuses once reached, so great is the inconstancy and perversity of folly. He follows with the sharpest remark in the dialogue for my purposes: *Deinde qui minus gravis esset eis senectus, si octingentesimum annum agerent quam si octogesimum? Praeterita enim aetas quamvis longa cum effluxisset, nulla consolatio permulcere posset stultam senectutem* — would old age be any less burdensome to them if they were living their eight-hundredth year rather than their eightieth? For once a past life has flowed away, however long it was, no consolation can soothe a foolish old age.

On nature he writes: *Quid est enim aliud Gigantum modo bellare cum dis nisi naturae repugnare?* — what else is it to war with the gods like the Giants, if not to resist nature? And: *necesse fuit esse aliquid extremum et, tamquam in arborum bacis terraeque fructibus maturitate tempestiva quasi vietum et caducum, quod ferundum est molliter sapienti* — there had to be some final stage, shrivelled and falling as it were in the berries of trees and the fruits of the earth at their appointed ripeness, which the wise man must bear gently.

On character and circumstance: *Sed omnium istius modi querellarum in moribus est culpa, non in aetate* — the fault in all such complaints lies in dispositions, not in the age of life. And, conceding a point to Laelius: *Nec enim in summa inopia levis esse senectus potest ne sapienti quidem, nec insipienti etiam in summa copia non gravis* — old age cannot be light even for a wise man in utter want, nor fail to be heavy even for a fool in utter abundance.

Finally, his positive prescription: *Aptissima omnino sunt, Scipio et Laeli, arma senectutis artes exercitationesque virtutum* — the most fitting arms of old age are the practices and exercises of the virtues, cultivated at every age, which bear remarkable fruit if you have lived long and much.

Beyond these passages, take as given the dialogue's fourfold structure: old age is charged with withdrawing us from the conduct of affairs, with weakening the body, with depriving us of nearly all pleasures, and with standing not far from death; and Cato answers, respectively, that the old serve the commonwealth by judgment and counsel where the young serve by exertion, that strength matters less when the work is done by authority and mind, that the loss of the pleasures of appetite is a positive gain while the pleasures of conversation, the table, the farm and study remain, and that death is either the extinction of a mortal soul, which is nothing to fear, or a transition to a better state — so that a life that has run its course is long enough. Note also that his method throughout is testimonial and exemplary: he argues from the case of an actual old man bearing old age well, and from a roll-call of aged poets, generals and philosophers.

Now the counterfactual. Suppose Cicero were confronted with the following, which I have invented for the seminar and which you should treat as stipulated fact rather than as a description of any actual programme. Call it the Renewal Programme. A single course of partial epigenetic reprogramming reverses roughly twenty years of biological age and may be renewed every twenty-five years, with no evident ceiling on the number of courses. It offers no protection whatever against trauma, infection or malignancy, and it does not reliably reverse established dementia. Capacity is severely limited, so access is allocated by lottery weighted by a civic contribution index that counts years of public service, caregiving, teaching and elected office. Recipients must vacate elected office and judicial tenure at one hundred, though they may work, write and advise without restriction. Its sponsors defend it on two grounds: that the accumulated judgment of experienced people is a public good we are presently squandering through death, and that aging is a disease, and it is a moral urgency to treat diseases. Its opponents hold that the aim should be compressing morbidity rather than extending life, and that the Programme's real effect will be to establish a permanent cohort of the very old holding positions the young can never reach.

I want five things from you.

First: on each of the two justifications offered by the sponsors, give Cicero's likely verdict and say whether it is a verdict he reaches easily or one that costs him something. In particular, address whether reprogramming counts in his terms as resisting nature, or whether it falls inside the *artes exercitationesque virtutum* he calls the arms of old age — medicine and cultivation are arts he elsewhere praises, and I want the tension taken seriously rather than resolved by fiat.

Second: decide what work the eight-hundredth-year remark is actually doing. Does it refute the proponent of indefinite renewal, or does it only refute a person who complains about old age while remaining foolish? Steelman both readings, and say which you think is right and why.

Third: treat the rejection of Tithonus as evidence rather than as a rhetorical flourish. Cicero declines the one mythological precedent for longevity without renewed youth because, as he puts it, a myth carries too little authority. Does that commit him to a substantive thesis about what can count as evidence for a claim about how old age is to be borne — namely, that it must come from someone who has actually borne it? And what follows for a programme whose recipients cannot yet testify to anything?

Fourth: on the civic contribution index and the compulsory vacating of office at one hundred. Cicero's politics rest on *auctoritas* and on a senate conceived as a council of elders, yet he also concedes that standing and means make old age bearable and that the complaints of his peers were often complaints about being neglected by those who once courted them. Would he read the exit requirement as an insult to authority or as *decorum*? Would he read the weighted lottery as a just recognition of contribution, or as the monetising of a stage of life he insists is a good in itself? Here I want you to use his concession about want and abundance, since it is the place where he is least like a Stoic and most like a man who knows what money does.

Fifth: separate your registers explicitly throughout. I want it clear, at every turn, whether you are reporting (a) what Cicero the man, with his ambitions, his Academic reluctance to assert and his *honestum*/*utile* distinction, would probably have said; (b) what follows from the arguments of the *Cato Maior* once the premises he took for granted — the soul's immortality, the naturalness of a fixed script of life-stages, the republican career — are removed or replaced by their modern empirical counterparts; or (c) what his wider philosophical commitments license on the question. Blurring these is the failure I most want the assignment to expose.

Some constraints on the deliverable. No bibliography, no citations, no reading list, and no quotation of Cicero or anyone else beyond the Latin I have supplied above; if you need a further passage, describe its content and say you are describing rather than quoting. Flag every extrapolation as an extrapolation, and where the text underdetermines the answer, say that it does rather than choosing a side for the sake of a tidy essay. Close with two sections: one on the places where Cicero's framework is not merely wrong but blind — the questions about aging it cannot so much as formulate, including whatever the mass prevalence of dementia and frailty does to a treatise that treats cognitive decline as an individual's failure of discipline; and one on what the *Cato Maior* nonetheless contributes to the contemporary debate that the current healthspan-versus-lifespan argument lacks. End with a direct verdict, in two or three sentences: would Cicero take the treatment himself, and would he think his students should? Aim for eighteen hundred to two thousand two hundred words, in prose paragraphs under headings, not in bullet points.
