Under the Basel capital rules for market risk, a bank using internal models must report a single figure — the loss it will not exceed with 99 percent confidence over a ten-day holding period, estimated from its own historical data — which supervisors then multiply up into a capital charge. The same regime requires stress tests: scenarios that supervisors describe as severe but plausible and whose likelihood they generally decline to assess, with results treated as an additional capital demand rather than as a correction to the first figure. A third provision, backtesting, counts how many days actual losses exceeded the reported number and deems the model deficient once that count passes a threshold. What conception of the future does the point estimate presuppose — that the distribution of losses is a measurable feature of the world which a year of past data can reveal, or something weaker, such as that a determinate number is a serviceable convention for comparing banks and coordinating supervision? Reconstruct the implicit epistemology, separating what the practice strictly requires from what it merely suits well, and identify where it is philosophically underdetermined, so that rival accounts of probability would license the very same rule. Then say why a regime built on the first view would nonetheless insist on probability-free scenarios and on counting exceptions against its own numbers, whether these arms rest on consistent assumptions about what can be known in advance or quietly contradict one another, and what would have to change — in what banks report, in what supervisors can compare, in how much capital is held — if the requirement of a single probability-weighted figure were abandoned.
