Two of my grad students have been re-fighting the same argument about the wine-test in the first book of the Laws all term and I've ran out of ways to referee it. One says the Athenian's proposal to get citizens drunk under a sober symposiarch — as a cheap, controlled alternative to exposing them to stronger temptations — is a genuine late-Plato innovation: a pharmacological, inoculation-style pedagogy that the Republic's quarantine of the guardians and the Phaedo's disgust at bodily pleasure could never have produced. The other says that's stylometric paranoia: the wine there is diagnostic, not formative, it reveals a soul rather than shaping one, and anyway the Republic already runs guardian-candidates through fears and pleasures to test them, so nothing has shifted; the drinking in the second book is a separate business, a relief for the sourness of old age plus some civic bonding. Each round they come back with more passages and nobody moves an inch.

My hunch — and it is only a hunch — is that they aren't disagreeing about Plato at all. They're disagreeing about whether testing and training can come apart in his vocabulary, i.e. whether an exposure that merely reveals character can still count as education, and once you settle that the passage-collecting is beside the point. Is that the right diagnosis? Tell me what kind of disagreement this really is: verbal, substantive, two questions sharing one label, or a hidden methodological principle (about which dialogue gets to be the fixed point) doing all the work underneath. Use what each side is actually committed to in the text rather than the general developmentalism literature, and tell me what would count as settling it — a type of passage, an interpretive rule, a distinction they're both missing — or whether nothing will, in which case I'll just tell them to write it up as two papers.
