I am a doctoral candidate in early modern philosophy, currently drafting a chapter of my dissertation on the metaphysics of sentience, and I have come across a summary of Descartes' position on animals that I cannot decide how far to trust. It appears in the opening pages of a chapter in a recent handbook of animal ethics; the author is a clear writer but not a Descartes specialist, and the passage is doing introductory rather than interpretive work. Here it is:

"Descartes is the origin of the modern idea that animals do not count morally, and the reason he gives is metaphysical rather than sentimental. Having identified the soul with thought, and having argued in the Meditations that thought belongs to a substance wholly distinct from extension, he had no room for an animal mind: a being that does not think at all is not a mind, so whatever an animal does must be done by body alone. Animals are therefore machines, automata of the same kind as the hydraulic figures that moved in the gardens at Saint-Germain, and Descartes is explicit that they feel nothing — the yelp of a struck dog is the noise of a pump, no more accompanied by pain than the striking of a clock is accompanied by an awareness of the hour. Because he made language the test of thought, and animals plainly lack language, this is not an empirical hypothesis he might have revised in the light of better natural history but a consequence of the cogito itself. His followers drew the moral corollary without embarrassment: the Port-Royal solitaries opened up living dogs on the reasoning that a creature which cannot think cannot suffer, and the doctrine went largely unchallenged until Bentham made suffering rather than reason the criterion. To be fair to Descartes, he was no simple denier: he granted animals memory, trainability, and outward signs of fear and anger, and he wrote to Newcastle that their behaviour follows dispositions fixed in them by nature. But since for him all of this was arrangement of the machine, the concessions cost nothing."

My own instinct is that the opening moves are defensible and that the trouble begins at the claim that Descartes is explicit that animals feel nothing — I have read Cottingham's argument that the standard picture runs thought and sensation together, and I suspect this passage assumes the very point at issue. But I may be over-correcting toward the revisionist side, and I have no confidence whatever about the Port-Royal material or about the claim that the doctrine went unchallenged until Bentham.

Could you audit the passage claim by claim? I would like: (1) a separation of what is straightforwardly accurate, what is a tolerable simplification, and what is a misrepresentation, with the misrepresentations explained rather than merely labelled; (2) an explicit flagging of every place where the author presents one side of a live interpretive dispute as settled textual fact, together with what the received reading and the revisionist reading would each have to give up to avoid the difficulty; (3) an assessment of whether the two argumentative conclusions — that the beast-machine doctrine follows from the cogito rather than from observation, and that the concessions about memory, trainability, and instinct are costless — survive once the underlying theses are stated correctly, including whether the summary's account of Descartes' criterion for thought is complete; and (4) a separate verdict on the historical and reception claims, since those are the ones I am least equipped to check myself, and I want to know whether the moral attributed to the whole passage — that Descartes rather than his readers is responsible for excluding animals from moral consideration — can stand once the metaphysical claims are corrected. Please end by telling me which sentences of the passage I could cite with a clear conscience and which I would have to rewrite first, and what I would need to establish independently before relying on the summary in my own chapter.
