I'm not an academic — I just watch a lot of Shakespeare and read ethics stuff on my own in the evenings — and I've been stuck on The Merchant of Venice for months with one idea in my head: moral trade. By that I mean the thought that some things can't be bought and sold without being ruined, so that a deal can be wrong even when both sides consent and both reckon they're getting a good price.

The bits I keep coming back to. The grudge starts over interest: Shylock's complaint is that Antonio lends money for nothing and so drags down what everyone else in Venice can charge. Then the bond turns Antonio's own body into collatoral. Then in court Bassanio offers triple the money and Shylock refuses it, which looks to me like the point — he's declining the trade, not failing at it. Portia beats him by reading the contract literally, flesh but no blood, rather than by saying the trade was immoral, which makes me wonder whether the play thinks the deal was never valid becuase the good in it isnt the kind of thing you can hand over at all. And then the ring: Portia refuses cash for her services and asks for the ring instead, the one token that was supposed to sit outside any price, and Antonio of all people is the one who pushes Bassanio to give it up. Meanwhile Jessica runs off with the ducats and Shylock can't keep the two losses apart in his own head.

So: can the play be read as an argument about moral trade, and which argument? Is the bond a failed exchange because flesh is inalienable, or is the real point that Venice has already priced everything — wives, daughters, friends — and the bond just says the quiet part out loud? Does the ring plot work as the version of the trade that the bond botches, or am I forcing that? And where does the play push back — where would an honest critic have to admit the reading doesn't fit, and what does the play end up showing about buying and selling that a straightforward argument about it wouldn't?

Bottom line first please, in the very first sentence, then the reasoning after it — no preamble and no rehashing the question back at me. Also flag which parts of this are settled among people who study the play seriously and which are genuinely contested; I don't want someone's disputed take handed to me as the consensus. British English.
