I am drafting a novel built almost entirely out of attempts at influence: a campaign strategist who talks a grieving widow out of a lawsuit, a mentor who quietly reshapes a student's ambitions over four years, a spouse who arranges the household so that one option simply stops being thinkable. Before I write these scenes I want an instrument for sorting them, and I would like you to build it: a decision tree that begins with a described act of influence and terminates at a named position on what separates manipulation from legitimate persuasion.

Construct it from the logical space rather than from a roll of philosophers. Five to seven branch nodes, each phrased as a question about the scene that a reader could answer without knowing which leaf it leads toward — for instance: does the influence engage the target's own evaluative standards or route around them; does it exploit a condition the target did not choose (grief, exhaustion, debt, first love); would it survive being narrated aloud to the target by a hostile third party who knows the whole mechanism; does it widen or narrow the target's set of live options; is the influencer's own description of his motives reliable? Then tell me candidly which of these criteria I should cut, which carry genuine diagnostic weight, and which node I have overlooked.

The leaves should be labeled positions rather than people — roughly: wrong only when it deceives; wrong whenever it bypasses reasons-responsiveness; wrong only when it exploits a known vulnerability; permissible so long as the mechanism would be disclosed on request; and the deflationary view that all persuasion is manipulation wearing better clothes. Mark at least one region of the space you judge unoccupied or incoherent, and say which; flag any pair of leaves your tree cannot actually separate. Finally, commit: rule on the widow scene, place it, and defend the placement rather than presenting me a menu.
