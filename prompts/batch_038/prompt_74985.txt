I lead the perception stack for a company that builds indoor service robots, and I have no philosophy training beyond what I have read on my own. Three months ago we shipped a fix for a slow drift in our cameras' spectral response: rather than replace hardware, we inserted a correction table upstream of everything else, which restored the pixel statistics the network had been trained on. The robots behaved exactly as before, and their spoken reports about colors were exactly as before. When a colleague asked whether the robots' colors had changed during the drift and then changed back, I found I could not say what a yes or a no would consist in. That led me to Dennett's 1988 paper on qualia, with the coffee taster and the neuroscientist, and I have since formed a position I have been invited to present in twenty-five minutes at a joint industry-and-academic workshop on evaluating machine perception.

The workshop room is small and mixed in a way that matters to me. There are two philosophers of mind: one works in the tradition that takes the conceivability arguments and the knowledge argument seriously and treats phenomenal character as a real further feature of the world that a functional description leaves out; the other is a representationalist who thinks phenomenal character is nothing over and above representational content of the right kind, and so will not be moved by the first philosopher's intuitions but also will not accept my conclusion as stated. There is one color-vision neuroscientist who runs adaptation and opponent-process experiments on human subjects. The rest are robotics and machine learning engineers who mostly want to know whether anything I say will change how they build or test systems, and who will be irritated by a talk that has no operational consequences.

My thesis has two parts. The narrow part: for our robots, once you hold fixed everything the system actually exposes — sensor inputs, motor commands, discriminative behavior, and its own reports — the question whether the colors look the same to it is not a hard question with a hidden answer, it is a question that has not been fully posed. The general part: the same deflation carries over to human beings, so that the familiar thought experiment in which a person's experience inverts while every disposition, judgment, and report stays exactly the same is not a description of a possible case but a failure to specify one. I want to state a principle and defend it. I have been calling it the interface principle: if two configurations of a system cannot be told apart at any interface that system actually has, then whatever difference remains between them is a difference in our description rather than a difference in the system. My supporting argument runs roughly as follows. First, in our calibration incident there were two internal pipelines, the drifted one and the corrected one, and no measurement, no design decision, no test in our suite, and no report distinguished them. Second, when I tried to write down what evidence would settle whether the reds looked different to the machine, every candidate I produced turned out to be a claim about behavior or about internal state that was already fixed by the functional description. Third, a question for which no possible observation counts either way, and which never enters any decision, is not a question about the system. Fourth, this is not special to machines: the coffee taster case is in the same shape, because holding his judgments and dispositions fixed while altering his experience leaves nothing for the alteration to consist in. Fifth, therefore the intuition that qualia could come apart from function is an artifact of underdescription, not a datum a theory has to accommodate.

Write me the preparation document I actually need for the question period, which is not a defense of the thesis and not a summary of the literature on it. I want the seven or eight objections this particular room will produce, arranged in the order I expect them to arrive and weighted by how much damage each can do to the talk. For each one, give me the objection in the vocabulary and register of the person most likely to raise it — I want to hear how the property dualist phrases it, how the representationalist phrases it, how the neuroscientist phrases it, and how a skeptical engineer who does not care about metaphysics phrases it — followed by a reply I can deliver in about ninety seconds, and then an honest note on where that reply is soft or where it quietly assumes the interface principle it is supposed to be defending. Tell me what I should concede in my opening five minutes so that the concessions do not have to be dragged out of me later, and tell me which single objection is genuinely unanswerable in the time available and should be deflected rather than met. Finally, deal with the question I am hoping nobody asks, which I suspect is some version of this: on my own account my report that there is nothing further going on in me than what my interfaces expose has no more authority than the robot's report, so what entitles me to stand up and assert it; and its sharper companion, whether I am prepared to say out loud that there is nothing it is like to be me. Do not soften that one or leave it out because it is uncomfortable. I would rather rehearse it.

Two practical constraints. I cannot look anything up while I prepare, so please do not give me references, a bibliography, a further-reading list, or attributions to particular papers and pages, and do not put anything in quotation marks as though you were citing a text; I want the argumentative content only, and where you need to gesture at a well-known argument, describe it in your own words. Where you can, translate the philosophical objection into engineering terms I can hold in my head while someone is talking at me. Please write the whole thing as continuous prose in the manner of a long letter, with no headings, no bullet points, no numbered lists, no bold or italic emphasis, and no tables — I will read it aloud to myself while I walk, and I want it to run as sentences and paragraphs, ordering the objections with ordinary words like first and second rather than with list formatting. Around eighteen hundred words is right; shorter and I will not have enough to work with.
