I teach a graduate seminar on early Christian ethics, and I close it each year with a piece of fiction rather than another article, because the argument on its own leaves students cold. This year I want the closing piece commissioned rather than assigned. The brief below is more specific than most fiction commissions, deliberately so; the specificity is the point.

**The material.** Augustine wrote twice on lying: an early treatise *On Lying* (c. 395) and, some twenty-five years later, *Against Lying*, prompted by a correspondent's question about whether it would be permissible to feign adherence to the Priscillianist sect — a group that itself held lying licit under persecution — in order to get close enough to gather evidence against them. Augustine says no, and the reason is what interests me: a lie is not wrong primarily because of what it costs the person deceived, but because it installs a double heart in the speaker, one thing held in the mind and another produced in speech, and so turns speech against the purpose that makes it speech. On that criterion someone who says something false in good faith does not lie, and someone who says something true while believing it false does. He also grades lies in a descending catalogue, running from lies about matters of religious teaching, through lies that injure one person and benefit another, lies told to save a life, lies told to protect someone's bodily purity, down to lies that harm nobody and profit somebody. He permits concealment: silence is not a lie. And he handles the awkward scriptural cases — the Hebrew midwives who deceived Pharaoh, Rahab on the roof — by insisting that what was rewarded in them was their mercy, not their lie.

**The story.** Two thousand to two thousand six hundred words, present day, one point of view. The protagonist is a credentialed medical interpreter at a large public hospital, bound by a code of ethics that requires complete and accurate rendition: no additions, no omissions, no substitutions, no counsel of her own. She is not an Augustinian and has never read him; her constraint simply has the same shape as his, because her mouth has been made over to another speaker's words. Over one long shift she encounters eight temptations. Structure the story in eight sections corresponding to the eight grades, running from the least grave to the gravest; each section must be recognizably that grade from its content alone. No grade may be numbered, named, or glossed in the fiction.

Two further requirements, and they are the ones I care most about. First, the dramatic stakes must not track the grades: the beat carrying the most urgent worldly consequences should sit in a middling grade, while the gravest grade — the one about religious teaching — should arrive in something that looks, to everyone present, trivial: a formula being rendered at a bedside, a dying man's instruction about what his family is to be told, the precise wording of an assurance a chaplain asks her to carry across a language. Let the reader feel the disproportion before she does. Second, the ending should turn on the seam in Augustine's criterion — an utterance that is literally accurate and faithfully rendered yet arranged to produce a false belief, or a silence that is technically permitted and functionally decisive. Whether she has lied must remain genuinely unsettled on the page.

Craft constraints: no philosopher named anywhere, no dialogue that expounds doctrine, no interior monologue that reads like a seminar paper, no framing device in which someone explains the ethics afterward, no twist signposted in advance. Make her a particular person with a life outside the building, and make the building smell like a hospital. It should work completely for a reader who has never heard of Augustine. I am not asking for sources, a bibliography, or a list of texts consulted.

**After the story,** in 250 to 400 words, tell me: where the fiction takes liberties with the position or leans on a contested reading of it; whether the seam the ending exploits is real, or whether a careful defender of Augustine blocks it — for instance by insisting that the will to deceive is itself what constitutes the double heart, so that a true utterance deployed to mislead is still a lie; which of my characterizations above you think wrong or overconfident; and anything you are not sure of that I should check before I put this in front of students, including the exact number and ordering of the grades.

Finally, and please take this seriously: if the commission rests on a confused premise, say so before writing anything rather than complying politely. I can think of at least three ways it might. Eight sections may be structurally impossible without reducing the story to a checklist. A medical interpreter's code may be a false parallel to Augustine, since it governs fidelity to another speaker's words rather than to one's own mind — and if that collapses the whole conceit, I would rather hear it now. And the topmost grade may be undramatizable in a secular institution without turning into a sermon. If any of that is right, or if you see a better version of the thing I am reaching for, tell me plainly and tell me what you would do instead. I would much rather be told the commission cannot be filled as specified than be handed a graceful story that has quietly abandoned half the brief.
