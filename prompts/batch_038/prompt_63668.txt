I am drafting a short paper on Kavka's toxin puzzle, and before I commit to the central move I would like your own credence on it, with the reasoning laid out rather than summarized.

Here is the variant I am relying on. Everything is as in Kavka's original except for one addition. The billionaire places before you a vial of toxin which, if drunk, will make you painfully ill for a day without lasting harm. He will pay you one million dollars tomorrow morning if, at midnight tonight, you intend to drink the toxin tomorrow afternoon; you need not drink it to be paid, the money will be in your account hours before the appointed time, and you are perfectly free to change your mind. The addition is this. Beside the toxin sits a second vial containing a neutralizer which, taken at any point after the payment lands and before three in the afternoon, completely blocks both the illness and the taste, so that swallowing the toxin at three o'clock is physiologically and phenomenally indistinguishable from swallowing a glass of room-temperature water. The neutralizer is free, is yours, has no side effects, and you are certain you will take it at nine in the morning. So at three o'clock the drinking is harmless, painless, tasteless, and utterly pointless: the money is already yours, the billionaire has left the country, nobody is watching, and nobody will ever learn whether you drank. To close the usual exits, assume there are no side-bets with third parties, no reputation or self-image benefits, no audience, no self-modification, and no precommitment device beyond the one described; assume also that the scanner cannot be fooled by imagery, rehearsal, or self-deception, so the only route to the money is genuinely being in the state at midnight.

The proposition I want a number on is this: in that variant, an ordinary fully informed agent can at midnight form a genuine intention to drink the toxin at three in the afternoon, where genuine means the state has the usual functional profile of an intention rather than being a wish, a rehearsal, or a hope.

Give me your credence and then decompose it. I want the sub-questions the number depends on, a rough probability for each with a sentence of justification, and how they combine into the final figure. At minimum I expect you to separate the question of whether the impossibility in the original case is constitutive of intention or merely downstream of the agent's forecast of their own reasons at three o'clock; the question of whether the sheer pointlessness of an action, plus the small effort it takes, is enough for a rational agent to predict with confidence that they will not perform it; and the question of whether one can intend to do something one expects to do for no reason at all. Add or drop terms as you see fit, but be explicit about what you have assumed away. Then give me a second, smaller number: the probability that an affirmative answer on the main question would actually show that Kavka's moral is hostage to the payoff structure rather than to the nature of intention, as opposed to merely relocating the difficulty. Tell me which single term the main number is most sensitive to, and what argument or empirical finding would move it furthest. My own credence is 0.65; a colleague who works on intention says 0.25 and insists the variant is nothing but the original with a smaller number attached. Tell me which term the gap between us most likely sits in, and whether his dismissal is right.

Please write this as continuous prose, the way you would write a letter or a long email: no headings, no bullet points, no bold, no tables. I am not after references or a reading list, only your reasoning.
