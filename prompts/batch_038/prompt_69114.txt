Write a pantoum — quatrains in which the second and fourth lines of each stanza recur as the first and third of the next, and the first stanza's opening and third lines return to close the poem — spoken by an agent addressing the progressively idealized version of herself that a theory of indirect normativity summons to answer in her stead: better informed, given more time, calmer, more vivid in its imaginings, more internally coherent, iterated until the answer stops changing.

Give her one ordinary, concrete question she actually wants settled, and let the stanzas be successive rounds of idealization in which that question is quietly replaced by better ones she never asked. The form should carry the argument: because each round inherits the previous round's wording, return the repeated lines re-punctuated, re-scoped, or re-emphasized, so that the same sentence no longer means what the speaker who first said it meant by it. In the final stanza the closing lines must be plainly recognizable as the opening ones and plainly no longer answerable to the person who uttered them.

Aim for five or six stanzas. The poem should keep two failures distinct — that the iteration may never stabilize, and that if it does stabilize it may stabilize somewhere that has forfeited any claim to speak for the original agent — without naming either as an objection, adjudicating between them, or mentioning any philosopher by name.
