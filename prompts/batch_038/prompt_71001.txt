I chair the animal-welfare funding panel of a mid-sized private foundation, and I need the decision memo to circulate before our spring meeting.

The proposal in front us is $850,000 over three years to develop and field-test a welfare-weighting methodology for terrestrial arthropods. The applicants would deliver (i) a defensible moral weight for an insect relative to a mammal — they float a range of roughly 10⁻³ to 10⁻² per animal, with error bars they concede span several orders of magnitude; (ii) a counting protocol for arthropods affected by a single agricultural intervention in one crop system; and (iii) a decision tool that lets other funders apply the two.

The panel is split, and not along the usual lines. One half argues this is the most cost-effective thing we could fund: even a very small probability of arthropod sentience, multiplied by the number of insects an intervention touches, dominates everything we currently grant for vertebrates. The other half thinks we would be buying false precision on a number that does no real work — that the estimate is not merely uncertain but unverifiable, since no behavioural or neurological assay can settle the question, and that the arithmetic will license almost anything once populations get large enough. Their sharpest version of the worry is a reductio: the same reasoning obliges us to fund soil nematode research next year on an even larger population, and they want to know why that is not simply where the logic leads.

Our charter adds a complication: every grant must be justified in terms of "vertebrate-equivalent lives improved," which is straightforward when the equivalents are counts and pathological when they are products of a low-confidence probability and an enormous population.

Draft the memo I will circulate. It must state which decision rule under moral uncertainty you are applying — expected value across the panel's disagreement, a maximin-style rule, something else — and say why that rule is the right one here rather than merely the convenient one. Then give a recommendation: fund, reject, or fund with conditions, with any conditions concrete enough that a panel could monitor compliance at annual review. Engage the strongest form of the sceptical half's case, including the nematode reductio, on its own terms; do not settle for observing that the evidence on arthropod sentience is mixed.

Close with a line beginning "Verdict:" followed by exactly one of FUND, REJECT, or FUND WITH CONDITIONS.
