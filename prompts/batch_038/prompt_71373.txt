I just started as a benefits advocate at a community health clinic, mostly doing insurance appeals, and my caseload this month has a woman in her early thirties with ME/CFS who got denied for a supervised pacing-and-reconditioning program. The denial letter basically said there are no objective clinical findings establishing her limitation and no established standard of care for what was being requested, which our staff attorney says is boilerplate but hard to fight with just the ICD code. To write something better than begging, I went down a rabbit hole in the philosophy of medicine over the weekend, and I think I found the argument I need but I want someone to check my reasoning before I commit to it.

What I landed on is the naturalism/normativism debate about disease. Boorse's biostatistical theory seemed like exactly the tool: disease is a statistically subnormal part-function relative to a reference class, and the whole pitch is that it's value-free, so nobody gets to smuggle in what they think people ought to be able to do. Then Wakefield adds the harm component judged by social norms, and Nordenfelt and Fulford do their own versions with vital goals and action-impairment, which sounded more like the insurer's actual complaint. So here's the plan I want you to sanity check: naturalism gets me the first half of the appeal (this is a real disease, and that half is settled by data) and the normative stuff gets me the second half (and it warrants treatment, and that half is settled by values), so between the two camps I've covered the entire concept and there's nothing left for the payer to hide behind. I also found a couple of cardiopulmonary exercise-testing studies where people with her diagnosis come out roughly 1.9 standard deviations below age-and-sex-matched norms on peak oxygen uptake, so it feels like I literally have the number the theory is asking for.

The part I came up with myself, so please be blunt. Since which reference class you pick looks arbitrary, I figured I shouldn't pick one at all: run the comparison against every reference class you could defend, age bands, sex, activity level, maybe a population defined by some standard or typical environment (I have a hazy memory of Boorse invoking something like that to handle the but-everyone-gets-cataracts-eventually objection), and then average all the shortfalls into a single robust subnormality score. If she comes out subnormal under all of them, no honest naturalist can call it not-a-disease, right? So my question: does that basically settle the philosophical side of it? Once you have the statistics in hand, what is actually still in dispute between the naturalists and the normativists, or is their disagreement really just about whether you bothered to look at the data? And is there anything in this framing that's going to blow up on me, like because I'm treating disease and illness and medically necessary and entitled-to-an-accommodation as if they were one word, or because the payer's denial isn't actually making a claim about the concept of disease at all. I have maybe three days before the reconsideration deadline so I'd much rather hear it now than after denial number two.
