For forty years I worked as a crystallographer; since retiring I have taken up philosophy, and a puzzle from the laboratory has followed me into it.

In my old field, two specimens with the same structure, composition, and defect chemistry were interchangeable, and provenance was a footnote on a museum label. The gem trade thinks otherwise. A grading certificate reports the intrinsic quality parameters — cut, color, clarity, carat weight — and then, as a separate line item, whether the stone formed in the earth or was grown in a laboratory; disclosure of that difference is required by consumer-protection rules in most large markets. The trade's taxonomy has three tiers: a simulant of different material sold as a diamond is fraud; a laboratory-grown diamond, identical in material and structure, is honest but must be labeled and sells for a fraction of the price; and a natural stone whose fractures have been filled or whose color has been altered by irradiation must have the treatment disclosed, on the ground that the improvement forms no part of the stone's own history. Note that origin cannot be read off anything visible at the counter: it is inferred from nitrogen aggregation, growth banding, and trace inclusions — present traces of a past process.

What theory of value, and of kinds, does this practice presuppose? Must the trade hold that origin is essential to what a stone is, surviving the identity of every intrinsic feature, or would a purely contractual story — disclose it because buyers care, and their caring is all there is to it — license everything the practice actually does? Please distinguish what the practice strictly requires from what merely fits it comfortably, and mark where it is underdetermined, so that rival theories would authorize the same certificate. Then tell me what would have to change, at the bench and in the law, if one adopted the view I was trained in: that worth supervenes on a thing's present physical properties, so that two specimens alike in every respect are alike in value.
