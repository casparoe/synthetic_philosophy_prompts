For eleven years I worked at a proxy-advisory firm, and I now run a four-person research team that maintains a proprietary governance scorecard for what we call controlled companies — issuers where a founder, a family, or a government holds enough ordinary votes, or a class of stock with enough votes per share, to determine outcomes regardless of what the minority holders want. The scorecard runs on dimensions like the independence of the audit and remuneration committees, disclosure and approval requirements around related-party transactions, whether a dual-class structure carries a sunset date, the minority approval threshold for fundamental transactions such as a going-private merger, the degree of information symmetry between controller and minorities, and whether succession is planned and key-person risk is priced. My colleagues and I think natively in this vocabulary: controlling shareholder, agency cost, tunneling, bonding mechanism, credible commitment, exit and voice, stewardship code, business judgment rule, residual claim. My political philosophy is close to nil — I have absorbed what little I know secondhand, from magazines and podcasts — and last month a client, a pension fund whose mandate lets it hold both mainland Chinese and Singaporean equities, asked whether the scorecard could be extended from issuers to jurisdictions. That request is the reason I am writing, because I suspect the answer turns on a conceptual question I am not equipped to formulate. I do not want the question explained to me in plain English. I want it translated into my language, and I want to be told what breaks in the translation.

The question is the one people argue about when they ask whether prosperity and order can justify rule without authorization. Take a regime that has delivered four decades of rapid growth, lifted hundreds of millions of people out of rural poverty, kept violent crime low and the enforcement of ordinary contracts predictable, and has done all of this without competitive elections for national leadership. Its defenders argue that delivery is sufficient: a government that supplies security, infrastructure, and rising living standards has earned the right to rule, and authorization by the governed is a procedural preference with no moral weight of its own. Political scientists have a distinction for this, between legitimacy that comes from what a government delivers and legitimacy that comes from how its rulers are selected; the economist's version is Mancur Olson's stationary bandit — a ruler who expects to remain in place long enough to collect the returns acquires an encompassing interest in his domain and will therefore supply public goods rather than loot. The critics reply that the arrangement is self-undermining: performance-based legitimacy sets a benchmark that must keep moving upward, gives the ruler no way to commit credibly to rules he announced himself, removes the only mechanism by which a society changes leadership without violence, and treats citizens as beneficiaries of competent management rather than as the source of authority. Render both sides, term by term, in the language of my scorecard — the state as controlled company, the leadership as controller, citizens as minority holders with no exit, growth and public goods as returns, elections and constitutional limits as minority protections, managed information as asymmetry maintained by the controller, suppressed protest as the elimination of shareholder proposals and derivative suits — as faithfully as you can, and then tell me whether my scorecard already contains an answer to the political question, and at which line item that answer was smuggled in.

Then give me the residue report, which is the part I actually want. Sort the central claims on each side into those that translate cleanly, those that translate only with a distortion you should name explicitly, and those that have no counterpart in the vocabulary at all. Test four suspicions of mine in particular: that the polity's principal includes people who hold no claim whatsoever — the unborn, resident non-citizens, those immiserated or displaced by the growth itself — whereas my minority holders are defined by their stake; that consent and authorization have no governance counterpart other than the founding contract and the vote, and that both fail here because shareholders can sell and citizens cannot; that the right to tax, imprison, and conscript is not a scaled-up version of a controller's contractual powers, so that the governance vocabulary can register such powers only as reductions in return; and that whatever is meant by standing as the source of authority, rather than the object of good management, simply has no line item. Tell me which way the residue cuts: is my framework missing concepts that pick out something real about political authority, in which case our scorecard measures something narrower than we advertise and should stop implying otherwise — or does the failure to translate suggest the original debate was less clear than it sounds, and that talk about the right to rule is mostly talk about the risk-adjusted durability of a control arrangement? Finally, run the translation on three cases so I can see whether it yields the verdicts I expect: the high-delivery regime with no national elections; Singapore, with regular competitive elections, one dominant party throughout its independent history, first-world delivery, and an information environment and succession process my scorecard would flag hard — does Singapore come out as a well-run controlled company with weak minority protections, or is there something in the political description the governance description cannot hold? — and a rentier autocracy with neither delivery nor elections, where my scorecard and the political verdict should visibly come apart. Two cautions. Do not settle the question by definition: if you translate legitimate as durable or as value-maximizing and then report that the debate dissolves, that is a substitution, not a translation, and I would rather be told the term has no counterpart. And do not treat property rights, contract enforcement, and the courts that interpret them as pre-political background my scorecard may take for granted; supplying them is part of what the regime does, and the governance vocabulary has a habit of treating them as the stage rather than the performance. I am not after a bibliography — if you invoke an argument, tell me what it says rather than who said it.
