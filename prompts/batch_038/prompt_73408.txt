Consider the following counterfactual. Benjamin Franklin — not the aphorist of the almanacs but the reflective author of the *Autobiography*, who as a young printer conceived the project of arriving at moral perfection by means of a little book containing a page for each of thirteen virtues, ruled in columns for the days of the week, with one virtue under focused examination per week and four cycles through the list per year, and who in old age recounted both the scheme and its failure — is brought into the present day, given a working command of contemporary English and technology, and shown three products, each marketed by its makers as an instrument of moral improvement.

**One.** A subscription habit-tracking application. It decomposes a user's character into a list of named traits, each scored by daily self-report; it maintains streaks, issues notifications at the hour a lapse is likeliest, and displays a cumulative dashboard of the year's faults. The free tier unlocks five traits; the remaining ones require a premium subscription. The complete ledger, including every recorded failure, is retained on the developer's servers, used to train the firm's models, and licensed in aggregate to advertisers.

**Two.** A bioethicist's proposal for pharmacological moral enhancement: a daily compound that reliably increases empathic responsiveness and dampens impulsive aggression, to be offered to volunteers and, in some jurisdictions, mandated for certain offenders as an alternative to incarceration.

**Three.** An artificial-intelligence accountability partner that reads a user's correspondence and calendar, infers which of the user's stated commitments the day's conduct has violated, and intervenes with corrective prompts — in effect, an automated version of the little book, with the examiner built in.

Your task is to predict Franklin's considered judgment. Please address the following:

1. For each of the three, a verdict — adopt as it stands, adopt only after specific redesign, or refuse — together with the reasoning that gets him there.
2. Which of the three he would most want to build and sell himself, and precisely what he would alter in its design, given what he says about the mechanics of his own experiment.
3. At least two places where the obvious prediction breaks down. Franklin is routinely cast as an Enlightenment optimizer, a proto-enthusiast of self-measurement, and the natural patron of all three products; explain where his own account of the scheme's limits resists that casting, drawing on his treatment of Order as the virtue he never mastered, the episode of the ax brought to the grindstone until the owner decided he preferred it speckled, his claim about what the attempt nevertheless yielded, and his unexecuted plans both to publish a manual on the art of virtue and to found a society of young men pledged to the scheme.
4. Whether he would regard a streak-preserving dashboard as cultivating virtue or only its outward appearance — a question on which his lifelong enjoyment of self-presentation, pseudonymous personae, and elaborate hoaxes cuts in both directions.
5. What a man who numbered Frugality and Industry among his virtues would make of a firm whose revenue derives from the record of its customers' moral failures: an honest trade, or an indecent one?

Write between 1,100 and 1,300 words, under brief headings, in continuous prose. Ground every claim about Franklin in some identifiable feature of his moral project as he describes it, but paraphrase throughout: do not quote him, cite editions or pages, or append a bibliography. Resist undifferentiated ambivalence — the three products should not all receive the same qualified verdict — and ensure that at least one of your conclusions runs against what a modern admirer of Franklin would expect of him.
