For six years I have co-organized a small annual workshop that pairs philosophers of biology with working molecular biologists, and the same argument recurs each time without anyone moving. The question is whether DNA literally carries information, or whether 'genetic information', 'the code', and talk of reading or editing a genome are metaphors that have hardened into jargon. The camps are stable, and I think I can state them without caricature. On one side, a deflationary line — associated in this literature with Sahotra Sarkar's skeptical treatment of the informational idioms of molecular biology — holds that the cell contains no semantic facts, only physical ones: a template, a catalyst, a set of concentration gradients, and a highly convenient way of talking. On the other side, defenders of literal information divide into at least two sub-camps: those who ground biological information in natural selection, on the Maynard Smith-style thought that a signal carries information only where the association between signal and response was shaped by selection, which DNA satisfies and which an inert chemical does not; and those who take a teleosemantic or naturalizing-content route, on which DNA's function is to represent features of the organism. In between stand the developmental systems theorists, who argue that the whole framing is a hangover from a pre-molecular picture: on causal parity, DNA is one developmental resource among many, and the informational vocabulary is precisely what smuggles in its privilege.

My working hypothesis — offered as a hypothesis, which I want tested rather than flattered — is that the workshop stalls because at least three distinct claims are being argued over under a single banner, and participants keep switching between them:

(i) a semantic claim: DNA has content; it is about something;
(ii) a coding claim: the mapping from nucleotide triplets to amino acids is combinatorial, largely arbitrary, and stable across contexts in the way a cipher or a telegraphic code is;
(iii) a causal-specificity claim: interventions on sequence produce more finely discriminated effects on phenotype than interventions on any other developmental resource.

My suspicion is that (ii) is nearly undisputed and does very little work in the argument; that critics of (i) are routinely heard as critics of (iii), which they need not be; and that defenders of (iii) — including those who appeal to the information-theoretic measures of causal specificity that have grown up around the parity debate — present their results as support for (i), which those results cannot supply, since a number telling us how much difference a difference in sequence makes says nothing about whether anything is about anything.

Diagnose the disagreement. Is it a verbal dispute over the word 'information', in the sense that the parties would stop disagreeing once the ambiguity were resolved and each said what they meant; a substantive disagreement about the metaphysics of content that the ambiguity merely obscures; or two different questions — one about scientific practice and what explanatory work the vocabulary does, one about whether semantic properties exist in nature at all — being pursued under one heading? Take up specifically the possibility that (iii) is a live empirical and formal question that has already been partly answered while (i) is not, and say whether that asymmetry explains the impasse. Then tell me what would count as progress: a distinction, a formal result about specificity, an experiment, a change of question — or whether the honest position is that no progress should be expected, and why. If you think my three-way split is wrong, say so and give a better decomposition. No bibliography, no reading list, and no citations; I want the diagnosis argued from the content of the positions as described here.

Begin with your bottom line in the first sentence — the diagnosis stated outright — and give the reasoning after it. No preamble, and no restating of the question.
