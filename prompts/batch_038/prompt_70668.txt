In a metaphysics seminar I attended, a participant waved off a question about quantum indistinguishability with the slogan "no two things can be exactly alike," arguing that if two particles in a symmetric state really shared every property, there would be no fact of the matter about there being two of them — so the worry dissolves. I am not convinced the slogan was doing any determinate work there, and I want to see what it could be doing.

Please lay out the distinct principles this slogan might stand for, varying at least the following: whether the properties quantified over include purely relational ones, spatiotemporal ones, and self-identifying ones such as being identical to a particular object; whether the claim concerns the actual world or any possible world; whether indiscernibility is said to entail numerical identity or merely to leave a purported numerical difference unmotivated; and whether the principle is offered as a conceptual truth or as an empirical regularity. For each reading, judge whether it is trivially true, substantive and defensible, or simply false, and say whether it survives the familiar thought experiment of a universe containing nothing but two exactly similar spheres held a fixed distance apart — noting which background assumptions about space and relation that thought experiment smuggles in.

Finally, identify which reading my seminar participant actually needed for his conclusion, and whether any reading on your list supports it.
