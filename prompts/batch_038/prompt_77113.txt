I'm the only program officer at a small private foundation — about $6M a year, three board members, one of whom made his money in venture capital and will not shut up about option value. Anything over $250k goes to the full board; under that I can approve it myself with one analyst signing alongside me.

Roughly a third of what lands in my inbox now justifies itself by pointing at an enormous but wildly uncertain payoff: AI governance research that might avert a catastrophe, a speculative stratospheric-aerosol field test, a "seed life on other worlds eventually" proposal, a standing prize for a hard verification problem. Our application form asks for expected impact, and these people cheerfully hand me a probability with four significant figures attached to a chain of assumptions nobody could check. Meanwhile the dull, well-evidenced stuff we also fund — lead abatement in old housing stock, vocational training — never gets mentioned in the same breath, and I can't tell whether that's because it's genuinely smaller or because my team and I are bad at comparing them.

What I need is a written decision procedure that my two part-time analysts and I can actually run on a proposal in about 45 minutes, with no more decision theory than an undergraduate econ course, that tells us when a huge-but-improbable payoff is allowed to dominate the decision and when it isn't. Not an essay about the debate — a sequence of steps, the questions in order, what counts as an adequate answer to each, the point at which the procedure just says no, and the points where it stops and hands the thing up to the board because the remaining question is about values rather than facts. If a step is there because of a known argument in the literature, say so in a clause, but don't hand me a reading list.

Things it has to deal with honestly, because we keep tripping over them:

- The probabilities are made up. What does the procedure do with a claimed 1-in-10,000 that comes out of the applicant's own speculative model rather than anything resembling a frequency or a track record? Is "divide it by ten" a legitimate move or a fudge dressed as rigor, and if not that, what?
- Selection. I see maybe eight of these a year, and the ones that reach me have already survived the applicants deciding a tail claim was worth pitching. Should the procedure treat the pool as biased upward, and how, without just becoming a blanket discount?
- Cheap information. Sometimes a small study would collapse most of the uncertainty. Should the procedure be able to output "fund the $40k thing that resolves the disagreement instead of the $2M thing that bets on it," and what stops that step from swallowing every proposal forever?
- Two-sided bets. The aerosol test has a huge claimed upside and a plausibly catastrophic downside. Do the two get to cancel each other in the arithmetic? Sometimes the math comes out positive and nobody on the board would sign it, and I want to know whether the procedure should record that gap or paper over it.
- Repetition. I make these decisions a few times a year for maybe the next thirty. Does the fact that it isn't a one-shot decision change what any single proposal is worth, and if it does, how do I write that into a step rather than a vibe?
- Our own scale. We are $6M. Does the procedure have anything to say about whether a marginal contribution that small can inherit the full value of the outcome it helps produce, or whether that's a question I should stop pretending to settle?
- The refusal problem, which is the one I care about most. A procedure that systematically discounts small probabilities will be badly wrong in exactly the case where the tail was real, and that case dwarfs everything else in the ledger. I don't want the procedure to be "ignore the tail," because that's the same mistake pointed the other way. Tell me what it does instead, and don't just split the difference.
- And the case where nobody can give me a number at all, and the honest answer is that the step is unscoreable.

Then run it end to end on three proposals: the aerosol field test; the lead abatement program competing for the same money in the same quarter; and one you invent that the procedure *passes* and that I will hate — I want to watch it misfire in the direction that doesn't flatter my priors, not only in the direction that does. Show the actual answers to each question, including where it terminates early and where it escalates.

Finally, tell me what the procedure can't do: which steps are judgment calls wearing a lab coat, what it will get wrong systematically over a decade of use, what it costs us to be wrong in each direction, and what I could watch for in our own grant records that would tell me its breaking.
