You are a philosopher of science specializing in Bayesian confirmation theory, and you have thought hard about the problem of old evidence. Think carefully, and before writing anything, brainstorm out loud: list two or three candidate settings, note in one line each which constraint strains them, then commit to one.

Your task is to construct a thought experiment in which the problem of old evidence bites in a way the standard fixes cannot evade — a genuine new difficulty, not a restatement of Glymour's original worry. Constraints:

- **Setting.** Realistic and non-canonical. No Einstein, no Mercury, no phlogiston, no heliocentrism. Give me a working scientist of an ordinary kind — a materials engineer, a plant physiologist, an actuary, an epidemiologist — with a long-familiar dataset.
- **The old datum.** E must have sat in her evidence base for years at probability exactly 1: already conditionalized on, re-measured repeatedly, no residual doubt. Rule out by construction the escape that her P(E) was really 0.995.
- **The new hypothesis.** T entails E but must not beg the question: no conjunct that is E, no relabelling of E. The entailment must take real work to see (she spends weeks deriving it), and T must carry independent content, including at least one untested novel prediction.
- **A rival.** Include T′, in her hypothesis space all along with the same prior as T, which does not entail E. The point is to make vivid that T looks confirmed relative to T′ and to force the question of where, formally, that confirmation could come from.
- No demons, no perfect predictors, no science fiction, no gerrymandered priors. Two paragraphs maximum.

Then deliver: (1) the arithmetic — her priors, what strict conditionalization on anything she can now learn gives for P(T), and the size of the gap between that number and the support the intuition demands; (2) each of the three familiar maneuvers run against this exact case — conditionalizing on the proposition that T entails E, lowering P(E) and updating kinetically, and excising E from the background — with a verdict on whether it survives here and, if not, which stipulation kills it; (3) which single stipulation does the most work, and what the case becomes if I delete it; (4) the strongest attack from a Bayesian who denies the problem is real and would call my case ill-posed rather than hard, plus your judgment on whether that attack is fair.

What the case is for: testing proposed solutions against realistic, non-idealized agents. So a scenario that announces its verdicts instead of generating them — one that tells me conditionalization delivers no support without deriving that from her stated probability assignments — fails.
