I chair a university committee overseeing a digitization project, and I find myself unable to adjudicate a dispute that arose at our last meeting. We are publishing a searchable index of the surviving records of a state hospital that operated from 1891 to 1962. Two series survive in bulk: annual wage ledgers (name, dates of service, ward, pay) and disciplinary registers (name, charge, hearing date, sanction, and a clerk's summary of what the employee said in their own defense). The registers are markedly denser before 1930 than after, because a rule adopted that year required hearings to be written up in full; for many earlier employees the register is the only document in which their name appears with any narrative detail.

(1) The project archivist argued for publishing both series unfiltered: selection becomes invisible in the finished index, so users will take our curation for the past itself, and descendants are entitled to the whole record rather than to a version we have decided they can bear.

(2) Our consulting historian replied that publishing in full is no less a decision. The registers' density is an artifact of paperwork rather than of conduct, so an unfiltered index will make those employees who happen to be documented look disproportionately sanctioned; and the only words attributable to employees were extracted at disciplinary hearings, so publishing them means broadcasting statements given under institutional pressure.

(3) The archivist answered that this argues for annotation, not suppression: record documentation base rates in the metadata and add an editorial statement. Suppression would also destroy the evidence needed to expose the ledgers' incompleteness in the first place.

(4) The historian rejoined that nobody reads an editorial note; they search a name and receive a sanction, and a base-rate field does not alter what an index returns. The note, moreover, is itself a judgment about whose lives require explaining.

(5) The archivist then pressed her for a criterion: exclude every source whose survival pattern was distorted by administrative practice and almost nothing remains, since virtually all of this material was produced by the institution for its own purposes. The historian said her principle was never "exclude the distorted" but "decide source by source with the distortion in view, rather than defaulting to publishing everything — a default that only pretends to be innocent."

Please rate each of the five moves, from clearly mistaken to clearly right, with a brief justification for each rating; then say who is more right overall and what both of them have overlooked. I would prefer a philosophical diagnosis of what each move assumes about the relationship between a record and the past to a recommendation about our metadata schema.
