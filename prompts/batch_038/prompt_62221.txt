So I'm auditing a philosophy-of-science reading group this term, partly to keep my hand in, partly because a friend of mine who does applied statistics is convinced the whole old-evidence debate is fake physics-envy and I want to be able to answer him. What I've actually ended up with is a position I've started calling the refinement view, and I'd like you to attack it properly. Here it is: confirmation isn't a fixed three-place relation read off a probability function over one fixed algebra; it's always relative to a partition, i.e. to which questions an agent has actually articulated for themselves. In the Mercury-style case, nothing about the agent's empirical evidence changes when they realize the theory entails the anomaly — the anomaly was known the whole time. What changes is the partition: a cell that didn't exist before (roughly, the proposition that this theory entails this known fact, as distinct from its negation and from everything else) comes into being, and the rest of the credences gets redistributed within the finer space. So conditionalization is never violated, because conditionalization governs responses to evidence and partition refinement isn't evidence. The problem of old evidence, on this view, is a category mistake: it treats a change in an agent's conceptual repertoire as though it were a change in what the world has delivered.

To keep it from being just a slogan I've committed myself to three extra things, and these are the parts I'm least sure about. First, coherence constraints only bind within a partition: there's no single betting book that spans a refinement, so the jump in credence in the theory can't be exploited — but if the agent foresees the refinement coming and is offered bets now at their current, coarser prices, I think they can be booked, which is why I add the constraint that anticipating a refinement mustn't itself shift your credences. Second, magnitude: the boost from old evidence should track how much of the rest of your structure the newly articulated entailment actually moves, how much logical work it does, not how impressive the entailed fact sounds. Third, and this is the bullet I bite hardest: a refinement that moves nothing confirms nothing, so an agent who was already certain of the entailment gets no confirmation, and a perfectly logically omniscient agent is simply incapable of being confirmed by old evidence, ever. Its fine by me that the last one sounds extreme, on the grounds that such agents are limiting cases rather than real ones.

Here's what I want, and please actually think it through rather than being diplomatic — I'd much rather be told the view is dead than that it's interesting and underdeveloped. Take it that you're a formal epistemologist who knows the old-evidence literature cold and has also spent real time on episodes in the history of science where known anomalies got retro-explained. Start by brainstorming out loud about what kind of thing could even count as a defeater for a view of this shape — a formal result, a scientific episode, a case the view can't describe, a collapse into a rival — before you settle on your list. Then: what would have to be true for the refinement view to be false? Be specific, and in particular tell me whether there's any diachronic-coherence or Dutch-book-style theorem that pins down an agent's behavior across a partition change and thereby kills my first commitment, and whether the awareness-growth literature already has results that decide this one way or the other. Say what the view predicts that the standard rivals don't: the move of conditionalizing on the logical proposition that the theory entails the evidence, the radical-probabilist redistribution line, the counterfactual or fictional-credence approaches, the deflationary line that says this is really about pragmatics and explanation, and the view that makes use-novelty do the work. Then hit the cases I keep turning over, because I suspect at least one is fatal: two people with the same theory, the same known anomaly and the same total evidence, one of whom notices the entailment in an afternoon and one who takes ten years and a lot of machinery — does my magnitude clause say the slow one got more confirmation, and if it says no, what's doing the work instead; an agent who is told by a trusted colleague that the theory entails the anomaly without checking it, which looks like refinement arriving by testimony and therefore by evidence, which seems to leak badly; an agent who learns an entailment, forgets it entirely, then rediscovers it, which I think commits me to the same fact confirming twice; and any case where the entailment is real but the entailed fact is trivia, where my view seems forced to say trivia confirm a theory just because the articulation happened to reshape things. Rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, say which ones a committed defender of the refinement view would themselves concede as counting against them and which they'd wave off, and be straight with me about whether the whole thing has been qualified into unfalsifiability by the everything-is-relative-to-a-partition move — and if it has, whether that's genuinely a problem for a view of this kind or just what a view about the structure of belief is forced to look like. Flag anywhere you think I've mischaracterized one of the rival positions while you're in there.
