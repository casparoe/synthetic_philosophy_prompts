I have been turning over a question about displays and what makes them convincing, and I would like to know whether my judgments add up to a single principle. Here is where I land, case by case. A company that backs its product with a lifetime warranty persuades me that the product is good; a company that buys advertisements saying it is the best does not. A man wearing a very expensive watch convinces me that he has money and simultaneously makes me trust him less. A friend who flies across the country to sit with me after a death moves me in a way that an elaborate bouquet from the same friend does not. A guest who pays ten thousand dollars a plate at a charity gala impresses me less than someone who gives the same sum and asks that it not be mentioned. A person who abandons a secure career to write a novel does not thereby convince me that he can write, even though what he has given up is enormous. The peacock's outlandish tail seems to me to say something true about the peacock. And here is the pair I cannot reconcile: a technology founder who turns up to a meeting in a rumpled t-shirt reads to me as confidence, while a candidate interviewing for a job with my firm dressed exactly the same way reads as contempt.

Is there one principle, stated precisely, that delivers all of these verdicts, with each clause tied to the case that motivates it? I have gathered that the usual answer has something to do with faking being too expensive, but I cannot see how that handles the man who quit his job to write, or the anonymous donor, who has no audience at all. Tell me also what the principle predicts about two cases I have not listed: a restaurant with a queue around the block, and a well-paid executive who takes a large pay cut to accept a public office. And if the only principle that saves everything turns out to be baroque, say so plainly and give me the simpler one instead, naming the verdict I have to surrender.
