hey — second year, nominally philosophy of language but drifting into feminist epistemology, and I've got a workshop deadline in three weeks for a paper on Hofstadter's Person Paper (the William Satire one in Metamagical Themas, where he transposes the generic-man fight onto race). Below is the skeleton plus the bits I've actually drafted. I'd rather get rough comments now than polite ones later.

---

**1. Set-up.** The piece transposes the argument over generic 'man' onto a different social category: an outraged narrator defends the ancient practice of using white as a cover term for human being — hence chairwhite, mailwhite, clergywhite, whiteslaughter, oneupuwhiteship — and treats the reformers, whom he calls negrists, as political fanatics who fabricated the charge of racism by analogy with sexism. Everyone I've talked to reports the same thing: somewhere around the middle you feel vaguely sick, and the case against generic 'man' lands harder than it does from any straightforward argument. My question is what kind of event that landing is.

**2. The standard reading, and why I think its wrong.** The standard reading is that the piece is a reductio: if generic white is offensive, generic man must be too, the only difference being which category gets marked. I want to argue it isn't doing that. The narrator never argues and the reader never evaluates a premise. What happens is that the reader is made to occupy the standpoint of someone with a stake in the cover term and discovers, from inside, that the position is special pleading. That's perspective-shifting, not reductio, and the difference matters: a reductio transfers its conclusion to anyone who grants the premises, whereas a perspective-shift only fires in readers who already have the relevant sensibility. Corollary: the piece is predictably misread as sincere crankery by exactly the audience it can't reach. (I have a footnote stub about the mail Hofstadter supposedly got, but I can't verify it, so it's probably getting cut.)

**3. What I'll call the transposition test.** Generalized: to find out whether a term's generic use is benign, transpose it onto another social category and see whether your intuition survives; if it doesn't, the original intuition was doing unacknowledged work. This gets used all over applied ethics — the species transpositions in animal ethics are the obvious case — and I want to say what it can and cannot deliver.

**4. The objection I'm most afraid of.** The transposition isn't symmetric. White was never in fact a cover term for human being in English, so the piece doesn't test a real linguistic practice at all; it tests a hypothetical one that sits next to a very real history of racial exclusion. If that's right, the queasy feeling is imported wholesale from that history rather than generated by the structure of cover terms, which would make the piece's evidential value zero and its rhetorical value huge. That's basically my thesis, but I worry it's a strawman: Hofstadter could just say the point was never evidential, and accusing a thought experiment of bad logic is a category error.

**5. Postscript: which reforms actually stuck.** Chair, firefighter, salesperson, police officer, humankind won; mailman, freshman, foreman, craftsman held. My hunch is that the winners are the ones where a same-length, same-register synonym was already available and the losers are lexicalized occupational titles with no cheap substitute — i.e. what changed was morphological convenience, not moral perception. Probably armchair linguistics; not sure whether to keep it.

**6. Conclusion.** The piece is a better instrument for diagnosing an audience's default assumptions than for establishing anything about meaning, and satire in philosophy should be assessed on those terms.
