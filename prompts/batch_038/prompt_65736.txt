I am co-teaching an upper-level undergraduate seminar on moral responsibility this term, and I need a matched pair of cases on the doctrine of double effect that my students cannot pattern-match. Every one of them has already met the trolley, the transplant surgeon, and the strategic bomber, and my colleague — a consequentialist who has promised to attack whatever I bring — has pointed out, fairly, that in most textbook versions the case has been narrated so that the intended verdict is simply announced rather than generated. So I want the pair set somewhere mundane and institutionally detailed. I had in mind a wildland fire incident commander trying to keep a fire out of a town, though I am open to a comparable civilian setting provided it is not medical triage, not military, and not a trolley wearing a costume. The two cases must involve exactly the same harm falling on exactly the same private party — say, the same acreage of a farmer's standing crop and the same equipment shed — and must deliver exactly the same benefit to exactly the same number of people, so that no arithmetic asymmetry and no difference in proportionality is available to do any of the work. The only intended difference is structural: in the first case the destruction of the property is part of what brings about the good, since a fuel break works precisely because the fuel is removed; in the second, an identical amount of destruction is a byproduct that contributes nothing to the outcome, as when the crew reaches a natural firebreak by another route and the same crop and shed are lost under heavy equipment on the way there.

The design constraints I care about most are these. First, the difference has to fall out of the causal and institutional structure of the two scenarios; you may not simply tell us in the narration that one commander intends the damage and the other merely foresees it, because that is the move that makes classroom versions of the doctrine feel rigged. If you characterize the agents' deliberation at all, do it through what each would do given an available alternative, and make sure both commanders pass the counterfactual test in the same way: each would gladly forgo the damage if it could be avoided at no cost to the operation. I want that symmetry to be visible, because it is what forces the analysis to say something other than "he would have avoided it if he could." Second, hold fixed everything else that might contaminate the comparison — the same warnings issued to the owner, the same attempt to reach him by radio, the same offer of compensation afterward, the same risk borne by the crew, the same confidence in the weather forecast, the same legal exposure. Third, tell me honestly whether the redescription strategy — the objection that the first commander can always describe his intention more narrowly, as removing combustible material rather than ruining this person's livelihood — collapses the pair, and if it does collapse them, say so rather than quietly engineering around it.

For the deliverable: the two cases in plain prose, no more than about two hundred words each and written so they could be handed out cold; then a walkthrough identifying which condition of the doctrine is doing the discriminating work in each case and why the remaining conditions are silent or equally satisfied; then a diagnosis of which single feature of the first case carries the weight, what happens to the analysis if that feature is removed, and the smallest realistic modification that would flip a double-effect theorist's verdict. Close with the strongest objection a skeptic of the doctrine would press against this specific pair — the objection that the distinction is a rationalization constructed after the fact by an agent who wants to be seen doing something rather than merely permitting it — and the best reply a defender can make without smuggling in facts about the setting that the cases do not already contain.
