You are a political philosopher who works on corruption and the normative foundations of campaign-finance and lobbying regulation. Please think this through deliberately: before settling on a reconstruction, sketch two or three rival candidate theories of what makes political influence undue, and note what each would predict about the shape of the rules.

I am a political theory PhD student drafting a chapter on the American regime, and three of its central features do not look to me like they belong to a single theory of influence. First, an individual's contribution to a candidate is capped and bribery requires a fairly definite official act given in return, yet there is no cap on what a candidate may spend of her own money and none on what an outside group may spend so long as it does not coordinate with the campaign. Second, paid lobbyists must register and name their clients, on the stated ground that once the public knows who is paying for which arguments the market for influence disciplines itself. Third, a former official's cooling-off period bars direct communications to and formal representations before her old agency, but does not bar her from advising a client, out of sight, on how to approach that same agency.

These features seem to locate the danger in three different places: in the exchange, in the invisibility of the payor, and in the personal relationship. What account of influence would make all three coherent, and which rival accounts do they rule out? Distinguish what the regime strictly presupposes from what it merely fits comfortably with, and tell me where the practice is underdetermined — where two incompatible theories would license it equally well.

Finally, my chapter considers a reform that would cap the number of hours of a legislator's and her staff's time that any one client may purchase in a year, treating staff time as the regulated currency. Is that licensed by the theory implicit in the existing rules, or does it belong to a different one?

Please state how confident you are in the reconstruction you offer, and single out the point in your answer you are least sure of.
