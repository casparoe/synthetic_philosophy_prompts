I cover science and medicine for a general-interest magazine. I have four months of reporting behind a long feature that is due to my editor in three weeks, and its subject is the line between treating people and improving them. I am not looking for a literature survey, a reading list, or names to name-check — I have a fact-checker, and I would much rather you not guess at sources. What I need is conceptual work on a set of verdicts I already hold, so that I can decide whether to keep using this vocabulary in print.

My working thesis is that the therapy/enhancement distinction is less a discovery about the nature of medicine than a mechanism for distributing credit and blame, and that the words do most of their work below the surface of the argument. Before I commit to that, I want to know whether there is a defensible version of the distinction that I cannot wave away — one stated precisely enough that I could apply it to a case I have never seen. If there is, I will use it honestly. If there is not, I have to stop using the words altogether, and the piece changes shape.

Here are my verdicts, gathered from the reporting rather than from any theory.

1. A violinist with a diagnosed anxiety disorder taking a beta-blocker before a concert: therapy. Her conservatory classmate, with no diagnosis, taking the same pill before a jury: enhancement. I am confident about this pair.

2. A long-haul driver formally diagnosed with shift-work sleep disorder on a wakefulness-promoting drug: therapy. An emergency resident pulling thirty-six-hour stretches, with no sleep disorder, on the same drug: enhancement — and yet I think her use is not merely permissible but sometimes close to required. My classification and my approval come apart here, and I want to know whether one distinction can carry both jobs or whether I am overloading a single term.

3. Growth hormone for a child with a diagnosed deficiency: therapy. For a healthy eleven-year-old whose parents want him to make a travel team: enhancement, and worse. The effect on the body is the same and the second child is not the one being eroded by a disease, so I cannot explain why the first feels clean.

4. A cochlear implant for a congenitally deaf child. I called this therapy in an earlier piece and received letters pointing out that the word had already assumed the child's condition was a loss, and that a baseline of deafness is a difference rather than a deficit. I now think my label was doing quiet work I had not noticed.

5. A gene therapy in trial for a muscle-wasting condition, with the same vector given to healthy volunteers producing above-average strength. Same molecule, opposite labels, difference entirely in the recipient. I am comfortable with that — until the next two.

6. A BRCA1 carrier with no disease undergoing prophylactic mastectomy: therapy. A healthy man with no diagnosis on a statin for a purely statistical risk: I have been filing this under prevention, which I treat as therapy. But structurally it is the same shape as my enhancements in 1 and 2 — a drug given to a well person to change a future probability. Either a great deal of ordinary medicine is enhancement, or my distinction is not really about the presence of disease.

7. A private admissions tutor at three hundred dollars an hour, and a pill that buys a few points of working memory. Both legal, both positional, both bought by whoever can pay. I call only the pill an enhancement, and I suspect that is because one route is biological and the other social, which is not obviously a moral difference.

8. An otherwise healthy seventy-two-year-old in a trial of an mTOR-inhibitor drug whose endpoint is not any disease but immune response to a vaccine. I keep flipping. Ageing is not a disease, so enhancement; the endpoint is his own younger immune function restored, so therapy. This is the case the piece turns on and the one I least want to fudge.

I have drafted two candidate rules and they will not agree. The first is a saturation test: an intervention counts as mere enhancement when its value to the user depends on others not having it, so that the benefit would shrink or vanish under universal adoption. Vaccines pass; the memory pill fails; the tutor fails identically, which is where rule one embarrasses me. The second is a restoration test: enhancement when the intervention does not return someone to a baseline they had or were owed. This handles the resident and the soccer player, but it stalls on the child who never had hearing, on the BRCA carrier who never had a tumour, and on the seventy-two-year-old, whose baseline is a moving target.

So: give me the principle, or the small number of competing candidates, stated as exactly as you can and then rendered in one sentence a general reader could follow without having read any philosophy. Tell me which of my cases motivates each clause of it. Give me its verdicts on three cases where I have none — a non-diabetic endurance athlete using a closed-loop insulin device, a drug that dulls one intrusive memory in a bereaved person who is grieving hard but is not diagnosable, and a treatment that cuts healthy adults' sleep requirement to five hours with no measurable deficit — and then propose two cases of your own that you think are most diagnostic, where your principle and ordinary usage of the word enhancement come apart. Tell me plainly which of my eight verdicts your principle forces me to abandon, and which two of them cannot be held together at all, so that I can concede the point in the piece rather than paper over it. Finally, tell me which single verdict a specialist reader is most likely to attack, and what the attack would be.
