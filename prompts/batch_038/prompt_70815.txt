So I volunteer two afternoons a week at the historical society in the county I grew up in. Mostly I do boxes — refile stuff, write labels, type up old indexes. And over a couple of years I've gotten this feeling I can't shake, which is that what's in our boxes isn't a random sample of what happened here. It's shaped. Almost everything from before 1940 comes out of the same handful of hands: the courthouse, three churches, one bank, the school board, and a newspaper owned by a guy who also sat on the school board. And the people who show up in that paper are there because they bought land, got married, got sued, got arrested, or died. You basically had to do one of those five things to become findable. Meanwhile there's a whole set of people who appear exactly once, in the police blotter or the poor farm register, and they show up in there way more often than anybody else in the room, if you see what I mean. So it isn't just that some folks are missing. It's that some folks are only ever present in the one genre that wasn't theirs.

I keep trying to explain this to people and I keep failing, because when I say it out loud it sounds like a conspiracy theory and it isnt one. Nobody sat down and decided anything. It's just what happens when a record is a byproduct of institutions instead of a picture of a place.

Here's my situation. I run a Wednesday night thing at the public library — mixed bag, 18 to 20 people, ages from a couple of high schoolers up to seventies — and I get 90 minutes once a month. I want them to feel this instead of being told it. Design an activity where they run into it themselves before anybody names it.

Rough shape I've been playing with, change whatever you want: half the room lives one year in a made-up small town, and the other half is the people who hold the only paper — clerk, minister, doctor, sheriff, landlord, editor, teacher, boss. There are more townspeople than paper-holders, so some people just never get written down at all. Then swap, so the paper-holders have to write the history of that year using only what survived, and the townspeople are holding the real answer key and score them. I also want a round where somebody has to destroy 60 percent of the collection because of shelf space, and I want the townspeople to be able to try to get themselves recorded on purpose and find out what it costs to do that.

Then give me the actual rules and the materials (assume pens, paper, a whiteboard, zero budget), a minute-by-minute plan for the 90, the answer-key mechanic so the second half doesn't collapse into an argument about who remembers what, a debrief that maps specific moments onto the real ideas — and please tell me the actual names for the things I'm fumbling at up there, because I don't know them — what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how to handle it, and, honestly, which part of the idea this activity can't reproduce so I know what I have to just say out loud myself.

Two constraints. Everything has to be invented; I'm not turning real local people's records into a game prop. And one of my regulars is razor sharp about this county's actual history and will call out anything that smells fake, so the made-up town has to be internally consistent enough to survive her.
