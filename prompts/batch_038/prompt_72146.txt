I am in the third year of a doctorate in the history of early modern moral philosophy, and my thesis reconstructs Bentham's treatment of sexual offences, with the unpublished essay on pederasty — withheld during his lifetime and printed only in the last century — as its centrepiece. In a paper I have just drafted for a departmental workshop I defend what I take to be a fairly strong interpretive claim, and I would like it stress-tested before I circulate the draft. The claim is this: Bentham's verdict that same-sex acts between competent consenting adults merit no punishment is not a local concession to his own sympathies, but a systematic consequence of the rest of his apparatus. More precisely, once the principle of sympathy and antipathy is barred from doing any independent justificatory work, the machinery he deploys elsewhere for self-regarding imprudence — drunkenness, prodigality and their like — is already sufficient to generate the exculpation, because on his own account of mischief the act produces no first-order harm to anyone but the parties, and the second-order items (alarm, danger of example) are in this case wholly parasitic on the very antipathy that has been disqualified as a ground of punishment. My reconstruction makes explicit two premises that Bentham leaves largely implicit, and I want your assessment of them as much as of the conclusion: a competence premise, on which the parties are treated as adequate judges of their own net balance of pleasure and pain; and what I have been calling an alarm discount, on which pain suffered by third parties counts for nothing when it is caused by nothing more than their belief that the act occurs. My supervisor thinks the second of these is doing all the work, and has told me — unhelpfully, but I suspect correctly — that I ought to be able to say exactly when it would fail.

Tell me what would have to be true for this claim to be false. I want the defeaters ranked from genuinely fatal to merely awkward, and I want the ranking justified rather than asserted. Among the things I would like considered: whether there are places in his own framework where the mischief of alarm or the danger of example is treated as an independent and weighty ground of punishment, such that the alarm discount looks like an ad hoc stipulation rather than the extraction of something already there; whether the competence premise is stable, given what he says about children, about the effects of habit and of what he calls corruption, and about those cases where a party's own judgement is precisely what the legislator is meant to correct; whether his replies to the standard eighteenth-century allegations about the social dangers of the practice commit him to empirical claims which, if false, would leave the exculpation without a utilitarian basis; and whether the framework gives him any principled way of marking off the consenting-adult case from adjacent ones — acts involving animals, or involving parties whose competence is impaired or contested. For each defeater, please say which ones a committed defender of my reading would themselves concede as telling against it and which they would dismiss as beside the point, and say what my thesis predicts that its rivals do not: the biographical reading, on which the verdict is best explained as a rationalisation of inclinations Bentham acknowledged in private, and the local-concession reading, on which it is an isolated judgement that his broader penal theory neither requires nor, in places, tolerates.

Finally, I want an honest verdict on exposure. Since a utilitarian can always restore the alarm term, or withdraw the competence premise, and thereby generate a different result, my thesis may be insulated by qualification to the point where no evidence about Bentham's texts could count against it. If so, please say whether that is a defect for a claim of this kind — an interpretive hypothesis about what follows from a system — or whether it is simply what such claims look like when they are doing their proper work. Lead with your bottom line in two or three sentences, then give the ranked map. Write in British English throughout, and keep the whole response under 800 words; I have the editions open beside me, so what I want is argument and structure rather than citations, attributions or suggestions for further reading.
