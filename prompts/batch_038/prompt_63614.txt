Hey — I'm helping put together a themed issue for a community college lit mag (theme: "who eats") and there's an open slot for a piece of fiction that I can't fill myself, because I don't actually write. Could you write it? Call it 1,500 words, 2,000 tops. Give it a title too.

Here's the setup I want. Invent a province — don't use any real country or region name, keep it geographically vague, sometime in the last century. There's a partial harvest failure and the drought is real, not a pretext. But the province's total grain supply that year comes in *above* its ten-year average. The people who starve are the ones paid in fixed money wages — boatmen, weavers, day laborers, rickshaw pullers — because the price of rice roughly triples over a few months once war procurement and hoarding rumors start moving grain. The people who grow the rice mostly eat fine. That inversion is the whole engine of the piece: the food is there, and the starvation is about who can command it, not about how much exists.

Now the hook, which is the part I care most about. The provincial relief board has a form. After a famine, an officer has to enter one number: the percentage of excess deaths attributable to "climatic cause," the remainder being counted as "administrative cause." If the climatic share is over half, it's logged as a calamity — the province gets an emergency grant, nobody is answerable, the file closes. If administrative is over half, it's a failure of administration: a smaller grant, and named officials can be dismissed or charged. My protagonist is the officer holding the form. He has three days.

He's honest, and he finds that he can't fill it in. Every number he tries feels like a lie in one direction or the other, because the drought really happened and it also only killed the people it killed because of what the price did, and the price did what it did because of a procurement order signed by a man who thought he was stopping a worse famine two provinces over.

A few requests:

- No speeches. Nobody is allowed to explain the thesis to anyone, including the reader. If the idea surfaces in dialogue at all it should come out sideways, in an argument about something else.
- At least one official doing something genuinely defensible — not a cartoon, not a secret sadist.
- Somewhere in the piece the aggregate numbers should physically appear — a memo, a table, a line read aloud — and I want a reader to be able to see with thier own eyes how those numbers hide the distribution underneath them.
- Save the words "disaster" and "crime" for at most one moment, near the end, and only once each if you use them at all.
- The ending shouldn't be a shrug. He does write a number, or he doesn't, and either way I want to feel that the form was a bad *question*, not just that he gave a bad answer.

Also: don't set it in a famine anyone will recognize, and don't name any real economists. I want the thing to stand on its own feet for a reader who's never heard of entitlements or exchange rates or any of it — the concepts should be load-bearing but invisible, the way they are in a good story.
