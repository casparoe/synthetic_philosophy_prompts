So I'm two years into an econ PhD and I've been the person in our reading group who won't shut up about Aumann's agreement theorem, and I want you to attack my actual position rather than give me the survey version. Here's what I hold, and I want to be precise because I think the usual sloppy statements of it are easy to knock over. My claim has two parts. First: when two careful, honest people have genuinely shared all their evidence and still disagree, that is never evidence that people can legitimately have different priors — it is always evidence that one of the two has slipped somewhere. Either they haven't actually shared the evidence (there's a private bit, or nobody has common knowledge of anybody else's posterior), or one of them isn't updating right, or they're reading the same sentence differently. Second, and this is the part I care about: the common prior assumption isn't a modelling convenience that I'm allowed to stipulate, it has teeth, because a real difference in priors would show up as a bet both sides would happily take, and the impossibility of agreeing on the profitability of such a bet is exactly what a common prior amounts to. So when someone says "well, you can never verify the common prior," I say that's the point — no such bet is ever on offer, and that absence is confirmation, not a limitation.

Now the thing that keeps me up, and I'd like you to treat it as the crux rather than as one worry among many. I think I can be cheaply redescribed into triviality. Take any pair of disagreeing posteriors you like. I invent a bigger state space whose points include facts like "which interpretive framework each of us is currently using" and "what each of us took the other's last message to mean," I slap a common prior over the whole thing, and I choose partitions that come out generating exactly those two posteriors. Boom — same prior, full Bayesian rationality, and the disagreement is now "explained" without my having learned a single thing about either of us. If that construction always goes through, then my first claim isn't a discovery about disagreement, it's a redescription available on demand, and my second claim is doing no work either. So: does that construction always go through, and if not, exactly where does it break? I also have a hazy memory that the equivalences between the common prior, the no-agreeing-to-disagree condition, and the bet condition get fragile once the state space stops being finite — I can't tell you whether that's right or which direction it cuts, and I'd want you to reason it out rather than just assert it. And I know there are results where people agree to disagree even holding onto a common prior, because the language they're exchanging is ambiguous — which sounds like it breaks my inference from "we disagree" to "one of us slipped" even if I concede the whole formal apparatus. Then there's the quantitative side: bounds on how far apart posteriors can be when common knowledge is weakened to high-probability belief, and results about priors that are merely close rather than identical. Do those help me or hurt me? My instinct is they hurt, because they suggest the honest question is never "same prior or not" but "how far apart," and a view that only has a yes/no answer to offer loses its grip.
