I work in methods support for a hospital research office, and I keep running into an asymmetry between two fields I have to deal with that I cannot reconcile.

On the clinical-trial side, we run randomized trials under protocols that specify interim analyses. An independent data monitoring committee examines the accumulating outcome data — unblinded, with treatment labels attached — at two or three pre-planned points. It may recommend stopping early for harm, and it may also recommend stopping early for overwhelming benefit. To hold the overall false-positive rate at five percent, the protocol applies a group-sequential adjustment: the significance threshold at each look is made considerably stricter than .05, usually by means of an alpha-spending function that allocates error probability across looks and permits the schedule of looks to shift within limits. Everyone treats this apparatus as a mark of rigor, and regulators expect it.

On the behavioral-science side, the very similar-looking practice of enrolling participants until the p-value falls below .05 — checking the data repeatedly and quitting when the result looks good — is treated as among the worst offenses available, a paradigm case of p-hacking, and it is genuinely destructive: it can inflate false-positive rates to a large multiple of the nominal level. The reform movement's remedy is preregistration, which requires the analyst to fix the sample size and the analysis plan before any data are seen.

Here is my difficulty. Both practices involve repeated looks at accumulating data under the possibility of early stopping. One is praised, the other is scorned. The obvious reply is that the trialist pre-commits to a stopping rule and pays for the extra looks with a stricter threshold, whereas the p-hacker does neither. But if that is the whole difference, then what the machinery protects does not look like the evidential force of the data actually gathered: two researchers who collect identical data and stop at an identical point receive different verdicts, because one of them held a different plan concerning observations that never occurred. I do not see how a plan about counterfactual data can be a property of evidence.

Could you work through what this arrangement presupposes? Specifically:

1. Reconstruct the implicit account of what a p-value is and what it measures, on which the trial's machinery is mandatory and the behavioral researcher's superficially parallel conduct is fatal. State as precisely as you can what each practice must take "the data" to consist of.

2. Say whether the standard reconciliation — pre-specification plus adjusted thresholds — actually answers my objection or merely relocates it. If it relocates it, say to where.

3. Separate what the practice strictly requires from what it merely sits comfortably with. In particular: which components of the trial apparatus would survive, and which would collapse, under a view on which inference consists in updating degrees of belief on the data in hand? Is preregistration's insistence on fixing the sample size in advance intelligible on such a view at all, or does it become a piece of ritual left over from the framework it replaced? I would also like to know where the existing arrangement is philosophically underdetermined — where more than one theory of evidence would license the same rules.

4. Identify where the two practices are inconsistent with each other, and where either is inconsistent with itself. Candidates I have in mind: a monitoring committee overriding its own boundary on clinical-judgment grounds; trials halted early for benefit systematically reporting larger effects than the same trials would have reported had they run to completion; journals demanding fixed-N preregistration while justifying the demand in the language of protecting the strength of the evidence.

5. Describe what would have to change institutionally — protocols, monitoring committees, journal requirements, regulatory review, the division of labor between statistician and clinician — if the presupposed view were replaced wholesale rather than patched.

I am not asking which framework is better in the abstract. I want the implicit theory of evidence that the existing arrangement already commits us to, and an assessment of whether we can hold it consistently.
