I have been trying to settle for myself whether Mencius or Xunzi is right about human nature — not by re-running their arguments, but by listing my own verdicts on cases and seeing what falls out.

1. A toddler who has never been praised for it hands half her cookie to a crying child. Genuinely good, not merely a promising precursor of goodness.
2. An adult who behaves decently only because he was drilled in etiquette and fears disgrace. Decent, but not good; the goodness is not in him.
3. A woman who has spent twenty years in disciplined training and now meets insult with an equanimity that costs her nothing. I rank this above case 1, even though case 1 is spontaneous and case 3 is manufactured.
4. A man raised where cruelty is routine, who is cruel and reports feeling no inner protest against it. Bad, but not blameworthy in the way someone is who betrays a protest he does feel.
5. Someone who feels a flash of delight at a rival's ruin, is appalled by it, and acts against it. Better than the person in whom the flash never arises.
6. Someone who does the right thing because she has calculated that it pays, and would stop if it ceased to pay. Not good at all, though her behavior matches case 1.
7. In years of plenty the young tend to be gentle, in years of scarcity violent. Same endowment, so what does the explaining looks like circumstance rather than nature.

My tentative principle: an act is morally good to the degree that it issues from a disposition that would have produced it without reward, punishment, or instruction. That gets 1, 2, 6, and 7 the way I want, and it matches why Mencius thinks the alarm at seeing a child about to fall into a well counts — it is felt without thought of the parents' favor or the neighbors' applause. But 3 and 5 break it, and 3 is the one that troubles me: my principle ranks the spontaneous toddler above the trained person, which is Mencian, while my verdict ranks them the other way, which sounds like Xunzi treating goodness as the product of deliberate effort rather than as inborn — bent wood made straight only in a frame, a vessel that is no part of the potter's nature.

So: (a) is there a principle that captures all seven, and if it must be more complicated than mine, which clause buys which case; (b) for each candidate, whether it is recognizably Mencian (the sprouts need only be extended, not lost), Xunzian (they must be remade), or neither; (c) which of my verdicts it forces me to give up, and whether "Mencian about origins, Xunzian about achievement" is a stable position or a fudge that assumes both are answering the same question about the same thing — which I suspect they are not; (d) what the principle says about a case I have not listed: someone with a real, untrained revulsion at cruelty who cannot bring herself to intervene when intervention would cost her.
