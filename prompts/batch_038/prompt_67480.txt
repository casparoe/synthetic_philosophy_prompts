So — four months to hand in a novel and there's a scene in chapter 21 I keep circling instead of writing. A podcast host has talked her way into one sit-down interview with a researcher who's been semi-retired from the AI-safety conversation for about a decade. I need the actual questions the host asks, because they go on the page more or less verbatim, and two of my beta readers work in this field and will absolutely tell me if it's soft.

The researcher — I've been calling her Vestergaard — holds a position I invented for the book, but I want it to be a real, arguable position rather than a strawwoman I can knock over in paragraph three. Here it is in my words, so you can hold me to it:

She thinks the whole tool-AI versus agent-AI debate has been aimed at the wrong object. Agency, for her, isn't a property you can go looking for inside the box — it's a property of the loop that includes the box, the person doing the asking, and whatever institution ends up acting on the answer. So the advertised safe design pattern (build an oracle with no goals of its own, keep a human hand on the wheel) describes a component, not an agent, and most of the formal work in the field has been work on a component while the thing that actually does damage is the assembled loop. Three consequences she draws:

- the boxed-oracle thought experiment isn't a limit you can safely approach asymptotically, because being genuinely useful in an open-ended domain forces the system to model the asker, and a model of an asker good enough to answer them well is already good enough to steer them;
- corrigibility is a property of the loop, not of the model, so a perfectly corrigible tool bolted onto a user who has drifted into automation bias is not a corrigible system, and the off-switch literature has been solving the easy half of the problem;
- therefore the design target isn't fewer goals in the machine, it's shorter, more legible, more interruptible loops — a target she thinks got negleted precisely because it doesn't formalize nicely and nobody can prove a theorem about it.

Her reflex deflection, which is the thing I want the sequence to wear down: hand her any counterexample — a calculator, a weather model, a chatbot that only ever answers — and she says you've re-individuated the system, you've quietly detached the box from its loop, and that detachment is the mistake, so the counterexample begs the question. Its a genuinely strong move. It's also a move you can hide behind for an entire hour, and my scene dies if she does that. I want the questions to make it progressively harder for her to reach for, and I want the last one to be something like: name me a case where you'd say the box is the agent and the loop is beside the point, or concede that your distinction isn't doing any work.

What I need from you: eight to ten questions, in the order she'd actually be asked them, and for each one (a) a follow-up keyed to the specific answer she's most likely to give — not a generic "and why is that?", the actual comeback the host would make having heard her out — and (b) a two or three sentence sketch of that likely answer. I have to write her side of the scene and I can't write it until I know what she's saying.

Content I want covered, at least one question each:

- the intentional-stance worry. If agency is just whichever description predicts better, hasn't she turned a substantive safety question into a pragmatic one about bookkeeping, and lost her grip? I picked this up from Dennett somewhere and I'm probably mangling it, so if I am, have the host mangle it slightly and let Vestergaard correct her — that's good characterisation and it costs me nothing.
