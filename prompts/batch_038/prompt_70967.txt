Last weekend I was on a long walk with two friends, Nadia and Dev. She's finishing a dissertation on seventeenth-century New England, he's a software engineer who reads an enormous amount of history, and about twenty minutes in they got into an argument about the Salem witch trials that they eventually asked me to referee. I have no idea who won. Roughly in order:

1. Nadia said the list-of-causes approach to Salem is a cop-out. Everyone rattles off five things: the frontier war and the refugees coming into Essex County from Maine, the village faction fight between the Putnam side and the mercantile town side, the girls' fits, Cotton Mather's writing on possession a few years earlier, and the legal mess — no settled charter, and then a court improvising with spectral evidence. But she said stacking causes isn't explaining. You have to separate what the event actually depended on from what was just background, and in her reading the war and the village politics did the work while the rest were conditions. Dev said that's Mackie's INUS business and it's question-begging, since it assumes there was only one sufficient package on the table.

2. Dev: if you insist on naming which package the outcome depended on, you're committed to a counterfactual nobody could ever check — remove the faction fight, no Salem 1692. Meanwhile accusations happened all over Europe and New England for a century, in villages with completely different social situations, which suggests several different packages each suffice. So Salem was probably overdetermined: more than one route was live, and any of them would have gotten there.

3. Nadia said thats the fallacy right there. Showing that different sufficient packages existed in different places and decades says nothing about whether two of them were both operative in this one. And in a single case you can't tell symmetric overdetermination apart from preemption — one thing fired and the others were just backups that never got used. Once the accusations started in the village, everything else was redundant.

4. Dev said preempted backups are still a real causal structure and not a shrug, and that Salem actually gives you evidence of two processes running rather than one plus a hypothetical: the accusations jumped out of the village to Salem Town and then Andover and a bunch of other towns, and by the time you get to Andover the Putnam/Porter business is irrelevant — different minister, different tensions, different accusers. So it wasn't one cause with backups, it was at least two.

5. Nadia said he'd slid from one sense of overdetermination to another. When historians call an event overdetermined they often mean the Freudian/Althusserian thing — one thing carrying too many meanings and pressures at once — and that's perfectly compatible with there being exactly one actual cause. In the analytic sense the word means redundancy, and redundancy is what makes the concept useless for history, because if the outcome was guaranteed by two independent routes you can't learn anything about which levers matter.

6. Dev said the oposite. Finding out a system was overdetermined is the most useful thing you could learn: it tells you the situation was fragile, that pulling any one lever wouldn't have prevented it, and that's exactly what generalizes to other cases. And anyway, he said, the idea that explanation has to be about intervention is a philosopher's tic; most of the time you just want the story to be right.

7. Nadia closed by saying he'd conceded her point without noticing: he's no longer defending overdetermination as the explanation of Salem, he's defending it as a claim about how fragile the whole region was, and those are different questions with different evidence.

Could you go through this and tell me who's right at each numbered step, not just who wins overall? Several of the moves look half right and half wrong to me and I'd like the halves separated. Also: is there something both of them are missing that would actually settle it, or at least reframe it?

Start with your bottom line in two sentences or less — who came out ahead and on what — then go move by move. Keep the whole thing under 600 words.
