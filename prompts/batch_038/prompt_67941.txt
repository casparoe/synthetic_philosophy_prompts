I am commissioning a piece for a departmental magazine, and I would like it written throughout in British English — spelling, idiom and institutional vocabulary alike.

**Form.** A consumer product test in the flat, practical house style of a British buyer's guide: a standfirst, five products on test, a results table, a Best Buy, a Don't Buy, and one boxed sidebar. 900–1,100 words.

**Premise.** The magazine has spent three months testing five commercial *causal apportionment systems* — software sold to insurers, coroners' offices and safety regulators to answer the question that arises after an incident in which nobody did anything and somebody died: which of the things that did not happen caused it, and in what shares. The five systems on test are:

1. **The Register** — admits only positive events to its ledger. An omission is not an event, so the system finds nothing to apportion and instead offers an ex gratia payment derived from a "nearby truth" about what would have happened had something occurred.
2. **The Baseline** — counts only departures from a default state. It ships with a schedule of the normal course of things, and an omission is causal only where that normal course was defeated.
3. **The But-For** — admits every counterfactual dependence without restriction, on the grounds that any line drawn would be arbitrary.
4. **The Quasi** — pays on omissions but withholds the word "cause" in its settlement letters, holding that omissions make a difference without causing.
5. **The Duty** — declines the metaphysics altogether and asks only whether the party was required to act.

Each mechanism should be stated once, plainly, in the voice of a product description, so that a reader who has never studied the subject can follow what is being bought.

**The testing.** All five systems are run over the same incident and the same three standardised tests. Please use these and do not substitute others.

*The incident.* At 03:40 on a Tuesday a pump at a water treatment works was not switched off. Nobody switched it off, because nobody was there: the night technician's shift had not been filled; the high-level alarm that would have called someone out had not been tested since commissioning; the maintenance contract that would have required testing had not been renewed; and the board paper that would have flagged the renewal had not been written. The tank overflowed into a substation. A contractor who had not been issued with an isolation permit entered the room and was killed. Eleven parties are agreed to have done nothing, and the insurer must apportion.

*Test 1 — Promiscuity.* Our reviewer did not, that morning, fly to a country she had no plans to visit and defuse a device that did not exist. A system that finds this omission relevant to anything fails. We also record the number of deaths since 1 January for which the system has made her liable, and the total share of the loss it has assigned to her; several systems exceeded one hundred per cent.

*Test 2 — Prevention of a prevention.* A safety interlock did not trip. Had it tripped it would have cut power to an extraction fan; had the fan stopped, vapour would not have been drawn away from an ignition source. Each system must report whether the interlock's inaction caused the vapour to be drawn away, whether that counts in the operator's favour, and whether the chain running back from the death to the unwritten board paper survives the same reasoning. Systems that treat a prevention as breaking the chain should say so in the manual; none of them did.

*Test 3 — Auditing the default.* A patient was not given a drug that was not on the hospital's formulary and that nobody there had ever prescribed. Each system must explain why that omission differs from the omission of a drug routinely given at that hour. We then asked to see the default schedule and who maintains it. A system that answers "the normal course of events" scores poorly: at this hospital the normal course of events is what the hospital does, and the hospital does not give the drug. We also asked whether a trust could improve its causation score by lowering its defaults. Two systems said yes and treated this as a feature.

**Results table.** Columns: verdict on the incident (who, and in what shares); promiscuity (deaths attributed to our reviewer); whether the system survives prevention of a prevention; whether its defaults are auditable; overall star rating.

**Satirical requirements.** The target is not any of the systems but the practice of turning the question *who caused this?* into a procurement decision, and the register of specification sheets in which that question is now asked. The humour must come from each system doing precisely what it claims to do: no system may be made to fail in a way that its real critics would not recognise, and the piece should be recognisable satire to a metaphysician while reading as a straightforward buyer's guide to anyone else. The reviewer's voice stays commercial and unflappable throughout — an ontological collapse is reported as an inconvenience of the same order as a short power lead or a subscription that renews itself. Do not name any philosopher, book, article, judgment or doctrine, and do not explain a joke. Nothing may appear in quotation marks as though drawn from a real text; all quoted speech must be invented, and a line from a claims handler and a line from a bereaved relative would both be welcome. The Best Buy must be awarded for a reason that is not metaphysical. The boxed sidebar, at the end, should concern the one question no system on test could answer, and should not answer it either.
