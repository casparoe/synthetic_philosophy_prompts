I am in the fourth year of a PhD in philosophy of science; my dissertation concerns calibration chains in the measurement sciences, that is, the question of how a laboratory establishes that an instrument is trustworthy and how that warrant is transmitted to the quantities the instrument is subsequently used to deliver. Our weekly reading group — a historian of chemistry, two statisticians, and me — has been going around in circles on a structural feature of these chains that none of us can name, and I am writing to ask whether it corresponds to anything already identified in epistemology or in the philosophy of measurement.

Here is the pattern. Two procedures are checked against one another over the range where both can be applied; the agreement is taken to establish that one of them is reliable; that procedure is then deployed in a range the other cannot reach, and the reliability it earned inside the overlap is treated as travelling with it into the new range; and finally its outputs in the new range are used to extend, anchor, or repair the very procedure that did the original checking. Tree-ring sequences supply the calendar against which radiocarbon ages are calibrated, and radiocarbon measurements on unanchored ring sequences are then used to float those sequences onto the calendar, lengthening the chronology that performed the calibration. Closer to my own material: a resistance thermometer is calibrated against fixed-point cells across the range those cells cover and is then used at temperatures no cell reaches, purely on the strength of the agreement observed where cells exist; a year later, that same thermometer is what certifies that a cell has not drifted. Or: an automatic evaluation metric is validated against human judgements on the corpora where annotators were available, is then used to rank systems in domains nobody has annotated, and the resulting rankings are cited back as evidence that the metric generalises. In none of these cases are the practitioners embarrassed. They will insist that the overlap licenses something substantive about the non-overlap, and that the loop is not vicious because what it concludes was not among its premises.

What I want to know is whether this configuration has an established name. I know the neighbouring territory: arguments in which a source is deployed to vouch for its own trustworthiness, and the standard diagnosis that they either fail outright or confer warrant far too cheaply; and I know that what I have described is usually waved away as mutual support, or filed under the ordinary risks of extrapolation. But neither response seems to reach the specific thing, which is that the warrant is earned inside a restricted region of agreement and then spent outside it, and that the step everyone is actually leaning on is not the reliability of the source at all but the tacit assumption that reliability is stable across ranges — that agreement in the overlap is not an artefact of the overlap. So: is there a recognised concept for the transfer of earned reliability from a calibrated range into an uncalibrated one, and if there is, how is it demarcated from plain circularity, from the reciprocal-support structures that coherentists welcome, and from track-record arguments that are commonly said to beg the question? Crucially, what would have to be true for such a transfer to be legitimate rather than vicious, and how much of the difference is a matter of degree? If nothing in the literature matches, please say so plainly rather than stretching a term to fit, and then coin one: supply a definition, state the conditions under which the transfer succeeds and those under which it fails, and locate the edges of the phenomenon, including at least one case that superficially resembles it but does not belong to it. A bare label would be of no use to me; the carving is the deliverable.
