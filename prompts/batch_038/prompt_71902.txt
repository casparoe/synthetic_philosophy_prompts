At my school's futurism club, a senior settled a long argument about mind uploading with the line "the mind is just software running on the brain's hardware." He deployed it twice in the same breath: first to argue that replacing your neurons one at a time with synthetic equivalents cannot possibly destroy you, since the software never stops running; then to argue that a scanner which vaporizes your brain while assembling an exact copy elsewhere preserves you just as well, for the same reason. The first conclusion strikes me as far more plausible than the second, and I suspect the single sentence is being made to do two different jobs, but I cannot say what they are.

Could you lay out the distinct precise theses that one such sentence might be expressing — ranging from claims about what computers can do, through claims about multiple realizability and functional roles, to claims about consciousness and about what personal identity consists in — and for each say whether it is a platitude, a substantive but defensible position, or simply false? Then tell me which readings, if any, actually license each of his two conclusions, whether any single reading licenses both, and where the gradual case and the scan-and-copy case come apart. A numbered menu I could bring to our next meeting would be ideal.
