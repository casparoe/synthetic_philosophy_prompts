I work as a backend engineer with no formal training in philosophy — nothing beyond what I have picked up from podcasts and from arguments with colleagues — so I would ask that you not presuppose specialist vocabulary on my part. Last month my team spent a weekend migrating a long-running simulation service (a few million stateful agents, nothing exotic, mostly queueing, scheduling and persistence) from one cloud provider to another, and in the retrospective afterwards someone asked whether it was really the same service once every container, every disk and every machine had been replaced. A colleague said that of course it was, because it is the software that matters and not the hardware: the same program on different silicon is the same thing. He then pushed the point considerably further than the migration warranted, arguing that by exactly the same reasoning, if you could construct a sufficiently faithful functional model of a brain out of anything whatsoever — a warehouse of cheap machines, an enormous volunteer network passing messages over radios, a municipal water system with the right valve topology — it would be a mind in precisely the way ours is. When someone objected that the whole thing would be absurdly slow, he said that made no difference either, because nothing about timing enters the program: a step is a step, whether it takes a nanosecond or an hour. We ordered pizza and dropped the subject, and I have been unable to shake it since, because I found all of this persuasive at the time and now I cannot say why, or locate the step where it goes wrong.

The load-bearing phrase was something like 'it is the software that matters, not the hardware'. I would like you to take that slogan apart. Please lay out, as a menu of readings rather than as one preferred interpretation, the distinct and precise claims it could be making, ranging from the ones I already rely on professionally — that hardware can be abstracted away when specifying behaviour, that a correct implementation on any substrate produces the same observable input/output pattern, that portability is an engineering virtue — through to the much stronger claims about what physical arrangement is irrelevant to, and about what it takes to be the same process, the same program, or the same mind across a migration. For each reading, give me a formulation specific enough that it could be true or false rather than merely evocative, and then a verdict: uncontroversial platitude, substantive but defensible, substantive and doubtful, or simply false. I am especially interested in two things. First, whether any of the readings actually licence the two inferences my colleague wanted, namely that an arbitrarily strange substrate suffices for mentality, and that the rate at which the implementation proceeds cannot matter. Second, whether there is a gap he glided over between a negative claim — that mental states are not identical to particular physical states, so the same state can be realised in many ways — and a positive one, that mental states just are functional or organisational states; it seems to me that showing the first does not by itself establish the second, and I would like to know what has to be added.

Please also address, in prose rather than by allusion, the distinction that keeps occurring to me from my day job between implementing something and simulating it. A hypervisor really does execute the instructions of the guest; a weather model does not produce any wind; a software-defined storage layer is not, in any obvious sense, a disk. If the slogan means 'organisation is all there is', it has to say which side of that line a mind falls on, and it is not obvious to me that the same answer comes out for a running program as for a model of one. I have not read the literature, so please describe the arguments and the counter-examples in full rather than naming their authors and moving on — names are welcome as signposts, but not as substitutes. Do not include a bibliography or a reading list; I want the reasoning, not the references. Finally, I would be grateful if you could write the whole response in British English.
