I am a high school junior, and I am writing the constructive case for my school's policy debate team on the topic of conditional welfare benefits. Our case argues that work requirements — the conditions that make a benefit depend on documented job search — cannot be justified. My coach read it and said the argument proves too much: that if it works, it condemns things nobody in the round wants to condemn, and excuses things we do not want excused either. I cannot find the exit, so I would like you to find it for me. Here is the argument as it now stands.

(1) A transfer out of the collective product is owed to a person only if that person has contributed to that product or is unable to contribute to it.
(2) Whether a person is unable to contribute is a fact about their circumstances — illness, impairment, caring obligations, the absence of work where they live — and not a fact about their willingness or their effort.
(3) A work requirement conditions a benefit on documented effort: hours of search logged, applications filed, appointments attended, offers not refused.
(4) What documentation of that kind measures is willingness and effort, which by (2) has no bearing on whether the transfer is owed.
(5) Therefore a work requirement makes an entitlement depend on a consideration that is irrelevant to the entitlement.
(6) Defenders answer that the requirement is not about desert at all but about detection: without it, people able to work would report that they could not, and the claims would exhaust the fund.
(7) But if detection is what justifies conditioning, then every transfer ought to be conditioned on proof of eligibility — including tax provisions that move money toward households with high incomes, and pensions paid without any test of need, where the sums are larger and the claimants are in principle easier to audit. Those are not conditioned, and the reason offered is that the administrative cost and the indignity of verifying each claimant outweigh whatever verification would recover.
(8) So the same reckoning that excuses verification for transfers flowing upward must excuse it for transfers flowing downward; (6) fails, and work requirements are unjustified.

I should tell you where my loyalties lie, because I suspect they are what is misleading me: I would give up (7) before I gave up (1) or (5), since (1) and (5) are the parts of the case I actually believe, and (7) was added late to answer an objection. I would rather be told what each option costs than be told what I want to hear. So, three things. Which step should I reject, and why did that step look safe to me when I wrote it? Taking the candidate rejections in turn, what does each one commit me to — what has to change elsewhere in the case if I abandon it, and which of those changes is the most expensive? And is there a version of the argument that survives the repair, or does what is left depend on a premise I would have to argue for at length rather than assume?
