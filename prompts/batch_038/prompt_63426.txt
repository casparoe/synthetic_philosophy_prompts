Right, so — slightly odd commission. I write the ideas-and-science column for a weekly magazine, and I've got a 900-word sidebar to run inside a package about why cosmologists still can't sleep over Boltzmann brains: the business where, if your picture of the universe hangs around in a high-entropy state for long enough, it becomes overwhelmingly more likely that any given observer is a momentary thermal fluke complete with false memories than a creature with a genuine evolutionary past behind it. The physics I can handle — Zermelo's recurrence objection in the 1890s, Boltzmann's two replies, the one that says the whole cosmos started low-entropy for no particular reason and the one, apparently first put up by his assistant Schütz, that says we're sitting in a rare fluctuation out of an otherwise dead equilibrium and of course we are, because nowhere else has observers in it. Then Eddington noticing in the 1930s that a small fluctuation is exponentially cheaper than a big one, and the moderns pushing that all the way to the minimal freak observer, plus the whole measure problem in eternal inflation and the argument that any theory which predicts you're a hallucination saws off the branch that got you to the theory. What I haven't got is a human angle, and my editor has suggested the obvious stunt: stage it as a conversation with Ludwig Boltzmann himself, on the grounds that the thing carries his name, that he floated the seed of it, and that he died in 1906 and never saw what grew out of it.

So: put on the hat of a historian of physics who has also done proper philosophy of cosmology, and think carefully rather than reaching for the usual great-man-gets-dropped-in-the-twenty-first-century schtick. Before you answer, brainstorm on the page — list which of his commitments could plausibly do work here, then say which ones actually bite. The candidates as I see them: his realism about unobservables against Mach and the energetics crowd; his Hertz-flavoured view that a theory is a picture we construct rather than a transcript of the world; his insistence that the second law is a statistical truth that explains and not a bookkeeping trick that merely saves the appearances; his attitude to applying probability to a single object like the whole universe; and, if you think it earns its place, the fact that he was ground down by opposition and took his own life — though please don't cheapen that by turning it into the punchline.

Then give me an actual position with reasons attached. Would he run the fluctuation counting as it's now deployed and conclude we probably are Boltzmann brains? Or would he insist the moderns have mangled his own idea — and if so, at which step: the combinatorics, the measure, the extrapolation to unbounded time, or the assumption that a distribution over observers is a claim about who we are rather than a claim about which states occur? Would he treat the self-undermining move as a legitimate reductio, or as exactly the kind of philosophical dodge he spent his career being accused of? And which does he land on: that the whole business vindicates him, because his statistical cosmology got taken seriously enough to be tested against itself, or that it's a category error wearing a lab coat?

Grade your confidence as you go, please. Mark what you'd defend on textual and philosophical grounds, what's extrapolation from temperament, and where you're frankly inventing because the record runs out — I'd rather have the seams showing than a confident fabrication. Also give me the strongest case for the opposite verdict, i.e. the most plausible story in which Boltzmann embraces Boltzmann brains cheerfully; I'd rather be argued out of a bad sidebar than publish one.

British English throughout, and keep it legible for a general reader — no equations unless you immediately say in words what they're doing. Deadline is Thursday, and the picture desk have already asked whether they can use the 1902 photograph, so, y'know. No pressure.
