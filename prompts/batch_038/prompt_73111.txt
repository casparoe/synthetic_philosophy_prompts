So — I write literary novels, and next Thursday I've got an hour on the phone with a philosopher of physics who works on the arrow of time. He agreed to be recorded mostly out of curiosity about what a novelist could possibly want from him. The book's back third runs in reverse: the same stretch of days re-narrated backwards, and the character at the center of it is a grad student who insists the reversal isn't a gimmick, because the underlying physics doesn't care which way you run the film — the only things that make one direction feel like *the* direction are the absurdly tidy arrangement of stuff at the beginning of everything, plus the fact that we happen to be the sort of system that lays down records.

I want questions that make him argue with my character. Not questions that let him hand me the airport-bookstore version of entropy. Specifically, I'd like:

- one on the old reversibility and recurrence complaints, and why he doesn't think they sink the whole statistical picture;
- one that forces him to say whether that tidy beginning is a law, a brute fact, or a fluctuation — and then presses the nasty consequence, that if we're a fluctuation, our memories are statistically less trustworthy than the past they claim to remember, which seems to make my whole reversed-section device not a stylistic choice but the correct metaphysics;
- one on whether the arrow is out there in the world or is an artifact of the coarse, blurry way we describe it — and what he'd say to a novelist who *wants* it to be an artifact, since that's dramatically cheaper;
- one on why there are traces of the past and none of the future, and whether that asymmetry actually explains anything or just restates the problem in a nicer hat;
- one about what observation could possibly count against his view.

And for each of those, give me the follow-up keyed to the answer he's most likely to give — he's very smooth, he'll have a deflection ready, and I'd rather be holding the second question than scrambling for it live.

Two extra things. I want a couple of questions engineered purely to get stealable sentences: ones that push him toward coffee cooling, footprints in sand, a shuffled deck, anything a reader can picture, and away from state spaces. And don't point me at anything to read — I've read the pop ones, I can't do the maths, and I'd rather have his own phrasing than a citation.
