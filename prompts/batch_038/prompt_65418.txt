Church's thesis is the name for the result that everything effectively computable is Turing-computable — a theorem, not a hypothesis, since the same class of functions turned out to be definable independently by Church, Gödel, and Post, and no one has ever exhibited a function that a person could compute by rule but a Turing machine could not. Turing then proved the halting problem unsolvable: no machine can tell, of an arbitrary machine and input, whether it will ever stop. This puts a permanent ceiling on mechanism. Because the brain is a physical system, and any physical process can in principle be simulated step by step on such a machine, whatever the brain does falls inside that ceiling as well. Gödel's incompleteness theorem is usually thought to escape it, on the grounds that we can see the truth of a machine's own undecidable sentence; but that trick requires standing outside the machine, and Turing, who knew the argument best, accepted the limitation while insisting it was beside the point of whether a machine could think. The upshot is that hypercomputation is not merely unbuilt but impossible, which is why proposals to find it in quantum computers, in real-number analog devices, or in the structure of relativistic spacetime turn out, on inspection, to be ways of doing ordinary Turing computation faster.
