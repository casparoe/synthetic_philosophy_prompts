Our department is staging a dramatized public interview for its history-of-science series: a colleague will appear in character as Johannes Kepler in the year 1619, soon after the appearance of the Harmony of the World, and I will moderate from the floor. Please draft a sequence of nine to eleven questions for me to put to him, together with, for each, a follow-up keyed to the answer he is most likely to give in character.

The questions must engage the actual content of the position rather than invite a general defence of astrology. The character I am interviewing holds: that the efficacious aspects are those whose angular intervals answer to regular figures constructible by ruler and compass, so that the received list of aspects rests on an unexamined tradition and requires revision (he introduces aspects derived from the pentagon and the decagon and is dismissive of much of the rest); that aspects obtain between celestial positions as such, not between the twelve signs of the zodiac or the divisions of the houses, which he treats as conveniences with no natural standing; that what the heavens act upon at birth is the soul's own innate faculty for geometry, which recognizes the configuration presented to it and thereby disposes the body and its humors across a lifetime; that this natural astrology of weather, epidemics, and temperament must be kept strictly apart from the judicial astrology of individual fortunes and elected times, which he repudiates; and that the practice nevertheless pays, which is how he accounts for casting nativities for generals and princes — he is fond of remarking that astrology is a beggarly daughter who keeps her mother astronomy from starving.

At least one question, with its follow-up, should address each of the following: the geometric criterion for aspects and what it implies for figures that admit no exact ruler-and-compass construction; the worry that a cause said to incline without compelling cannot underwrite the prediction of anything in particular, together with the charge from his Lutheran critics regarding free will; the distance between a theory of harmonic configurations and the manifest failure rate of his forecasts of weather and epidemics, and whether his appeal to the differing responses of differing individual souls leaves any observation capable of telling against him; and the charge that a practice stripped of the signs, the houses, and the traditional natures and rulerships of the planets is no longer astrology in any recognizable sense, so that what he has actually vindicated is a piece of natural philosophy concerning geometry and the air.

Close with a question about what he would count as evidence that his reformed astrology has no natural foundation whatsoever, and a follow-up directed at the deflection he is most likely to offer in response.

Keep the register conversational, since these are to be spoken aloud, and ensure that no question turns on his having read anything written after 1619 or on his naming modern scholarship.
