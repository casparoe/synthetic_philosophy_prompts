So I got into a three-hour argument with a friend last night and I still haven't won it, which means I need to outsource it. I'm the first-pass reader for a comparative economic history working-paper series — not a specialist in any region, just the person who decides which submissions go out to the area specialists and which get bounced back with a note. A lot of what lands on my desk is phrased as one big contrastive question: why Britain and not the Yangzi delta, why Europe and not China, why did X miss the boat, why did industrialization start where and when it did. My friend thinks these are perfectly good questions and that anyone who says otherwise is a philosopher wasting historians' time. I think most of them aren't actually well posed — that "why A and not B" quietly smuggles in a choice of foil, a choice of unit (state? region? civilization? river basin? firm?), and a definition of the outcome that the author never states and often couldn't defend. His counter is that my scepticism, applied consistently, would kill every ambitious question in macro-history, and I don't have a good answer, partly because I can't articulate what my standard actually is beyond a vibe. So: turn the vibe into a procedure.

What I want is a checklist a non-specialist can run in about thirty minutes on a submission whose central claim is a contrastive why-A-and-not-B question. Ordered steps, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure terminates in "not well posed, return with this specific repair," where it hands off to a specialist because what's left is an empirical matter rather than a conceptual one, and — this matters — which steps are judgement calls wearing a procedure costume. Ground it in whatever the philosophy of explanation actually says that's usable: that explanations are contrastive so the foil does real work, that a difference-based comparison needs some shared background between the case and the contrast, selection on the dependent variable, the sample-of-one problem, the difference between a concept being vague at the edges and a concept being broken. But write it so someone with no philosophy training can execute it without knowing where those ideas come from. Two things I specifically want built in, because they're how I catch the bad ones in practice: a re-description ladder, where the reader has to restate the question one unit-level up and one level down (Europe → England → Lancashire, or China → Yangzi delta → Suzhou) and check whether the question survives unchanged, dissolves, or changes its answer; and a one-sentence paraphrase test, where the reader has to restate the question so that a competent outsider could say what evidence would settle it, and if they can't, the reader has to decide whether that's vagueness or ill-posedness.

Then run it on three submissions I'm making up but which are close to things I've actually recieved. First: "Why England and not the Yangzi delta? Wages, coal, and colonial markets around 1750." This is my friend's favourite framing and he considers it settled, so I want an honest verdict — if it fails, say precisely which check kills it, what repair the author would have to make, and whether the repaired question is still the interesting one or just a defensible one. Second: "Why didn't the Roman economy industrialize?" I suspect this is junk, because it applies an outcome concept to a society that arguably lacks half its components and because there's no plausible foil, but I want the procedure to say so rather than me. Third: "Why did industrialization begin in the eighteenth century rather than the fourteenth?" I personally find this unfalsifiable hand-waving, so if it passes, tell me bluntly that I'm wrong and which of my instincts is the bad one. Finally, answer my friend directly: give me the strongest version of his objection, say which checks in your procedure are most likely to be over-applied by a lazy or timid reader and how the procedure should protect against that, where you deliberately left slack so that big speculative questions aren't strangled, and what the procedure flatly cannot do — it doesn't tell me whether the answer to a well-posed question is correct, and I assume it can't detect a contrast chosen for ideological reasons, but I want you to say what else it's blind to.
