I teach a metaphysics seminar and every year the unit on dispositions goes the same way: we grind through the conditional analysis, then finks, then masks, and by week three the students can recite the taxonomy but they cant feel the difference between the cases. So this year I want to hand them a story instead of another thought experiment dressed up as a paragraph. Can you write it for me? Around 1,500 words, first person.

Here's the setup, which I made up, so please don't soften it or trade it for something more familiar:

The narrator is a certifier for a firm that inspects load-bearing components — bridge cables, elevator ropes, that kind of thing — before they go into service. In this world there is a treatment, call it annealing, that permanently takes away a component's disposition to part under a given load. Its not a coating or a brace or a sleeve: it changes what the material is, so the load has nothing left to bite on. Separately, there's the test rig — a harness the component sits in while the machine pulls — which can be locked, so that the load never actually reaches the component no matter what the machine does. And separately again, there are components with no such disposition at all that nevertheless part during the test, because something in the environment did the breaking for them, say a resonance set up by a freight train going past the shed at the wrong moment.

Certification is by test only. You apply the load, you watch. So an annealed component, a component quietly riding in a locked rig, and a component that was just unlucky all produce the identical record: passed.

The engine of the story: the narrator signs off on a component, it fails in service years later, somebody is hurt or killed, and an inquiry has to establish what that component was at the time it was certified. Not what it did — what it was set up to do. I want the inquiry scene to be genuinely unresolved. An annealing that slowly decayed back into the disposition, a rig that was locked and then somehow wasn't, and the freight train explanation all fit the same evidence equally well, and the narrator has to pick one of them for the report.

Constraints, and these matter more to me than the plot:

- The words "disposition," "dispositional," "fink," "mask," "mimic," "conditional," and "counterfactual" appear nowhere in the story. Not once, not in dialogue, not in a section header. The metaphysics has to be carried entirely by what people do and say.
- No philosophers get named and nobody lectures. If a character starts explaining the taxonomy to another character, cut the scene.
- One scene where the narrator's own instrument is shown to be untrustworthy in a way that rhymes with the whole problem: the thing he uses to check whether the rig is locked can itself be locked or unlocked by something he can't see from where he's standing.
- End on the narrator's verdict as it goes into the report, and let the reader disagree with it. Don't wink about whether it's right.

A few smaller things. Give the annealing a cost — expensive, slow, something somebody profits from — so there's an actual reason a component might be riding in a locked rig instead of being treated. Give the person who's hurt a name and one specific physical detail. Keep the prose plain, because I'm reading this aloud in class and anything purple will get laughed at by nineteen-year-olds.

And one thing I keep going back and forth on, so build it in somewhere: my narrator believes that what a component is set up to do is fixed entirely by what's inside it, and I want the resonance case to make that belief hurt. If you can get him to say that belief once, in his own unphilosophical words, and then have the train go past the shed right after, that's the whole story.
