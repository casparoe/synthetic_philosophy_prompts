I draft regulations for a living and run a small evening reading group on vagueness on the side, and for our first session I want to build a sorting instrument: a decision tree that takes someone from how they answer a handful of concrete cases to a named position in the debate, without them ever seeing a label first. Half the group are practising lawyers, half are philosophy postgrads, and the lawyers will happily announce that they're epistemicists just because it sounds like a confident thing to be, so the instrument has to sort people by what they actually say about cases.

The angle I want — and it's the one I think the standard write-ups serve worst — is that the tree should be built out of the second-order questions, not the first. Please don't make the opening node anything like "is there a sharp boundary?", since everyone leads with that and it barely discriminates: too many views can say yes, no, or "it depends what you mean by sharp" and end up in the same place. Lead instead with whether the trouble comes back a level up, and with what people are willing to say about their own refusal to commit.

Four cases of mine have to be in there somewhere, because they're the ones I care about:

1. Two swatches from the same tin of paint. On each one separately I won't tell you whether it counts as red. But I'll bet the house that either both count or neither does. The tree needs a node about whether that kind of linked uncertainty can be explained, because that's where I suspect supervaluationism and the degree theories actually part company — argue with me if you think I'm wrong.
2. An all-knowing observer and a heap being dismantled one grain at a time. Does that observer know the exact grain at which it stopped being a heap, or is there nothing for them to know, or is there something to know that even they can't get at? Those are three different answers and I want them on separate branches.
3. The lawyer who, having refused to commit on whether some extension counts as "small", is then asked whether they'd refuse to commit on whether they should refuse. Does the hesitation itself hesitate?
4. A regulatory threshhold of the kind I actually write: "temporary" use of land, where the rule is silent on how long is temporary. I want the leaves to have something to say here, because my real question is whether the answer changes when a jurisdiction stipulates its own number, or whether the stipulation just fixes something that was already fixed and merely obscure.

Required leaves: epistemicism of the Williamson kind; supervaluationism; the degree/many-valued family, but split it — the versions where the degrees themselves bottom out somewhere and the versions where they don't are not the same view and shouldn't share a leaf; contextualism or indexicalism; the view where the world itself is unsettled rather than our talk of it; error theory or nihilism about such predicates; and any position your tree generates that nobody actually defends, which should be labelled "unnamed" and kept rather than quietly dropped.

Two structural demands. First, build the logical space before you build the tree: set out the questions and what combinations of answers they permit, then locate the actual views in it, then point out at least two combinations that come out empty and diagnose each one in a line — is it incoherent, merely unattractive, or just unexplored? Second, be honest about limits: flag at least one pair of leaves your instrument genuinely cannot seperate, and say what question would pull them apart if anything could, or why nothing can.

One more constraint, and it's the hard one: the question stems and answer options must not contain any of the trade vocabulary. No "vague", "borderline", "sharp", "determinate", "truth value", "precisification", "degree". A lawyer who's never read any of this should be able to answer every question cold. You'll have to phrase the cases so the distinction comes out of the case anyway, and I want to hear how you managed it.

Please give me the whole thing in British English, no bibliography or further-reading suggestions — this has to be usable in the room on the night, printed on two sides of A4.

Sections, in this order:

- **Ground rules** (~150 words): the vocabulary ban, what you substituted for each banned term, and anything you had to give up to keep it clean.
- **The questions** (the bulk of it): eight cases, each with a short stem, two to four answer options, and a one-line note on which node it feeds into.
- **The logical space**: the questions treated as dimensions, the combinations they allow, and where the named views sit.
- **The tree**: nodes in order, with the branch condition at each one spelled out.
- **Scoring key**: answer patterns mapped onto positions, including what to make of someone whose pattern is inconsistent.
- **Empty cells**: the unoccupied combinations, one line of diagnosis each.
- **What it can't separate** (~200 words): the indistinguishable leaves, and the best available test for telling them apart.
