I run a monthly reading group that's about a third philosophy grads, a third people who build AI systems, and a third just interested laypeople, and we're doing two sessions on the cogito. Every time it comes up someone says well, at least *that* much is proved, and everyone nods, and I'm now pretty sure there are five or six incompatible beliefs in the room all wearing the same name. So before the session I want people to fill in a sorting quiz. Not an explanation of the argument — an instrument.

Give me 8 to 10 items. Each one a concrete situation or a forced choice about what follows from what, two or three options, answerable by someone who has never read the Meditations and knows none of the labels. No item may mention Descartes, or ask whether you believe there's a self, or ask which philosopher you side with — the entire point is to sort people by their verdicts on cases, not by what they call themselves.

The axes I want probed (add one if you think I've missed something that matters more):

- whether the step from thinking to existing is an inference at all, and if it is, whether it needs a suppressed general premise — get that premise spelled out *inside* an item as one of the options, don't lecture about it;
- what sort of thing is supposed to have been shown to exist: a thinking goings-on with no owner, an owner that isn't a substance, a substance distinct from the body;
- whether the certainty is a matter of knowing something or a matter of the denial being impossible to assert sincerely and coherently;
- whether the first person word is doing referring work at all, or whether what's carrying the certainty is that no identification or recognition is involved;
- whether whatever got established carries over — to the same thinker five minutes later, and to other thinkers.

At least two items should be about persistence in time and at least one about other minds, because that's where I expect the room splits hardest. And given who's in it: one item should ask what, if anything, transfers to a system that generates reports about its own states without ordinary introspective access — but keep it as a case with options, not as an AI debate.

Then the scoring key. For each item, say which axis it's probing and which options point where, and score people into leaves. Leaves I want at minimum: Descartes's own substance conclusion; the impersonal/bundle-style view on which all that's established is thought-occurrences (Lichtenberg's complaint that the honest phrasing uses a dummy subject, like the it in it thunders); the Anscombe-style view that the first person word doesn't refer to an object at all; the performative or self-defeating-denial reading in Hintikka's spirit; the plain valid inference with the general premise supplied; immunity-to-error-through-misidentification based views; and a full deflation on which the utterance establishes nothing whatsoever.

Two extra things. First, tell me which pairs of leaves your items can't actually seperate and what kind of case would be needed to pull them apart. Second, after the key, lay out the logical space as a grid over the axes and mark the empty cells — for each one, a single line on whether it's empty because the combination is incoherent, because it's unattractive, or because nobody's bothered to look there yet.

No bibliography, no reading list, no suggested papers. I want it usable in about fifteen minutes with a pen, and I don't want anyone leaving with homework.
