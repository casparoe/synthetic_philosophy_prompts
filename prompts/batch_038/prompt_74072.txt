Just had a four hour argument with a friend about Malthus and I still don't know who won, so I'm outsourcing the refereeing.

The view I ended up defending, maybe too hard: Malthus's principle of population was right but early. The geometric-versus-arithmetic claim isn't a howler, it's a real tendency whose effects got swamped for a couple of centuries by an offsetting force nobody could have anticipated in 1798, and the theory only looks refuted because we happen to be living inside the exception. My friend's counter is that this is a rescue dressed up as a vindication, and that "right but early" is the single most convenient thing you can say about a long-run prediction, since there's never a deadline you can be held to — he called it chronological immunity. His parting shot: I get to posit exactly one unmodelled offsetting force, I've already spent it on agricultural technology, so I've got nothing left in reserve to explain why fertility fell voluntarily just as incomes rose.

What I want from you is a map of the exposure of my position, not a survey of Malthus scholarship or of who said what about him. Treat the view as a live hypothesis and tell me what would count against it.

- Sort the escape routes I'm leaning on into kinds — I'd guess something like adding an unmodelled counter-force, pushing back the timing, and shrinking the domain to preindustrial societies — and for each one say whether it's a legitimate auxillary hypothesis (independently testable, earns its keep by predicting something it wasn't built to explain) or ad hoc in the damaging sense. Be concrete about what observation would make each of them testable rather than decorative.

- Which of these would a smart neo-Malthusian themselves concede counts against the view, and which would they wave off? I want their own standard applied, not just what the cornucopians say about them.

- Does my view predict anything its rivals don't? Rivals being roughly: induced-innovation accounts where population pressure causes the technology rather than being crushed by it, the unified-growth / demographic-transition modelling, and the flat "he simply misjudged human beings" position. If my view turns out observationally indistinguishable from all three, say so plainly.

- Rank the defeaters, fatal down to merely awkward, and include ones that have arguably already happened. Candidates worth considering: sustained below-replacement fertility at high and now middle incomes, the direction of real wages across preindustrial Europe, the outcome of the Simon–Ehrlich metals bet — noting that people have since run the bet across every window in the century and found Simon would have won well under half of them, and that stretching his own window to 2011 flips four of the five metals against him — and Malthus's own move in the later editions, adding moral restraint as a third check alongside misery and vice, which looks an awful lot like immunising the theory against the objection that it left no room for virtue.

- Then the meta question, which is the one I actually care about. Is "right but early" insulated to the point of unfalsifiability, and if it is, is that a sin for a theory of this particular kind? You can't rerun history, the relevant timescale is longer than a career, and the whole point is a claim about tendencies that get masked by other tendencies — so maybe the standard falsificationist complaint is aimed at the wrong target here. Argue it through. I don't want a split-the-difference ending.

Last thing: name the one piece of evidence that, if it turned up, should make me drop the view fastest — specifically the one I'd be most tempted to explain away.
