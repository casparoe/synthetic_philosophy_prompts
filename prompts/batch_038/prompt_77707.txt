I'm about two-thirds through a novel set mostly in a British university maths department, and I've hit the one scene I'm dreading because I don't have the equipment for it. Two of my characters are mathematicians who were once close and have fallen out over foundations, and chapter 21 needs them alone in the department common room at about seven in the evening, after a seminar has broken up, arguing it out. One is Nadia, 47, a set theorist of the old school: her whole research programme assumes the axiom of choice without a second thought, and she'd tell you she's never once had to justify it, it just works, it's what everybody uses. The other is Owen, 31, a new lecturer who read Bishop as an undergraduate and has become increasingly militant about constructive mathematics — he's started saying out loud, in print, that large parts of Nadia's field are elaborate bookkeeping about objects that don't exist. The bone of contention in this particular scene is Zorn's lemma: Nadia has a new result whose whole content comes from a maximal-element argument, so the object it produces is guaranteed but never exhibited, and Owen has read the preprint and considers it worthless.

What I need is the dialogue itself, roughly 1400 words, plus the bare minimum of stage direction to keep me oriented (kettle, coats, whoever can't sit still). Two things matter more than anything else. First, neither of them may be a stooge — I've read too many novels where the "wrong" character makes three weak points and is then demolished, and my readers will include several mathematicians who'd smell that instantly. Owen should land blows that genuinely hurt: the Banach–Tarski business, the fact that a maximal ideal nobody can point to does no work in any applied setting, the suspicion that an existence proof which never hands you a witness has smuggled in a logical principle rather than proved a mathematical one. Nadia should hit back just as hard: that constructivists use countable choice all day long without blushing, that "I can't exhibit it" isn't the same claim as "it isn't there", that Zorn's lemma isn't a exotic add-on but equivalent to choice itself, and that if Owen really wants to throw it out he should be honest about how much ordinary mathematics goes with it — bases for arbitrary vector spaces, Hahn–Banach, Tychonoff. Someone once told me there's a result, Diaconescu's I think, from 1975, showing that the axiom of choice actually forces the law of excluded middle, which would mean Owen can't quietly keep choice and drop the rest of classical logic; if I've got that right let Nadia wield it, and if I've garbled it, fix it or leave it out entirely. Second, no reconciliation: they leave the room still disagreeing, but by the end the reader should be able to see what the quarrel is really about — whether it's a dispute over truth, over what "exists" means, or over what counts as a proof — and that the two of them aren't sure those are even the same quarrel.

Please write it in British English. Keep the mathematics specific enough to be credible but let the emotional register carry the scene; I dont want a glossary or a lecture aimed at the reader, and if a term needs unpacking, one of them should unpack it because they're being sarcastic or impatient with the other, not for our benefit. Small thing: Owen is Welsh and gets more formal when he's angry, and Nadia swears a bit when she's cornered.
