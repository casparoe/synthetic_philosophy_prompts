Stuck on a Pascal fragment and hoping someone can walk me through it. It's from the batch of notes that usually gets filed under something like justice and the reason of effects in the Pensées — the stuff he left as jottings and never wrote up into the apology. I can't check a published translation right now (offline, and my copy is at the office), so what's below is my own quick crib version and I care more about getting the argument straight than about pretty prose. If my wording makes it come out wrong, say so.

Roughly how it runs:

It is just that what is just should be followed; it is necessary that what is strongest should be followed.
Justice without force is contradicted, because there are always wrongdoers. Force without justice is accused.
So justice and force have to be put together; and to do that, either make what is just strong, or make what is strong just.
Justice is open to dispute; force is very recognisable and is not disputed. That is why we could not give force to justice, because force contradicted justice and declared that it was force, not justice, that is just.
And so, not being able to make what is just strong, we have made what is strong just.

What I dont get:

1. The term I put down as contradicted. Contradicted by whom, and in what sense — by the wrongdoers, or by force itself? Is it a logical point (a norm nobody can enforce somehow undercuts itself), or is it more like a speech act thing, where the guy who breaks the rule is simply saying no to it? And then the second half flips: force without justice isn't contradicted, it's accused. Is there a real difference in kind between being contradicted and being accused that he needs, or is he just balancing the sentence?

2. Is the middle stretch an argument or an antithesis? It looks to me like the neatness of the chiasmus is carrying a lot of the weight. If you strip it down into premises and a conclusion, does anything survive, or does it fall apart?

3. The last line I can read two ways and can't decide between them. Either it's a genealogy and a complaint: as a matter of fact we ended up calling whatever is strong just. Or it's a resolution, almost an instruction: given that force is undisputed and justice is endlessly disputable, the only way to get a workable rule of law at all is to declare the established order just. Which is it? Can he mean both at once without being confused? And who is the we — legislators, humanity, or Pascal's own party?

4. Does this just collapse into the old line that justice is the interest of the stronger? He seems elsewhere to want us to obey custom because it is custom, and to treat hunting for a genuinely just political order as a kind of vanity. If so, is the fragment a skeptical attack on natural law, an argument that positive law is the only law there can be, or something else — and does it matter that he's writing as a Christian who thinks there is a real justice we just can't reach?

5. Is he sliding between two senses of just — morally right versus legally valid? Because if he isn't, the conclusion looks like a category mistake, and if he is, it looks like a cheat, and I want to know whether there's a third option where the slide is deliberate and does the philosophical work.

Plain prose please, and skip the references and the suggested reading — I can't look any of it up and I'd rather have the mechanics than a bibliography. Just don't tell me the fragment is profound; I want to know how the gears turn.
