I am a sixth-form student writing an extended essay for my school's philosophy society, and I would be grateful for help with a question I have found much harder to settle than I expected. My topic is the language of thought hypothesis: roughly, the claim associated with Jerry Fodor that thinking is carried out in an internal representational system with a combinatorial syntax and semantics, distinct from whatever natural language we happen to speak. What interests me is what text-trained neural networks have to do with that claim, since the people I have read appear to draw opposite morals from the same facts.

To sharpen the issue, consider the following hypothetical study. Please treat it as a stipulated thought experiment rather than as a report of something that has actually happened. Researchers train a large text-only model on the entire written record of an invented natural language that contains no expressions of negation or conditionality: its speakers never use anything corresponding to 'not', 'if', 'unless', or 'only if', and get by instead with loose paraphrase and context. (The language is invented precisely because no attested human language seems to lack these resources altogether, and I do not want the case to turn on that empirical question.) After training, the model is tested on tasks that plainly require negation and conditional reasoning, presented in an ordinary language that does have such expressions, and it performs well on structures it could not simply have memorised. Probing its internal activations then reveals two recurring components that behave rather like operators: the pattern elicited by a conjunction of an affirmative clause with a negated one can be rebuilt from the same two components, differently arranged, as the pattern elicited by the mirror-image case; and artificially manipulating one component flips the model's verdict across a wide range of otherwise unrelated inputs.

The controversy I want adjudicated is what such a result would establish. On one reading, it vindicates the hypothesis: the training language demonstrably lacked the resources for the reasoning the model displays, so the model must have constructed a compositional internal code of its own, and the familiar arguments from the productivity and systematicity of thought acquire a new kind of support. On the opposing reading, it establishes nothing of the sort, because the system is ultimately a continuous, distributed mapping from inputs to outputs; the supposed operators are regions of a high-dimensional vector space with no discrete syntactic structure, and any system flexible enough to pass these tests could be described after the fact as implementing some compositional code or other. On this second reading, the case does not confirm the hypothesis so much as expose it as unfalsifiable, since it can be fitted to whatever the model turns out to do.

Please write an essay of roughly 1,200 to 1,500 words that presents both readings as charitably as possible, examines whether the disagreement is genuinely empirical or merely a clash of equally acceptable descriptions, and considers what evidence would in principle break the deadlock — including whether the model's internal states could be said to have content of their own, or only content borrowed from the interpretations we impose on them. I should like a real conclusion rather than a survey that splits the difference: please commit to a verdict on whether the stipulated result would support the language of thought hypothesis, and defend it. Kindly write in British English throughout.
