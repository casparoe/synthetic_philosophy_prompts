I teach a second-year philosophy of mind course, and in week four I devote one seventy-five-minute seminar to von Neumann's argument in *The Computer and the Brain*. The students have encountered Turing machines and the Church–Turing thesis earlier in the term; they have no information theory, no electronics, and no more neuroanatomy than the schematic neuron in a secondary-school textbook. I have a whiteboard and no slides. I am not asking you to explain the argument to me — I know it well. I am asking for a plan for teaching it, and I want the plan to be built around a thesis about how it should be taught, which is mine and which you are welcome to contest.

The thesis is that von Neumann is doing design reasoning under a component budget, not metaphysics about minds. He is asking what one is obliged to build when the parts one is given are slow, cheap, and unreliable; and his answers — enormous parallelism in place of long chains of operations, redundancy in place of component precision, a memory that is statistical and folded into the very elements that compute rather than a separate addressed store, and hence a code of a different character from the one a digital machine uses — all fall out of the same constraint. If the seminar succeeds, my students should stop hearing "the brain is a computer" and start hearing that implementation-independence is not free: it is purchased, and von Neumann is quoting the price. Treat that framing as the spine, and tell me where it distorts what he actually says.

With that in mind, give me:

- The order in which the pieces must be introduced, with an explicit account of the dependency structure — what cannot be stated before what, and what looks unintelligible if introduced out of sequence.
- One carrying example to build the whole session around. I am torn between a bank of unreliable switches voting, a relay that misfires at some fixed rate, and the retina. Tell me which will bear the weight, what such an example must have in order to work and what it must conspicuously lack, and why the others will poison the room.
- The misconceptions the students will form, in the order in which they will form them — I anticipate that analog means richer than digital, that low component precision is an argument against computationalism, that von Neumann was somehow anticipating connectionism, that a different logic means an ineffable one, and that redundancy is mere duplication — together with the precise point in the sequence at which each should be headed off, before rather than after it has taken hold.
- Where, and how explicitly, I should mark the difference between the parts of the argument that survive the disappearance of the vacuum tube and the parts that were hostage to it, given that error-correcting codes and systems trained with deliberately noisy arithmetic have changed what a component budget can look like. I want honesty here without the session collapsing into a verdict that he was simply wrong.
- One closing question that discriminates between students who have understood the argument and students who have merely enjoyed it.
- What I should refuse to explain at this level, and how to refuse it without seeming evasive.

One further complication. A computer science concentrator sits in the second row and will open fire on any engineering hand-waving. Tell me at which two or three points in the plan he will do so, and what the least damaging answer is at each.
