I'm giving a 25-minute talk with 30 minutes of discussion at a half-day workshop on how to measure moral status across species — it's co-hosted by a vet school and a funder that wants to put numbers into its animal-welfare prioritization work. I need a Q&A prep sheet, and I'd rather it be blunt than flattering.

My thesis: none of the three neural metrics people reach for — absolute brain volume, encephalization quotient, total neuron count — can do the job, but not for the reason usually given (that sentience needs very little hardware). My argument is that the metrics aren't even measuring the same dimension, so the debate looks deadlocked for a boring methodological reason rather than a deep one. Then I offer a two-factor replacement: (1) something I call experiential bandwith — how much can be going on at once inside a single subject's experience, which plausibly tracks the neural resource devoted to one unified representational space; and (2) a subjecthood threshold — whether there's a single point of view at all for that bandwith to be bandwith-of. The threshold is architectural and close to binary; the bandwith is graded. Capacity for welfare multiplies rather than adds, so ranking species on one scalar is a category error, and the honest output is a matrix, not an ordering.

I also spend a chunk of the talk arguing that neuron counting, which was supposed to rescue us from EQ, quietly re-imports a worse problem: in mammals something like four fifths of all brain neurons sit in cerebellum, and if you don't think cerebellar neurons are constituents of the field of experience, you've already made an architectural commitment that is doing all the moral work and never gets defended. Switch to counting only pallial/telencephalic neurons and you're being honest but you're also no longer measuring anything brain-size-like.

Audience: about twenty people. Mostly comparative neuroanatomists and behavioural ecologists who care about allometric scaling and phylogenetic non-independence and who think EQ tells you more about how a species moves than about what it can feel. A few welfare scientists who run preference tests and judgement-bias/pessimism paradigms and want converging behavioural evidence. One or two philosophers of biology. And the funder's program officer, who has to write a weighting into a cost-effectiveness model within a few months and does not have the luxury of a matrix.

Give me roughly the eight objections I'll actually get, ordered by likelihood times how much damage they do — and phrase each one the way that person would phrase it, in their own vocabulary, not in philosophy-jargon translation. For each: a reply I can deliver in two or three minutes, what I should concede outright, and where the reply is softest so I know what not to lean on. Also tell me what to concede up front in the talk itself to take the cheap shots off the table, which single reply absolutely has to land for the funder, and the question I'm hoping nobody asks — I suspect it's either how my threshold is falsifiable, or what I'm supposed to say about shrimp once you admit the output isn't a ranking. I know the literature, so skip references entirely and don't tell me what to cite.
