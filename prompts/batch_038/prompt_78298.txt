So I chair the review panel for a small private foundation — we fund about eight "high-risk interdisciplinary" projects a year out of roughly 120 applications, and there are six of us on the panel. Current process: every application goes to two independent readers who write scored reports, then we all sit in a room for a full day, talk about everything, and decide by open show of hands with me breaking ties. We override the readers' scores a lot. Last cycle we pulled one proposal from 14th place up to funded and killed one that had scored top-three, and the trustees noticed.

A new program officer, who's quantitatively minded, has sent the board a proposal to scrap the meeting entirely: average the readers' scores, fund the top eight, done. His case is (a) deliberation lets the two loudest people in the room drag everyone else around, and (b) the Condorcet jury theorem shows that independent votes from people who are each more likely right than wrong converge on the truth fast, so we should protect independence jealously and also make the panel bigger — he wants 24 members. He also floats weighting each member's vote by their historical hit rate, since we do track which funded projects turned out well.

The board wants a memo from me before their meeting on the 19th, and I don't want to write the memo I'd normally write, which is a paragraph reciting the theorem's assumptions and then saying "they don't hold here." That's true and it's useless.

So: draft the memo, a page and a half or so. It needs a real recommendation — mechanical aggregation, keep the discussion day, or a specific hybrid — with the design spelled out tightly enough that the board could vote on it: who reads what, what gets discussed and what doesn't, what an override costs, how big the panel should be, and what we'd actually change for the cycle that opens in about ten weeks. Take the officer's argument seriously; his best point is a good one and I want it stated better then he stated it. Note that independence for us isn't just a matter of not talking — all six of us read the same application text, we're from overlapping fields, and three of the panel were trained by the same two people, so we can plausibly be wrong together in silence just as easily as in a room.

And I want the memo to be honest about a worry of mine that he hasn't registered: if I really believe the aggregate is more reliable than any single one of us, then when the vote goes against me I should defer — but so should everyone else, and each of us deferring to an aggregate that's made up of everyone else's deference looks like it goes around in a circle. Does that worry have teeth, or is it just vertigo? Relatedly, be clear about where our decisions aren't even the kind of thing that has a right answer: whether a budget is honest or whether the methods can support the claims are fact questions, but "is this one of the eight best bets" is a comparison between proposals that don't share a scale, and I suspect the theorem gets no grip there at all. Say something about the weighting idea too, and about what we tell the 112 people we decline if the process becomes an average — right now they get reasons.

End with what would change your mind, and if there's a cheap way to test the recommendation during the next cycle instead of just arguing about it, propose it concretely.
