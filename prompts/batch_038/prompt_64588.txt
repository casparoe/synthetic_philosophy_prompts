I run a little sci-fi book club with some coworkers (I'm a backend dev, no philosophy training whatsoever) and we're doing The War of the Worlds next week and I got volunteered to lead it. I want to build the discussion around an idea I keep circling but can't get straight: when does a non-human cause in a story actually count as an actor rather than just scenery?

Here's my home-grown taxonomy, tell me if it leaks:
- backdrop: the environment is there and shapes things, but nothing turns on it (rain in a detective novel)
- instrument: a non-human thing gets used by somebody, so the agency stays human (the poison in a whodunit)
- actor: a non-human cause does something no character intended, changes who wins, and the story treats it as changing what the events mean

By that standard the ending looks like catagory three to me — nobody in the book does anything that stops the Martians, they get taken out by ordinary terrestrial bacteria, and the narrator obviously thinks that means something. My friend says that's exactly why it's a deus ex machina in a lab coat: the germs show up at the last minute, do all the work, and the humans are just witnesses, so calling them an actor is dressing up the author's plot device in a costume. Who's right? Can the same thing be a real historical actor and a plot convenience at once, or does my third bucket need a fourth condition? I'd also like the taxonomy stress-tested on the red weed — the Martian plant that chokes the English rivers and then gets eaten away by the same kind of disease. Instrument of the invasion, or its own actor?

Second thing, and this is where I'm out of my depth. The book spends real time explaining why the Martians are the way they are: cold dying planet, scarcity hardened them, they've turned into brains with machines for bodies. That's an environmental explanation of a moral character. Historians get in trouble for doing that to humans — climate made them do it, nobody decided anything — and I keep hearing that's a way of letting actual decision-makers off the hook. Does Wells get away with it because they're aliens, or is the book showing us the trap? There's also that bit right at the start where the narrator says we're hardly in a position to complain about Martian brutality given what European settlers did in Tasmania, not to mention the bison and the dodo. Does that cut for or against the determinism reading?
