I direct the "learning from events" function at a 700-bed academic hospital. There are two of us, we run roughly forty interviews a year with clinicians after serious safety events — a medication error that reached the patient, a wrong-site procedure, a deteriorating patient who went unrecognized for four hours, a clinician who disabled an alarm and forgot to re-enable it — and each interview runs sixty to ninety minutes, three to fourteen days after the event. The clinician has no contemporaneous notes of their own thinking. Our written report goes to a review panel that includes the medical director, and on some events it travels onward to an external regulator. The interviews are formally non-punitive, but every clinician knows that.

The problem is evidential. A large fraction of what our reports rely on is the clinician's account of their own mind: what they noticed, what they were thinking at the moment of the decision, how confident or how tired they felt, whether they saw the second allergy entry on the screen, why they dismissed the alert, whether they considered the alternative diagnosis. At present we treat these reports as decent evidence about what happened, qualified only by the thought that people shade things in their own favor. I have come to believe this is naive in a subtler way: that many such reports are not distorted readings of an accurate inner observation but reconstructions generated on demand, sometimes sincerely, sometimes within minutes of being asked — and that the work on introspection and confabulation, from Nisbett and Wilson's arguments about our lack of access to the processes behind our own judgments through Schwitzgebel's case against treating naive introspection as authoritative, has something specific to say about which kinds of self-report carry weight and which do not.

Please write me a protocol my two-person team could actually run, on the assumption that neither of us has training in psychology or philosophy.

The protocol should include:

1. A classification of first-person report by kind, and a rule for sorting any given statement in a transcript into one of the kinds. I would like you to work with at least a distinction between reports of occurrent conscious experience, reports of what one noticed or attended to, reports of one's own reasons and decision process, reports of one's confidence or certainty at the time, and reports of one's own level of impairment or fatigue — but revise or subdivide this if you think the real joints are elsewhere, and say why.

2. A triage of interview question formats by the risk that they produce confabulation rather than retrieval. Assign each format a weight between 0 and 1 expressing how much credence an answer of that type deserves when nothing corroborates it, and state what kind of corroboration would raise it. Include at least one question type you believe we should stop asking altogether, and say what to ask instead.

3. A set of rewrites: eight to ten questions we currently ask in some form, paired with the version the protocol endorses and one sentence on what the rewrite does. Take the originals from wherever you think they come from in this kind of interview; I would rather you invent plausible ones than soften them.

4. Explicit stopping points. Where the protocol must record the item as undetermined rather than infer, say so, and give the exact phrasing we should put in the report, since a sentence that concedes ignorance has to survive being read by a lawyer and by a grieving family.

5. A reflexivity clause. The investigator's own sense that a clinician is inventing an answer on the spot is itself an introspective judgment about my own perceptual and inferential processes. Tell me what weight my read of the room deserves under the same standard the protocol applies to the clinician, and what would count as corroboration of it.

6. A short appendix naming the philosophical bets the protocol is making — for instance, whether introspection is more like inner perception or more like self-interpretation using a folk theory; whether the transparency route to one's own attitudes gives better access to belief than to desire or emotion; whether privileged access holds for occurrent states but not for dispositions — and, for each, what breaks in the protocol if the bet is wrong.

Then run the protocol on three cases, in enough detail that I can see the weights and the stopping points doing work:

- A confident, articulate nurse who gives a coherent minute-by-minute account of why she administered the drug, and whose account is consistent with everything else we know, but who was not asked about it until eleven days later and who, in an earlier brief exchange on the day itself, told a colleague she could not remember which line she had drawn it up from.
- A resident who says he was exhausted and that this is why he missed the finding, in an interview where his fatigue is the only thing he volunteers against himself, and where the roster independently confirms two consecutive night shifts.
- An attending whose self-report is the sole evidence on the central question of whether he considered and rejected the diagnosis that was later confirmed, with no documentation, no colleague present, and a plaintiff's firm already involved.

For the third case, I expect the protocol to reach a conclusion I will dislike — that we cannot report what he considered — and I want you to say plainly what the report should nevertheless contain, and what the honest justification is for withholding a judgment that everyone on the panel will want.

Finally, mark the places where the procedure is judgment wearing the costume of method. I would rather have five or six of those flagged candidly than a protocol that pretends the weights are measurements.
