So I help run a little community woodshop out of a two-car garage — fourteen of us, we pay a few bucks a month, everybody has a key. Most decisions we just vote on, but over the last year I've been keeping notes about when I actually think a vote is the right way to settle something and when it isn't, because my gut keeps splitting in ways I can't put into words. Here's the pile of cases, roughly in the order they happened.

Painting the hallway and the front door: vote, majority wins, and I think the guy who lost just has to live with it, no complaint. Same for the hours the planer is allowed to run, since the noise goes through the wall into the apartment above. But then there's Dale's bench. Dale's bench is a disaster, offcuts everywhere, and I'd bet all fourteen of us would rather it be tidy, including honestly Dale himself on most days. Still, when someone proposed we vote on bench standards, I said no, it's his bench, he decides, and I'd say that even though literally everyone prefers otherwise and even if it mildly annoys all of us. The case that broke my brain: Ana has the window bench and gets to decide whether the window is open or shut, and Ben has the corner and gets to decide whether he runs his radio in the evening. Ana would rather Ben not run the radio. Ben would rather Ana keep the window shut, because of the dust. So we ended up with window open AND radio on, which is the one combination both of them rank below the other one — they'd both happily have window shut and no radio. I stuck to my rule and said each of them keeps their own call, and we're stuck with the outcome nobody wants. I still think that was right but I felt like I was forcing something on them, and I can't say what. Then Dale, when pushed, said "fine, you guys vote on my bench" — and the second he waived it, the vote felt completely legitimate to me, no residue at all. Here's the pair I can't reconcile: a member wanted to keep a can of solvent in his own locker, and I overrode him, fire risk, no vote needed; another member keeps a shelf of taxidermy and old dentures that creep almost everyone out, and more people complained about that than about the solvent smell, but I would never let us vote that away. Both cases are "everybody else has a preference about what's in his locker," and I treat them oppositely. Also: two guys swapped who gets the window bench and who gets the corner, and I thought that was great and it basically dissolved a standing feud. And the newbie who wanted to use the tablesaw with no training — I stopped him, but I'd file that under a different heading, like looking out for someone, not rights at all.

My best guess at my own principle is: you get to decide about what's yours and what only affects you, unless it actually hurts somebody else, and everything else we vote on. Test it against the list and tell me where it bends. I want a real principle, or two or three competing ones, stated precisely enough that I could hand it to the shop and use it — with which of my cases motivates each clause, what it says about cases I haven't thought of (say, harm that only shows up years later, like something slowly offgassing; or everyone having a strong preference about how another member treats his own body, tattoos and that kind of thing; or a member who wants the shop to play music, which is his radio but everybody's ears), and honestly which of my verdicts it has to throw out. If a simpler principle overturns one verdict and a messier one saves all of them, tell me which trade you'd take and why. Oh, and a friend once half-described some formal result by an economist with "liberal" in the title, something about rights and unanimity not fitting together, and I have a feeling my Ana-and-Ben thing is the same shape as whatever that is — say whether it is, and whether the standard fix people propose for it actually works in a fourteen-person garage or only on paper. Don't bother with references or a reading list, I just want the reasoning laid out.
