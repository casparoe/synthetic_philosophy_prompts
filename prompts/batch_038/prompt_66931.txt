Right, so I run a little reading group with two friends — one in the philosophy department here, one doing welfare economics at LSE — and the format is that each of us writes up our own position as a numbered list of commitments and the other two spend an evening trying to break it. Mine's next week and I'd rather get broken by you first, in private. I'm not after a lecture on the history of the debate and I don't want a reading list or names dropped at me; assume I've read the standard material and just give me the reasoning. Here's my package, on aggregation:

(1) Welfarism, taken strictly. The moral ranking of two social states depends only on the distribution of individual wellbeing across the people in them — not on rights, desert, or equality for its own sake — and I mean that as a supervenience claim: same wellbeing distribution, same ranking. (2) Harsanyi's aggregation theorem is a genuine theorem and a substantive one: individual preference orderings over prospects satisfy the expected utility axioms, the social ordering does too, and the Pareto condition holds in both its weak and strict forms; therefore social welfare is a positive weighted sum of individual expected utilities. I don't think its a case of the conclusion being smuggled into the premises, and I think it puts hard constraints on what a theory of distributive justice is allowed to say. (3) But it does not establish utilitarianism. The weights the theorem hands you are just arbitrary positive numbers; getting them all equal needs an extra premise — that the impartial observer has no reason to weight one person's chance of being anyone more than another's — and that premise is a substantive moral assumption, not part of the theorem. So I'm perfectly happy to accept (2) while insisting its partisans overclaim. (4) I'm a prioritarian, and specifically an ex post one: for each state of the world you apply a strictly concave transform to each person's realised wellbeing, sum, and only then take expectations over states. Priority to the worse off applied to outcomes, not to prospects. This presupposes that wellbeing is cardinally measurable and interpersonally comparable with a common zero and a common unit, and I take that comparability to be a fact about wellbeing rather than a convention I've picked for convenience — otherwise (4) would be empty. (5) I accept ex ante Pareto and I think it's the one premise in this whole business that isn't up for grabs: if every individual, ranking the prospects by their own lights under uncertainty, prefers A to B, then society must prefer A to B. I can't see how you deny that without ending up saying society can prefer an option that literally nobody prefers. (6) This is my own contribution and the bit I most want stress-tested. I think the weights in (2) should be filled in empirically, from actual patterns of identification — whom people in a society really take the perspective of, measured with survey and time-use data, whose fortunes they track, who they spend time thinking about. Call it identification weighting. I don't see it as a rival to Harsanyi but as supplying the missing premise from (3) with data instead of with an armchair symmetry assumption, and I'm happy for the weights to be dynamic, re-estimated every few years as the society changes. If you object that attitudes aren't wellbeing, my answer is that the attitudes are only the instrument by which we discover the morally relevant weights, so nothing non-welfarist enters through them. (7) Whatever the weights turn out to be, the resulting ranking must not systematically favour the better off; I'd be embarrassed by a weighting rule that did, and I regard that as a constraint on (6), not a rival to it.
