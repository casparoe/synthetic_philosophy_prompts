I've been listening to this audiobook on rationality on my commute — the author has an econ PhD and writes for a general audience, and there's a chapter he calls "the arithmetic of consistency" that I keep rewinding. I'm in a reading group with a couple of friends who've had more stats than me and they basically shrugged at it, so I want a second opinion. Here's roughly how he lays it out. I'm paraphrasing, but I've tried to keep his structure and his emphasis intact:

"Three separate projects in the middle of the last century — Ramsey working out what partial belief has to look like, von Neumann and Morgenstern axiomatizing choices among lotteries, and Savage deriving both belief and desire out of preferences over acts — all converged on the same theorem, which is why you can safely treat them as one result. The engine is the Dutch Book: if your degrees of belief violate the probability calculus, a bookie who knows them can offer you a package of bets you'd accept one by one but that, taken together, guarantee you come out poorer no matter how things turn out. So incoherence isn't a stylistic preference or a difference of temperament; it is literally cash. The mirror image holds for desire: if your preferences over gambles satisfy a small handful of obviously reasonable axioms, then you can be described as maximizing expected utility, and both your probabilities and your utilities are pinned down uniquely. Savage was careful to insist this is a formal theorem rather than an empirical claim about how brains tick, and that's precisely what makes it normative — it tells you what you'd have to do to be consistent, whether you happen to like it or not. Which also settles the fashionable objections. People who violate the axioms, like the ones who take the sure million in the Allais problem, aren't revealing some subtle wisdom about risk that the math can't see. They're just wrong, and the theorem is what proves it."

Two things bug me and I can't tell if they're real problems or me being non-technical. One, "it is literally cash" sounds way too strong to me. I have this hazy intuition that the bets get quoted in dollars but rationality is supposed to be about something else, and that some assumption about how much you value money is doing quiet work in there — but I couldn't say what the assumption is, and my friends said I was overthinking it. Two, he says Savage was careful to keep the result formal rather than empirical, and I have a memory of reading somewhere that Savage himself attached some sort of limitation about the range of situations the framework is meant to cover, which this summary just doesn't mention at all. I might be thinking of a different author entirely though.

So: how much of this would an actual decision theorist sign off on? Go through it piece by piece and tell me what's straightforwardly right, what's right-but-sleight-of-hand, and what's just false. Specifically — is it defensible to say the three projects proved one theorem, or do they differ in ways that matter? Are the probabilities and utilities really unique, or unique only up to something? Does his Allais line follow, i.e. does a representation theorem actually establish that axiom-violators are irrational, or only describe what falls out once you've granted the axioms? And where does that leave the normativity — is there a real gap between "these preferences are representable" and "you should have these preferences"? Finally, once you strip out everything wrong or inflated, tell me whether a solid argument survives for being coherence-minded about beliefs and preferences, or whether the honest version turns out to be almost toothless. I lean toward thinking something real survives, but I've taken this guy's word before and been embarassed.
