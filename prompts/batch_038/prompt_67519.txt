I would like a number, and I would like the arithmetic that produces it.

The claim I have in mind — call it the Access Claim — is this: for a typical psychologically healthy adult with no special training in first-person reporting, spontaneous retrospective reports about the occurrent character of their own conscious thinking over the preceding few seconds track how things actually were with them. Concretely, if such a person is asked, unprompted and without leading questions, whether in the moment just before being asked they had been talking silently to themselves, whether they had been visualizing anything, whether they had been thinking in a way that involved neither words nor images, and whether their present mood was positive or negative and roughly how intense it was, then an observer enjoying unlimited epistemic access to that person's mind at that moment would concur with those answers in more than three-quarters of cases.

Two points to fix before you begin. First, the threshold is deliberately a number rather than a metaphor: treat 0.75 as a substantive standard, and if you think the Access Claim should be read as a claim about some threshold rather than this one, say so and adjust the target accordingly rather than dissolving the question. Second, the ideal observer in my formulation is a device for pinning down a truth condition, and the device is itself questionable. Whether there exists any report-independent fact about what a given moment of a person's thinking was like is one of the terms in your decomposition, and you must state explicitly how you propose to handle the possibility that the Access Claim is neither true nor false — whether you treat that possibility as making the claim false, as removing it from the sample space, or as something else, and what you do with your number in that case.

So that the question does not depend on what you happen to remember, here is the empirical material I have in view. In 1977 Nisbett and Wilson argued, on the basis of a series of experiments, that verbal reports about one's own higher cognitive processes are largely constructions assembled from culturally available theories and from cues in the situation rather than read-offs of the processes themselves; in one of their better-known demonstrations, the spatial position of identical items in an array strongly determined which item subjects chose, yet subjects confidently cited properties of the chosen item as their reason and denied that position had played any role. The choice-blindness paradigm developed in the 2000s presses the same point against a domain usually thought to be introspectively safe: subjects are shown two photographs of faces and asked to point to the more attractive, after which the experimenter covertly hands back the rejected photograph by means of a double-card sleight; many subjects do not detect the substitution and proceed to offer detailed, specific reasons for a preference they never expressed. On the other side of the ledger, Hurlburt's descriptive experience sampling is designed to remove exactly these confounds: the subject wears a device that beeps at unpredictable intervals, jots down whatever was occurring at the moment just before the beep, and then works through a sequence of training interviews aimed at producing presupposition-free descriptions of those moments. Hurlburt's conclusion from this apparatus is that a majority of sampled moments contain no inner speaking at all, that some participants report none whatsoever, and that a distinctive form of thinking occurring with neither words nor images is common — results he takes to show that ordinary generalizations about one's own inner life are badly wrong in both directions. Schwitzgebel has argued in reply that the method's own category scheme and the interview training may be manufacturing the very phenomena the reports describe, and, independently, that our errors extend beyond the causes of our mental states to their present conscious character, as in his arguments that we know surprisingly little about the visual experience we supposedly have at the periphery of our field of view while looking straight ahead. Most instructively for my purposes, the two of them once ran a single-subject study together, reporting on one trained participant they called Melanie: they worked from the same beep reports, discussed them at length, and still ended the book disagreeing in their closing remarks, the proponent optimistic about the accuracy of carefully elicited first-person description and the skeptic unconvinced.

Now the request. Give me your credence that the Access Claim is true, as a single number between 0 and 1, with one or two sentences of justification. Then decompose it. At minimum I want terms for: the probability that there is a determinate report-independent fact of the matter about these moments; the probability that findings obtained from small, intensively trained, self-selected samples generalize to untrained people reporting spontaneously; the probability that the confabulation Nisbett and Wilson documented for processes also infects reports of occurrent contents, given that they themselves distinguished the two; the probability that choice-blindness failures reflect a defect in ordinary introspection rather than an artifact of the manipulation's speed and the attentional demands of the task; and the probability that a proponent and a skeptic can look at one and the same dataset and remain in disagreement after extended discussion without either being simply wrong about the data. Add any further terms you think belong, assign each a rough probability, and — this is the part people usually skip — state how you are combining them: multiplication, averaging, weighted aggregation, or something disjunctive, together with a sentence on why that is the right structure for this claim rather than another.

Then split the target. Give separate numbers for the narrower claim that spontaneous reports about whether one is presently engaged in inner speech are accurate at the same threshold, and for the narrower claim that spontaneous reports about the valence of one's present mood are. I suspect these two come apart; tell me whether you agree, and in which direction.

Finally, tell me which single term my number is most sensitive to: if you could settle one sub-question by fiat, which would move the overall credence furthest and by roughly how much, and what observation — not argument, but a datum you could actually go and collect — would move it the most. My two colleagues hold credences of about 0.85 and about 0.15 respectively, and they have both read the same studies, including the single-subject collaboration. Locate their disagreement: which term or terms do they most likely assign different values to, and how much of the gap do you think is substantive disagreement about minds as opposed to disagreement about how to read a formulation like the Access Claim, for instance about what would count as the observer concurring with the subject's report?
