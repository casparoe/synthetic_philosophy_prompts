For four years I have been at work on a novel set in a polity that has abolished elections for its second chamber and replaced them with an assembly drawn by lot: three hundred members, stratified against the civic register so that the room looks like the country, serving a single non-renewable term of two years, and disqualified thereafter, permanently, from standing for or being appointed to any public office whatsoever. That last clause is the one my readers will find most exotic, and it exists to prevent what my architect character calls a citizen-politician class — people who make a career out of being amateurs. She is the book's most sympathetic intelligence and also its most dangerous one, and near the middle there is a long set-piece in which she must defend the whole design before a committee that suspects it both of contempt for ordinary voters and of quietly surrendering power to the permanent staff who brief them. I cannot write the scene until I know whether her argument holds. A friend who teaches political theory sketched the following for me on the back of an envelope; I have numbered it so the steps can be checked rather than admired.

(1) A body may make law binding on me only if its members are chosen by a procedure that gives every citizen an exactly equal antecedent chance of sitting in it; a procedure that gives some citizens a better chance than others treats the rest as political inferiors, and a state that treats its own members as inferiors cannot claim their allegiance. (2) Elections fail (1). My chance of sitting in an elected chamber is not equal to that of a party official, a former chief of staff, a donor's cousin, or a broadcasting regular, because candidacy is filtered long before the ballot — by money, nomination, media access, and a decade of prior work inside politics; the effective pool is a class of professionals, and the vote merely ratifies its selections. (3) Term limits do not repair this. Removing an incumbent changes who occupies a seat but not who is able to win one: the pipeline that produced the professional class is left untouched, and a chamber of first-timers is still recruited through the same narrow channel. (4) Sortition satisfies (1) by construction. The lot is indifferent to everything about us — wealth, name, connections, ambition, résumé — and so assigns each citizen an identical probability of holding office without any need to police money, parties, or seniority at all. (5) Hence an assembly filled by lot is legitimate in a way no elected chamber can be, and since (1) makes equal antecedent chance the whole of the matter, it is legitimate to the maximum degree. (6) Legitimacy of this kind comes in degrees, and the degree is fixed by two things together: how equal the antecedent chances are, and how much turns upon the office. (7) The maximum of that combination is reached not by a large assembly but by a single draw — one citizen, chosen by lot, holding absolute and unappealable power, every other citizen's chance of being that one precisely equal to everyone else's. (8) Therefore (1) through (6) entail that a despot selected at random is the most legitimate ruler available to us, and that my architect's three hundred are a grudging compromise with injustice. (9) That cannot stand: she cannot say it on the page without becoming a monster, and the novel would collapse with her.

What I want is the diagnosis, not a reading list. Which premise or which transition do you reject, and why did it look perfectly innocent on a first reading — I went through (1) to (6) twice without hearing anything out of tune and only began to smell smoke at (7)? If the argument is valid and one of the premises is simply false, tell me which is cheapest to surrender and price the surrender. If it is invalid, name the slide, and then say what would have to be added to make it valid, since an invalid argument can usually be repaired with one more premise and I need to know whether the repaired version is something she could live with. Be explicit about what is left standing for sortition afterward — whether the case for the lot has to be rebuilt on some ground other than equal chance, and on what that ground is — and about the fate of (3): if the lot is the right device, does the lifetime disqualification still earn its place, or is it a redundant decoration she is keeping out of taste? My own instinct is that the trouble sits somewhere around (6), but I cannot defend that instinct without already assuming the conclusion I am trying to test, and I would rather be told plainly that I am wrong. The claims I should most like to keep are (2) and (4), because everything sympathetic in her character depends on her being right about what elections actually do.
