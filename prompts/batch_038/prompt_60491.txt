I am giving a forty-minute paper at a philosophy-of-psychiatry and clinical ethics colloquium in six weeks, and I want to prepare for the question period in advance rather than improvise it. What I need from you is a prep sheet, not a defence of the paper.

**The thesis.** I defend a Strawsonian claim with a clinical consequence. Strawson's picture, in my paraphrase, is that the reactive attitudes — resentment, indignation, guilt, gratitude, forgiveness — are responses to the quality of will others display toward us, and that the objective attitude, the stance we take up toward those we regard as immature, impaired, or abnormal, is one we can adopt but cannot occupy wholesale without ceasing to stand in personal relations at all. My paper presses the view in a direction Strawson did not go. I argue that the reactive attitudes are not merely responses that happen to track quality of will; they are among the means by which we come to know it. Resentment is a mode of moral attention, not a distortion of moral attention. It follows that where a psychotropic drug narrows a patient's affective range below what was normal for that patient — not where it removes a pathological excess, but where it suppresses responses that were fitting — the loss is not only a matter of subjective texture. It is an epistemic loss to the patient about their own situation, and a loss of claims to the people who owe and are owed something by them. A patient who can no longer feel guilt about a real wrong, or indignation about being exploited, has lost access to a fact; their counterpart has lost a claim that would otherwise have been pressed. My conclusion is modest but awkward for practice: informed consent, outcome measurement, and the very notion of a side-effect burden are organised around symptom reduction and functioning, and so have no vocabulary in which a harm of this kind can even be recorded.

**Sketch of the argument, so the objections have something to bite on.** (1) Strawson's distinction between the reactive attitudes and the objective attitude. (2) The objective attitude is not a purely theoretical stance: to adopt it toward someone is, in part, to suspend those attitudes toward them. (3) Resentment, indignation and guilt are ways of registering a fact about how one is being treated; they can be mistaken, but their default status is epistemic rather than disruptive. (4) Therefore affective capacity is a channel of moral knowledge, and its suppression can be a harm of a distinctive kind. (5) Distinguish restoring a normal range from pathological excess, which is unobjectionable, from narrowing below the normal range, which is the case that generates my worry. (6) Clinical practice tracks symptom scores, not the distinction in (5), so the harm is structurally invisible.

**The audience.** Roughly thirty people: mostly practising psychiatrists who prescribe, several clinical psychologists doing CBT and trauma work, two philosophers of psychiatry, one neuroethicist. There are no moral responsibility specialists in the room, and almost nobody will have encountered more of Strawson than a textbook paragraph. Their commitments: symptom reduction and restored function are the goods; distress is not intrinsically valuable; the patient's own report is authoritative about their quality of life; they are allergic to anything that sounds like an instruction to leave people suffering; they are deeply suspicious of armchair claims about what a drug does to experience, and will ask about dose-dependence, reversibility, and how common the phenomenon actually is; they think in the register of informed consent and shared decision-making rather than moral standing. Two of them will be listening for signs that my argument could be recruited by people who want to refuse or withdraw treatment. One psychiatrist who read the abstract has already told me that I am asking clinicians to preserve guilt as a diagnostic instrument — not what I mean, but near enough that I must be ready for it.

**Format.** Please use the following sections, in this order, with roughly the lengths indicated:

1. **The room's priors** (~200 words). What this audience already believes going in: which of my premises they will grant without argument, which they will contest immediately, and which they will not even recognise as premises.
2. **The objections, in order** (~1,500 words). Eight to ten of them, ranked by the damage each would do — likelihood multiplied by force, not by topic. For each: the objection as one of these people would actually put it, one or two sentences in the first person and in their vocabulary; a note on who in the room raises it, distinguishing the clinicians from the two philosophers of psychiatry, since they will press in different places; a reply I can deliver in about ninety seconds; and a line headed "Where this is soft", saying where the reply fails, or where it depends on a premise I should not assume they share.
3. **What to concede in the first five minutes** (~200 words). Three or four concessions that buy credibility and cost me nothing essential, and one thing I must not concede even under sustained pressure.
4. **The load-bearing reply** (~150 words). The single response that has to land for the paper to survive the session, and how I should set it up earlier in the talk so that it does not have to be improvised from the floor.
5. **The question I hope nobody asks** (~200 words). The objection I have no good answer to, why it is unusually likely from this particular room, and the least-bad holding action if it comes.
6. **Confidence and self-criticism** (~250 words). How sure you are about the ranking and about each individual reply; where you suspect you are inventing an objection no one in that room would think of; and which of my steps — especially (3) and (5) — you think are simply false or question-begging.

Be committed rather than even-handed: rank by damage, do not soften an objection into a form I can answer easily, and tell me straight if you think one of my replies is bad. Paraphrase Strawson; do not put words in his mouth inside quotation marks, since a garbled quotation in my notes is worse than none. Do not supply references, study citations, or effect sizes. Where the force of an objection or a reply turns on an empirical matter — how common affective blunting is, whether it is dose-dependent, whether it reverses on taper — name the empirical question and flag that I need to check it myself, rather than asserting a number.

Finally, if you think the framing is confused — if my use of Strawson is a misreading, if the objective attitude will not bear the weight I put on step (2), or if you judge that this audience would simply not engage the paper on these terms and the exercise is misconceived — say so at the top of your response, plainly, instead of producing a polished sheet for a talk that should not be given in this form.
