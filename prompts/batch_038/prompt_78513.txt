I run a Tuesday-night folk song session and I've gotten weirdly obsessed with a question that came up after someone lent me a battered copy of The Selfish Gene. It was the last chapter that got me, the 1976 one where Dawkins coins "meme" and offers tunes, catchphrases and ways of making pots as examples — and where he himself seems to hedge about whether the copying is faithful enough for the whole thing to be more than a suggestive analogy. Half the internet treats memes as literal Darwinian replicators and the other half says the theory is an empty analogy that explains everything and therefore nothing, and one person in my reading group insists we should just stop using the word. I dont want a history of that quarrel. What I want is to find out what my own intuitions add up to, so here are my verdicts on concrete cases. A playground jumping-rope chant that kids pass on for decades, never written down, words mostly intact: replicator, obviously. My grandmother's soup recipe, transmitted orally, changed a bit by every generation so that nobody can reproduce the original, and yet we all still say "that's grandma's soup": replicator, yes, even though the fidelity is awful. A rumor that mutates at every retelling because, as far as I can tell, each teller rebuilds it from the gist rather than repeating anything: NOT a replicator — some shape keeps reappearing because human memory gravitates toward it, but nothing is being copied. A ballad that a song collector wrote down in forty village versions, no two alike, all recognizably the same tune: replicator, though it unsettles me that there's nowhere the tune is stored, no vehicle, just people doing it after hearing it. A vowel change sweeping through a city over two generations, noticed by nobody, intended by nobody, and never reversed: NOT a replicator, that's drift, there's nothing you can point to and say this structure got passed along. A liturgical rite preserved with obsessive precision by an institution that spots and punishes deviations: replicator, except on reflection I'd say the institution is the replicator and the rite is just what it makes copies of, which makes me nervous that the question has been pushed up a level forever. An ad slogan an agency produced by testing two hundred variants on focus groups and keeping the winner: NOT a replicator, that's intelligent design, not natural selection — although the testing really is differential retention among variants, so maybe I'm not being consistent. And a scientific theory, deliberately and cumulatively revised in response to evidence: replicator, but this is the one that bugs me most, because the modifications are acquired during the item's own career and then inherited, which is straight-up Lamarckian, so maybe "the Darwinian theory of culture" is a misnomer at the level of items even when it works at the level of populations.

My tentative principle, which I wrote on the back of a setlist, is that something is a cultural replicator iff it gets remade over and over, the remakings resemble earlier ones more than they resemble random things, and — this is the clause I like — when a remaking goes wrong the wrongness can itself be passed on, i.e. the tradition can be botched in a way that stays botched. I can already see it wobbling. The vowel change passes my botch test perfectly, since a sound change is exactly a botch that stays botched permanently, and yet I refuse to call it replication; and the rumor case I reject for a completely different reason, something about rebuilding rather than copying, which the botch clause doesn't capture at all; and in the slogan case I seem to be smuggling in a demand that the variation be blind, which isn't anywhere in my stated principle. So: is there a principle that gets all eight of my verdicts, or close? If it can't, tell me straight which verdicts have to go and rank the losses by how much they'd cost me, because I suspect I care about the recipe and the ballad a lot more than I care about the rumor. I'd also like you to test whatever you land on against cases I haven't listed: a house coding style that spreads through a codebase by copy-paste until a linter enforces it; a joke template where the format gets copied and the content swapped each time — is the replicator the template or the individual filled-in joke?; a dance step or a handshake; and the word "meme" itself, which seems to be a case where the item and its own survival strategy are the same item. Finally, tell me whether "is X a replicator" is even a substantive question or whether I'm just negotiating a stipulation and dressing it up as metaphysics.

Please act as a philosopher of biology who has genuinely worked through the cultural-evolution and memetics debates — someone mentioned "replicators and interactors" and "attractors" to me as existing technical notions, and I'm happy for you to use those tools if they earn their keep, but I don't want a recitation of who said what, and I definitely don't want a reading list or citations, since I have no way of chasing them down. Think carefully before you commit to an answer: if it helps, sketch two or three candidate principles in prose, push each against the case it handles worst, and only then settle on the one you'd defend, making sure I can follow which clause is doing the work on which case. Format-wise, write it to me as continuous prose, like an email or a letter — no headings, no bullet points, no bold, no tables, just paragraphs — and don't pad it out with a survey.
