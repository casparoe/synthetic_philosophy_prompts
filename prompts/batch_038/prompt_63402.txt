I'm meant to be writing a chapter about how Bayesian epistemology handles your own future self, and I've got thoroughly stuck on Van Fraassen's Reflection principle — roughly, that if you're certain your later credence in some claim X will be r, then your present credence in X should already be r, or in its more careful conditional form, that your current credence in X given that your later credence in X will be r should itself be r. I'd like an essay that works through a case I've made up for the chapter and then actually commits to a verdict rather than splitting the difference down the middle.

The case goes like this. You're the statistician on a drug trial. You wrote the analysis plan yourself, you've modelled the plausible outcomes in detail, and you're currently sitting at 0.6 on the hypothesis H that the drug works. You're also near-certain that six months from now, once the trial has unblinded, you'll be at 0.85 on H. But — and this is the wrinkle — the reason you know what your future credence will be is that you can already work out what the data will probably show and what a Bayesian update on it would deliver. Your future 0.85 isn't mysterious to you at all; it's just the answer to a calculation you havent finished. Whereas in the stock counterexamples to Reflection, where you know you'll be drugged or hypnotised or will have forgotten your evidence, the future credence is one you can't explain from the inside and have every reason to distrust. So my worry is that Reflection might not be doing any independent work in cases like mine. Where you can predict your future credence precisely because you can predict the evidence that produces it, Reflection looks like a redundant restatement of conditionalisation — a nag to finish your sums. And where you know the future credence but not the evidence behind it, that combination starts to look unstable, or at least like a case where you've got a piece of information you should be updating on directly rather than deferring to. If that's right, is there any residue left for Reflection to govern, and what would it look like?

Please weigh the considerations on both sides before answering: the dynamic-coherence and diachronic Dutch book arguments in its favour, the thought that Reflection is just a special case of a more general norm about deferring to a better-informed self, the objections from expected irrationality and evidence loss, and the more radical option of giving up on diachronic constraints altogether in favour of a view where rationality only relates the attitudes you hold at a single time. Then land somewhere definite. A qualified verdict is fine — Reflection survives but only in some restricted form, or it survives only for agents lacking certain kinds of knowledge about their own futures — but "it depends on the case" isn't an ending I can use. If your argument leans on a reading of the literature that's itself disputed, flag that in a sentence at the end.
