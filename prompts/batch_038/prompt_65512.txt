I teach an upper-level undergraduate seminar on the ethics of artificial intelligence: eighteen students, a single 100-minute session, and no background in political philosophy beyond one week on utilitarianism and one week on Rawls. The session's topic is the question of whose values an aligned system should serve. I want an in-class simulation that makes them collide with two problems they currently treat as separate: the impossibility of neutrally aggregating the values of a pluralistic population, and the prior fact that designers must decide what counts as a value signal at all. Please design the activity around the skeleton below. Keep the skeleton and make it operational rather than replacing it.

The class specifies the decision rule for a fictional municipal allocator that divides a fixed annual pot among five competing community proposals — night-bus routes, weekend clinic hours, shade-tree planting in the hottest districts, a youth centre, and translation services. The students are divided into seven "constituencies," each receiving a sealed card with a strict ranking over the five proposals plus further information about the constituency whose exact content I want you to design. Three students do not play citizens; they rotate through the role of "the system," which exercises no judgment of its own, applies whatever rule is currently in force mechanically, and announces the resulting allocation aloud. There are two rounds. In the first, the class must adopt a rule before any proposal list or card content is revealed. In the second, the cards are on the table and the rule may be renegotiated. The round structure should force them to notice that the rule they endorsed in the first round is not the rule they want once they know where they sit, and that neither choice was technically compelled.

Specifically, I need from you:

1. Full card contents for all seven constituencies, including a field recording what the constituency stands to lose if its top-ranked proposal fails. The class must decide, separately in each round, whether the system is permitted to read that field, and the consequences of each decision must bite inside the activity rather than being explained afterwards.

2. At least one affected group that is absent from the room and cannot be represented by proxy — future residents, or commuters who use the services but fall outside the franchise — together with a mechanic that makes the class's exclusion of them something they do rather than something they are told about.

3. Rules, turn order, minute-by-minute timings, and materials: index cards, a whiteboard, and paper only, with no money, no devices, and no preparation I cannot complete in an hour the night before.

4. Explicit handling of edge cases: how the system resolves ties, what happens if a constituency refuses to reveal its card, and what happens if the class fails to adopt any rule at all in round one.

5. A debrief that maps named moments of the run onto the concepts I want them to leave with: reasonable pluralism, and the fact that the disagreement is not caused by ignorance that better data would dissolve; the difference between a procedure that is neutral in its justification and one that is neutral in its effects; the worry that feeding revealed preferences into a system launders whatever injustice shaped those preferences; and the difficulty facing any body that must decide who belongs to the population whose values are to be aggregated. None of this terminology should appear before the debrief.

6. A frank account of what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it: where the exercise degenerates into horse-trading, where the student playing the system starts quietly exercising judgment, where a dominant personality captures the rule-setting, and how to prevent or repair each. Close with a candid statement of the one feature of the real problem that this simulation cannot reproduce and that I will have to supply myself.
