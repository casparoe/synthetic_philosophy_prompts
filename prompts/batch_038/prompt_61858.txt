Not a historian, I run logistics for a grocery chain and read economic history at night, but I've gotten stuck on something and I cant find my way out of it. It's about when a "why X and not Y" question about the past is an actual question versus one that just sounds profound. Here's where I've landed on a bunch of them. What I want is the principle underneath, because I don't have one that gets all of these.

Ones I think are fine:

- Why did the western half of the Roman empire lose its central government in the 400s while the eastern half kept one for another thousand years? One polity, one century, two outcomes, and you can point at stuff.
- Why did the plague of the 1340s push western Europe toward free labor and better wages for the survivors, and eastern Europe back toward serfdom over the following century or two? Again, same shock, two directions.
- Why did I get the flu last winter and not my wife, when we share a bed, a kitchen, and a toddler in daycare?

Ones I think are broken:

- Why did Rome fall? I can't say what event this names, or what the alternative was supposed to be.
- Why did the Maya collapse? Different cities, different centuries, and the northern lowlands were doing fine the whole time.
- Why didn't China have an industrial revolution in the 1700s? This one feels like it assumes industrialization was lying around waiting to be picked up and China fumbled it.

Ones I flip on:

- Why Britain and not the Yangzi delta? People who argue this seriously start from the claim that around 1750 the two were not obviously different on real wages, market development, life expectancy. If that's the starting point, the question seems fine to me. But then it looks like the same question as "why didn't China industrialize," with one side deleted, and that one I just called broken.
- Why did printing with movable metal type catch on in Europe in the 1450s and not in Korea, where it had already been used in the 1300s?
- Why did Zheng He's treasure fleets stop sailing in the 1430s and Columbus's start in 1492? My gut says this is two questions wearing a trench coat, and neither half has a fair comparison on the other side.
- Why did Britain industrialize decades before France rather than the other way around? The first version seems fine. The mirror — why did France fail to beat Britain to it — seems like a trick, as if there's nothing to explain about France at all. But it's literally the same set of facts. That's the one that bugs me most.

My tentative principle, which I know doesn't hold: a contrast question is only legitimate if both sides were, at some datable time, running the same process, and you can point to something that happened on one side and not the other. That saves Rome east/west and the plague case. It seems like it should kill Britain-vs-the-delta, since "the same process" is exactly what's in dispute, but the 1750 similarity claim maybe rescues it. And it says nothing at all about why the direction of the question should matter, which is the France thing.

Here's the knot. I want to say a divergence question is only fine if you can state the comparison without the answer already baked into it. But every time I try, the comparison carries the answer: "rather than China" presumes China was the natural candidate and Britain the surprise; "rather than nowhere" quietly swaps in a different and maybe better question, which is why industrialization happened at all, anywhere, when it did. Is that a verdict on the divergence question specifically, or does every historical why-question have this disease and the divergence one is just the loud case?

So: give me the principle, or two or three rival ones if that's the honest answer. State it clause by clause, and for each clause say which of my verdicts it's there to save. Then tell me which verdicts it can't handle and which single one you'd make me give up, and argue for giving it up. Then run it on two I haven't listed — why did Buddhism fade in the land of its birth and thrive in China, and why did Kodak invent the digital camera and get destroyed by it while Fuji made roughly the same transition and survived — and tell me what it says about the France mirror, which is the one I actually care about. No reading list, please, and no name-dropping of historians I should go look up. I want the principle and its costs.
