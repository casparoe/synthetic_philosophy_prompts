'The deepest problem with hedonism is not the one you usually hear. The familiar objection is the experience machine: we would refuse a life of pure pleasant experience, so pleasure cannot be all that matters. But notice that this objection is really just the heterogeneity problem wearing a thought experiment as a disguise — the machine would be churning out pleasures of wildly different kinds, and that is precisely why what it produces looks worthless to us. Before we can even run the machine, we have to say what pleasure is, and hedonists cannot. Compare the pleasure of a hot shower after a long day, of scratching an itch, of finally seeing why a proof goes through, of hearing an old friend start to laugh, of the first bite of a cold peach. Introspection finds nothing running through all of these: no shared ingredient, no common hedonic tone, no felt quality that is present in each. So "pleasure" does not pick out a natural kind, and that leaves the hedonist with two options, each of them fatal. The first is to define pleasure functionally, as whatever state an organism is disposed to seek out and prolong. But then hedonism is just a preference theory with a different vocabulary, and the claim that pleasure is the only intrinsic good becomes true by definition — a piece of bookkeeping rather than a view about the world. The second is to insist that pleasure is a distinctive felt quality after all, in which case introspection has already refuted you. Either way, hedonism is not so much false as empty: there is no statement of it that could come out wrong. This is also why hedonistic utilitarians cannot do arithmetic. If pleasures differ in kind rather than merely in degree, then there is nothing there to add up, and the whole Benthamite project of computing net pleasure across persons dissolves. It is worth noticing that Mill effectively conceded the point when he introduced higher and lower pleasures. Once you say that some pleasures outrank others even when the intensity is equal, you are ranking values by something other than pleasure, and you are no longer a hedonist — which is why Bentham's remark to the effect that, the quantity of pleasure being equal, a children's game is worth as much as poetry remains the only self-consistent formulation of the view. Even the philosophers who tried to escape the problem ended up proving it. Ryle argued that pleasure is never an inner episode at all, and the contemporary attitudinalists say pleasure is just a matter of taking a certain pro-attitude toward some state of affairs. Fine — but a hedonism whose basic good is not a feeling has surrendered the one thing that made the theory attractive in the first place.'
