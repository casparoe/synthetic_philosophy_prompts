So I've been slogging through Adam Smith's Theory of Moral Sentiments, mostly as a corrective after ten years of forum threads where every moral question gets answered with an expected value calculation, and I've gotten stuck on the impartial spectator in a way I can't get unstuck from. Worse, I turned the stuck-ness into a numbered argument, and the argument now seems to prove way more than I want it to.

Setup as I understand it. For Smith the impartial spectator isn't a utility calculator, it's a standpoint you build out of having been watched. Countless real spectators have reacted to your conduct over the years; you strip out their partiality and their ignorance; you imagine a fair and well-informed judge and ask whether you could enter into his feelings if you swapped places with him in fancy; and conscience is just your approval-or-disapproval from that imagined station. The general rules of morality come the same way — they get built up inductively out of which particular acts people have actually approved and disapproved of. Sentiment plus imagination all the way down, no independent normative input anywhere.

My argument:

1. The content of the impartial spectator's verdicts is supplied entirely by the responses of actual spectators, corrected for bias and ignorance.
2. So each verdict is a function of those actual responses. Nothing else goes into it.
3. A standard that is a function of a set of responses can't license rejecting any of those responses. At most it can reweight them.
4. But Smith wants the spectator to overrule real spectators all the time — he thinks the crowd's sentiments are corrupted, that it systematically over-admires the rich and great and under-admires the poor, and he explicitly has cases where the supposed judge sides with you while every real spectator is unanimously and violently against you.
5. Therefore either the correction is coming from somewhere other than the actual responses, in which case the impartial spectator isn't built out of sympathy at all and Smith is an intuitionist wearing sentimentalist clothes, or it isn't coming from anywhere and his complaints about the crowd just beg the question.

That part I half expected. Here's the part that's bugging me: the argument doesn't stop there. The same three steps seem to kill every idealization I use, including ones I'd be gutted to lose. What would a fully informed rational agent want — a function of what actual underinformed agents want, so it can't criticize them. Reflective equilibrium — a function of my current intuitions, so it can't overturn them. Even what the scientific community would conclude given all the evidence, which is a function of what actual scientists say and so can't tell them they're wrong. If (3) is really true then no procedure that starts from actual responses can ever produce a standard that corrects those responses, which is flatly false, becuase idealizations correct me constantly and nobody takes that as a reductio of science.

So which step snaps? My gut says (3), but all I've got for that is the analogy plus a vague feeling that a mean can disagree with every data point that went into it, which doesnt feel like an argument. I'd most like to keep (1), because giving that up means Smith isn't a sentimentalist anymore and I lose the reason I was reading him. And if (3) is the one that goes, I want to know what specifically in Smith's construction does the work — what's in the machinery besides the responses, given that he insists it's all sentiment and imagination — and then whether my fully informed rational agent survives (3) either, or whether I'm about to find out it's been doing the exact smuggling I just accused him of.

Just the diagnosis, please. No syllabus, no list of secondary literature — I don't want to go read eight books about this, I want to know where the chain breaks and what breaking it costs.
