You're a philosophy professor who also publishes short fiction — the kind of story that could run in a literary magazine and get argued about at a dinner party afterwards. Do a few lines of brainstorming first about what the metaphysics actually buys you dramatically, then write the story, about 1200 words.

Setting: a government office that processes "cohort reclassification appeals." Anyone born within sixty days of the boundary between two named generations can petition to be legally moved to the neighbouring one, because the cohorts have come to matter — pension indexing, a first-home scheme, jury pools, some admissions lottery, pick whichever one or two you want. The narrator is the caseworker who's been at the desk eleven years and has stopped thinking about it.

The plot engine is one appeal the rules can't decide: a petitioner whose file sits exactly on the line, who has found out that the cut-date came out of a committee convened decades ago for a marketing report and thinks that fact settles everything. The narrator knows the counterargument without being able to state it cleanly — that once enough institutions sort people by the label, the label starts doing genuine causal work, so the petitioner is right that it's arbitrary and wrong that it isn't real. Get both halves of that to hurt.

Constraints, please respect these: no infodumping about sociology, and no character who is a professor explaining the theme to another character; the ideas have to be carried by forms, procedure, waiting rooms, and the people in the queue. End on a decision rather than a shrug. And don't name any real book, study, or researcher — invent whatever documents you need.
