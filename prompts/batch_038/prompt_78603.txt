So our foundation's grantmaking committee — five of us, mostly non-specialists plus one economist — has burned three meetings on the same allocation and nobodies budging. We have about $3m for this round and two finalists that each cost roughly that over their lifetime, so it's genuinely either/or (there's a smaller pot next spring we could push one of them to).

Program M: cash transfers plus livestock support for 40,000 people in a very poor region (call it Region A, income around $900 per person per year). Evidence from comparable programs nearby: about +0.4 on a 0–10 self-anchoring life-satisfaction ladder, sustained across five years.

Program D: community health worker–delivered group therapy for perinatal depression, 12,000 women in Region B, three years. Measured effect is big early and fades — +1.8 at twelve months, down to +0.5 by month 36, call it +1.2 averaged over the three years.

Run the naive arithmetic, points times people times years, and M wins about 80,000 to 43,000. That's where the argument starts, and then three things get thrown at it.

(1) Scale. Region A's respondents bunch at the top of the ladder, and anchoring-vignette work done there suggests they run roughly 1.4 points high against a common frame, with the top end squashed by about a factor of two — so their +0.4 might be more like +0.8, which doubles M's total.

(2) Adaptation. The D advocate says Region A's contentment *is* the deprivation: people have adjusted their expectations, the ladder measures how your life compares to what you've learned to want, so you shouldn't credit M's gains as welfare gains at all, whereas depression is reported as awful by sufferers both prospectively and retrospectively and visibly wrecks functioning. Sen on adaptive preferences, he says, and this survey is exactly the sour-grapes case.

(3) Prioritarian weighting. Our chair proposes weighting gains by how badly off the recipient is, which sounds like the compromise until you ask badly off by what — by income it's Region A, by reported satisfaction it's Region B's clients. So the tie-breaking rule needs the very comparison that's in dispute.

Underneath all of it our economist keeps repeating that per-person utilities in a von Neumann–Morgenstern setup are only fixed up to a positive affine transform each, so summing "points" across two populations requires a common unit and a common zero that the ladder doesn't supply, and that Harsanyi-style aggregation assumes that rather than deriving it. The chair's answer is that every framework we could adopt assumes something like it, and the only question is whether it bites harder here. (An earlier staffer's suggestion of valuing things by willingness to pay got dropped for the same kind of reason — it would automatically favor whichever of two populations is richer.)

I need the short memo I'll circulate before Thursday. It should recommend M, D, or a specific split, with a timeline including whether we should fund a re-anchoring module in Region A before committing anything; give reasons the D advocate could actually respond to rather than just outvote; take the strongest version of whichever program loses seriously instead of waving at it; and propose the rule the committee should adopt for future rounds — a weighting formula, or a decision procedure for when you don't trust your own metric, whichever you think is right.

And honestly, between us: my hunch is that this isn't a factual dispute at all, it's a dispute about what welfare is and what the instrument measures, and no amount of additional data would end it. Is that right? And is the economist's objection fatal to what we're doing, or just a condition everyone in this business works under without saying so?
