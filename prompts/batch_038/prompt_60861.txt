You're a philosophy of action person — someone who works on shared agency, joint commitment, "we-intentions," all of that. I need you to referee an argument I watched happen at a neighborhood meeting and couldn't make head or tail of. Please actually think it through carefully rather than reflexively splitting the difference.

The setup: I live on a street where two neighbors, Dana and Ezra, share one long sidewalk out front. After a heavy snow last January they both went out with shovels at roughly the same time, each starting on their own seperate section. They worked toward each other, crossed paths a couple times, Ezra kicked a pile out of Dana's way, Dana stepped back to let Ezra swing the shovel, and about fourty minutes later the whole stretch in front of both houses was clear and continuous. They barely said ten words to each other the entire time. I was watching from my porch.

A few weeks later the neighborhood association was discussing who keeps the block's sidewalks clear, and Dana said something to the effect of "Ezra and I did that section together." Ezra cut in immediately: no, they didn't, they'd each just been shoveling their own snow and it happened to line up. Then they argued about it in front of everybody for like fifteen minutes. The exchange went roughly like this:

1. Ezra: Joint action requires some kind of agreement or shared plan. We never agreed to anything, we never even discussed it beforehand. Two independent projects that coincide in time isn't one joint project.
2. Dana: But you were responding to me constantly. You shoveled toward me, you waited when I stepped back, you moved that pile that was blocking me. That meshing is precisely what makes something collaborative — two people waltzing who never speak are still dancing together.
3. Ezra: Responsiveness can't be sufficient, it's too cheap. Two cars merging onto a highway respond to each other; people in a crowded doorway do a constant little dance of "after you" / "no, after you." Nobody says the doorway crowd exited the doorway together. So mutual adjustment is necessary at best.
4. Dana: Fine, add the knowledge part then. We each knew the other was doing it and knew the other knew — we made eye contact, you nodded at me twice, I nodded back. And I kept going partly *because* you were keeping going. The merging cars don't have that.
5. Ezra: That's still two people doing their own thing with excellent situational awareness. Here's my test: if you'd gone inside halfway through for a coffee, I'd have had no complaint against you. Nothing you'd owe me an apology for. In genuine joint action you're on the hook to the other person — bailing out *wrongs* them, it doesn't just inconvenience them. We had no hook like that, so we weren't in it together.
6. Dana: That test gets things backwards. The obligation you're describing is supposed to arise *from* the joint activity, so you can't use the presence of the obligation to decide whether the joint activity was there. You're arguing in a circle.
7. Ezra: Not circular — the obligation comes first. It comes from each party openly expressing willingness to be in it together, and that expression is what creates the entitlement. We never did any expressing, so there was nothing to create one.
8. Dana: Then you're committed to saying that two strangers who spontaneously hoist a bicycle off a pedestrian who's fallen under it — no prior expressions, no nodding ceremony — didn't lift it *together*. That's absurd.
9. Ezra: It's not absurd, it's just unfamiliar. And I suspect a lot of what gets called spontaneous teamwork is really well-synchronized individual behavior wearing a costume.
10. Dana: Notice what you're doing, though. You only started insisting on this once I said it out loud in public and it got me credit for the whole stretch. Privately you'd have happily described it as us doing it together.

I honestly don't know who to side with. Ezra sounds rigorous but 9 and 10 struck me as evasive, and Dana sounds like plain common sense but might just be grabbing credit by way of the word "we."

So: take the ten moves one at a time and rate each — clearly wrong, partly right, or clearly right — with a sentence or two justifying each rating. Please don't just declare a draw at the end; tell me who is more right overall and about which specific points. Then tell me what you think both of them are missing, and in particular whether "did they do it together" is even a determinate question here or whether it shifts depending on what Dana is trying to accomplish by saying it. Start with a short brainstorm about what the candidate standards for joint action actually are, before you grade anything, so your ratings aren't arbitrary. And don't tell me which famous philosophers each of them sounds like — I know they do; I want the arguments graded on their merits, not sorted into camps.
