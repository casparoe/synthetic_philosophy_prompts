In our reading group on self-deception, Feynman's line from the 1974 Caltech address — "The first principle is that you must not fool yourself — and you are the easiest person to fool" — has become the go-to move for both sides, and they're clearly getting opposite conclusions out of the same sentence. Priya uses it to say self-deception is easy precisely *because* there's no external auditor: the checks that make lying to other people hard (they know the facts, they'll notice the inconsistency, they have reasons to catch you out) just aren't there. Dan uses it to argue the opposite — that self-deception has to be shallow and short-lived, because you're simultaneously the liar and the audience and the audience already has the truth in hand, so any genuine case must involve something like two subsystems that don't talk to each other.

Can you pull the sentence apart into the distinct claims it could be making? I'm most interested in what's supposed to be comparative about it: easiest person to fool *compared to whom*, measured how — success rate, effort required, time until the lie gets caught, how bad the consequences are when it does? — and whether any of those readings survives contact with the idea that we have privileged access to our own states, which seems to point the other way entirely (you'd think you're the *hardest* person to fool about what you yourself believe). Tag each reading as platitude / substantive and plausible / dubious / flat-out false, then say which readings actually license Priya's inference and which license Dan's, if either. And flag any reading that quietly assumes self-deception is intentional, since half the group denies that and we keep talking past each other on that point.
