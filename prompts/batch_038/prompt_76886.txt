I'm about 200 pages into a novel and one of my characters has argued herself into a corner. She's the part-time archivist of a small mill town whose founding story is that the founders took in a band of refugees one hard winter, and she's found a letter in the basement of the historical society showing they turned them away at the bridge. She doesn't want to publish it. Here's the case I've given her — I wanted it wrong-but-tempting, but my beta reader says it's too easy, that the same six lines would let anyone keep any comfortable belief, which isn't what I was after:

1. What a community is, its identity across time, is fixed by the story it tells about itself, not by what actually happened.
2. This town's story includes the sheltering.
3. The letter shows the sheltering didnt happen.
4. If the town accepts the letter its story changes, and if the story changes then the town that accepted it isn't the town that told it — so accepting the letter doesn't correct the town, it replaces it.
5. Nobody is obliged to accept evidence that would destroy them.
6. So the town is entitled to keep the story, and she's entitled, maybe obliged, to leave the letter in the drawer.

If I have to lose one I'd rather lose (5) than (1), tho I'm not sure that's the real problem. I want to know which step to cut, why it looked innocent sitting there, what cutting it costs her — and whether anything survives of the intuition that a community has standing here that a lone individual doesn't (she's read her Halbwachs, which is why I let her talk like this).

Give it to me as exactly five numbered items, a sentence or two each, no intro and no conclusion: (i) the step she has to give up, (ii) why it looked fine, (iii) what giving it up costs, (iv) the strongest repaired version of the argument that doesn't prove too much, (v) your verdict on the residue — is there any real asymmetry between a town and a person here, or was that just decoration I liked the sound of.
