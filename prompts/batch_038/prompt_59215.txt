Two members of a hospital ethics reading group I sit in spent an entire session arguing over a case one of them had invented, then asked me to keep score, which I'm not equipped to do. Here is the case as they framed it.

A regional health authority holds one batch of a drug, enough for exactly one of two uses. Used the first way, it cures Ada, a patient who will certainly die within the year without it. Used the second way, it can be divided among a million people, each of whom otherwise faces a one-in-a-million chance of dying within the year, and the drug removes that chance outright. The second use therefore prevents one death in expectation, and the authority's choice is between one identified patient who will definitely die and a million unidentified people who, between them, will very probably lose exactly one of their number either way.

Move 1 — Ruth said Scanlonian contractualism settles this for Ada, and settles it cleanly. Compare complaints one person at a time: against a principle that lets her die, Ada's complaint is her death; against a principle that saves her instead, any single member of the million has only a one-in-a-million shift in her own risk, which nobody could reasonably treat as grounds for rejection. Those complaints may not be added together, she said, because there is no person whose situation is improved by the sum.

Move 2 — David said this is the reductio rather than the result. The two uses are equal in the death they contain, and if a theory prefers the one that kills somebody nobody has met yet purely because of where she stands at the moment of decision, it is not tracking anything morally real; run this allocation a million times and the second use saves a million people the first lets die. He added that Ruth has written the identifiable-victim effect — a well-documented distortion in how people respond to named sufferers as against statistical ones — into the definition of right and wrong, and a theory that reproduces a known bias on demand cannot claim to be vindicating our intuitions about it.

Move 3 — Ruth said the objection only looks forceful because David keeps changing when the comparison is made. Looked at backward, each option leaves one dead person, so the ex post comparison cannot rank them at all; it can only locate a victim after the fact, which is not the same as finding a complaint. A principle has to be justified to people in the standpoints they occupy when the decision is made, and in that standpoint each of the million genuinely stands to lose almost nothing — while weighting one person's own possible outcomes by their probability is not summing across persons but simply stating her complaint correctly.

Move 4 — David said then scale it up: give each of the million a one-in-a-thousand chance of death instead, so the second use now prevents a thousand expected deaths. Ex post there are a thousand actual dead people, each with a complaint precisely as strong as Ada's, and one person on the other side to pair them against, so Ruth's own pairwise test, applied honestly, comes out for the million. That shows, he said, that the test was never pairwise: it is a refusal to look at numbers dressed up as a procedure.

Move 5 — Ruth said the scaled case proves her position rather than his. In it each of the million still has an ex ante complaint of one-in-a-thousand, and a thousand such complaints are not a thousand complaints of anybody — there is no person for whom the total is a loss. She accepted the consequence outright: the drug goes to Ada even when a thousand statistical lives ride on the other choice, because a theory that will not say that has stopped being about what we owe to each other and become arithmetic over populations.

Please adjudicate the exchange move by move. Rate each of the five moves somewhere on a scale running from clearly mistaken to clearly right, and give your reasoning; where something important has been overlooked by both of them — a distinction they are sliding past, a resource within the theory that neither side deploys, a step that does not follow — flag it inside the item for the move where it first becomes visible.

Deliver this as exactly five numbered items, one per move, each no longer than two sentences, with no introduction and no conclusion.
