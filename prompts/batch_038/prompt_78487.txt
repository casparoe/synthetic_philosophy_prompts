A friend of mine who works in political risk analysis and I spent most of last night on the same disagreement, and neither of us could close it, so I would like a careful third-party reading.

The episode is the collapse of Suharto's New Order in 1997–98. The theory is performance legitimacy: the claim that an authoritarian regime's right to rule is underwritten by delivered material results, so that when delivery fails the claim on obedience lapses and the regime becomes vulnerable.

The facts I would like treated as given, roughly as I have them: Indonesia averaged growth near seven percent a year for three decades and cut the share of the population in poverty from a majority in the early 1970s to something like a tenth by the mid-1990s. The crisis began in Thailand in July 1997 and spread; the rupiah fell from roughly 2,400 to the dollar to well over fourteen thousand during 1998; food and fuel prices surged after subsidy cuts taken under IMF programs; student demonstrations grew, students were shot dead at Trisakti University in mid-May 1998, and riots followed in Jakarta, Medan, and Surakarta, directed in part at ethnic Chinese businesses. Suharto had been reappointed unopposed for another term by the People's Consultative Assembly in March 1998; within weeks the Golkar chairman, who also presided over that assembly, publicly urged him to step down, cabinet ministers resigned, and he resigned on 21 May, succeeded by his vice president. Malaysia, hit by the same regional shock, refused the IMF route, imposed capital controls and a fixed exchange rate in late 1998, removed its deputy prime minister, and its ruling coalition won the general election the following year.

What I want from you:

1. State performance legitimacy as a conditional with content: who the actors are, what counts as the delivered performance, and what would count as evidence that legitimacy has been withdrawn. Then confront the measurement problem — there were no credible surveys, so withdrawal of legitimacy must be read off elite defection and street mobilization, which is also the thing the theory is supposed to explain. Is that circularity fatal, or manageable with the right discipline?

2. Run the episode through it. Where does the theory earn its keep — the sequence, the speed, the fact that thirty years of delivery bought not one year of grace? Where is it silent or actively misleading — on lags, on whether performance is judged in absolute terms or against a reference point that three decades of growth kept raising, on identifying who exactly is doing the withdrawing?

3. My friend's sharper objection: performance legitimacy comes in at least two forms, a mass-belief version, on which the population withholds consent, and an elite-bargain version, on which the army, Golkar, and the bureaucratic and business networks stop paying because the distributive spoils have shrunk. Only the second seems to describe May 1998. Is the elite version the same theory, a different theory wearing the same name, or a refinement the theory always needed and lacked?

4. Take up the endogeneity worry. The New Order's delivery record was produced by the same patronage machinery whose liabilities — crony-connected credit, concentrated corporate exposure, a family that captured the rents — converted a regional currency shock into a domestic price catastrophe. Can a theory that treats delivered performance as an exogenous input accommodate a regime whose legitimacy asset is simultaneously its vulnerability?

5. Take the Malaysia contrast seriously. Same shock, comparable developmental record, regime survived. Is that a refutation, or does the theorist's reply — diffuse support, an ethnic bargain, no succession vacuum, a military without autonomous political standing — reveal that performance legitimacy is doing no independent explanatory work at all? Say explicitly which observation would count against performance legitimacy as applied to a named regime, and what the concept cannot speak to in principle.

6. Then reverse the direction and tell me what the case does to the concept: a confirmation, an anomaly, or something it never anticipated — for instance a regime that had to keep delivering in order to stay entitled, and therefore could not afford the political slack that would have let it survive a single bad year.

I want the analysis disciplined about the mapping and honest about where the theory falls quiet rather than pressed into service. No references or further reading, please.
