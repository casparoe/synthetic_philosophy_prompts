I am in the second year of a history of science doctorate, working on the reception of Newtonian natural philosophy in eighteenth-century Britain, and I am trying to acquire enough command of Newton's own theological writings to read the secondary literature critically rather than simply defer to it. Over the weekend I read the closing chapter of a well-regarded trade history of the seventeenth century — the sort of book that is scrupulous with the physics and less scrupulous elsewhere — and its section on Newton's religion contains a passage that I have transcribed below. Before I carry any of it into a seminar, I would like a sober audit of it: which claims a Newton scholar would accept without qualification, which are legitimate simplifications that nonetheless cost something, and which are wrong or misleading in a way that matters.

The passage reads:

"Newton wrote far more, by volume, about theology and alchemy than about mathematics and the natural world, and much of it stayed in manuscript because his conclusions were heretical: he had satisfied himself by collating the Greek text that the doctrine of the Trinity rests on passages inserted into Scripture in the fourth century, and he was consequently something close to a deist in private, a Unitarian before Unitarianism had a name. His physics and his heresy pointed the same way. The God of the Principia is a clockmaker, and because the clock is imperfectly made he has to intervene from time to time to restore the planets to their proper courses — a dependence Newton acknowledged openly and was mocked for by Leibniz, whose objection was that a watch needing to be rewound betrays a clumsy workman. Newton had no answer to that, and the concession is what makes his theology hostage to his astronomy: the argument for God in his system depends on showing that the clock runs down, so once Laplace could account for the long-term stability of the solar system mathematically, the God of the Scholium ceased to be a hypothesis anyone needed. Newton seems to have sensed as much, which is why his last decades went into dating the prophecies and calculating when the apocalypse would arrive, and why almost nothing of his real belief was known until his papers were bought at auction in the twentieth century."

I will say where my own suspicions lie, though I do not yet trust them. I suspect the deist label is a category mistake rather than a defensible shorthand, since what I have read suggests Newton was about as far from a rationalist critic of revelation as one could be while still rejecting the Nicene faith; and I suspect the Leibniz episode is being narrated with the burden of the argument reversed. What I cannot yet do is say what the correct picture would be in its place, or how much of the passage survives once those two things are repaired.

So, taking the passage claim by claim: which parts are accurate, which are simplifications a specialist would tolerate, and which are false?
