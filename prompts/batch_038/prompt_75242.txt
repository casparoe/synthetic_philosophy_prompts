The question as it appeared on the paper: "Explain the non-identity problem and say what it shows about our obligations to future people. Does it have any bearing on the repugnant conclusion?" Time allowed: one hour, one of four questions.

Here's the script:

Parfit's non-identity problem starts from a case of social policy. Suppose a society has to choose between a policy of conservation and a high-energy policy of depletion. Depletion leaves everyone in the future somewhat worse off — call it quality of life 70 instead of 90 — but it also, through the ordinary butterfly effects of timing, changes which particular people get conceived. Different jobs, different trains caught, different people meeting: conception is so sensitive to timing that the people who live in the depleted future are not the same people who would have lived in the conserved one.

So "is depletion worse for them?" gets a strange answer. For any particular person who exists in the depleted future, the only alternative was never existing at all, and their life is worth living. Depletion is therefore not worse for them, and arguably they have reason to be glad of it. Nor is anyone in the conserved future wronged, since those people never exist. And yet we feel quite strongly that depletion is the wrong choice and that we would be failing the future.

The difficulty is that our ordinary moral thinking is person-affecting: an act can be wrong only if it is worse for some particular person. Parfit's conclusion is that we have to give up that restriction, or at least supplement it with an impersonal principle, because otherwise we can't explain what's bad about depletion. That's the real work the problem does, and it's why Parfit says we're looking for a Theory X that can deal with future generations rather than assuming our existing principles will stretch to cover them.

I think the same reasoning also takes the sting out of the repugnant conclusion. There we're told that an enormous population whose lives are barely worth living is better than a smaller and wonderful one, and this is supposed to be absurd. But it's only absurd if you assume that adding all those extra people is worse for somebody. It isn't. None of the people in the Z population would otherwise have existed, so they aren't harmed by being brought into a barely-worth-living life, and nobody in the A population is made worse off by their existing either. This is exactly the non-identity point: when our choices determine who exists, we can't evaluate the result by asking who has been harmed. So the repugnant conclusion isn't a reductio of total utilitarianism at all, it's just the correct impersonal verdict wearing a person-affecting disguise, and the right response is to stop hunting around for someone who has been wronged in the comparison between A and Z.

There's a further reason not to make so much of identity here. Parfit's own account of persons says identity is not what matters — what matters is psychological continuity and connectedness. If identity doesn't matter in survival, it can't be what matters in the ethics of future generations either, and so the bare fact that the depleted future contains numerically different people from the conserved future loses its grip. The non-identity problem is really just Parfit's reductionism about persons applied to population policy, and once you accept the reductionism it stops looking like a paradox and starts looking like a reminder to care about the quality of the lives that will be lived, whoever ends up living them.

All of this has practical consequences. Climate decisions taken now will determine who exists in two hundred years, so very few future complaints can be framed as harm to an identifiable person. If we want to say that emitting now is wrong we have to say it impersonally, which is what Parfit wants — but we should also be careful that impersonal reasoning doesn't license trading real, concrete suffering against the mere addition of extra happy lives.
