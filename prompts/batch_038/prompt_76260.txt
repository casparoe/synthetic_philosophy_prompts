Taking a phil of science seminar and this week's reading is all Laplaces demon and whether determinism actually buys you predictability, and for my response paper I want to use a case I designed instead of the same tired weather/butterfly example everyone else will turn in. So can you build me a thought experiment to spec?

The specs: a system that is (a) straight-up deterministic — the laws plus the complete state at one time fix a unique future, no randomness anywhere; (b) not chaotic at all, so that if you nudge the initial conditions by a hair the later behaviour moves by a hair or not at all (nobody should be able to say I'm just smuggling in the butterfly effect); (c) totally transparent to the predictor, who knows the exact rule and the exact full state with no measurement error and no ignorance left over; and (d) nevertheless defeats a prediction of one specific, finite item — a single bit, or a single number, that the system will output at a named later time — where the defeat is in-principle and not just "you'd need a faster computer."

The mechanism I want you to use: the prediction has to be fed back into the system before the moment in question. Make it concrete and ordinary — a bench instrument, a clocked gadget in a lab, a display that gets read by the thing being predicted, whatever — not a sci-fi superintelligence.

Hard rules: no quantum anything, no appeals to uncomputability or the halting problem, no chaos. The device's rule has to be dumb, something you could build from a NOT gate and a latch; it must not mention predictions or "know" it's being predicted, because then you've just stipulated the answer instead of generating it. No free-willed or spiteful agents either — if there's a person in the case they're just more machinery. And please double-check that once the prediction is inside the loop the system still has a well-defined unique future; if your gadget ends up with no consistent state at all (the ring-oscillator trap) then determinism is broken and the whole case is useless to me.

Scenario one paragraph, plausible enough that a physicist wouldn't roll their eyes at it. Then answer as exactly five numbered items, no introduction and no conclusion, each a sentence or two (only item 1 may run to a short paragraph):

1. The scenario itself.
2. The compliance check: walk (a) through (d) one at a time and say whether the case actually delivers each, especially the no-chaos and well-defined-future conditions.
3. Why the prediction fails even though the rule and the full state are known, and whether that failure says anything at all against determinism.
4. Which single feature of the case is doing all the work, and what happens if you remove it — specifically, does the case collapse if the predictor is allowed to keep the prediction private and never feed it in?
5. The strongest attack a die-hard Laplacean would make on my case, e.g. that I've just built the liar paradox in hardware and learned nothing about the physical world, and how I'd have to answer it.
