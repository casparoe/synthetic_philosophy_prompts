Terri Schiavo is the episode I want to work through, but from the advance-directive side rather than the culture-war side. Facts I'm taking as given, correct me only if I've got something badly wrong: she collapsed in early 1990 and was eventually diagnosed as permanently vegetative, she never wrote anything down, and then her husband and her parents spent fifteen years fighting over her feeding tube. It came out twice before the final removal in March 2005, with the Florida legislature and then the US Congress passing laws aimed at her specific case in between. The courts eventually found she wouldn't have wanted to be kept alive that way, on the basis of things she'd reportedly said about other people's situations.

So here's what I want. Take Dworkin's account — precedent autonomy, the split between experiential and critical interests, the idea that the competent earlier self gets to govern the incompetent later one — and run the actual case through it carefully rather than in the abstract.

First, the mapping problem. What exactly is the earlier self's directive in Schiavo, given that there never was a document? It seems to me the case forces apart two things that usually travel together: authority that comes from something she actually expressed, and authority that comes from what she'd plausibly have wanted. Almost all the work in this case was done by the second kind. Is that a gap the framework can fill, or does it quietly substitute a different justification — best interests, a fiduciary judgment — while still talking the language of autonomy? I'd like you to be specific about which move is doing the load-bearing.

Second, and this is the part I think gets skipped: both sides had a double bind. The people wanting the tube out had to hold that there was no subject left with any interests at all, and also that this non-subject's autonomy demanded the tube come out — which sounds like it needs somebody for whom things can go better or worse. The Schindlers had to argue she was still in there, aware, tracking people with her eyes, while also arguing that her earlier wishes were unknowable. Untangle that. Which of those positions is more coherent on Dworkin's own terms, and does his framework have to concede that the "she's still in there" claim, if true, actually weakens the directive rather than supporting it?

Third, the interpreter question. The husband had remarried and had children with a new partner; the parents had a religious reading of who she was. Both claimed to speak for her. Does the theory say anything at all about who gets to interpret, and about discounting an interpreter's stake in the outcome? Or is that outside it entirely?

Then run it the other way. What did the public fight actually turn on? My sense is that after a while almost nobody was arguing about her earlier wishes; the live questions were whether she was conscious, whether withdrawing food and water is killing or letting die, and which level of government had jurisdiction. If that's right, what does it show about precedent autonomy as a theory of these disputes — that it's a framing lawyers and philosophers brought to a fight that was never about it, or that the surface fight was about it in a form nobody recognized?

And finally the identity worry. If you take psychological continuity seriously, the post-1990 Terri is arguably not the person who would have made any directive, which is supposed to be the deep reason directives bind. But the same reasoning seems to undercut everyone here, including the people who wanted the tube kept in on her behalf. Does the objection land, does it overgeneralize, and is there a version of earlier-self authority that doesn't lean on identity at all?

Where the theory is silent, say so. I'd much rather read "Dworkin has no resources for this" than watch you invent some for him. No bibliography needed, no quotes, just the analysis.
