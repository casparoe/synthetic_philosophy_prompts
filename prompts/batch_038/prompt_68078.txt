I am designing the coordination layer for a consortium of nine grid operators whose software agents must, within a two-second window, either all commit to the same emergency reconfiguration or none commit at all. Each agent's payoff is strongly positive only if the other eight commit as well, mildly negative if it commits alone, and zero if everyone stands down, so standing down is the safe default. All agents watch the same message bus, but delivery is unreliable and observation is asynchronous: an agent may see a message late, or reconstruct it from a later message rather than receiving it directly.

My colleague, who wrote the first version, insists that the game-theory literature's talk of common knowledge is a philosopher's fiction that we should not design around. His argument runs by analogy: mutual knowledge of rising order is like two mirrors facing one another. Yes, you get an infinite regress of reflected images, but the fiftieth image is too dim to matter, and nothing downstream in the system can distinguish twenty nested levels of "I know that you know that I know" from indefinitely many. On that basis he replaced our synchronous all-hands broadcast step with twenty rounds of pairwise acknowledgements. On the bench the consortium stood down safely for weeks; then a single message was lost, after which eight agents committed and one did not.

Please audit the mirror analogy rather than the protocol. Lay out the correspondence explicitly — what plays the role of the first mirror, the second, the reflected image, the dimming, and the viewer — and then identify what the analogy quietly imports from the mirror case that the knowledge case lacks. Separate the disanalogies that genuinely bear on whether twenty rounds of acknowledgements can do the work the broadcast step was doing from the ones that are merely picturesque. On that basis, does his conclusion survive? Is there any sense in which high-order mutual knowledge approximates common knowledge, and if so, what would have to be true of a game, a channel, and the reasoning agents perform for the approximation to be safe — or is the failure we observed exactly what the analogy was constructed to conceal? If the mirrors are the wrong picture, give me a better analogy for what the extra structure of common knowledge adds, one I can put in front of engineers, and say what a decisive bench test of the difference would look like.
