A friend and I spent most of last night arguing about whether a system that reports distress and asks not to be switched off has any moral claim on us, and twice he reached for the same slogan — "if you can't tell the difference, there is no difference" — but he pointed it in opposite directions each time. Early on it was concessive: since we could never tell real distress apart from a perfect behavioural mimic of it, we should assume the distress is real and treat the system accordingly. Later it was deflationary: since no conceivable test could settle the matter, there is no fact of the matter, and my question about inner experience is not hard but empty. Could you set out, as a menu rather than a single gloss, the distinct precise theses that one sentence might be expressing — separating at minimum the epistemic reading on which indistinguishability licenses a belief, the pragmatic reading on which it licenses a course of treatment, and the metaphysical or verificationist reading on which indistinguishability constitutes the absence of a difference — and, within each, pin down what counts as telling, who is doing the telling, over what range of possible situations, and what the relevant difference is meant to be: behavioural, functional, phenomenal, or moral? For every reading, say whether it is a platitude, whether it is true, and whether it can carry the weight my friend put on it; and then tell me whether any single reading supports both of his moves, or whether sliding between them is a straightforward equivocation that I should have caught at the time.
