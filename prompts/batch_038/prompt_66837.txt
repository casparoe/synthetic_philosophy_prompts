I have no academic training in philosophy — I work in freight logistics — but I have been turning over a puzzle about blood donation for a couple of years now, and I would like a careful reconstruction of what the practice actually assumes. Let me lay out the facts as I understand them, so the analysis does not depend on my getting the details exactly right.

Most wealthy countries run their whole-blood supplies on unpaid volunteers, and the World Health Organization has spent decades urging member states to build their blood services on voluntary, non-remunerated donation. In the United States it is nonetheless entirely legal and completely ordinary to pay people for plasma. Paid plasma clinics advertise in low-income neighbourhoods, pay something in the tens of dollars per visit, and may take the same donor back as often as twice a week; the unpaid whole-blood donor gets a sandwich, a drink, a sticker and a thank-you, and is eligible again only after roughly eight weeks. The two operations sometimes sit in the same building, and one person can be an unpaid hero in one chair and a paid supplier in the other. Reimbursed travel and small token gifts are treated as unproblematic. A cheque of equivalent value is treated as a change of kind, not of degree.

Add a second piece. Since the mid-1980s, American law has made it a crime to transfer human organs for valuable consideration. Bone marrow is not an organ, yet compensation for marrow donation was prohibited anyway, at a time when harvesting it meant surgery under anaesthesia. Later, a technique emerged that collects the relevant cells by a process close to ordinary blood donation. A public-interest law firm sued, and in 2011 a federal appeals court held unanimously that marrow obtained that way could be compensated, because on its method of collection it fell outside the statutory definition. Officials promptly moved to redefine the category so that the ban would bite again. For a period, then, whether paying a donor was a felony turned on which machine was used, not on how much the donor was asked to bear.

What I want is an account of what this whole arrangement presupposes about acts that go beyond what anyone is required to do. Please consider at least the following readings and tell me which the practice genuinely needs and which it merely sits comfortably alongside:

- that money degrades the act and makes it worth less than the identical act done for nothing;
- that money leaves the act exactly as good but makes it owed — the paid donor who fails to appear has broken a commitment and can reasonably be blamed, while the unpaid donor who never appears has done nothing wrong at all, so payment moves the act across a boundary from above-and-beyond to required rather than down a scale of value;
- that nothing about the donor or the act is at issue, and the point is about the audience: public praise is a limited resource that cash dilutes, so the unpaid system is protected for the sake of those who do the praising;
- that there is no moral theory here at all, only public health — paid donors have an incentive to conceal risk factors — and the moral vocabulary is decoration on an epidemiological rule.

Then: how could one tell these apart from the practice itself? If the fourth were operative, we would expect payment to be permitted wherever screening was good enough, and we would not expect the plasma exception; if the third were operative, the rules should track how visibly the donor is celebrated. Say what the existing pattern of exceptions actually points to.

I would also like the internal tensions addressed. We still thank the paid donor, and we still call what he does a donation. Is that loose speech, or does the practice depend on a category of paid-yet-praiseworthy acts? Does going beyond the call of duty require that the act be owed by nobody, or only that it not be owed by this agent? Why should the form of a transfer matter more than its size — what account of value makes a bus fare innocent and fifty pounds corrupting? And in the marrow episode, is there any moral theory on which the distinction between an organ and a non-organ collected by a machine can do real work, or is the practice simply borrowing moral language from a legal definition?

Finally, run it in the other direction. Suppose we concluded that paying donors was permissible and would save lives. What would we lose conceptually, as distinct from politically — which words currently doing work in the practice would stop having a job? And is there any reading on which the mere availability of a paid option changes what the unpaid donor is doing: making the gift more impressive, or exposing the unpaid system as a way of getting the poor to supply the rich for free?

Where the practice does not decide between rival accounts, please say so plainly rather than settling on one confident story. If these positions have names in the literature, use the names, but I care about the reasoning rather than the labels.
