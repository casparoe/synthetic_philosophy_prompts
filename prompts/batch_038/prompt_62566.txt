I write novels for a living, and for the last fifteen years I have also been a member of Bellwether Press, a writer-owned cooperative press and reading series in a city of about two hundred thousand people. When nine of us founded it, I was the only one who enjoyed that sort of composition, so I was handed a legal pad and told to produce a Charter; I did, in a weekend, and being fresh at the time from a self-amending board game I'd played in graduate school — one in which the players vote to alter the rules while the game is in progress — I built the amendment article around exactly that idea, and then, in a fit of prudence, locked it down. Article IX as it stood until last March read: "Section 1. Any provision of this Charter may be amended by the affirmative vote of two-thirds of all Members in good standing, taken at a stated meeting, provided that the text of the proposed amendment was circulated in writing to every Member not fewer than thirty days before the meeting. Section 2. This Article may not be amended, and no amendment adopted under it may alter the requirement of a two-thirds vote set out in Section 1." On March 14, with thirty-one members on the roll, notice having been duly circulated, a faction moved to strike the two-thirds requirement in favor of a simple majority for all articles. The chair ruled the motion out of order under Section 2. A member then moved that the meeting regard Section 2 as void, "on the ground that no assembly can be bound against its will by a rule laid down by an earlier assembly"; that resolution carried 19–12, and the threshold amendment immediately carried 19–12 as well — a majority of the members present, but short of the twenty-one votes the old rule would have demanded.

Everything since has been conducted under the simple-majority rule, and the tangle has compounded. In April we passed Amendment 10, restructuring dues, by 22–9, with proper notice; I should say plainly that it would have carried under the two-thirds rule too. In May we passed Amendment 11, admitting translators as voting members, by 16–15; the roll grew to thirty-six. In October we passed Amendment 12, which permits the press to take outside capital in exchange for a nonvoting share of revenue and redirects a portion of backlist royalties from the equal pool into a fund administered by a designee of the investor — a small foundation run by the daughter of one of our earliest subscribers. Amendment 12 carried 19–17, and five of the translators seated by Amendment 11 voted in favor of it, which is a wrinkle several of us keep staring at: the body that decided the case may itself be a creature of the contested procedure. Last week four members, two of them among the original nine, filed a written appeal to the board asserting that Amendment 12 and everything after March 14 is void, since a rule adopted without authority cannot confer authority, and that the March maneuver was not an amendment but a small successful coup. The other side answers that eleven months of minutes, dues, rosters, elections, and published books have all been conducted under the new rule, that a procedure universally treated as valid by the people it governs cannot simply be un-rung, and that the appealants' position would also unseat the translators, whom most of us are fond of and two of whom have said they will resign if Amendment 12 is unwound. The foundation has already advanced us forty thousand dollars; roughly thirty-one thousand of it is sitting in a new binding line, so any declaration of invalidity carries a restitution problem as well as a constitutional one. Our board is six members — two novelists, a poet, a bookbinder, a retired municipal lawyer, and a librarian — we meet Thursday, the foundation wants a countersigned term sheet by the end of the month, and I have been asked to circulate a written opinion in advance. You should know that my standing to hold a view is compromised: I drafted Section 2, and I have an obvious interest in not being told that I wrote something incoherent, so I want the memo to read as the board's reasoning rather than as my advocacy.

Please draft that opinion, in American English, somewhere between 1,200 and 1,800 words, in a register that a bookbinder and a librarian can follow without being condescended to. It must come to a definite bottom line — declare the March resolution and its progeny void and revert to the two-thirds rule; treat March 14 as a founding act that replaced the Charter and is now simply what our rules are; void it prospectively while curing the past by re-vote; or some fourth course you think better — and it must specify the mechanics and the calendar: what notice goes out and when, what is re-voted and under which threshold and by which roll, what we say to the foundation before the end of the month, and what we do about the money already spent. It should also engage the two questions the appealants and their opponents keep circling without ever quite putting them: whether a rule of change can forbid its own amendment at all, and what it means if it cannot (our retired municipal lawyer observes, I think correctly, that the federal Constitution itself shields at least one provision from amendment — no state may be deprived of its equal vote in the Senate without that state's consent — and nobody treats that shield as decorative); and whether the validity of a procedure is ultimately a matter of its pedigree or of the fact that the people governed by it have gone on accepting it, so that a change made outside the rules can become law by succeeding. Give the losing side its strongest form rather than a straw version, and address the bootstrap difficulty in any cure you propose: if the tainted amendments are void, by what authority does the body re-vote them, and do the translators seated under Amendment 11 get a vote on the question of whether Amendment 11 was validly adopted. Finally, close with a short paragraph, visibly separate from the recommendation, stating how confident you are in the course of action you propose and naming the single premise on which it most depends and about which you are least sure, along with what fact or argument would most change your mind — I would rather have your uncertainty marked than smoothed over, since three of us on the board have been reading this affair as though it were a plot, and plots are exactly where I am supposed to be the expert.
