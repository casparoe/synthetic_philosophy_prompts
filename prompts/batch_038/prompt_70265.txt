I'm a third-year PhD student in philosophy (decision theory mostly, drifting toward the economics of risk) and our reading group has been going back and forth on that ergodicity-economics stuff for a couple of weeks now. The version of the argument that's really gotten under my skin comes from a friend who works in a quant shop, and he always reaches for the same picture to explain why he thinks expected utility theory is a dead end. The picture goes like this: when you ask what the expected value of a gamble is, you're really asking what happens on average across a huge crowd of copies of you, each of whom takes the gamble exactly once, in a bunch of parallel universes — and then averaging across the crowd. But you're not a clone in a parallel universe, you're one person living one life in one direction, so the only average you can ever actually experience is the one along your own timeline: what happens to your wealth if you take the same gamble again and again. Those two averages come apart as soon as wealth compounds, and the one you should care about is the second. His favorite demo is the fair coin that multiplies your stake by 1.5 on heads and by 0.6 on tails: the clone-crowd average says +5% a round, the along-your-own-life average says about −5% a round, so the crowd statistic is telling you to take a bet that grinds the actual you toward zero. From this he concludes three things: that utility functions are a psychological patch over a mathematical mistake, that Kelly-style growth-rate maximization isn't a special case of expected utility but a replacement for it, and that risk aversion isn't a taste parameter at all, just a failure to notice which of the two averages you're computing.

My advisors response is basically that the analogy is rhetorical shrink-wrap around one line of algebra — the expected value of the log isn't the log of the expected value — and that if you plug log utility into expected utility theory you get exactly the same recommendations as growth-rate maximization in his compounding examples, so nothing has been refuted, only renamed. He adds that Samuelson made the same point decades ago, and that the whole edifice wobbles the moment you allow for finite horizons, bequests, insurance, or an agent who genuinely just likes to gamble. I can see the pull of both sides, and what I want is the analogy itself audited rather than the literature summarized.

So: give me the correspondence table. What in the source picture (parallel universes, the crowd of clones, one life lived forward, the act of averaging) maps onto what in the target (the probability distribution over outcomes, the stochastic process, the time average, the growth rate)? Then sort the disanalogies into the ones that actually threaten his three conclusions and the ones that are just decoration. Specifically, I suspect the clone picture smuggles in stationarity, multiplicative dynamics, and the premise that the only thing worth caring about is the realized trajectory of a single agent — and that it quietly converts a descriptive claim about which statistic is bigger into a normative claim about which statistic you ought to use. Tell me where I'm wrong about that, if I am. Are there settings where the clone reading is literally correct — a fund allocating across many simultaneous clients, a policymaker choosing for a whole population, an insurer pricing a book — and does the existence of those settings help him or hurt him? And if the analogy can't carry the weight: what would be a better analogy for the actual technical point, and do any of his three conclusions survive the swap?
