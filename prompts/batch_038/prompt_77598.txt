I'm a philosophy professor and for a few years now I've co-run a methods module with the psychology department here — the bit where MSc and first-year PhD students have to justify any study that leans on what people say about their own experience. I see fifteen or so protocols a term, twenty minutes each if I'm lucky, and I'm the only person on the panel who's read any philosophy of mind. When I'm away the postdoc who signs things off has no philosophy at all, so whatever I give them has to work without me in the room and without the students having to read anything.

The thing I actually believe, and want the procedure to enforce, is roughly Dennett's line: there's no theatre. No single place and moment where processing gets presented to somebody, which means there often isn't a determinate fact about what the experience was like before the subject was asked, or about which of two experiences came first. Reports are constructions, and the probe is part of what fixes the content. I keep trying to say this in seminars and it lands either as nihilism (so self-report is worthless) or as mush. I want it as a checklist instead.

So: write the procedure they run over their own design before submission. Numbered questions in order; for each, what a passing answer looks like; and what happens on a failure — crucially, which failures are 'your measurement is bad, fix it' and which are 'no measurement would answer this, so rewrite the claim'. I want at least one gate that specifically catches a design assuming a determinate temporal order of experience, and one that catches treating the absence of a report as the absence of an experience. There's also that pair of ways a report can end up revised — the one where the memory gets edited afterwards and the one where the story is already settled before it's ever told — I can never remember which label goes with which, and I don't want the students to have to either: if that distinction earns its place, keep it, but in plain description, not in the literary nicknames. Drop whatever else can go without the procedure turning to soup, and tell me what you dropped and why.

Then run it on three designs: (a) a behavioural masking study where people report whether the word appeared before or after the mask; (b) an experience-sampling app that pings people eight times a day and asks them to describe the thought they were just having; (c) my own pilot, where people mark to the nearest tenth of a second the moment they made up their mind during a hard choice and I correlate that with the movement. Dont spare my feelings on (c) — if its dead, say so, and say what could be salvaged from it.

Last thing: be straight with me about which of the steps are genuinely mechanical and which are just my own judgement wearing a clipboard, and what the whole apparatus can't do. It shouldn't pretend to settle whose theory of consciousness is right, and I'd rather it said so than overclaimed.
