So next month I'm giving a talk at a cultural evolution workshop and I want a prep sheet for the Q&A, not a defense of my view. The room is mostly modelers — people who build population-level transmission models in the dual-inheritance vein — plus two cognitive anthropologists who work on attractors and minimally counterintuitive content, one historian of science who studies lab notebooks and instruments, and a linguist who works on sound change. Nobody in there is a Dawkins fan. They think "the meme" is an unmeasurable unit and they've spent twenty years building perfectly good selectionist models of culture without it, so they're not hostile to selection, they're hostile to replicator talk.

The thesis: the thing that gets copied in cultural transmission isn't a mental representation, it's a procedure stored in an external medium — a guild pattern book, a ledger format, a liturgy, a syllabus, a codebase. Brains are read/write heads, not the storage. Selection acts on archives: which ones survive, which get instantiated into new archives, which get made mandatory by some authority that can enforce the checksum. My payoff claim is that this relocates the copying-fidelity parameter out of the learner's cognition and into the medium, which is where it always should have been — you can hold psychology fixed across two populations and get completely different transmission dynamics just by changing how the procedure is stored.

The two worked examples I'm building the talk around are (a) craft guild pattern books, where I want to say the tradition outlives the loss of individual masters, and (b) liturgical language: fixed wording preserved for centuries by communities that don't understand a word of it, which I take to be genuinely awkward for the mental-replicator story. I also have a short section on oral traditions where I argue they're drift-without-a-checksum, and I know that one's going to draw fire.

What I want: maybe eight objections this specific room will actually raise, ordered by how much damage they'd do. Phrase them the way these people would phrase them — a modeler's objection sounds nothing like an anthropologist's, and I want to hear the difference. For each, a two-minute reply and a note on where the reply is soft or where I'm quietly changing the subject to survive. Tell me which ones I should just concede in the first two minutes before someone does it for me, and which single reply has to land or the whole talk collapses. And include the question I'm praying nobody asks. No reading list and no citations, I know the literature, I want the arguments.
