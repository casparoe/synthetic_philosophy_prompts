I'm drafting a methods section for a consortium that assigns functional annotations to gene products, and the philosophers on our advisory panel have told me, more or less unanimously, that I can't write the section until I've picked a theory of function. Before I pick anything, I want the specification: what would a theory of biological function have to deliver to be usable for this job?

Here is what makes the job hard. The same pipeline has to issue function claims about entities with wildly different histories: conserved enzymes with deep orthologs across the tree of life; genes duplicated recently, with both copies retained and neither yet clearly specialized; genes acquired by horizontal transfer from a lineage whose ecology we know nothing about; de novo genes that arose from previously noncoding sequence and are found in a single genus; and wholly synthetic constructs designed in silico and placed into a minimal-cell chassis that has itself had most of its genome deleted. Any theory that quietly assumes its target always has a long selective pedigree behind it will misdescribe a substantial fraction of our annotations, and any theory that ignores history entirely will misdescribe the majority.

Write the desiderata. For each, give the case that motivates it, and say whether it is a genuine constraint on an adequate theory or a commitment of one camp wearing the mask of a neutral requirement — I suspect a fair number of the demands made in this literature are just etiological or causal-role assumptions restated as criteria. At a minimum, address how a theory is to handle:

- malfunction versus absence of function: what it says about a pseudogene, a vestigial structure, a catalytically dead enzyme that is still bound and doing structural work in a complex, and a designed construct that reliably does what it was built to do in vivo but was never selected for anything;
- promiscuity and moonlighting: most proteins have a dominant activity plus weak secondary activities that matter only in unusual conditions, or that later become the raw material for something new. The theory must say which of these count as functions, whether they count in degrees, and relative to which containing system;
- level and grain: the same molecule gets annotated at the level of a chemical reaction, a pathway, a cell, a tissue, and an organism, and our database mixes these freely. Are these competing answers to one question, or different questions?
- epistemic output: the pipeline returns "unknown" millions of times over. The theory should tell us whether that is a claim about the world or about us, and what an annotator is entitled to assert from partial evidence.
- anhistoricity (my name for it, not a term of art): the theory should not require a selection history as a precondition for there being a function, and it should say what does ground a function claim when no history exists to appeal to.

Then give me the joint-satisfiability analysis, which is the part I actually need. I have been told that accommodating malfunction and accommodating anhistoric entities pull in opposite directions — that the former requires history and the latter forbids it — and I want to know whether that is a real incompatibility, an artifact of how the two requirements are usually phrased, or a genuine tradeoff with a stable midpoint. If some subset of the desiderata cannot all be met, say which, and say what the least damaging surrender looks like.

Identify which single requirement does the most to eliminate candidates, and then score the standard families — selected-effect accounts, causal-role accounts, goal-contribution accounts, propensity theories, and the pluralist or consensus positions — briefly against the list. A short paragraph each is plenty, and no references; I'll do my own reading.

One standing constraint on your assessment: the theory will be applied by people who are sometimes wrong and who cannot see the whole system. Wherever a requirement would make function claims unknowable without complete phylogenetic knowledge or a full causal map of the organism, mark it as failing an applicability constraint and say what weaker formulation of it might survive that objection.
