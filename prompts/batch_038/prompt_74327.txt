Six weeks ago I was appointed to the standing committee at our regional health authority that decides which treatments receive public funding, and by the end of my second meeting I was in an argument with another member that has now become a daily correspondence. The chair has asked the two of us to submit a joint memo before the next sitting, since the committee is split along exactly our line. I am writing because I would like that memo drafted properly rather than negotiated badly.

His position is contractualist in the Scanlonian sense: a rule is just only if no single person affected by it could reasonably reject it, and the test is carried out by setting one person's complaint against another's, pairwise, never by summing complaints across people. Mine is aggregationist: I hold that a sufficiently large number of sufficiently serious harms can together outweigh one death, and that his procedure delivers aggregative-looking verdicts only when the numbers happen to fall on the side his pairwise contest already favors, which I regard as luck rather than principle.

Please write the joint statement such a collaboration would produce. It must contain no references, no bibliography, and no name-dropping; I want the reasoning laid out so that a colleague who has never read either of us can follow it, and I want every claim worded so that its own side would sign it without reservation.

Specifically:

1. A numbered list of claims both of us can accept. This should include at least one concession from each side about a case the other treats as decisive — a case in which summing seems plainly wrong to me, and a case in which pairwise comparison seems plainly wrong to him — plus an explicit statement of what our disagreement is *not* about.

2. A table of the residual disagreements: the disagreement, his formulation in wording he would endorse, my formulation in wording I would endorse, and a marking of whether each row is substantive or merely verbal. I suspect a good deal of it is verbal, in particular whether "adding up" is being used in two senses — combining magnitudes versus counting complaints that each have full weight — and I want that separated out rather than smoothed over.

3. An assessment of the intermediate position, on which many complaints may be combined only once each reaches a threshold of seriousness. Does that view dissolve our dispute, relocate it, or leave it exactly where it was?

4. Four to six cruxes: sub-questions such that settling one would actually move at least one of us. For each, give the concrete case both of us would agree bears on it, stated in plain numbers and symptoms, and record each side's prediction in advance of what the other will say about it and of what would count as a concession.

5. Three knots I want handled explicitly, because they have dominated our correspondence:
   - Our funding rules require a rejected application to be accompanied by a statement of *who* could reasonably reject the rule and what their complaint is. That makes an identified patient easy to write down and a class of six thousand statistical patients awkward. Does the pairwise procedure inherit this asymmetry, or is it a defect of our paperwork rather than of his view?
   - Complaint strength is standardly measured by how much worse off a person would be under the rejected rule, which appears to make the procedure blind to what a treatment costs. So a cheap cure for a moderate condition and an enormous outlay for a dire one can look identical from inside the comparison. Both of us should say whether that blindness is a virtue or a fatal defect, and whether it is fixable.
   - Whether the pairwise verdict is invariant when we add a third funding option that nobody prefers. He claims it is; I claim that adding an option nobody wants can change which pairwise contest gets run, and therefore change the ranking.

6. A short closing section stating which disagreements remain genuinely substantive after all of the above, and flagging anything either of us would be signing only under protest.

Finally, apply the whole apparatus to our live case, and state it in the memo: with one budget line we can fund either a drug that prevents death within twelve months for roughly forty patients with a rare condition, or a programme that prevents two years of severe, disabling pain for roughly six thousand patients with a common one. Record his verdict and my verdict, the reasoning each of us would give in wording the other would not dispute as a description, and whether the cruxes above identify anything that would settle the case for both of us rather than merely for one. Do not tell me who is right; the point of the exercise is that the committee can see precisely where we still differ.
