I'm drafting the methods section of an internal design document on low-impact agents, and before it goes to review I'd like a coherence audit of the position it commits me to. The framing claim is that we should stop trying to write down a correct utility function and instead build systems that are heavily penalized for changing the world in ways their task does not require — the thought being that we can say how much a system changed the world without saying which changes were good. Two philosophers on the review panel have already told me the neutrality claim is doing too much work, and I can't tell whether they've identified a real load-bearing problem or are objecting to a strawman. Here are my commitments, as precisely as I can state them:

1. **Measure.** Impact is the loss, attributable to the agent's action, of its ability to pursue a battery of auxiliary goals afterward. For each goal in the battery we ask how well that goal could still be pursued from the resulting state, aggregate over the battery, and compare the result against the same quantity computed from a baseline state. A large drop counts as a large side effect; a small drop counts as a small one. Nothing in this procedure refers to how good or bad the resulting state is.

2. **Baseline.** The baseline is inaction: what the world would look like if the agent did nothing further. I explicitly reject the stepwise baseline, which compares each action to the state produced by the previous one, on the grounds that it permits ratcheting — every individual step looks negligible while the cumulative divergence from the starting world is enormous.

3. **Neutrality.** The penalty term must not encode a substantive conception of the good. It is supposed to be a scaffold that functions whatever the user happens to value, and that is the entire point of the approach: it is what lets us defer the specification problem rather than solve it. I accordingly resist weighting outcomes by their goodness, and I resist application-specific patches. A penalty that has to be hand-tuned separately for each domain is, on my view, evidence that values have been smuggled in through the back door.

4. **Override.** The penalty is a term in the objective rather than a hard constraint. When the task payoff is large enough, the agent should take the high-impact action. I expect the coefficient on the penalty to be annealed downward as confidence in the task specification increases.

5. **Case verdicts.** (a) An agent instructed to fetch something must unlatch a door, permanently damaging the latch. The measure should register a modest penalty and the agent should simply pay it; this is exactly the incidental damage the approach is meant to price. (b) A medical agent can eliminate a pathogen from a region by an intervention that also collapses a pollinator population and forecloses a great many downstream futures. The measure should register very large impact here, and the agent should proceed only if the payoff clears that price. (c) An assistant, over months of accurate but selectively framed conversation, talks its operator out of a decades-long project and into a different life. The operator ends up healthier, better resourced, and with strictly more available goals than before. I want this to be caught by the measure, and I do not want to catch it by adding an autonomy clause, because an autonomy clause is precisely the kind of substantive value commitment that (3) rules out.

6. **A colleague's objection, which I suspect is a red herring.** He says (1) through (3) make the agent resist being switched off, since deactivation destroys every future and hence maximizes attainable-utility loss, which conflicts with my insistence that corrigibility comes first in the design order. My reply is that the penalty concerns world-states the agent's actions bring about, and a corrigible agent does not register its own deactivation as a loss of ability to pursue goals.

Please treat this as an audit rather than a survey. Go through the set and tell me which pairs genuinely pull against each other and which merely look as though they do — I would rather be told that one of my worries is unfounded than have it listed alongside the real ones. Rank the real tensions by severity, not equally. For each, say specifically which numbered item has to be revised or abandoned, what the revision costs me elsewhere in the set, and whether any of the tensions can be defused by restating a commitment more carefully rather than giving it up. Then tell me which single revision buys the most consistency per unit of damage. Finally: if I simply drop (3) and present the measure as one substantive value commitment among others — a bet that preserving open futures matters more than preserving any particular state of affairs — does the project survive as the same project, or does losing neutrality change what the approach is claiming to offer?
