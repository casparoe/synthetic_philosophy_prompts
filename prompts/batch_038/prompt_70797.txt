I'm three years into a philosophy PhD and my chapter is supposed to be about what you have to know before objective chance can tell you anything. Last week I sketched an argument for a workshop, aiming at a modest point about admissibility, and it came out proving something I can't live with, and now I cant see where I went over the line. Notation: X(p) = x means the chance of p is x; H_x is the chance hypothesis that X(p) = x; p is some concrete unsettled event, say the next flip of coin C or the next decay of this tritium atom; A is a complete description of all particular matters of fact up to now; M is a complete description of the whole mosaic, past present and future.

(1) PP: for any event p, any chance hypothesis H_x, and any admissible evidence E, rational credence obeys Cr(p | H_x & E) = x.

(2) Admissible evidence, on the gloss everybody uses, is evidence whose only bearing on p goes via the chances; in practice that means evidence either entailed by chance hypotheses or else entirely about matters of fact before p's time. But the gloss can't be read off tense. If a seer who has never once been wrong wrote down yesterday what today's decay will be, her note is grammatically about the past and still inadmissible, because what you've really got is information about how things turn out. Call this the no-smuggling reading of (2).

(3) Humeanism about chance: the chance function supervenes on the mosaic. So for every H_x there's some complete mosaic proposition M such that M entails H_x, and mosaics sufficiently unlike M make H_x false.

(4) So by (3), every chance hypothesis is entailed by some inadmissible proposition -- the M that entails it -- and no chance hypothesis is entailed by anything confined to the past, unless the past already fixes everything.

(5) Closed-world case. Take a world containing one coin, flipped exactly a googol times, and nothing else ever happening. Let A describe everything up to and including the last flip. A is entirely past-tensed, since there's no future left, and by (3) A entails the true chance hypothesis H* and A entails every outcome proposition. So in that world the only evidence strong enough to pin down the chance also pins down the outcomes, and is inadmissible with respect to each of them. You can't know a chance hypothesis there while retaining only admissible evidence, so the antecedent of (1) is never satisfied and PP never applies.

(6) Generalize. By (3), chance facts are nothing over and above mosaic facts, so the only route to knowing H_x runs through mosaic evidence. By (4), any mosaic evidence sufficient for H_x reaches past the admissible boundary. Hence an agent who knows a chance hypothesis never has only admissible evidence. Hence PP never applies in any Humean world.

(7) Therefore, given Humeanism, PP is a norm with an empty domain, and objective chance is epistemically idle: positing chance facts could never change what anybody ought to believe, because the one principle connecting them to belief is never triggered. But that's absurd. Half-lives and cross-sections are exactly the cases where we take ourselves to know the chance and take knowing it to settle what to believe about the next event.

I want to keep (1), and I want to keep (3), because my advisor built her career on the best-system analysis and I'm not handing in a chapter that refutes her at step three. I've stared at (4), (5) and (6) for days and I honestly don't know whether one of them is bad or whether the whole thing is valid and I just have to swallow (7). I know this sits somewhere near Lewis's Big Bad Bug and the undermining literature, but I don't think it is that argument -- nothing in mine turns on improbable futures -- and I'd rather not be told to go read about the New Principle and call it a day. So: which step do I cut, why did that step look completely innocent to me when I wrote it down, and what does cutting it cost? Give me the price on the alternatives too, in case you think the honest move is to give up (3) or to reformulate (2). And if one of my steps is doing illegitimate work, tell me what the legitimate version of it would look like, and whether I could rebuild the argument out of that instead -- I present again Thursday and I'd like to walk in with either a fix or a funeral.
