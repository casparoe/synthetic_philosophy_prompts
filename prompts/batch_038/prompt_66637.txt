I've been reading the moral trade / compromise threads since the old Overcoming Bias days, so I figured I could referee this one. I couldnt. Two people from my local group got into it at dinner — call them N and J, both pretty sophisticated, neither one strawmanning — about whether moral trade actually holds together as a thing. It went six rounds without anyone backing down and I lost track of who was winning where.

The setup (call it move 0): N is a hardline animal-welfare prioritiser, J is a global-health prioritarian, and they'd made a little pact — N directs 20% of his giving budget to J's charities, J directs 20% to animal advocacy. Each of them did it partly as a hedge under moral uncertainty and partly to help build a norm of cooperation between people with different values.

1. N: this pact is just gains from trade. If we disagree about value but each of us cares somewhat about the other's cause, then giving up a little of my own expected moral value buys a bigger chunk of yours, so it's positive-EV for both of us. Refusing to trade is refusing a bet you'd take in any other domain.

2. J: You're running two different arguments and sliding between them. Hedging under moral uncertainty is something I can do solo — I don't need your pact to split my own budget. And if I'm actually confident my values are right, there's no uncertainty left to hedge, so that leg dies; you're stuck with pure trade, and you still haven't said what trade gets me that solo splitting doesn't.

3. N: Coordination. My 20% toward your cause is only worth paying if I expect you to pay the same tax toward mine. The point isn't this particular deal, it's the equilibrium where value-diverse agents cooperate instead of fighting each other, which matters enormously once you scale up to movements, companies, governments.

4. J: Then your framework systematically rewards the most extreme values, because bargaining power in this kind of trade scales with how intensely someone cares. An agent whose values are genuinely monstrous but very intense extracts the biggest concessions from everyone else. A compromise machine that maximizes joint satisfaction is a subsidy to extremism — it's the old utility-monster worry, and I don't see any escape from it here.

5. N: Only if you let every claim into the market. You can reprehension-proof it: trade only over claims about what's worth promoting, never over claims whose entire content is "I object to what you're doing." Otherwise it really is protection money, agreed. But that's a restriction on which values get admitted to the bargaining set, not a reason to abandon trade.

6. J: And you can't draw that line. Nearly every real moral claim has a stop-doing-that component — my objection to factory farming just is an objection to what other people are doing to chickens. Exclude anything that functions as a demand on others and you've excluded almost all of morality, so there's no trade left; include it and you're back at extortion. So the framework is either empty or exploitable.

Please adjudicate it move by move: for each of 1–6 give a rating on the scale clearly mistaken / mostly mistaken / mixed / mostly right / clearly right, with a sentence or two justifying each rating, and say explicitly where someone is equivocating or quietly swapping the subject. Then tell me what both of them are missing — in particular, is there any version of the move-0 pact that survives objection 6, or does J basically win by default? And if the disagreement has a single load-bearing point that everything else hangs off, name it.

End with a last line in exactly this form: Verdict: N | J | tie — plus no more than ten words saying why.
