I teach philosophy of mind at a research university and, for the past six years, have also co-supervised a series of interview-based studies with colleagues in our school of nursing, which has left me unusually fluent in the working vocabulary of qualitative research: verbatim transcripts and purposive sampling, codebooks and bottom-up coding, themes and superordinate themes, thematic saturation, intercoder reliability, member checking, triangulation, audit trails, reflexivity, the analyst's suspension of preconceptions, and the double hermeneutic by which the researcher makes sense of the participant making sense of their own experience. I know Dennett's heterophenomenology in the other direction — as someone who has taught it in seminar and who is neither an adherent nor a critic by trade — and I have recently found myself unable to settle an argument that began at a methods workshop, when a colleague from the nursing group remarked, half in jest, that heterophenomenology is simply interpretative phenomenological analysis with the interpretation stripped out and a metaphysics bolted on. The remark has stuck, and a methods chapter I am co-writing with two of those colleagues has stalled at precisely this point: we cannot agree whether the two approaches are doing the same work under different descriptions or are separated by something that no amount of translation will bridge.

What I would like is a clause-by-clause translation of heterophenomenology into the vocabulary I have just listed, rendered as faithfully as you can manage, and then a ledger of what failed to survive it. Please take up, one at a time: the primacy of the recorded report as the datum from which analysis begins; the deliberate suspension of the question whether that report is accurate about anything; the ascription of a coherent world to the participant by treating the report as expressing beliefs and intentions, that is, what Dennett calls the participant's heterophenomenological world; the insistence that this ascribed world is the theorist's construction rather than a private possession to which the participant has privileged access; the claim that the resulting body of data, together with concurrent neural and environmental measurement, is exhaustive for a science of consciousness, so that nothing is being left out pending better evidence; the operationalist move according to which a distinction the participant cannot detect is not a distinction worth reporting; and the use of imaging and behavioral measures to adjudicate what the participant says. For each item, tell me whether it translates cleanly, whether it translates only on condition that something be bent out of shape (and name the bend), or whether it has no expression at all in the codebook idiom.

Two constraints and then three questions. First, keep the translation honest: qualitative practice carries normative commitments — that the participant's account has standing as knowledge of their own experience, that interpretation is answerable to something the participant could in principle recognize, that the analyst's subjectivity is a contaminant to be managed — and heterophenomenology refuses some of these. Flag every point at which either framework's assumptions are smuggled in under the guise of translation, in either direction. Second, no survey of either literature and no citations or recommended readings; I want the analysis, not the apparatus. Then: (a) the language of bracketing or suspension names opposite objects in the two practices — in the qualitative idiom the analyst suspends her own preconceptions in order to grasp a world she treats as real, whereas the heterophenomenologist suspends the reality of the world the participant reports — and I want to know whether that is a merely verbal collision over a shared term of art or a genuine methodological divergence, and which of the two practices is left more exposed to skepticism as a result. (b) Is there anything in the qualitative toolkit that could serve as a check on the theorist's own intuitions, in the way reflexivity and member checking discipline the analyst's, or is that control simply absent from heterophenomenology, and if absent, what does the absence cost it? (c) Your verdict on the residue: does what refuses to translate reveal a real limit of the codebook vocabulary — its structural inability to state an ontological thesis at all, since a methodology is answerable only to procedures and not to what exists — or does it expose heterophenomenology's slide from a method into a claim about what there is as unsupported, a slide that qualitative researchers, who keep procedure and metaphysics scrupulously apart, would decline to make? Finally, tell me plainly whether my colleague's jest survives the exercise. Please write the whole response in American English.
