I chair the ethics committee of a fertility group that operates clinics in three countries and must clear every surrogacy and gamete-donor application that comes through us. In six years we have accumulated a substantial record of approvals and refusals, but we have never managed to write down the principle behind them, and I need to know what that record commits us to before we take up a difficult application on Thursday. Assume we already require psychological screening and independent legal advice for every carrier and donor; I am not asking for generic counsel about either.

Our decisions to date:

- We approved a gestational surrogacy for a woman born without a uterus: local carrier, the ordinary agency fee, and a pre-birth parentage order available in that state.
- We approved a male couple's application on materially the same terms, and separately a single woman's application on the same terms.
- We refused an application in which intended parents resident in that same state wanted to recruit a carrier in a far poorer country for a fee equivalent to roughly three years of that country's average wage. The arrangement was lawful in both places.
- We refused a donor-egg cycle in which the fee was scaled to the donor's university ranking and athletic record, several multiples of the ordinary local figure; the medicine itself was unremarkable.
- We approved a surrogacy in the country where only unpaid surrogacy is permitted: the carrier was a friend of the couple and received expenses only, though the couple could easily have afforded to travel.
- We approved with conditions a request to transfer two embryos in order to shorten the odds of needing a second cycle; we permitted one.
- We refused an application from a couple whose home country criminalises paying a carrier and would not recognise either of them as a parent: we declined to arrange the cycle or to assist with documents, even though the carrier would have been local and fairly paid.

Thursday's application differs from every one of these in several respects at once. The intended parents live in a country that does not criminalise what they propose, but whose courts will not simply register a foreign birth order: recognition requires an adoption proceeding that has been refused in some recent cases, and the couple has been told their child could go years without settled legal parents. The intended mother has carried two children; her physicians strongly advise against a third pregnancy but would not call it impossible. The couple has refinanced their house to pay and has chosen the cheapest reputable agency in the state. The carrier is a 38-year-old nurse practitioner, married, with a household income above the state median; this would be her third compensated surrogacy, she approached the agency herself, and she has asked for the standard fee plus a bonus payable only if the pregnancy ends without complications. She also wants continuing contact with the child. The egg comes from an unremarkable compensated donor at the standard rate. Finally, the couple asks us to issue a letter, on our letterhead, stating that they are the child's parents, for use in the proceedings at home.

What I need from you:

1. How will our committee rule — approve, refuse, or approve with conditions? Commit to one answer and defend it.
2. Which of our past decisions is the closest analog to this one, and precisely where does that analogy fail?
3. What rules are each consistent with our whole record so far? Show that at least two of them diverge on Thursday's application, and say which features of the application drive them apart.
4. If the record genuinely underdetermines the case, state plainly which principle we would be adopting by approving and which by refusing — the one we would have to live with across the next dozen applications, including what it would oblige us to say to the couple in the refusal we issued above about the country that criminalises payment.
5. Describe one further application that would settle which rule we are actually running, and why the candidate rules disagree about it.

I want a committed answer rather than a survey of options: tell me how you would rule and why, and confine your hedging to the one or two points where you think our own record honestly permits the opposite verdict.
