I retired three years ago after thirty-eight years of experimental work, mostly on transport properties of solids, with a long detour through systems far from equilibrium. Since then I have been reading philosophy seriously for the first time, and I have arrived at the dispute between Carlyle's thesis — that the history of the world amounts to the biography of its great men — and the structural or systemic account that replaced it in professional historiography. My interest is not in adjudicating the slogans but in what the two positions actually claim when they are applied to a documented case, and I have deliberately chosen a case from my own trade rather than the political ones usually wheeled out, because in the political cases the outcome is a bundle of too many separable things for anyone to know what they are asserting.

The case is the composition of the Principia between 1684 and 1687. The facts I take to be settled, and which you should assume rather than recount: the inverse-square conjecture and its connection to Kepler's third rule were being discussed in London in the early 1680s among Wren, Hooke, and Halley; Wren is said to have offered a prize for a demonstration, and Hooke claimed to possess one that he would not produce; Halley, frustrated, went to Cambridge in 1684 and put the question to Newton, who is reported to have answered at once that the orbit would be an ellipse and that he had already worked it out; Newton then sent Halley the short tract on orbital motion that autumn, expanded it into the three books published in 1687, and afterward quarrelled bitterly with Hooke over priority, going so far as to tell Halley he was inclined to suppress the final book before being talked into acknowledging that Hooke, Wren, and Halley had independently arrived at the inverse-square relation. Set beside that: Hooke had proposed a general system of mutual attraction and the deflection of straight-line motion into curves in the 1670s and had prompted Newton by correspondence in 1679; Huygens had published a rigorous treatment of centrifugal force in 1673 and never accepted attraction across a void; Leibniz developed a rival dynamics of the heavens in the same years. And within Newton's own output there is a useful contrast, since the calculus looks like the textbook pattern of independent near-simultaneous discovery, complete with a priority fight, whereas the dynamical synthesis of the Principia does not.

I would like you to analyze this episode as a test of the great-man versus structural debate, using the following apparatus, which I offer as a hypothesis about how the debate should be conducted rather than as established doctrine. First, decompose the claim that an individual made the difference into three separate claims: whether the outcome occurs at all, when it occurs, and in what form it occurs — its content, its level of rigor, and the credibility it commands. I suspect the two sides mostly disagree because they are making claims on different axes. Second, treat the structuralist's argument as what it appears to be, an implicit claim about a search rate: a vacancy gets filled because enough qualified people are looking, for long enough, with sufficient incentive. That is a quantitative-sounding claim, and the record lets one ask roughly how much qualified search the twenty years before 1684 actually document. Third, note that counterfactual questions require an antecedent, and that the great-man theorist wants a minimal rewrite — remove the man, hold the milieu fixed — while the structuralist wants the system permitted to re-equilibrate, which is not a counterfactual at all but an existence claim about the availability of substitutes. Say explicitly which antecedent you are using whenever you make a counterfactual claim, and say whether the debate survives contact with that requirement.

Please organize the response in these sections, in this order, at roughly these lengths:

1. The explanandum (about 200 words). Pin down what event is being asserted to have been inevitable or not — the conjecture, the demonstration, the tract, the book, the acceptance of action at a distance — and argue for one specification at one level of granularity, explaining why the others are not what the debate is really about.

2. The counterfactual, precisely stated (about 250 words). Write the antecedent you will use, list what you hold fixed and what you let vary, and state the three separate questions the decomposition yields for your chosen explanandum.

3. What the structural account gets right (about 300 words). The evidence for it from the episode, including the calculus contrast and the fact that Newton was himself prompted.

4. What it cannot accommodate (about 300 words). Where it is silent, where it misdescribes, and what the documented failure of the London circle to produce a demonstration in twenty years costs the search-rate picture.

5. What the episode does to the theory (about 350 words). Whether the case confirms, strains, or simply was never anticipated by each position; whether the priority quarrel itself is evidence about something; and whether my granularity hypothesis explains the stalemate or is just a redescription of it.

6. Verdict (about 200 words). A committed answer to the counterfactual as you specified it in section 2, with your reasons.

I want a position, not a balance sheet. Where you are genuinely uncertain, say what would settle it; where you are relying on a detail that historians dispute rather than one that is settled, flag it in a clause rather than building an argument on it. And tell me whether the vocabulary I have imported from my own field — degeneracy, criticality, a system's sensitivity to a single fluctuation versus the robustness of its attractors — is doing real work in this analysis or is merely decorative, since I cannot tell from the inside.
