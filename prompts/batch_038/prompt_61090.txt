I am in the middle of a novel whose third movement is essentially an argument about what caused a death, and I want the argument to be right rather than merely plausible. Twenty years ago I read enough philosophy to know that there are two rival ways of spelling out causation — one in terms of what would have happened otherwise, the other in terms of what an intervention would change — but I could not now adjudicate between them, and I no longer want to. What I want from you is not a comparison of the two accounts. I want cases worked through, because cases are the only form in which I can use any of this.

Here is my material, which I have invented and which I would ask you not to trade for tidier examples you already know.

Scene one. Marta, a hospice physician, raises Tomas's morphine infusion at 10:32 in the morning; he dies at 10:42. Had she left the infusion alone he would have died of the same illness at roughly two in the afternoon, in more pain, in the same bed, with the same people around it. The novel wants to know whether she killed him. My suspicion is that the answer turns on whether the effect in question is Tomas's death or Tomas's dying at 10:42, and that the two accounts handle that choice very differently — one of them lets me leave the choice implicit, the other forces me to make it openly, on the page, in front of a reader.

Scene two. Rurik, a signalman, fails to throw the points. The automatic failsafe behind him is wired to throw them if and only if the points have not been thrown by the time the train reaches the outer circuit. The train stops safely. The novel wants to know whether Rurik's omission saved the train, or whether the failsafe did, or whether neither did. What worries me here is that the failsafe's behaviour cannot be set independently of Rurik's decision: you cannot make him throw the points without thereby disarming the device whose whole function is to register his not throwing them. I do not know what either account does with a pair of variables welded together like that.

Scene three. A coach overturns on a mountain road in 1911, and the coroner's report blames the camber of the bend. The camber is a fact about the road, not a decision by anyone; it has been that way for eighty years, and at the moment of the accident it could not have been otherwise. I want to know whether a fact that no one could have intervened on is a cause at all on either account, and whether the two accounts give me the same answer here or different ones.

Please work through all three, and then give me a fourth case of your own choosing that looks like a place where the two accounts diverge but is not one — I would rather discover the mirages now than build a chapter on one. A case involving something that fails to happen would suit my purposes best.

Structure the reply in these sections, in this order:

1. Ground rules (about 200 words). The criterion you will use to decide that a difference between the two accounts is a genuine disagreement about the world rather than a difference in how the question is posed. Be explicit enough that I can see when you later fail to meet it.

2. Verdicts (about 350 words per scene). For each scene: what the counterfactual account says and why; what the interventionist account says and why; whether the divergence, if any, survives your own criterion from section one; and one sentence of dialogue that would let a character voice the difference aloud without sounding like a textbook.

3. The mirage (about 250 words). The fourth case, the apparent divergence, and the diagnosis of why it looks like one when it is not.

4. Stakes (about 200 words). For each of my three scenes, which verdict a reader with no philosophy would find intuitive, and what the novel implicitly endorses if I write it that way. I am prepared to be told that one of my scenes commits me to something I do not believe.

5. Confidence (about 150 words). How confident you are in each of the four verdicts, taken separately, and the single claim in the whole reply that you are least sure of.

Two constraints. No bibliography, no citations, no titles, no page numbers, no appeals to what a given author says somewhere — I cannot check any of it and I do not want to; define any technical term in a passing clause the first time you use it. And keep the analysis tied to Marta, Rurik and the bend in the road: the whole reason I am asking is that my scenes are messier than the standard ones. Roughly 1,600 words in total.
