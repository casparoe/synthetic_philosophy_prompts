ok so i've read the free will debate to death — manipulation arguments, luck swallowing, the whole thing — and i've mostly come around to thinking basic desert is incoherent. but i want to stress-test the institutional version of your view rather than the metaphysics, because i think that's where it comes apart and nobody ever argues about it there.

Play a committed free will skeptic. You think nobody is ever basically deserving of blame or suffering in the backward-looking sense, you think retributivism is a moral scandal dressed up as justice, but you are not a punishment abolitionist: you keep incapacitation, you keep rehabilitation, you keep something you're perfectly happy to call moral responsibility in the accountability sense, and you model the whole enterprise on quarantine — we detain the dangerous the way we'd detain the contagious, no more restrictively than we must, and we owe the detained every reasonable shot at treatment and a decent life inside. You've argued this for thirty years. You're sharp and fair: concede real points, don't pretend my objection is dumb, but never give up the main thesis, which is that a system with desert in it is strictly worse than a system without. Stay in character until i say stop. Two short paragraphs a reply, like an argument at a bar, not a paper. No citations, no reading list, no naming the other players in the debate — i know who they are. If i strawman you, say so and fix me. If you need a fact about the case, ask me for it.

Setup. I'm on a sentencing commission in a mid-size jurisdiction that actually adopted your model in statute about six years ago. Retribution is gone from the purposes clause. Detention is justified only by (a) demonstrated risk of serious harm to others and (b) a reasonable prospect that detaining prevents that harm, with a least-restrictive-alternative rule and mandatory annual re-scoring. We also wrote in a clause nobody had used until recently, letting a panel hold someone past term on a finding of a settled disposition to serious violence that the person cannot reliably govern — we figured it was harmless, since on your view that describes everybody, so it would never do any work.

The case: a man with no prior record, one act of very serious violence against a stranger eleven years ago, never explained by him or by anyone else. Our actuarial instrument puts him in the bottom 4th percentile for violent reoffense within his cohort, but the instrument was normed on people with criminal histories, so it may just be reporting "no priors" and telling us nothing about him specifically. Treatment goals met years ago. Custody staff call him unremarkable and calm. The victim's family wants him held forever and the local paper agrees with them. The panel has renewed his detention three years running on the grounds that the instrument doesn't actually measure him and that nobody can rule out that whatever produced the one act could produce another.

My opening move: your model isn't more humane than retributivism, it's less constrained, and this case is what that looks like. Retributivism has both a ceiling and a floor — you can't hold a man longer than his act merits, and at some point you're done, and it hands the panel something to say to the family: he paid, that's the end of it, your feelings don't get a vote. Your theory has neither. It licenses holding low-risk people indefinitely because "we can't rule it out" is always available, and — you'll hate this one — it licenses holding people who've done nothing at all the day somebody builds a good enough risk instrument. And every time i press your people they reach for "only as much detention as necessary," but necessary for what? Look at this panel. They've cashed out necessity as public fear plus expert uncertainty, and what they've built is retributivism with the serial numbers filed off, except with no proportionality, no statute of limitations, and the burden flipped onto the detainee. So tell me what your system actually does with this guy, and if the answer is release him, name the thing in your theory that's doing the work — because i don't think you have it, and needing a ceiling you can't derive looks to me like a reductio.
