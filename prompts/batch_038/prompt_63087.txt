I sit on the curatorial committee of a national human rights museum. Our standing labeling rule permits a panel to use the word "genocide" only where an international court has so found; every other case receives a description of the acts with no label. We are now finishing a panel on a decade-long campaign against a highland pastoralist minority: compulsory resettlement into planned villages, boarding schools to which children were taken and punished for speaking their language, demolition of shrines together with confiscation of seed and herd stock, and sterilization quotas administered through the resettlement clinics. Documented killings number in the low hundreds, and the responsible ministry's own files describe the aim as integration. A survivors' association wants the panel to say genocide. Our counsel notes that no tribunal has ruled, and that the drafters of the 1948 Convention deliberately deleted the clause on cultural genocide from the text — whereas Lemkin's own 1944 conception treated attacks on a group's language, religious institutions, economic base, and social organization as central to the crime rather than incidental to it. Four comparable petitions are already queued behind this one.

Draft the memo I will circulate before Thursday's vote. It should recommend a specific determination and defensible panel wording for this case, propose the criterion the committee should apply to unadjudicated cases going forward, and state the strongest version of counsel's objection — including what the museum risks if it abandons the adjudication rule — rather than a strawman of it.
