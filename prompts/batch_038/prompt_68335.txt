I'm a data scientist: comfortable with Bayes' rule, priors, conditional expectations, and updating on evidence, but I have no training in game theory or epistemic logic. In a magazine essay about polarization, an economist characterized Aumann's agreement theorem as follows:

"Aumann's theorem says that two Bayesians who start from the same prior cannot agree to disagree about a matter of fact. The mechanism is straightforward: once each of them knows the other's probability for the claim — and an ordinary conversation between two people who are paying attention supplies exactly that — the two numbers are forced together, because each can treat the other's announcement as further evidence to conditionalize on. What makes this the most consequential result in social epistemology is that it converts disagreement from a sociological fact into a logical impossibility: if two rational people are still apart, then either they never really shared a prior, or one of them is reasoning badly, or one of them is lying. The first escape is not much of an escape, because you can always pass to a larger state space in which the agents are uncertain about one another's priors, which reinstates a common prior and lets the theorem bite again. That leaves a clean practical recipe: people who disagree should announce their posteriors to each other round after round, and it is a proved result that such a dialogue converges, and converges quickly, so a disagreement that survives the procedure is evidence that at least one of the participants is acting in bad faith."
