I write about cognitive science and its discontents for a general-interest magazine, and in three weeks I am giving the opening outsider's lecture at a workshop on predictive processing and computational psychiatry: twenty-five minutes, followed by an hour of discussion I have every reason to dread. The room will be around forty people — mostly computational neuroscientists and psychiatric modelers who fit hierarchical predictive-coding models to EEG and fMRI data, for whom the mismatch negativity is bread and butter — plus two or three philosophers of mind broadly sympathetic to the framework, and at least one machine-learning researcher who does variational inference for a living. Nearly all of them regard the framework as an empirical research programme rather than a metaphysics, and several have already read the magazine essay this talk is drawn from, which means they will be listening for where the essay's framing overreaches.

My thesis is deliberately unfashionable in that room. In outline: first, the explanatory appeal of predictive processing is carried largely by its vocabulary — prediction, expectation, surprise, belief, inference — borrowed from the psychology of whole persons and applied to neural populations, and at the extreme to any self-organizing system describable behind a Markov blanket. Second, once that intentional vocabulary is stripped away, what remains is either near-trivially true of any feedback system, or an engineering hypothesis about particular circuits that has been tested only in a handful of domains. Third, the framework therefore unifies by redescription rather than by mechanism: anomalies are absorbed by adding a level to the hierarchy or a new prior, rather than by risking anything that could come out false. Fourth, which is why, after the better part of two decades, the clinical payoff in psychiatry has been so thin.

What I want is not a defense of that thesis but a preparation sheet for the question period. Give me the eight or so objections this particular audience will actually raise, ordered by how much damage each would do to my case if I fumbled it — and specifically not the objections I would get from other journalists, from my editor, or from a general philosophy-of-science crowd, but the ones from people who run these models and know exactly which distinctions a magazine word limit flattens. Phrase each objection the way its proponent would phrase it, in their own idiom and with their own tone, and tag whether it comes from the empirical modelers, the sympathetic philosophers, or the machine-learning person, since I expect those three groups to attack different joints of the argument. For each, give me a reply I can deliver in roughly two minutes without losing the room, and an honest note on where that reply is soft — the places where I would be trading on their unfamiliarity with my framing rather than actually answering them.

Then tell me what I should concede in the first five minutes in order to buy the credibility to say the rest, which single reply has to land for the talk not to be written off as a journalist's hit piece, and the question I am privately hoping nobody asks because I have no good answer to it. Finally, if any of my four steps is phrased in a way a specialist would call a category error — most obviously, a conflation of predictive coding as a neuroscientific hypothesis with the broader free-energy framework as a general account of adaptive systems — flag that separately at the top. I would far rather be corrected by you now than in front of forty people who have been waiting a long time to say it.
