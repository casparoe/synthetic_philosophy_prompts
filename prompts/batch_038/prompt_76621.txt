**The question as set:**

"In not more than 1,000 words: (a) Explain the parity principle and the Otto–Inga case, and say what kind of thesis Clark and Chalmers take themselves to be advancing. (b) Set out two objections to the extended mind thesis. Which do you take to be the most serious, and why?"

**The student's answer:**

"Clark and Chalmers open their 1998 paper in *Analysis* by asking where the mind stops and the rest of the world begins. Their answer is governed by what has come to be called the parity principle. The principle invites us to consider some process that goes on in the world, and to ask whether we would hesitate to call it cognitive if it were happening inside the head. If we would not hesitate, then, on their view, we should not hesitate in the actual case either: the process is cognitive, and part of the mind, wherever it happens. The skin and skull, they argue, cannot be treated as a boundary with independent authority, because whether that boundary is the right place to draw the line around cognition is precisely the question under dispute.

Their illustration is the case of Otto and Inga. Inga hears of an exhibition at the Museum of Modern Art, recalls from biological memory that it is on 53rd Street, and walks there. Otto has Alzheimer's disease and carries a notebook in which he has written everything he needs to get through the day; hearing of the same exhibition, he consults the notebook, reads that the museum is on 53rd Street, and walks there. Clark and Chalmers insist there is no principled difference. Otto trusts the notebook, relies on it constantly, and endorses its contents automatically when he reads them; these are the trust-and-glue conditions the notebook must meet to count as part of his cognitive system. Elsewhere in the paper they make the same point with Tetris players who physically rotate falling pieces rather than rotating them mentally, calling the former an epistemic action: the rotation is doing cognitive work even though some of it is being done by a hand and a button rather than by neurons.

It is important to see what kind of thesis this is. Clark and Chalmers describe themselves as active externalists, and active externalism is best understood as semantic externalism carried one step further. Putnam's and Burge's arguments show that what a belief is about is fixed partly by the believer's environment rather than by what is in the head. Once that is granted, it is a short step to conceding that the belief's physical realiser may also be outside the head; the Twin Earth cases already show a mind reaching outward into its surroundings. So the extended mind thesis is not a new doctrine but the completion of an old one.

The completion is licensed by functionalism. Clark and Chalmers are functionalists, and functionalism entails multiple realisability: the same belief could be realised in silicon as readily as in neurons. From this it follows that Otto's notebook can realise his belief that the museum is on 53rd Street. The belief is individuated by its causal role, and the notebook occupies that role.

There is an asymmetry worth noting, however. Inga's belief is dispositional: she is not consciously thinking about 53rd Street until she needs to. Otto's belief, by contrast, becomes occurrent at the moment he opens the notebook and reads the entry. This asymmetry explains why the trust-and-glue conditions are needed. Their job is to convert Otto's repeated occurrent encounters with the notebook into something that deserves to be called a standing belief, and to guarantee that the notebook is available whenever the occurrent state is needed.

Two objections deserve attention. The first, from Adams and Aizawa, is empirical in character. External resources are slower to access, less reliable, and more easily lost or damaged than neural ones; Otto's notebook can be stolen, soaked, or left at home, whereas Inga's memory cannot. Adams and Aizawa's charge of a coupling-constitution fallacy presses this same point in more abstract dress: because the notebook's contribution is intermittent and contingent, it is merely coupled to Otto, and mere coupling to a cognitive system does not make a thing part of that system. They add that cognition proper has a mark — genuine, non-derived content — which notebook entries, being derived from Otto's own mental acts, plainly lack.

The second objection comes from Rupert, and it is sometimes called the cognitive bloat worry. If we let in the notebook, there is no principled place to stop: the person in a library, the driver with a satellite navigation system, and the office worker with a well-organised filing system all have cognitive systems of extraordinary size. Rupert argues that these extended wholes lack the integration and the persisting organisation that characterise actual cognitive systems, and so we should not treat them as cognitive systems at all.

I think the most serious objection is neither of these. It is Searle's Chinese Room. Searle showed that a system which manipulates symbols according to rules without understanding them does not thereby possess genuine cognition. A notebook manipulates and stores symbols without understanding anything whatever. It therefore cannot be a cognitive organ, and the coupled Otto-plus-notebook system inherits the deficiency of its component, just as a chain is only as strong as its weakest link. Adams and Aizawa's empirical complaint can be met by a sufficiently robust notebook, and Rupert's bloat worry can be met by tightening the trust-and-glue conditions, but Searle's argument attacks the very idea that an understanding-free resource could contribute to a mind. If it succeeds, the parity principle has no purchase, because there is nothing inside the head to be on a parity with."
