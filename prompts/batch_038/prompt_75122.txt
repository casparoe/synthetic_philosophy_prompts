So — weird favor. I'm a fourth-year in a philosophy PhD program (aesthetics, mostly), and the small art college I went to for my BFA has asked me to give the address at their graduation in three weeks. 140-ish students: illustration, ceramics, game art, photography, maybe a dozen who genuinely intend to be painters. Their parents and grandparents will be the majority of the room by headcount. It's in a converted mill with terrible acoustics, and I've got seven minutes, not the twenty they originally mentioned.

Here's the situation I have to speak into, which is why I'm asking for help and not just writing it myself. Last spring the school cut the required second-year survey sequence and dropped all-school crit from weekly to monthly. Cost reasons, officially. But the framing in the catalogue and in the chair's announcement was that no one's taste outranks anyone else's, that aesthetic standards are local, that the old requirements were smuggling in a hierarchy. Then in October a visiting artist gave a lecture and said, flatly, that some of the work in the student show wasn't good and no amount of context was going to make it good, and the room basically split in half. Half the students were furious, and honestly half of them were right about something, and the faculty went into full de-escalation mode.

I want to give the speech Hume would have given if he were less of a jerk and better at public speaking. You know the apparatus: the standard isn't a rule or a list of great works, it's the joint verdict of judges who've actually put in the practice, made the comparisons, kept their senses delicate, brought good sense to bear, and gotten clear of prejudice. That's the structure I want under the whole thing — practice and comparison especially, because those are precisely the two things the school just took away from these students, and the speech has to be honest that this room is now less equipped than the class of five years ago was, without turning into a grievance speech or handing the administration a soundbite.

Ground rules, please actually follow them:

- Name Hume at most once, in passing, and don't explain him. Nobody in that room needs to know there's philosophy happening.
- No quotations of any kind, from Hume or anyone else. Paraphrase everything in ordinary language. If it needs a citation to be believed, cut it.
- Don't use the wine-tasters story from the essay. Everyone uses that story.
- No "beauty is in the eye of the beholder," no canon lists, no dead-white-men framing, no talk about the real world.
- One faculty member in the front row thinks the whole qualified-judge idea is just power wearing a tuxedo. The speech should let that objection be felt rather than argued away, but it should not win.
- Handle the circularity thing — we pick the good judges by the work they praise and praise the work because they picked it — in two sentences a grandmother can follow, because that's what keeps the whole view from being authoritarian, and it's the most useful thing I can give these students.

Sentences have to survive the acoustics: short, no three-deep clauses, nothing where you need to hear item four to make sense of item one. About 850 words. And it should end with something they can do next Tuesday that isn't "keep making art" — I'm thinking something about deliberately building a small circle of people whose judgment you trust enough that it hurts when they're unimpressed, and earning a place in someone else's. Make that land as a gift, not a homework assignment.
