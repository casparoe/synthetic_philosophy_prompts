I am drafting the framing section of a dissertation chapter on structural realism, and before circulating it I would like an audit of my own stated commitments — not an introduction to the debate, and not a survey of who holds what. Here is the package as I would put it to an examiner.

1. My starting point is defensive. The pessimistic meta-induction shows that the ontological posits of once-successful theories are typically discarded, the luminiferous ether being the paradigm, while a good deal of the mathematical structure survives: the wave equations of Fresnel's optics reappear inside Maxwell's theory. Structural realism is the position that takes this pattern at face value and claims for science only what has in fact been retained.

2. I take the structural content of a theory to be given by Ramsification: replace every theoretical term with a variable, bind those variables with existential second-order quantifiers, and keep the resulting sentence. That sentence, and nothing beyond it, is what a theory is entitled to assert.

3. I accept that Newman-style worries have force against unrestricted Ramsification — a sentence of that form seems true of any domain of suitable cardinality, and so says nothing — but I think the fix is to restrict the second-order quantifiers to causal relations. I do not want the rival fix of restricting them to metaphysically natural or eligible properties, which imports a prior and contentious metaphysics of properties.

4. I hold that which portion of a theory is structural is settled retrospectively. The structural content of T is the Ramsified content of that part of T which T's successful successor turns out to preserve. Since the Ramsey sentences of successive theories are generally not related by entailment, the only non-question-begging criterion of what was preserved is what the later theory actually keeps.

5. I am a realist about structure, not an empiricist in disguise. The structures our best theories describe are mind-independent, and we know them; the Ramsey sentence of a well-confirmed theory is believed, not merely treated as empirically adequate.

6. I am an agnostic about intrinsic natures. We cannot know, and ought not claim to know, what the relata of these structural relations are like in themselves. Claims of that kind are precisely what the history in (1) shows to be transient.

7. The positive argument for realism is the no-miracles argument: the predictive and technological success of our theories would be a miracle unless those theories were approximately true. The pessimistic meta-induction does defensive work only.

What I want is a consistency audit of this set. Tell me which of these commitments genuinely pull against one another and which only appear to; assess the objections on their merits rather than assuming the package is coherent, and equally do not flag every pair as equally troubled. In particular, one charge I have already received is that the whole thing collapses into constructive empiricism — I want that assessed alongside the rest, not set aside because I happen to reject it. Rank the real tensions by severity. For each, say precisely what would have to be surrendered or weakened, and what the position loses in exchange, including whether the revision costs me the distinctiveness of structural realism as a middle way between full-blooded scientific realism and empiricism. Then identify the single change that would resolve the most, and do not assume I am attached to any of the seven: I am as willing to be told that (2) or (4) must go as any other. Finally, distinguish carefully between places where my position is actually inconsistent and places where you think I have merely left something unspecified; in the latter case, say what needs specifying and what the plausible options are. I am not after references or a bibliography — I want the reasoning.
