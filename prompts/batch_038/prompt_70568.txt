I'm setting the final for an upper-level ethics seminar (mostly seniors, two grads) and I want one essay question on the absent-minded driver problem that they can't get through by summarising the case they've all read. 45 minutes, 20 marks.

Here's the wrinkle I want built in. Rosa is a night-shift paramedic. Her service requires anyone driving back to base between 3 and 5 a.m. to pull into a checkpoint and rest for twenty minutes, because that's the fatigue window. Rosa has skipped it on and off for about a year. On the night in question she skips it again and, driving home fatigued, doesn't see a cyclist and kills him. Afterwards she's honest about it: she didn't decide to skip that night, she simply never thought about the checkpoint at all. Then a control case — Ana, same habit, same shift, no cyclist.

I also want the question to hand them a candidate principle to attack, roughly: an agent is blameworthy for an act done in ignorance only if that act executes a plan or policy she adopted at an earlier time when she wasn't ignorant, and the adopting was itself blameworthy. The idea is that this seems to fit the familiar two-martinis driver but does badly on Rosa, since there's no discrete act of adoption — just a slow drift into a habit. They should say what a repaired version has to do: cover the one-off case, cover habit formation, and not blame her twice for one thing.

Two more things I want forced into the answer. First, whether anything Rosa does at the time of the killing is itself blameworthy, given that her ocurent mental states look perfectly clean, and what a quality-of-will account owes us if it wants to locate the relevant will in a standing disposition rather than an occurrent state. Second, whether the intuition that Rosa is worse than Ana pushes you into resultant moral luck or can be captured by a risk-based view of blame.

Deliverables: the question exactly as it would appear on the paper, a model answer of around 800 words, and a marking scheme out of 20 listing the moves a first-class answer has to make with marks attached, plus a line each on the mistakes that should cost marks — e.g. treating the whole thing as plain moral luck, or blaming both the earlier habit and the killing without noticing that's double counting. Say in the question itself that they don't need to cite anyone: I want argument, not name-dropping, so please no reading list or bibliography in the model answer either.
