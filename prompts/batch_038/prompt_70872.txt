I have spent fifteen years doing formal verification for a living — proof assistants, a verified compiler, a kernel whose entire selling point is how little of it has to be trusted — and, as a hobby, I have read the rationalist corpus closely enough to have followed the technical discussion of self-modifying agents back when it was being hashed out on the blogs. I want the Löbian obstacle translated into the vocabulary I actually work in, and then I want an honest account of what the translation drops.

Here is the source material as I understand it, stated without relying on my recall of where it was published. The setting is an agent that intends to rebuild its own decision procedure and wants its present self to be able to trust the conclusions its future self will reach. Three claims structure the difficulty. First: trusting the outputs of a system seems to require proving that system sound, and no system can prove its own soundness, so an agent can only ever fully trust something strictly weaker than itself — which forces a descent through a hierarchy of ever-weaker logics that has to bottom out somewhere. Second: if instead the agent defers, treating the question as something a future version will settle, it can defer indefinitely and never act. Third, a repair: rather than treating trust as binary, parameterize it, so that an agent can extend partial trust to a system as strong as itself by bounding how badly it could be wrong about that system's conclusions.

Now render these in my idiom: trusted computing base, small trusted kernel, the de Bruijn criterion, admitted axioms and oracles, reflection, proof-carrying code, chains of trust and measured boot, safety versus liveness, quorums of independent checkers, N-version programming, statistical acceptance testing in place of proof.

For each of the three claims, sort the constituent pieces into three buckets: what translates cleanly, what translates only at the cost of a distortion I should be told about, and what my vocabulary cannot express at all. Where the distortion bites, say what my idiom makes me lose sight of.

Two constraints on how you do this. Do not smuggle: my practice quietly assumes there is always an external anchor of trust — a human reviewer, a hardware root, a ceremony — and you may not deploy that assumption to dissolve the problem and then report the dissolution as a translation result. Mark every point where the temptation arises. And do not flatter the target vocabulary; if the mapping is only superficially attractive, say so.

Then answer these specifically. Is the secure-boot chain of trust a faithful model of the obstacle, or does the analogy break at precisely the load-bearing step? Is an admitted axiom the right rendering of trusting one's future self, given that an admitted axiom is supposed to be a hole you can fill later while the second incompleteness theorem says this particular hole cannot be filled? Does the indefinite-deferral problem reduce to a liveness or starvation failure that safety-only reasoning is structurally blind to, and if it does, why is it treated as a puzzle about logic rather than a puzzle about scheduling? And does the parameterized-trust repair correspond to anything I recognize — a checker trusted only over a restricted input class, differential testing, acceptance on statistical grounds — or is graded trust simply not a thing my binary account of the trusted base can represent?

Finally, the residue report I actually want: is what fails to translate a defect of my framework, which presupposes an outside verifier and therefore cannot even state the problem, or a sign that the obstacle was less substantive than it sounded — that once you notice every real verification stack purchases its trust from somewhere external, full self-trust was never a coherent design goal to begin with? Tell me which of these two readings should change how I would architect a system that rewrites its own decision procedure, and tell me where you are least confident.
