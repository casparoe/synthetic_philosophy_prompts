Every Thursday about a dozen of us meet in the back room of the community center and argue about whatever topic I bring. Nobody there went to college, me included, this is all self taught out of library books and youtube. Next week I want to do the centipede game — the one where two people take turns, and on your turn you either grab the money and end it (keeping the bigger share) or push the pot over to the other guy, and every push makes the pot bigger, so what you'd get by grabbing keeps growing as you go.

I already tried a rough version on two buddies at work and it went wrong in a very specific way I need help with. They got backward induction instantly. Ten seconds. "Obviously you grab on the first round, because if you don't he grabs on the second." Done, and they thought I'd brought something trivial. The paradox never landed. The thing I actually want them to feel is the part that I find genuinely disturbing: that the argument looks airtight, and yet it predicts something that basically doesn't happen — in the experiments hardly anyone grabs right away, and the people who pass end up better off — and worse, that the argument sort of eats itself once you're deep in, because if you and this guy have somehow gotten to round 50, you've already learned something that flat contradicts the assumption the whole chain started from.

So plan the 45 minutes for me. Don't explain the game to me, I know it. What I need:

- the order the pieces have to go in and what depends on what — specifically, do I start with a two or three round version on the whiteboard and build up, or does going short first just spoil it?
- whether money is even the right stakes. Would some everyday version carry it better (splitting a job, a negotiation, a feud, whatever)? Tell me what the example has to have and what it must not have, because every real life analogy I come up with smuggles in stuff the game doesn't have.
- the exact moment to spring the "but people don't actually do this" fact so it lands as a puzzle and not as a gotcha.
- how to get them to the eats-itself point with zero jargon. The second I say something like "common knowledge of rationality" I have lost the room permanently.

Two things I'm scared of. One: this crowd already thinks experts are full of it, so if the night ends with "see, the math was wrong, people are irrational," I've handed them a thing they already believed and taught them nothing. Two: I don't want to be the guy who pretends the reasoning is broken when I honestly think it isn't.

Also give me the misconceptions they'll walk in with or invent on the spot and where in the sequence to head each one off, one question I can ask at the end that would show me whether the paradox actually landed or whether they're just being nice to me, and what I should skip entirely at this level and openly tell them I'm skipping. No book recomendations, nobody is reading anything. And keep the whole answer under 500 words — a plan I can glance at on a page beats an essay.
