I am in the last weeks of a seminar on the ethics of public office, and my final paper is supposed to defend an account of corruption against the objection that it simply universalizes a distinctively modern, distinctively suspicious attitude toward gift exchange. Before I commit to an account, I would like to know what my own case-by-case verdicts add up to. Here they are, in roughly descending order of confidence.

1. A district attorney accepts a bottle of whisky worth about two hundred dollars from a bar owner he has twice prosecuted and twice failed to convict. Wrong, straightforwardly.
2. The same district attorney accepts a bottle of the same value from his former law partner, who now defends clients before him most weeks. Also wrong, but I want to say wrongly for a different reason, and I cannot articulate what that reason is.
3. A councilor in a town where refusing a gift is read as an insult accepts a handwoven blanket from a constituent whose building permit she will vote on next month, and enters it in the public register of gifts. Not corrupt. I am most attached to this verdict, because it is the case on which the charge that anti-corruption norms are just Western moralizing about other people's sociality usually rests — and yet the blanket and the whisky are worth the same amount.
4. A national politician promises the son of a union official a job at a state-owned plant in exchange for the union's endorsement. The son turns out to be the best-qualified applicant and would have been hired anyway. Wrong — and this verdict surprises me, since nothing worse happened than would have happened anyway.
5. A regulator leaves her agency eighteen months after finalizing the rule that now governs her former industry, and takes a position at a regulated firm for five times her public salary. No promise was made, nothing was said, nothing was illegal. Wrong; though if I am honest I do not know whether what is wrong here is her conduct or the structure that made that conduct rational.
6. A village headman distributes the government's fertilizer allocation to his own relatives' households rather than following the official needs formula, and his relatives are in fact the poorest households in the village. Not wrong, or at worst a venial failing. I am somewhat embarrassed by this verdict.
7. A philanthropist funds a hospital wing on the condition that a particular physician be appointed to it. The physician is excellent. Wrong.
8. A judge receives an annual case of wine from the parent of a former student who now clerks for her; the parent is a practicing lawyer, and the judge recuses herself from every matter in which that lawyer appears. Genuinely uncertain — I have gone back and forth on this one three times.

The pairing I cannot reconcile is 4 against 6. In 4 the official gives away a private advantage and receives a political one, and the outcome is not distorted; I say wrong. In 6 the official takes a private advantage and gives away a public one, and the outcome tracks need better than the formula did; I say not wrong. A principle built on the abuse of a public role for private advantage — which is roughly how the anti-corruption agencies define the thing — handles 6 and mishandles 4, and I suspect it also mishandles 3. A principle built on whether the exchange distorts the outcome mishandles 4 in the other direction. A principle built on whether the parties could justify the terms of their exchange to those who bear its costs seems to do better on 3 and 4, but I expect it goes silent or turns out too permissive on 5, where there was no agreement to justify in the first place.

So: is there a principle, or a short ordered set of them, that delivers all or nearly all of these verdicts? I would like each candidate stated precisely enough that its application to an unfamiliar case is determinate rather than a matter of taste, and I would like the case from my list that motivates each clause identified. Then tell me which of my verdicts the principle cannot accommodate, and be blunt about whether the honest move is to drop the verdict or to buy it with an additional clause: if saving all eight requires a clause per case, I would rather be told that 3 or 6 has to go. Two cases it should be made to predict, neither of which appears above: a minister who four years after leaving office, with no prior arrangement and no expectation on either side, is offered a well-paid board seat by a firm he had consistently ruled against while in office; and the same minister, who had consistently ruled in its favor. Which of those two is corrupt, and does the principle's answer depend on anything other than the direction of the earlier rulings?

Finally, and this is what the paper turns on. If the principle that survives distinguishes 3 from 1, does it do so by making the extension of corruption depend on local convention? And if it does, can it still support the claim that the norms international institutions press on other states are universal rather than merely ours? I would like the answer to commit to one of two things: a universal concept with a culturally variable extension, in which case I want the criterion that fixes the extension; or no universal core at all, in which case I want to know what is left of the objection my paper is meant to answer.

I will supply the references myself, so please do not assemble a bibliography or survey the literature by name — I want the formulations and the argument for them.
