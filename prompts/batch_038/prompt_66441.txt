I have just taken a seat on a hospital's assisted-reproduction ethics committee. Our remit is narrow: we rule on referrals from clinicians when a proposed use of preimplantation genetic testing is contested, and by our own charter we are to decide new referrals by reference to our prior rulings rather than from first principles. I am voting on a referral next week and want to be clear about what our record actually commits us to.

Our five rulings, in order:

1. A couple asked whether the clinic might rank their eight viable embryos by expected health profile; they said they had no preference of their own. We permitted the ranking and permitted implantation of the top-ranked embryo, adding in the written opinion that implanting any of the others would not have wronged the child who would have resulted, and that the ranking created no obligation to use it.

2. A couple, both congenitally deaf, asked us to select for the variant they carry so that their child would share their deafness. Refused, 6–1.

3. A couple, both of whom have achondroplasia, asked us to select for the variant they carry. Refused, 4–3. The majority noted that every projection placed the resulting child's life comfortably above any threshold this committee has ever applied; the dissent held that we have no business overriding parents who are confident they can raise such a child well and who do not regard the trait as a misfortune.

4. A couple carrying a variant for a childhood-onset degenerative condition asked us to select against it. The only unaffected embryos were ones that would otherwise have been discarded. We permitted the selection and wrote that the couple was not merely permitted but required to choose an unaffected embryo wherever selection was available — while also writing that the discarded embryos were not wronged, since no one who would otherwise have existed was made worse off.

5. An advisory opinion, not a referral: a 41-year-old patient asked whether we would consider it wrongful for her to postpone conception by three years, given that the child who would then exist would be a numerically different person carrying a somewhat elevated risk of a manageable but significant condition. We wrote that the postponement would wrong no identifiable person, that we would therefore not advise against it on that ground, and that we would instead counsel her about the risk she would be accepting.

The referral before me. A couple has three frozen embryos created six years ago. Testing that did not exist then can now be run, but only by thawing, biopsying, and refreezing, with a small chance of losing whichever embryo is biopsied. The clinic expects to find that one of the three carries a dominantly inherited variant for progressive bilateral hearing loss beginning in early middle age — substantial residual hearing for decades, no other expected health effects, and on every projection a life comfortably above any threshold we have applied. The couple asks us to approve implanting that embryo specifically. Their stated reasons: they were told six years ago that it was the first embryo created and they do not want to sort their children; two members of the family are deaf and they do not regard hearing loss as a misfortune; and they would welcome any of the three. They also inform us, and the clinic confirms, that if we refuse they will decline the testing altogether and have one embryo thawed and implanted at random, in which case the affected embryo may be implanted anyway and the clinic will never know which.

Note that every contested ruling above concerns selecting for or against a trait; this referral concerns declining to select against one, and the couple's refusal changes which child exists rather than how any particular child fares.

Please address the following:

- Which of our five rulings is the closest analog to this referral, and in which respects the analogy holds and in which it breaks.
- At least two candidate rules that jointly fit all five rulings but diverge on this one. State each rule so it can be checked against the record, and say what each implies about my vote.
- Your recommendation for how I should vote if consistency with our own precedents is the governing constraint, with the reasons for preferring one rule over the others, and an honest account of how much the record really settles.
- One further referral, described concretely, that would discriminate between the leading candidate rules.
- What principle the committee would be adopting, wittingly or not, by approving and by refusing respectively — including whether the couple's announcement about what they will do if refused should be treated as a reason or as a threat.

Two requests about how you present this. First, mark clearly where you are imputing a rationale the committee never articulated, as distinct from where the written opinions actually commit us. Second, separate what is settled among people who work on nonidentity and procreative ethics from what remains genuinely contested — several of the positions that could be read into rulings 1 through 5 are live disputes, and I would rather see them flagged as such than absorbed silently into an argument as though they were agreed.
