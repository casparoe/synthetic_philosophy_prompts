I'm a staff engineer at a company that operates long-lived infrastructure — control systems for remote sites — and I have no formal training in philosophy; I read on my own and I write things up the way I would write a design doc. Six weeks ago I was offered a fourteen-month winter-over as the sole systems operator at an inland research station: no flights in or out for most of that window, nine people on site, and every mechanical and software system there is mine to keep alive. Two of the three people I interviewed who had done a winter-over told me, unprompted, that they did not recognize themselves afterward, and the program's own medical screening material warns about sustained mood change. I could not decide, so on a colleague's recommendation I read L. A. Paul's *Transformative Experience*, and then I tried to reconstruct the argument I took from it as a numbered chain. That is where I got into trouble, because the chain I wrote down seems to prove far more than the book was offering, and I cannot find the leak.

Here is the reconstruction, with my own extension beginning at (6). (1) A choice is rationally made by me only if I can compare its possible outcomes by the utility I would assign them and weight those utilities by their probabilities — that is, only if an expected-utility comparison over the outcomes is defined and decisive. (2) For any outcome whose value is substantially experiential, the utility I would assign it is a function of what it would be like for me to have it, so assigning that utility requires knowing what it would be like. (3) Some experiences are epistemically transformative: a person who has not had them cannot know what they are like, and no quantity of testimony, documentation, or inference from other people's reports supplies the missing knowledge. (4) Some experiences are personally transformative: having them changes what you fundamentally care about, so that the person who evaluates the outcome afterward is not the person who chose. (5) Therefore, for an option whose principal outcomes are both epistemically and personally transformative, I cannot now assign utilities to those outcomes; and even if I could, the utilities that ought to be authoritative are not the ones I have. Such a choice cannot be made by expected utility. (6) Every option whose significant consequences lie more than a few years out has such outcomes, because change accumulated over that horizon is not different in kind from a personally transformative experience, and I can find no principled place to draw a line between drift and transformation. (7) So expected-utility deliberation has no domain beyond short-horizon, low-stakes choices: what to eat, which of two similar job offers to take, whether to buy the cheaper part. (8) If there were no rational way to make long-horizon decisions, then my practice of reasoning carefully about them, and everyone else's practice of advising, praising, and blaming people for them, would be unfounded. (9) That practice is not unfounded; it is among the most reliable things I do. (10) So at least one of (1) through (6) is false. I would rather keep (3) and (9) than (1) — the claim that some knowledge is only available from the inside is what pulled me into the book — but I am not confident that is the right trade.

A friend of mine, also an engineer, says the framing itself is wrong and that the rational move is to treat this as an investment in information about my own future preferences: choose the option that maximizes what I will learn about the post-experience preference set rather than the option that maximizes utility under the current one. I keep picturing this as a versioning problem. I have prefs_v1 loaded now; the winter-over would install prefs_v2; and there is no prefs_v0 standing above both from which to choose. His proposal looks to me like installing prefs_v1.5, a version whose only job is curiosity about v2 — which is still v1 doing the choosing, so I cannot see how it escapes (5). What I want from you is a diagnosis rather than a survey. Tell me whether the chain is invalid or valid with a false premise; name the earliest premise or inferential step you would reject; explain what made that step look obviously true to me when I wrote it; and price the alternatives — specifically what I lose if I reject (2) instead of (6), what I lose if I reject (6) and am then obliged to say where transformation stops and drift begins, and what I lose if I concede (7) and try to rebuild rational long-horizon deliberation on something other than expected utility. Also tell me plainly whether I have misread Paul at (1) or (5), and whether my friend's information-based proposal is a real third option or a relabeled version of what I already have. Please work from the premises as I have stated them: I have no access to the secondary literature, so I do not want a bibliography, a reading list, or a roll call of which philosopher holds which view. End your reply with a final line in exactly this form: Verdict: <the number of the earliest premise or step you reject>.
