I am preparing a handout for a graduate seminar on moral status that devotes one week to relational, ubuntu-inflected accounts of personhood, and I have run into an objection that I cannot answer to my own satisfaction. The problem is not that the objection is weak; it is that it seems to prove far too much, which usually means I have mis-stated it somewhere, but I cannot find where.

Here is the target view, as I will present it to the students. On the relational account, being a person is not a matter of instantiating some intrinsic property in isolation — not rationality, not sentience, not membership in a species — but of standing in the right kind of relations to other persons: sharing a way of life with them, identifying with them, and acting out of solidarity with them. This is the idea behind the widely cited Nguni maxim that a person is a person through other persons. The account has two prominent strands that I want to keep distinct for the seminar. In the communitarian strand associated with Menkiti, personhood is something one acquires over the course of a life by discharging one's obligations within a community, so it is processual, earned, and admits of degrees: a person can be more or less fully a person. In the more recent, capacity-oriented strand developed by Metz, moral status is grounded not in relations one actually enjoys but in one's capacity to be a party to communal relationships with us, which is meant to preserve graded status for beings like animals while avoiding the harsh consequences of the first strand.

A colleague pressed the following argument against me. I have written it out as carefully as I can, and every step seemed to me to be either a plain commitment of the view or an innocent inference.

1. On the relational account, personhood consists in actually participating in communal relations with other persons.
2. Because participation comes in degrees of depth and extent, personhood itself comes in degrees; one can be more or less fully a person.
3. Moral status is a function of personhood: the degree of personhood a being has fixes how much moral weight its interests carry.
4. Therefore, a being's moral status varies with the degree of its actual participation in communal relations.
5. Some human beings participate very little: the patient in a persistent vegetative state whom no one visits, the long-term hermit, the infant abandoned at birth and never bonded with, the perpetrator of atrocities repudiated by everyone who once knew him.
6. By (4) and (5), these human beings have very little moral status.
7. An account on which the comatose patient, the hermit, and the abandoned infant have very little moral status is not merely revisionary but intolerable, and so is any account that makes a victim's standing depend on how other people happen to treat him.
8. Therefore the relational account of personhood is false.

My own instincts, which I offer only to indicate where I am stuck and not to steer you: (7) is not negotiable for me, and I do not want to abandon the relational starting point in (1), since I think it captures something that property-based individualist accounts miss. I would rather surrender (2) or (3), but I cannot see that either is doing anything illicit, and I worry that a relational view that gives them up has quietly stopped being a relational view.

Please give me a diagnosis. Specifically:

- Identify the step or steps you would reject, and explain precisely why each of them looked unobjectionable when I wrote it down — what ambiguity, scope slide, or suppressed assumption made it seem to follow.
- Price the alternatives. For each candidate rejection — (2), (3), (5), (6) — say what the relational view would have to give up, whether the resulting position remains distinctively relational or collapses into a capacity account that individualists already hold, and which rejection you judge least costly overall.
- Assess whether the move to a capacity-based strand genuinely dissolves the argument or merely relocates it. If moral status tracks the capacity for communal relationship rather than its exercise, does the objection lose its force at the hermit and the abandoned infant and simply reappear for human beings who permanently lack the capacity altogether? Say what the relational theorist owes in that harder case.
- State whether the argument is a genuine reductio of the relational account or a reductio of one gloss on it, and if the latter, reconstruct the strongest version of the objection that survives your repairs. I want to know whether the relational theorist can end the seminar week with a defensible position or only with a less badly wounded one.
- Address separately the thought I put at the end of (7): even if the degrees problem is fixed, does grounding status in relations of any kind make a being's standing hostage to the conduct of others, so that wronging someone can reduce what is owed to them? If this is a distinct problem from the one in (5) and (6), say so and say whether the same fix handles it.

Do not provide a bibliography, a reading list, or citations; work from the material as I have set it out. Please write your answer in American English.
