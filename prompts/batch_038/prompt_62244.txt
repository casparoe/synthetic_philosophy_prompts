Two members of a reading group on Aristotle's *Politics* and *Nicomachean Ethics* argued for the better part of an evening about how his critique of money-making actually works. I was asked to referee and could not. Here is the exchange as I took it down, move by move:

1. **Marcus**: Aristotle's target is not any particular transaction but the absence of a stopping point. Managing a household aims at a definite stock of goods sufficient for living well; making money aims at more money, which has no natural terminus. That is why the same deal can be legitimate in one context and vicious in another.
2. **Elena**: The limit point is right, but Marcus has the mechanism wrong. On Aristotle's account money is a conventional stand-in for need: it exists so that unlike things — a house, a pair of shoes — can be set against each other, and need is what holds a community together. Charging interest is objectionable because it turns money into a source of more money, cutting it off from the need it exists to serve. The line about money not breeding is decoration on that argument, not part of it.
3. **Marcus**: It is not decoration. Exchange has to equalize the products of different crafts, and Aristotle thinks that happens through a proportion among the producers and their products — the builder to the shoemaker as their works stand to one another. Money merely records a comparison that the underlying proportion has already settled. That proportion is what he means by nature here, and interest is unnatural because it makes the measure itself the thing measured.
4. **Elena**: If nature is doing that work, Aristotle owes an explanation of why one and the same object is natural in one use and unnatural in another. The same coins that buy what a household lacks are perfectly acceptable; lent at interest they are not. Unless the difference lies in the end the user is pursuing — which is my point about need — he has no non-arbitrary line to draw.
5. **Marcus**: Fine — the difference is in the end, not in the object. The same coins figure in natural acquisition when the aim is supplying the household and in unnatural acquisition when the aim is accumulation as such. Interest is the limiting case because there the thing being pursued is the measure itself.
6. **Elena**: Then Aristotle cannot consistently hold, as he also does, that money is purely conventional — a token worth whatever people agree it is worth, and liable to be devalued or demonetized — while claiming that its nature fixes which uses of it are legitimate. Convention and nature are pulling against each other inside a single argument.
