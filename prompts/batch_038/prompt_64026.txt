I'm in a Bayesian epistemology seminar and our unit this week is on the common prior assumption and Aumann's result that agents who share a prior cannot, once their posteriors are common knowledge, disagree. I want to write a short story (roughly 1,800–2,500 words) that turns the theorem into institutional dogma, and I'd like you to draft it.

Here is the concey I have in mind. In an unnamed theocratic polity, the Order of the Concordance holds that every soul is created with the same Measure — an identical prior over the world — and its whole legal system rests on the consequence: two licensed readers who have disclosed all their evidence and whose assessments are common knowledge between them must converge. Persistent divergence is therefore not an intellectual matter but a juridical one, and there is a tribunal whose only task is to diagnose which of the Order's premises has failed. The story should follow one such case: two readers, of high standing and genuine good faith, who cannot agree about some proposition of real consequence (a harvest failure, a border incursion, whether a child is who the records say she is), and who have completed full disclosure without converging.

The plot should be the diagnosis, and the reader should feel that each available verdict carries a different cost. At minimum I want the tribunal to weigh: that the two never in fact shared a Measure, because one was formed in circumstances the Order refuses to countenance; that common knowledge breaks down somewhere in the iterated hierarchy, so the theorem's condition was never met even though everyone assumed it was; that one reader is not updating coherently, or is withholding something; and that the Order's idealization simply does not describe real readers at all. Please do not let the story resolve into the easy version where someone was lying or mad all along, and do not let the heresy reading win by default — pick a verdict, but make the losing options argue for themselves.

After the story, add a short author's note saying which premises you dramatized, where you took liberties with the theorem's actual assumptions, and which claims in the story are settled results rather than contested interpretations. Please be careful to separate what is established about the theorem's conditions and conclusion from what is genuinely disputed among people who work on it — in particular, the standing of the common prior assumption itself as a normative or descriptive idealization is not a matter of consensus, and I don't want the story or the note to present one side of that dispute as though everyone accepted it.
