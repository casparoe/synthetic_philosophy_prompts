You're an animal-welfare ethicist who moonlights as a comedy writer — you know the insect sentience and wild-animal-suffering literature properly, not just the headlines, and you also know exactly how corporate policy documents sound. I want you to think carefully and do this well.

Task: write a parody of a pest-control company's official arthropod welfare policy. Invented company, give it a bland name like "TruGuard Pest Services," a couple hundred franchise locations. The document is sincere in-universe: it's Annex C of their sustainability report, drafted by a committee that has genuinely read the philosophy. That's where the comedy lives — not that they're clueless, but that they're serious, and the seriousness has to survive contact with a spreadsheet, a technician who wants to finish his route by four, and a customer who just wants the roaches gone.

Structure: numbered clauses (say 10-12), one annex containing a "sentience tier" table, and a short FAQ for franchisees at the end. Around 800 words total.

Stuff to bake in, without ever naming any of it as an objection:

- the neuron-count-as-proxy move, and what happens when a honeybee and a German cockroach land in the same tier for completely different reasons
- a "if in doubt, treat it as sentient" precautionary principle that gets immediately qualified by an exception clause, after which the exception clause quietly does all the work
- the larval problem: the policy's precautionary threshold may cover almost none of the individuals actually killed, because most of them are immature stages, and the committee notices this and handles it
- a unit-of-counting clause — what counts as one individual when the thing you're treating is an ant nest, and whether fumigating it is one death or four hundred thousand
- the fast knockdown spray vs the slow-acting targeted bait tradeoff: one is over in minutes but drifts onto everything else in the garden, the other is species-specific and takes days
- non-target effects, i.e. the spiders and the birds who were doing the job for free
- the aggregate numbers: billions of individuals, tiny per-capita harms, and arithmetic that doesn't care which direction you run it. At least once, the utilitarian math has to produce a conclusion the marketing department cannot use. Follow the math honestly — let the company wriggle only in the ways real committees wriggle
- the gap between what the welfare science can currently support and what the policy confidently asserts, handled the way institutions handle that gap

One FAQ item should be a franchisee asking whether he can bill a welfare surcharge. Let the answer be truthful about what customers are actually paying for.

Register: deadpan, flat, HR-adjacent, lots of "shall" and "where practicable." No winking, no exclamation marks, nobody in the document breaking character to say the quiet part out loud. A reader should be able to tell precisely what's being ironized from the clauses alone. If you catch yourself writing "critics argue" or "some have questioned," delete the sentence.

Aim for this: someone who works on insect welfare laughs, thinks it's fair, and comes away having actually felt the question of whether aggregating trivial harms across astronomical numbers makes a practice monstrous or makes the question unanswerable.

Start with a short brainstorm first — 6 to 8 bullets listing the specific moves you want to embed and which clause each one lands in — then write the piece. Don't cite real papers, real companies or real people, and no bibliography or further-reading list at the end. Invented names, invented data, invented committee minutes.
