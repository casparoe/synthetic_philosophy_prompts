ok so I work in a national statistics unit and for about two years now we've been stuck on whether to publish our own wellbeing dashboard, and the argument has gone nowhere. It's the same argument that surrounds those country rankings where everybody answers a ladder question — 0 to 10, top of the ladder is the best possible life for you, where do you stand — and then the countries get ranked and the same handful of Nordic ones win every single year.

Two people run our pilot and they're on opposite sides. A thinks the ladder is a real measure of how well a life is going, comparable across countries once you deal with translation and scale-use habits, and that the rankings are the best single summary anyone has produced. B thinks the ladder measures a comparative self-assessment — how your life stacks up against a standard you absorbed from news and social media — so what it actually reports is achieved-status-as-judged, not anything you'd recognise as feeling good on an ordinary Tuesday. B wants affect questions and time-use stuff instead.

What makes me think this isn't a normal factual dispute: they agree on every number. And we ran a thing in the pilot that made it worse rather than better. A third of respondents got the plain ladder question, a third were shown the national income distribution first, and a third were shown a chart of where our country sits against a dozen others. Ladder means came out different across the three arms — ranking-chart arm about half a point up, distribution arm a bit down — while the affect items didn't move at all. B says that's proof the ladder is tracking comparison, not welfare. A says it proves nothing, because showing people a chart changes what question they're answering, so it's not evidence about the construct. And then when A points to the stability of ladder scores over time and their correlation with things you'd expect, B says of course, that's just what durable status-beliefs look like. Neither of them can be touched by the other's data.

But here's the bit I can't explain. I asked each of them separately, if you could only track one number for the next decade, which would it be. Both said the ladder. B said it and then looked annoyed.

So: diagnose this for me. My guess — just a hypothesis, and I might be wrong — is that the two of them are answering different questions under one word, one asking what wellbeing is and the other asking what a statistic is for, and that 'happiness' is doing double duty as a name for a mental state and a name for a normative verdict on a life. But maybe the real content is somewhere else: a hidden factual question about which measure causes which behaviour, a disagreement about whether a measure has to be ungameable to be worth publishing, or bedrock intuitions about whether welfare is a state or a judgement that neither side can argue for. Is the question ill-posed, or just hard?

Related: I keep noticing the two of them sliding between does-the-ranking-measure-happiness and should-we-try-to-top-the-ranking, and I suspect only one of those is an empirical question. Sort that out too.

Give me the diagnosis with reasons, using what A and B actually say above — no literature survey, no reading list, no names I'd have to look up. Then tell me what would count as progress: some distinction, a measurement-invariance result, a decision-theoretic argument about which target gets gamed, a change of question, anything. And if your honest answer is that no progress should be expected, say so and tell me why, because I have to decide whether to keep funding this joint pilot or split it in two. Maybe 1000 words, definately not more.
