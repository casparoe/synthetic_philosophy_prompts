Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz died in 1716, and I would like a reasoned prediction of how he would have answered the two most serious attacks on his principle of the identity of indiscernibles, both of which postdate him.

The first is a thought experiment published in *Mind* in 1952 by Max Black: a universe containing nothing but two iron spheres, exactly alike in diameter, colour, temperature, and every other intrinsic respect, standing at some distance from one another. If such a universe is possible, then two things can differ in number alone while sharing all their qualitative properties, and the principle fails. You do not need to have read Black; that description is the whole of the case you need.

The second comes from quantum mechanics. Take the two electrons in a helium atom. On the standard reading of the formalism their joint state is antisymmetrized, and there is no property belonging to one and not the other: it is not merely unknown which electron is which, but, on that reading, there is no fact of the matter. Defenders of the principle reply that such particles remain weakly discernible — distinguished not by any property either one has, but by an irreflexive, symmetric relation holding between them, such as being a certain distance apart or being non-identical with each other. Critics answer that this either smuggles in the numerical difference it was supposed to explain, or reduces the principle to a triviality that no one ever doubted.

Predict Leibniz's response to each case. I want the prediction argued from the rest of his system, not from the generic insistence that there must surely be some difference somewhere. Work through, in order:

1. Whether the principle of sufficient reason does the work for him here — if God made the two spheres, why this one here and that one there — and whether that argument can survive Black's reframing of the issue as one of metaphysical possibility rather than of divine choice, where no chooser is involved at all.
2. Whether his relationism about space and time lets him deny that the example is even coherent: if space is nothing over and above the order of coexisting things, then "each sphere occupies a different place" names no difference at all, and the counterexample may never get off the ground.
3. What his account of substance contributes. A sphere of homogeneous, inert matter is not on his view a genuine unity, since matter is infinitely divided into organic, perceiving substances. Say plainly whether he would treat Black's universe as a world of things differing *solo numero*, or as no world whatever.
4. Whether the complete-concept doctrine forecloses the question in advance — if the individual notion of each substance contains all of its predicates, two indiscernible substances would share one notion and so be one substance.
5. Whether he would accept a bare haecceity, an ungrounded thisness, as a fallback, and whether weak discernibility is a resource his relationism can use or one it positively forbids, given that he may not appeal to absolute positions to underwrite a distance relation.

Then say whether his two answers would come apart. My suspicion is that he would greet the quantum case as a vindication and dismiss the spheres as a badly described fiction, and I want to know whether that is right or merely tidy. Close by naming the single point at which your prediction is most likely to be wrong, and why it is vulnerable there.

Argue this out from the doctrines themselves; do not send me to the literature and do not supply citations. Avoid hedging formulas of the "it is difficult to know what a long-dead philosopher would think" variety — commit to a prediction and defend it.

Format: lead with your bottom line, a single sentence stating what Leibniz would say about the two cases, and only then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restatement of the question. Write in American English, in roughly 800 to 1,100 words, with short headings marking the five points above.
