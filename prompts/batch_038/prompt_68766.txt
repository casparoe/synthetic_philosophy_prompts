im putting together the last session of a philosophy of mind reading group — its the panpsychism week and we always end up stuck in the same place, which is the combination problem. i want something to read aloud to close it: a sestina, in the voice of the thing that's supposed to result. the many little experiences trying to become one subject and not obviously succeeding. i want the form to do the argument, not the imagery just illustrating it.

the six end-words are the whole palette the poem gets, and thats the point. pick six that slide between a singular and a collective sense (something in the neighborhood of note, grain, field, part, sum, one — you dont have to use mine) and then never introduce a seventh thing. the rotation should be visably the only combinatorial operation on offer, the same six rearranged into new orders, and the reader should come away feeling that no permutation of a closed set ever delivers anything that wasnt already in the set. thats the quality half of the problem and it belongs in the six sestets.

but the palette dilemma has two horns and you should either pick one or let the diction pick it for you: either the six words are too thin to describe so much as a headache, or they turn out to be rich enough to carry a whole human morning — in which case the poem was never about small simple things and the explanation bought nothing. whichever horn you land on, i want to be able to point at it in the room.

then the envoi. three lines, all six words gathered in, half of them buried mid-line the way the form does it rather than everything landing on a line ending — because the gathering is supposed to be the arrival of a point of view. be honest about whether anybody shows up. all six accounted for, all of them folded into the same three lines, and still open whether theres a subject: the gap between "everything is present" and "someone is home" is what those last three lines should turn on. dont resolve it. and if you can make one stanza betray the arbitrariness of the boundary — why these six and not six more, why the set stops where it stops — do that too, i think its the part everyone forgets.

39 lines all told. no philosophers named, no theory named, no cute little feelings: the micro-experiences should be as impersonal as the words allow, more like weather than like pets. someone whos never heard of the combination problem should get a poem about a crowd that cant become a voice, and someone who has should be able to say exactly which lines are the palette problem and which are the subject problem. if you think a sestina is genuinely too rigid for this, say so and tell me what form would work better — but im pretty sure the closed set of end-words is the whole idea.
