So me and three others run a little hardship fund in our town — proper ex-mining area, about 14,000 people, one high street with a pound shop and two bookies in it. The pot's around nine grand a year: a donation from a bloke who grew up here and did alright for himself down south, plus whatever the Christmas bring-and-buy raises. We get three or four applications a month, mostly between £150 and £700 — a washing machine, a car repair so someone can keep a shift pattern going, dental work the NHS can't get them near in time, a deposit to move out of somewhere awful. We meet fortnightly on a Tuesday and we've got maybe a quarter of an hour per case, because there's also the minutes and the bank forms to do afterwards. Two of us are ex-council, one's a retired nurse who'd give away the furniture, one's the treasurer who counts every penny, and I'm the one who does the website. Nobody's got any training in welfare or benefits or anything, and we've slowly realised we're basically deciding by gut and then writing down a reason afterwards. Two of the four have to agree, and the treasurer won't release money without a written reason, so the reason we make up is the thing that actually ends up on file.

Here's what I've been chewing on. What we seem to be trying to do is help people with bad things that happened *to* them rather than bad things they went and did themselves — and I found out that distinction has proper names in philosophy, something like brute luck and option luck, and that people have been arguing about it for decades, including that it's cruel because it lets you walk away from anyone who made a daft choice. I don't want an essay about that, and please don't give me a reading list or any references, I won't read them. What I want is a procedure we could actually run on a Tuesday night: the questions we ask in order, what counts as a good enough answer to each one, the point where the procedure just stops and the answer is no, and the points where it isn't a factual question at all and has to be handed over to us as humans making a value call. Short enough that we could laminate it, plus an even shorter emergency version, because sometimes it's a same-day phone call between meetings and two of us have to decide without the other two. And it's got to work with what we actually have, which is a form someone filled in at the library and one phone call — we can't cross-examine people, and honestly we don't want to, because half these questions would be us sitting in judgement of somebody's whole life. One more wrinkle: last year we started asking folk to put a bit back in the pot when they're on their feet again, and that's caused a row — tell me whether your procedure needs to know about that or whether we should park it.

Three of ours to run it through. First, a lad of 23 who took out a loan at a shocking rate on a car because there's no bus at all to the industrial estate where he'd got a warehouse job; the job went after six weeks, he's behind now, and the car's about to be taken — half the panel will say that was his choice and half will say he never had one. Second, a woman of 58 who gave up work eleven years ago to look after her mam, her mam died in the spring, and she's got no pension, no recent references and no transport; she's asking for help with the funeral and the rent arrears. She obviously chose that, but nobody in the room is going to call it a gamble. Third, a bloke wanting £540 for a tooth that's been rotting since March; he smokes and he drinks, and one of us will definately say he did that to himself, though our own written rules don't mention either. Run all three through it and be straight with me about which one the procedure gets wrong, or says no to when we all know we'll pay it anyway — I'd much rather hear that from you than find out in the room with the person's form on the table. And please mark every step where the procedure only looks like it's deciding something but is really just one of us deciding in a posher way, and say whether two sensible people following it word for word could still end up disagreeing, because at the moment they do.
