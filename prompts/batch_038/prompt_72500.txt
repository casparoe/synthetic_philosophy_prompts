The question I set was: "Explain Bernard Williams's integrity objection to utilitarianism. What exactly does the objection claim, and what work does the example of George do in it? Does the 'one thought too many' remark belong to the same argument, or is it doing something different?"

The entry:

"Williams's objection is basically that utilitarianism asks to much of us. It demands we hand over our own projects and commitments whenever that would produce more good overall, which is a level of sacrifice no actual person could keep up, and it flattens everything we care about into just one more number in a calculation. His clearest illustration is George, a young man with a fresh doctorate, bad health, and a wife and kids to support, who gets offered a job in a laboratory doing research on chemical and biological weapons. Williams points out that the utilitarian arithmetic favours taking the job, since the other candidate is an older man who is far keener on that line of research and would push it harder. But George can't take it: his pacifism runs so deep that accepting would wreck him, so a theory that can only tell George to take the job is not something a moral agent could live with. This is the same point as what Williams calls 'one thought too many' — in the drowning example the man saves his wife, and if he stopped to confirm that impartial morality permits favouring her, he'd have one thought too many. Likewise George would have one thought too many if he stood back and weighed the general consequences against his own convictions. In both cases the theory is telling people to think about something they should simply be acting from."
