Write a parody appellate court opinion of 800–1000 words in a civil action seeking a declaratory judgment about the concave surface of the water in a spinning bucket. Hold the register at relentless judicial deadpan throughout — caption and docket number, a Findings of Fact section, numbered paragraphs of analysis, a short concurrence, a dissent, and a disposition — and let every joke come from getting the underlying physics and metaphysics right rather than from courtroom boilerplate.

The brief:

- Findings of Fact must state only what the record supports: the relative motion between bucket and water at each stage of the experiment, and the shape of the water's surface at each stage, including the awkward fact that these come apart (greatest relative motion, perfectly flat water; zero relative motion, greatest concavity).
- The majority must try to identify the thing with respect to which the water rotates, and must fail to serve process on it: the defendant has no properties that vary from place to place, cannot be detected by any experiment conducted in uniform motion, and yet appears indispensable to explaining a measurable effect. Plead this as a problem of standing, or of adequate notice, or of whether a party that can never be impleaded can be bound by a judgment.
- Include a stipulated sub-case the court cannot decline to reach: two globes joined by a cord, revolving about their common centre of gravity, with nothing else in the universe. The cord is taut. The court divides over what, if anything, it is taut with respect to.
- The concurrence should attempt to resolve everything by appeal to a general-relativistic apparatus — the metric field, the dragging of local inertial frames by rotating mass — and should leave the dispute worse off than it found it, without noticing.
- The dissent should be Machian in substance, propose the far cheaper remedy of spinning the rest of the universe instead of the bucket, and then be met with the objection that no experiment distinguishes the two situations. The dissent should embrace that objection as its entire argument rather than retreat from it.

No winking, no asides to the reader, and no invented verbatim quotations from real historical texts. Historical figures may appear as parties or counsel, but the target of the satire is the debate itself — specifically the habit of treating a detectable effect as proof of a detectable thing, and the further habit of declaring victory whenever the opponent cannot point at anything. A reader with no physics should still be able to follow it as a lawsuit; a reader who knows the bucket literature should recognize every move and judge the piece fair.
