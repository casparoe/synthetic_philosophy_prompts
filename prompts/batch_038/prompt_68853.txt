Here's the argument my reading group built. It was supposed to show that brain size is morally relevant, in the sense that it should make us revise our picture of which animals matter. But the person who built it doesn't accept where it lands, nobody else does either, and now we can't figure out where it breaks.

(1) If some capacity C is what makes a being's interests count morally, then any reliable measure of C inherits that moral significance, and we should weight moral status by the measure.
(2) C is cognitive capacity — sentience, capacity to suffer, having plans, social bonds, selfhood; take your pick, the argument is meant to run for all of them.
(3) Absolute brain size is the best cross-species measure of C available, and the version of "size" that actually predicts behavioral flexibility, tool use, mirror self-recognition and the rest is not gross mass but neuron count.
(4) The data, so nobody has to go looking: an adult African elephant's brain weighs about 4.6 kg, roughly three times a human brain, and holds about 257 billion neurons against our 86 billion — but 97.5% of those are in the cerebellum, and its cerebral cortex holds only about 5.6 billion, roughly a third of the 16 billion or so in a human cortex, even though the elephant's cortex is about twice the mass of ours. On encephalization quotient, humans come in around 7–8, cetaceans around 3, African elephants around 1.3. Meanwhile parrots and corvids do remarkably well on a lot of the same tests with walnut-sized brains.
(5) So rank species by mass and elephants and the big whales come out above humans; rank them by cortical neurons and humans come out above elephants; rank them by EQ and humans come out above cetaceans and cetaceans above elephants. Any two of these rankings disagree about who matters more.
(6) But whichever measure we settle on, moral status comes out as a scalar that varies by species, and we're obliged to weigh individual interests in proportion to it — so a wildlife management decision, or a rescue, should count one elephant or one whale for more than one person.
(7) Nobody in the group accepts (6), and nobody at all accepts the intra-species version, since (1) applies just as readily to differences in cortical volume and neuron count between individual humans, which would have us ranking people by their MRI scans.
(8) Therefore brain size isn't morally relevant after all and (3) is false — none of these measures tracks anything of moral significance.

Two things I can't shake. First, (8) feels like it's aimed at the wrong premise; we rejected a conclusion and then blamed the science for it. Second, I'd much rather keep (4) — it's just data, I'm not going to argue with it — and I'd rather keep (2) than (6). But if (8) is wrong, then brain size is relevant somehow, and I can't say how without just running the whole argument again.
