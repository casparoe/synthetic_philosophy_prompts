So I'm a staff engineer, twelve years in distributed systems and the last few in inference infra for serving large models. No philosophy training at all — I read this stuff on my own at night, mostly because a friend from college who's now a postdoc in a systems neuro lab kept sending me papers and arguing with me over dinner. She runs a little invited-talk series at her institute, basically a journal club that occasionally lets outsiders in, 25 minutes to talk plus another 25 of questions, and I've somehow commited to giving one next month on the claim that the brain literally is a computer. Not "is like" one, not "it's useful to model it as" one — actually implements computations, in the same sense in which I'd say a fleet of pods implements an API.

Here's the version I want to defend, so you have something to aim the objections at. Step one: I want to *grant* the old triviality problem, the one where if you let the mapping from physical states to computational states be arbitrary then any old physical system implements any finite automaton, so "the brain computes" would be vacuously true and therefore say nothing. I'm not going to fight that. Step two: what rescues the claim is that the mapping isn't allowed to be arbitrary in the specific way that matters — the system's state transitions have to track the program's transitions counterfactually, under a stated set of interventions, and keep tracking them across a range of conditions. Step three, this is the part I actually care about: that makes computationalism not a metaphysical thesis but a claim about *which level of description has the most durable contract*, exactly like when we say a service implements an interface and mean that the contract holds under retries, restarts, and partial failure rather than on the happy path. So "is the brain a computer?" becomes "for which candidate descriptions does the brain's contract actually hold under perturbation?" — an empirical question with some yeses and a lot of nos. Step four, the risky bit: on that reading, the "there are no representations, cognition is just coupled dynamics" objection doesn't refute computationalism at all, it's just a negative result about one candidate contract, i.e. an answer *within* the framework rather than an attack on it.

Audience is maybe fifteen people. Mostly systems and cognitive neuroscientists — electrophysiology, calcium imaging, population-level decoding, dimensionality reduction of neural activity, people who live in trial-to-trial variability. Two philosophers: one works on mechanism explanation in the life sciences, one is the embodied/anti-representationalist type who thinks I'm reifying my models. A couple of ML grad students who are on my side and will ask me softballs I don't need help with. Almost nobody will have read the philosophy literature and nobody cares about it; what they care about is whether my claim is distinguishable from "we can fit a model to your dynamics," what experiment would settle it, and why "computation" earns its keep. So: give me a Q&A prep sheet, the eight or so objections this room actually raises — not the ones I'd get from a philosophy seminar, and not the ones from people who already agree with me. Order them by how much damage they'd do, and mark which ones are likely to come in the first five minutes versus later once people warm up. Phrase each one the way that person would actually say it out loud, in their vocabulary, so I recognize it when I hear it; then a reply I can deliver in about two minutes without sounding like I'm giving a lecture on ism's; then one line on where my reply is soft and what they'll say next if I bluff. Also tell me which two things I should concede in my opening three minutes to buy credibility, what the single reply that has to land is for the talk to count as a success, and the question I'm praying nobody asks — with a rough sketch of how I'd survive it anyway if it comes.
