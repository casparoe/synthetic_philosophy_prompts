I am a third-year PhD student in the history and philosophy of science, and I co-convene a term-long reading group on the physics of information: about fifteen of us, a mixture of philosophers, physicists, and two historians. Next week is our last session, and we are spending it on Maxwell's demon and the long series of attempts to exorcise it. By our tradition the convener opens the meeting by reading something aloud, and this year I would like that something to be a poem rather than a paper. I would like you to write it.

The form should be a sestina: six six-line stanzas and a three-line envoy, thirty-nine lines in all, with the end-words rotating in the standard pattern (if the first stanza ends, in order, A B C D E F, the second ends F A E B D C, and so on, until a hypothetical seventh stanza would restore the original order). That last feature is why the form suits the subject. The rotation is closed, and the poem should exploit the fact: the middle stanzas should feel as though something is being accumulated — a sorted gas, a lengthening ledger of bits, a weight steadily climbing — and then the form should close the cycle on the reader without warning, so that the accumulation turns out to have been precisely the illusion the second law forbids. The envoy is where the erasure happens, and it should feel like a compression: all six end-words crammed into three lines, three of them at the line-ends and three buried inside the lines.

I would like to fix the six end-words myself, because their ambiguity is part of the plan. They are: door, fast, slow, bit, work, heat. Two of these do double duty in ordinary English and I want the poem to use that. "Fast" should mean swift in most of its appearances and mean fixed or held fast in at least one; "heat" should be permitted some non-technical pressure at least once. More generally, each end-word should be serviceable on either side of the partition, so that the reader cannot always tell whether a given line is describing the gas or the being that watches it. That indeterminacy is the point — a good deal of the literature turns on whose the bit is.

On content. The first two stanzas should give us Maxwell's own picture, in which the agent is not a spirit but a finite being with a trapdoor, sorting the swift from the sluggish at apparently no cost; at least one stanza should register that the door is itself a physical object, sitting at the temperature of the gas and liable to be jostled. The middle should get the quantitative core right: one molecule in a box, a partition inserted, one bit learned, and an isothermal expansion that lifts a weight, the work obtained per bit being kT ln 2. The turn must locate the bill where Bennett located it and not where Brillouin did: the cost is paid when a finite memory has to be overwritten in order for the cycle to repeat, not in the looking, which can in principle be done without dissipation. It should be accurate enough that the physicists in the room do not wince.

Two prohibitions. The word demon must not appear anywhere in the poem, body or title — Maxwell did not use it of his agent, and it was Thomson who attached it in Nature in 1874; I would rather the room recognise the subject by the third stanza than be told its name. And no epigraphs, no footnotes, no physicists named in the lines: the argument must be carried by the door, the molecules, and the arithmetic of the form.

One decision you have to make and own. There are broadly two ways of stating the moral, and they are not the same claim: either information is a fuel that gets burned and must be repaid in heat, or it is a ledger entry that lets an agent borrow against the second law, with the debt settling when the memory resets. The envoy should commit to one. If you want to gesture at the modern stochastic-thermodynamics framing, in which information figures as a term in an inequality rather than as a substance, do it in a single line at most and do not let the tercet turn into a seminar.

Give the poem a title of your choosing, and after it add a note of no more than 250 words addressed to me rather than to the room, saying: where the poem takes liberties with the physics or buys a line by fudging it; which of the two morals you committed to and which rival resolutions of the paradox you had to suppress to keep the form clean; how confident you are in each of the technical claims the poem makes; and, singled out explicitly, the one point you are least sure of and why. I would much rather have a candid note about a weak joint than a smooth account of a poem that quietly gets the thermodynamics wrong.
