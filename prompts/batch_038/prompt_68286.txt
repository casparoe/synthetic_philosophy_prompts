I'm assembling test cases for the claim that a state's causal responsibility for a displacement can convert a permissible exclusion into an impermissible one, and I want a case engineered so that responsibility and the right to exclude genuinely conflict instead of conveniently coinciding.

Specifications: realistic and contemporary; the scenario itself a single paragraph; the people at the border must fail the persecution criterion — nobody is singling them out, they are fleeing a harm no one chose to inflict; the state asked to admit them must be materially and demonstrably responsible for that harm; and excluding them must not be catastrophic in aggregate, because a poorer neighboring state will take all of them in return for a payment the responsible state would far rather make than open its doors. Stipulate that the payment is fully adequate, so that the people end up safe and reasonably provided for, and the case cannot be smuggled through by the neighbors mistreating them.

Then give the verdicts, with reasons, from four positions: a freedom-of-association account on which a state may exclude anyone it is not leaving in desperate need; a responsibility-sensitive account on which a state that helped create the displacement owes more than its fair share of the burden; a cosmopolitan account on which borders carry no intrinsic moral weight; and the persecution criterion treated as a moral test rather than a legal one. Each should answer the same question — may this state pay instead of admit?

Finish by identifying which single feature of the case is doing the work, what the case collapses into if you delete the state's causal responsibility, and how a defender of the right to exclude would attack the design.
