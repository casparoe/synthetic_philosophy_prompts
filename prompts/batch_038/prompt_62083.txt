I am a doctoral student in philosophy of science, and next term I lead one 90-minute session of an upper-level undergraduate seminar — fourteen students, roughly half with a year of calculus, none with any coursework in dynamical systems — on determinism, prediction, and explanation. I want a forecasting game in which the students run into the gap between determinism and predictability themselves, well before anyone utters the word "chaos," and in which the gap shows up in their score sheets rather than in my exposition.

The apparatus I have sketched, and which I would like you to develop, correct, or replace: the state of the system is an infinite binary string; the law of motion is to drop the leading digit; admissible strings are those that never contain two consecutive 1s. Teams are shown only the first *k* digits of a seed and earn points forecasting later digits, under a proper scoring rule and with no money involved. The full seed is written on a card, sealed in an envelope, and handed around the room at the start, so that the entire future is visibly already fixed and physically present while remaining strictly unreadable. Rounds escalate the forecast horizon, and teams may spend points to buy further digits of the seed, so that they discover for themselves the marginal value of precision.

Please work out the following, and tell me where my plan is wrong:

1. Whether this shift is the right system. My worry is that its unpredictability comes from a representational convention about how much of a fixed string I reveal, rather than from any amplification mechanism. Compare it against the doubling map and the logistic map at full parameter value, say what each would cost me in class time and hand computation, and note that the doubling map's invariant measure is uniform, which makes distributional forecasting trivial — the reason I favor the constrained shift, where I calculate the long-run frequency of 1s at about 0.382 and the entropy at about 0.48 nats. Check those figures.
2. The scoring scheme: separate pointwise and distributional forecasts, proper, computable with pen and paper, and robust to two failure modes I anticipate — pointwise payoffs generous enough to invite hedging, and distributional payoffs easy enough that teams abandon trajectory forecasting before they have felt its collapse.
3. The round arithmetic: how many digits to reveal, which horizons to set, what to charge for extra digits, such that both effects appear — the naive expectation of one digit lost per step, and the discovery that the constraint gives back roughly 0.3 bits per step, which they should be able to test rather than merely be told. Tell me whether to forbid a longer block to widen that gap.
4. A seed-generation procedure the class can see is fair, given that flipping a coin and rejecting inadmissible strings does not yield the measure of maximal entropy.
5. One-sided versus two-sided shift. Non-invertibility hands me determinism-without-reversibility as a debrief topic, but I fear it invites the inference that irreversibility is what destroys predictability. Decide and justify.

Then the debrief: map specific moments of play onto determinism versus predictability, determinism versus reversibility, trajectory versus statistical prediction, sensitive dependence as a metrical rather than causal notion, computational irreducibility, and the shadowing point — that every admissible string a team writes down is the exact future of some state compatible with its data, so a miss is not a failure of the law. Include what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this (arithmetic slips, inadmissible strings, a team that games the rule, a fast team demoralizing the room, students concluding "so it is random," the envelope read as a conjuring trick) and how to handle each; and how to meet the student who objects that a demon holding the envelope predicts perfectly, so all I have demonstrated is our ignorance. Finally, be frank about which feature of the phenomenon the activity cannot reproduce and I must supply by argument. I suspect it is precisely the amplification worry from point 1; tell me whether that is right, and if so, what the honest framing sentence is.
