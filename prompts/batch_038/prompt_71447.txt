I have been defending the usual way of deciding under moral uncertainty, and a colleague turned my own principle against me until I stopped being able to answer. The chain runs as follows. (1) When you are uncertain which moral theory is true, you ought to perform the act with the greatest expected moral value, weighting each candidate theory's assessment of your options by your credence in that theory. (2) (1) is not a truth of logic but a substantive normative thesis, competing with others — act on the theory you rate most probable, or minimize the worst verdict across the theories you take seriously — so a reflective agent is less than certain of it. (3) Uncertainty about (1) is uncertainty of exactly the kind that (1) itself governs, so you should choose the option with the greatest expected expected moral value, weighting second-order theses about how to decide by your credence in them. (4) But (3) is again a substantive normative thesis about which you can be uncertain, so the same move lifts you to a third level, and then a fourth, without end. (5) Either this ascent stops at some level or it does not. (6) If it stops, the principle applied at the top is endorsed with no better warrant than a first-order theory picked out of a hat, since by hypothesis nothing recommends it beyond your own confidence in it; the whole procedure rests on an arbitrary foundation. (7) If it does not stop, an agent must work through infinitely many levels before choosing, and a principle that no agent can apply cannot tell anyone what to do. (8) Therefore (1) is either unfounded or unusable, and either way should be rejected. I would most like to keep (1), and if something has to go I would rather lose (7) than (2). Tell me which step I should actually give up, why that step looked unobjectionable when I first wrote it down, and what giving it up costs — including what it costs the rival procedures mentioned in (2), which I suspect inherit the same trouble.
