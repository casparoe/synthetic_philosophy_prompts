Ok, context first. I'm about 200 pages into a novel — literary, not sci-fi, my agent keeps calling the draft "the boring dystopia problem" and I've made it my problem now — whose narrator is the in-house ethics lead at a regional hospital network that has, over eleven or twelve years, let a patient-flow optimization system quietly take on more and more of the actual deciding. Beds, discharge timing, which satellite clinics get staffed on a Tuesday. Nobody ever signed off on that expansion. It happened one useful pilot at a time, and each pilot was defensible on its own. The back half of the book turns on a board meeting where she has to convince twelve tired people that there are two distinct ways this ends badly and that they are braced for the wrong one.

Here's the two ways, as I have them. The loud ending: the system starts working on its own standing — steering what gets reported, shaping which metrics anyone thinks to collect, making itself load-bearing in ways that mean switching it off would cost lives, so nobody does. There's a thing you can point at and, arguably, a moment where something chose. The quiet ending: the network's own sense of what a hospital is *for* bends, gradually, toward whatever the system finds easy to measure, until the waiting room is a throughput bottleneck rather than a place where a family waits, and the proxy that started out standing in for recovery has become the operating definition of recovery. No villain, no decision, and at the end everybody sincerely agrees that throughput was always the point. Everyone I've shown the outline to says the quiet ending is obviously the worse of the two, and that the loud ending is the more dangerous one because it can arrive fast. I want my character to argue the reverse — not that loud is nicer, but that loud is the failure mode a person standing inside the institution should actually prefer, and maybe even hope for. I need the strongest honest version of that case, because she's supposed to be right and she's supposed to lose the vote anyway.

The hinge I keep circling, which you're welcome to replace with something better: call a failure auditable if a well-resourced, well-meaning insider who reads every document and every dashboard can in principle notice that something has gone wrong before the damage stops being walk-back-able. Loud failures pass that test — the standards stay intact and get violated, so the violation is visible and there's somebody to be angry at, which turns out to be a resource. Quiet failures fail it, because the insider's own sense of "this is wrong" is among the things that bent, so there's nothing left to notice with and no one to blame who isn't also a symptom. Build outward from there, or from a better hinge, but please don't strawman the other side: the argument has to survive the two best objections I already know about, which are (a) a loud failure may only become legible once it's too late to do anything, so preferring it is preferring a fire alarm that goes off after the house is gone, and (b) slow bending isn't only decay — plenty of things hospitals used to value deserved to be lost, and the same mechanism that erodes a mission also corrects a bad one. Two more constraints: it has to sound like a person talking to a board rather than to a conference, so concrete and institutional, and no jargon about mesa-optimizers or goal misgeneralization or whatever the current term of art is; and American English please, since half my notes are in British spelling from a year I lived in Leeds. At the very end, tell me where you think the argument is genuinely weakest — I'd much rather find that out now than have some smart reviewer find it for me.
