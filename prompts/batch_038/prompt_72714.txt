I have just been awarded a modest four-year grant to run a small research program at the intersection of metaethics and cross-cultural psychology. Its stated aim, in the language of the proposal, is to determine whether the empirical record of cross-cultural variation in moral judgment licenses any substantive conclusion about moral objectivity. My resources are narrow and I want the agenda built around them rather than around an idealized project: two funded doctoral students, no postdocs, a collaborating lab that can field large online panels in roughly a dozen countries, and a budget for two workshops. I have no capacity for long-term ethnographic work in small-scale societies and no realistic prospect of acquiring any.

The questions I keep circling are the following. First, whether the documented variation is variation in the content of moral judgment, variation in which norms people moralize (that is, in the scope of morality rather than in its deliverances), or variation only in second-order self-reports about the status of morality — the three diagnoses have very different implications, and I have rarely seen them separated within a single instrument. Second, whether the instruments survive translation at all: in our pilot, the English items built around 'wrong' behaved quite differently from their closest counterparts at two sites, where the available vocabulary sorted transgressions by shame and contamination rather than by harm, and the standard dilemma materials lost their grip entirely. Third, whether the empirical base for genuinely alternative moralities is as wide as the debate assumes, given how much of the supposedly non-WEIRD evidence still comes from university students in industrializing economies, or from communities with a century or more of exposure to state legal systems and proselytizing religions. Our pilot also produced a result I do not know what to do with: at three sites, the participants who gave the most objectivist answers to the second-order questions were the ones whose first-order judgments moved most with framing. Fourth, whether the diversity argument is self-undermining — if construct validity fails across languages, it looks as though we lose both the evidence of deep disagreement and the shared subject matter that disagreement would have to be about.

What I want from you is a defended agenda, not a survey of the debate. Select the four to six open questions this program should actually spend four years on, rank them, and tell me for each: how far upstream it sits, in the sense that settling it would reshape or dissolve the others; how tractable it is with the resources described above rather than with those of a well-funded team; how neglected it is relative to the attention it currently receives; and whether its answer would change the philosophical bottom line about objectivity or would merely be empirically interesting. Then name one or two fashionable questions in this area that I should refuse to fund, and explain what makes them traps. Finally, tell me which specific results over the next two years would move items up or down your ordering — I want to know in advance which findings should make me redesign the program. Please do not include a reading list, bibliography, or paper recommendations; I am after the reasoning, and I would much rather you argue from the structure of the problem than reach for citations you may be unsure of. Write in American English, and close with a single line of the form \answer{...} containing a short label of no more than about six words for the one question you would fund first.
