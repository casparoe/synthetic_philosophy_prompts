I'm a third-year PhD student in philosophy of science, and last week I got roped into sitting in on a joint methods seminar we run with the economic history department. It turned into a genuine fight about the old Needham-style question — why did modern science and self-sustaining growth take off in western Europe rather than in China — and the organizer has asked me to write the exchange up for the mailing list. I want to stay neutral, so I'm not going to tell you which way I leaned (partly because I still can't work it out). Here's roughly how it went, as best I could transcribe it.

**Nadia (economic history), first turn:** the question is malformed, for three seperate reasons. (a) The explanandum is a bundle: Britain industrialized without producing much of what we'd call modern science, and seventeenth-century natural philosophy flourished in places that industrialized late or not at all, so before asking why it happened here, say which of these things you mean. (b) The contrast is arbitrary: you could equally ask why not India, Japan, the Ottoman lands, or everywhere else, and each foil makes different features salient. If the answer changes when you swap the foil without any new evidence coming in, the question isn't tracking anything out there. (c) The expectation is manufactured: China only looks like it "should" have gotten there because we know what happened and then compile a list of Chinese achievements — printing, cast iron, the compass, exam-based recruitment, big integrated grain markets — that look like precursors. Nothing tells you which items belong on the precursor list except having already seen where the outcome landed.

**Ray (philosophy of science, our department), first turn:** two of those are old objections and one is confused. On (b), context-dependence is how "why" works generally: "why did the pipe burst rather than the radiator" gets its foil from the situation and the asker's interests, and nobody calls plumbing questions ill-posed because they're indexical. A foil selects a difference-maker, and different foils selecting different difference-makers is a feature, so (b) shows the question needs sharpening, not that it's broken. On (c), circularity is avoidable — build the precondition list from cases that don't contain the outcome. Rank Song China, Abbasid Iraq, Gupta India and the seventeenth-century Dutch on urbanization, literacy, market integration and state capacity, and several of them score at least as high as post-1650 England; that's an ex ante expectation that doesn't presuppose the British case. On (a), fine, regiment it: sustained per-capita growth after 1800, measurable and dated. Vagueness in the original phrasing doesn't infect every precise version.

**Nadia, second turn:** the regimentation is the disease, not the cure. Compare England with the whole Qing empire and divergence shows up around 1750–1800. Compare the Yangzi delta with England — the Pomeranz move — and the two look startlingly alike well into the eighteenth century on commercialization, grain-market integration, life expectancy, and, on the wage estimates she prefers, living standards; the gap then opens late and fast, for reasons having to do with where the coal was and what the New World did for land constraints, not with anything on Ray's cultural-institutional list. Same precise question, materially different answers, and the difference is entirely a function of how you slice the map. Second: the standard explanans is a disjunction — fragmented competitive states, weak kin-based corporate structures, high wages and cheap energy, coal geology, New World land, property law, print, Protestantism, revenue-hungry imperial states — and you can strike out any single disjunct, keep the rest, and still tell the story. When each element is individually dispensable you haven't identified a difference-maker, you've catalogued everything that differed.

**Ray, second turn:** the disjunction point is the good point and it's fatal to a lot of the pop versions, but it's an objection to particular answers, not to the question; well-posed questions get bad answers all the time. Also, "individually dispensable" isn't a special defect here — singular-event causation generally comes in INUS packages, insufficient but non-redundant parts of conditions that are themselves unnecessary but sufficient — and demanding one clean difference-maker for a macro-outcome sets a bar no historical explanation has ever cleared, including ones Nadia is happy to give. On units: yes, the slice matters, which is equally true of "why did plague hit Marseille harder than Lyon"; you handle it by fixing the unit explicitly and reporting which conclusions survive the change and which don't. And the wage evidence cuts both ways — measured in silver rather than grain, English wages were multiples of the Yangzi delta's long before 1750, so the "they looked the same" premise may itself be an artifact of a framing choice. Finally, the cost: if malformation is the verdict, every institutional claim goes with it, since institutional claims are contrastive through and through.

**Nadia, third turn:** not all of them, and she makes plain causal claims without foils all day. What she objects to is the counterfactual the foil smuggles in. "Why Britain rather than China" doesn't just ask what Britain had; it presupposes that in the nearest world where Britain lacked it, China would have gotten there, and that's precisely what nobody can underwrite — you'd have to model a whole world system with the British factor removed and show Chinese development picking up the slack. Ray's pipe doesn't need that because pipes are local and the causal structure is given; a global economy in 1750 isn't. And the question isn't neutral in the way Ray makes out: it doesn't get asked out of idle curiosity, it gets asked to license claims about civilizational endowments, and once you see that the interest structure is what's selecting the foils, "the context fixes the foil" stops sounding benign.

**Ray, third turn:** the counterfactual is optional, not smuggled. On the weak reading — what features of Britain were causally operative in the transition that Qing China lacked — you need no global counterfactual at all, and that's the reading most working historians have in mind. So Nadia knocks down the strong reading and then calls the weak one empty. And on interest: a question asked for bad reasons can still have a determinate answer; motive doesn't infect semantics. If anything, declaring the whole arguement malformed leaves the field to the people who ask it badly.

So, six turns. Can you go through them one at a time and tell me for each whether the point lands, half-lands, or misses, with a couple of sentences of justification? I'm especially interested in whether Ray's indexicality reply actually answers (b) or just renames the problem, and whether the counterfactual worry in Nadia's third turn is a real cost or a red herring. Then: what single issue does the whole thing turn on, is there a version of the question both of them could sign up to, and what's the strongest objection to whichever side you end up favoring that neither of them raised? Also flag anywhere one of them is just talking past the other — I suspect it's happening at least once and I can't pin down where.
