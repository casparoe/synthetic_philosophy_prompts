I tag along with a history reading group (not my field, Im just there for the free coffee) and last week two of the regulars, Dana and Marcus, got into a long argument about who started the Cold War that turned into an argument about something more general. I couldn't tell who came out ahead, so I'm passing it along without taking a side.

They actually agreed on the timeline: Kennan's long telegram in Feb '46, the Truman Doctrine and the Harvard speech in '47, Moscow pulling Poland and Czechoslovakia out of the Marshall Plan talks that summer, the coup in Prague and the blockade of Berlin in '48, NATO and the first Soviet bomb in '49. What they fought about was what to do with it.

1. Dana said the question has a determinate answer if you define it properly. Her test: the starter is whoever first did something that (a) they knew the other side would read as hostile and (b) wasnt itself a response to a hostile act already on the table. She said on that test it's Stalin, because the Soviet consolidation of '45-'48 needs no American move to explain it, whereas containment and the Marshall Plan do — they're reactions, and a reaction cant be a first move by definition.

2. Marcus said clause (b) is doing no work whatsoever. In a security dilemma anything can be narrated as a response to something, so (b) never excludes anything and the test just spits out whoever you already sympathized with. He said run it from the other end: the abrupt cutoff of Lend-Lease right after V-J Day, the stalled loan talks, four years of atomic monopoly — plenty of American acts that a Soviet planner in '46 would read as hostile. Dana only gets Stalin, he said, because she's pre-labeled Soviet acts "offensive" and American ones "defensive" before the test ever runs.

3. Dana said that's an accusation of bias, not an argument, and the test is symmetrical: if two people apply it and get different answers, that's evidence they applied it badly, not that it's broken. Then she made what I thought was her best point — give up on the first-mover question and you cant grade anything anymore. A purely structural account files the Marshall Plan and the Prague coup in the same drawer, as things that happened to people because of the shape of the system, and then historiography has thrown out the one thing it's actually for.

4. Marcus said he wasn't dropping grading, he was dropping the frame, and he offered a replacement: the starter is whoever, had they acted differently in the closest world where they could have, there'd have been no Cold War. Dana turned it on him immediately — run it for Stalin and the US still keeps the atomic monopoly and the loan pressure; run it for Truman and Stalin still keeps the bloc — so the counterfactual is symmetric too, and "they both started it" isn't an answer to "who started it," it's a dissolution wearing an answer's clothes.

5. Marcus said symmetry is a substantive result, not a dodge: it says responsibility cant be seperated out into one party, which is precisely his point. Dana said fine, then admit the view has no content, and added that dissolutions like this always land wherever your own government's current enemies are, so it's propaganda with better manners. Marcus shot back that the same goes for her test: in 1950 the first-mover answer came out as Moscow, and twenty years later lots of people running what looked like the same kind of test got Washington, and the test didn't change, the politics did.

That's roughly where it stalled. Can you go through it move by move and rate each of the five from clearly wrong to clearly right, with a sentence justifying each rating? Then say who's more right overall. And finally: is there something both of them are treating as settled that shouldn't be? I keep getting the feeling they're arguing past each other about something that never got put on the table — like whether "the Cold War" is even one thing with one onset rather than a bundle of separate conflicts each with its own — but I cant articulate it properly.
