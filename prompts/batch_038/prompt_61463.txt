im in week 6 of a climate ethics seminar and we're on discounting, section is thursday and i want to defend pure time preference, which is a deeply unpopular thing to say in that room. every week someone rolls out the Ramsey line — that discounting later enjoyments just because they're later is "ethically indefensible" and comes "merely from the weakness of the imagination" — and then everyone nods and we move on to how the growth term ηg does all the legitimate work and δ should be zero or near it.

i think there's a defensible δ and i have a sketch but its a mess. here's the position i want you to argue for, seriously, not strawmanned:

a positive pure rate of time preference is not the claim that the wellbeing of future people counts for less than ours. it's a claim about the reliability of our own evaluative framework. the further out we project, the more likely it is that the thing we're projecting — "welfare", "a life going well", what counts as a benefit or a catastrophe — has drifted away from what we mean by those terms, because values and the goods that constitute a good life change over long horizons in ways we cannot audit from inside our own time. so δ is a discount on our confidence that our current framework correctly identifies the value of far-future states of affairs, not a discount on their value once correctly identified. on this reading δ isn't an ethical weight at all, it's an epistemic one, and it never collides with impartiality, because impartiality governs how you weigh interests you've already correctly identified.

what i need:

1. build the argument properly. the crux for me is that this δ must not double-count: it has to be kept seperate from the growth term ηg and from ordinary uncertainty about whether future people exist at all or whether the climate system delivers the outcomes we predict. a good version of the view needs to explain what's left over for δ once those are already in the model, and why the leftover is time-shaped rather than just generic uncertainty.

2. handle these objections, in this order, and say which one you think actually lands:
   (a) the parfit-style one — mere location in time is morally irrelevant, and the people in 2400 will have lives that go well or badly by *their* lights, so why is our framework the measuring stick at all? if their standards are the right ones, evaluative drift isn't a loss of information, it's just different information.
   (b) isn't "we can't reliably imagine what far-future value will be" literally ramsey's weakness of the imagination wearing a nicer hat? either distinguish the two sharply or concede it.
   (c) compounding. even a tiny δ, compounded over a thousand years, drives the far future toward zero weight, which looks like a reductio if the whole selling point was that this is a modest epistemic correction.
   (d) symmetry — values drift backwards too, so why doesn't the past deserve *more* weight than we give it? if the view can't answer this it's not about drift, it's about us.
