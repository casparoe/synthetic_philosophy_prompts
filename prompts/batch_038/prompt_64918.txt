I don't have any training in economics, but over the last few months I've been reading on my own about how climate policy weighs costs today against harms decades from now, and I came across a chapter in a general-audience book that explains discounting this way:

"Discounting isn't a moral claim, it's arithmetic about time. People prefer a dollar now to a dollar later, economists call that pure time preference — impatience — and it's just a fact about human beings. What climate economists do is take that fact, scale it up to the level of society, and turn it into a social discount rate, which is how costs today get compared against damages a century out. That rate typically lands between 2 and 5 percent, which is why a unit of harm fifty years from now counts for much less than the same harm today: under ten cents on the dollar at the high end of that range. People object that all this writes off our grandchildren, but they're objecting to a straw man. Almost none of the discounting comes from any judgment that later people matter less; nearly all of it comes from expecting that they'll be richer, so a given loss takes a smaller bite out of their lives. Strip that piece out and pure time preference shrinks to nearly nothing. The remaining disagreement is technical — about growth projections — not ethical."

I half-suspect something slippery is going on in here, and I half-suspect I'm the confused one. Could you go through it sentence by sentence and tell me what a working climate economist would sign off on, what an economist or philosopher who sides with the critics would call a distortion, and what is simply wrong? Two things I especially want pinned down: whether the claim that the rate is arithmetic rather than moral can survive once you look at how each of its ingredients gets chosen, and whether the "they'll be richer" part is really value-free or whether it smuggles in an assumption about how one person's wellbeing is to be weighed against another's. My own inclination, which I can't defend properly, is that if I wouldn't count my daughter's suffering less just because it sits further off in time, then something morally loaded has to be hiding in the arithmetic somewhere. Finally: if the passage were corrected as fully as possible, would its conclusion — that the dispute is technical rather than ethical — still stand, or does that conclusion depend on the parts that don't survive?
