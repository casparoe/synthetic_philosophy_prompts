I sit on the research-strategy committee for the safety group at a mid-sized frontier lab, and last month's budget meeting came down to a single historical analogy.

One of our more senior colleagues, who is broadly sympathetic to alignment work, argued that the case for treating misaligned frontier models as an existential risk has the same shape as the case made for Y2K in 1998–99. In both instances, on his telling: a small group of technically literate specialists, reasoning from real but narrow observations about systems they understood better than the public did, persuaded a great many intelligent people that a catastrophe was coming; enormous money and attention were mobilised (worldwide remediation spend is usually estimated in the hundreds of billions of dollars); and then, when nothing visible happened, the non-event was reinterpreted as proof that the warnings had been heeded rather than as evidence that they had been overblown. His conclusion was that we should discount the current x-risk literature by a comparable factor and move a large share of our budget toward near-term, measurable harms. A second colleague replied that the analogy, taken seriously, points the other way: Y2K was averted precisely because a handful of people took a low-credibility warning seriously and did years of unglamorous remediation in advance, and a field that never gets to observe its own success is structurally unable to disconfirm itself — so a quiet decade is the expected signature of the safety programme working, not of the risk being illusory.

Both are clever and both are arguing in good faith, and I suspect the analogy is carrying considerably more weight than either of them has admitted. Before I write my recommendation I would like it audited properly. No sources or references, please — I want the reasoning, and where you are unsure of a historical detail, flag it rather than filling the gap.

Please structure your answer with exactly these sections, in this order:

**Bottom line** — about 100 words. State plainly whether the analogy supports the discount my senior colleague wants, and what you would tell the committee.

**The mapping** — roughly 400 words, and a table is welcome. Lay out what corresponds to what: the specialists, the narrow technical evidence, the deadline, the remediation work, the spending, the quiet aftermath, the credit dispute. For each row, say how tight the correspondence actually is.

**Load-bearing disanalogies** — about 500 words. The mismatches that genuinely break the inference, rather than merely sounding impressive. I want an explicit verdict on four candidates: that Y2K had a knowable date and a binary, observable outcome; that the defect was enumerable and testable in advance by the parties who owned the systems; that nothing was optimising to make the failure worse; and that the size of the harm was estimable from engineering rather than argued from a story about values. Explain why each of these matters, or does not, for the specific use being made of the analogy.

**Cosmetic disanalogies** — about 200 words. The objections that get raised and that a defender of the analogy can safely absorb.

**What the analogy smuggles in** — about 300 words. The unstated premises about how probabilities should be estimated, about base rates for expert forecasts of catastrophe, and about the evidential weight of "nothing bad has happened yet."

**Is the second reply self-sealing?** — about 350 words. This is the part I most want thought through. Is my colleague's point a real insight about prevention, or an unfalsifiable defence? Is there any observation that could distinguish "the warnings were overblown" from "the warnings were heeded," and if so, what would it look like in our case?

**Better analogies** — about 250 words. If the Y2K frame is the wrong one for either side, propose a substitute for each and say what work the substitute can and cannot do.

**Verdict and recommendation** — about 250 words. Whether the discount survives the audit, in what modified form if any, and how I should write the memo.
