I am assembling a unit for a graduate seminar on moral desert in which I want to treat karma not as a piece of religious lore but as a live candidate theory of desert. The theory, roughly as I have been presenting it, holds that every asymmetry in how lives go is something its subject earned; that the accounting is exact and self-executing rather than administered by any judge; and that desert is inalienably personal, so that a being owns its own actions and inherits their results and nothing else. It is the third clause I want to press on, because it appears to sit badly with a practice that lies at the center of lay religious life in the very traditions that teach it.

The practice, as I understand it (please correct me wherever I have garbled the details): a layperson makes a gift to the monastic community, often on the occasion of a funeral or of an annual commemoration of a dead relative, and at the close of the ceremony dedicates the merit of that gift to the deceased relative, or to departed relatives generally, or in some versions to all sentient beings. The dedication is a mental act rather than a physical handing-over; in some countries it is accompanied by the slow pouring of water from one vessel into another while a short formula is spoken, and the assembled company responds with a single word of approval. Four further features strike me as philosophically load-bearing. First, the donor's own stock of merit is not depleted by the dedication: the traditional image is of one candle lighting another, where the first flame is not thereby diminished. Second, the intended beneficiary can actually benefit only under narrow conditions — early texts, as I understand them, hold that the dead cannot receive a dedication if they have been reborn in a heaven, in a hell, as an animal, or as another human being, but only if they are among the hungry ghosts. Third, even in that case the beneficiary must assent: the recipient has to approve of, or rejoice in, the good deed done on their behalf, and traditional catalogues of meritorious deeds list rejoicing in the merit of others as an item in its own right, sitting alongside dedicating one's own merit to others. Fourth, if the dedication fails to reach anyone at all, the dedicator nevertheless gains merit for having made it.

The tension I keep coming back to is this. The surface grammar of the practice — this merit is given to that person, who is thereby relieved — looks like it presupposes a frankly proprietary theory of desert, on which desert is a fungible, storable, alienable asset that can be earmarked for a beneficiary much as an inheritance can be. But that is precisely what the doctrine of personal karma denies. The harmonization available inside the tradition is that nothing is in fact transferred: the dedication supplies the departed with an occasion, and it is their own act of approval, being their own, that generates their own fresh merit and improves their condition. On that reading the ritual's surface grammar systematically misdescribes what the ritual accomplishes, and the practice's real presupposition is not transfer but enablement — merit as an opportunity-generating good rather than a conveyable one. I am not sure whether that move rescues the practice or merely relocates the difficulty.

Writing as a professional philosopher who works on both analytic theories of desert and Buddhist ethics, please reconstruct the theory of desert that this practice actually presupposes. I want you to be scrupulous about a distinction: what the practice strictly requires, in the sense that it could not function or be intelligible to its participants without it, versus what it merely fits comfortably with. Which rival accounts of desert does the practice rule out — in particular, does it rule out the strictly personal, non-transferable account, or does the rejoicing mechanism genuinely reconcile the two? Does that reconciliation succeed, and does it generalize to the variant in which merit is dedicated to all sentient beings, where no one can be informed that a dedication has been made on their behalf and where most addressees are by hypothesis reborn somewhere they cannot receive it? I would also like you to mark clearly where the practice is philosophically underdetermined, that is, where several incompatible theories would license it equally well. Two candidates occur to me and I would like them assessed and others added: a purely social reading, on which the ritual is an enactment of continuing filial obligation that does its moral work among the living and commits no one to any metaphysics of desert; and a reading on which the underlying concept is not desert at all but something more like a potency or purity that accumulates and ripens naturally, in which case my own framing of karma as a theory of moral desert — and the English word I have been using for it — may rest on a mistranslation. Then tell me what would have to change in the practice if the strictly personal view were enforced, and finally, the question I actually want for the seminar: does any of this give secular theorizing about desert a purchase it otherwise lacks, for instance on whether desert can ever be derivative (owed to someone in virtue of another's act), on collective desert as predicated of families or firms or nations, or on the thought that being a fitting recipient of someone else's good deed is itself something one can deserve?

Before committing to an interpretation, begin with a short brainstorming section: list at least four candidate theories of desert that could underwrite the practice, including ones I have not mentioned, and for each say in a sentence or two which of the features above it explains and which it struggles with. Only then proceed to the main analysis. Think the problem through rather than surveying positions, and do not end on a both-sides note — I want a definite verdict about what the practice presupposes and about whether that presupposition is coherent.
