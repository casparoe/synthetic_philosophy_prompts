Here's the draft, about 1,100 words when its done, this is the spine of it:

Working title: "What the Bulldozer Doesn't Kill"

1. Habitat destruction is the closest thing environmental politics has to a settled wrong. Nobody with a column argues for draining the wetland. And because the wrong is settled, the accounting that goes with it has gone unexamined: acres lost, species lost, individual animals displaced or killed. A body count. It's the right instinct and I think its also, in a specific way, incomplete — because the biggest thing a habitat does isn't hold animals, it manufactures them.

2. Most wild animals never grow up. A cod releases millions of eggs and a couple become adults. Insects, fish, amphibians, small rodents — for a huge share of the biomass on earth, the life cycle runs on producing enormous numbers of offspring of whom almost all die small, hungry, or eaten, usually within weeks. So when a wetland gets drained, what mostly disappears is not a community of adult animals. It's a machine for turning resources into very large quantities of very short, very bad lives. I want a name for the animals that machine would have made and now won't. Call them the phantom cohort: the ones who would have been born into a habitat that became a logistics park, and who therefore never experience anything at all.

3. Now the step I'm scared of. If the average life that habitat produced was worse than no life, then destroying it subtracted more suffering than it added, and the body-count framing has the sign backwards. Which means two kinds of accounting come apart. Grief accounting is what happens to us when we watch the excavators — the loss of a place, a lineage, a particular heron. Welfare accounting is what happens to the total of experience. I'm not saying grief accounting is fake. I'm saying it's answering a different question, and that in the case of habitat loss it may be systematically pointing us at the wrong answer.
