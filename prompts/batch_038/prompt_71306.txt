I'm working out what to demand of a theory that ranks outcomes containing infinitely many locations of value — infinitely many people, say, each with a finite lifetime well-being — since the long-run future may well be such an outcome. Before I read any proposals, write the specification: the conditions an adequate ranking must satisfy, with the case or consideration behind each. The sorts of thing I mean include that it must not call two outcomes equally good when everyone is strictly better off in one; that it must not turn on how we happen to number the people; that it must have something to say about adding one happy person to an already infinite population, where the total is infinity either way; and that it must not treat a continuum-sized population and a countable one as belonging to different problems. For each condition, mark whether it is non-negotiable or a substantive commitment that only some camps insist on, and say which single condition rules out the most candidate theories.

Most importantly: which subsets of the list cannot be jointly satisfied? My suspicion is that anonymity in the strong sense — invariance under every permutation of the population — is incompatible even with a weak Pareto condition, and I want the reasoning laid out, not a citation. Also tell me which conditions look like requirements but are really artifacts of assuming the finite case carries over. A table is fine for the conditions; no bibliography.
