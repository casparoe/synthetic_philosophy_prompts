I'm in the fourth year of a PhD in political theory, and next year I'm running an eight-session interdisciplinary seminar on the boundary problem in democratic theory. The participants are a mix of political theorists, people whose training is in formal epistemology and social choice, and two migration lawyers. I want the series built around a sequence of open questions rather than around texts or around the literature — I know the literature, and a survey would be a waste of the format. What I need help with is the ordering, and I have to commit to it now.

Here is where I'm stuck. The debate has a fairly standard shape: no procedure for fixing who belongs to the demos can itself be democratically authorized, since authorizing it would presuppose the very body it is meant to constitute. The familiar responses — enfranchise everyone whose interests are affected by the decision, enfranchise everyone subject to the coercion of the law, accept that boundaries are settled by some prior, non-democratic process, or dissolve the question across a system of differently constituted units — each looks either like a regress-stopper imported from outside democracy or like a quiet change of subject. Then there is the temporal version of the difficulty: whatever demos is in place now decides questions that bind the composition of the demos later, so the second-order question never appears to close.

What I'd like from you is a defended agenda. Rank the open questions you think eight sessions should be organized around, and justify the ranking against criteria you state explicitly rather than leave implicit: (a) downstream weight — how much else turns on the answer, and in particular whether an answer would change anything substantive about long-term resident non-citizens, about the standing of future generations in decisions like climate policy, or about who ought to have a say over private rule-makers whose decisions reach across borders; (b) tractability in one term by non-specialists with some formal background — whether there is an argument that can actually be advanced in a session rather than a position that can only be restated; and (c) neglectedness — whether the question is genuinely unresolved or merely unfashionable. Where those criteria pull against each other, say which you are trading off and why. Then tell me which two of the most-discussed questions in this area I should leave out precisely because settling them would change nothing downstream, and defend that exclusion rather than just asserting it.

One point I want you to take a stand on instead of hedging. My own suspicion is that the regress is at bottom a problem of self-application — a demos being asked to fix its own extension — and that the interesting question is consequently whether a typed solution (a first-order demos, a second-order demos, and so on up the hierarchy) or a fixed-point solution (a membership rule that is stable under its own application) could dissolve it, or at least isolate exactly where the normativity re-enters once the logical structure is pinned down. If you think that reframing is a dead end, or a way of dressing up a substantive normative choice as a puzzle about self-reference, say so plainly and explain what is lost by the translation; if you think it is promising, describe what the first non-trivial result in that direction would look like and which of your ranked questions it would bear on.

Finally, please be explicit about the fragility of your own recommendations: which of your criteria is doing most of the work, which of your judgment calls you would expect to reverse under sustained pressure, and what argument or consideration — normative or formal — would move a question up or down your list. Where you are least confident, I would rather be told so directly than have the uncertainty smoothed over.
