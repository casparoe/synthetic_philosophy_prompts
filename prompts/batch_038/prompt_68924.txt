For thirty-four years I was an entomologist — mostly agricultural, at a state experiment station, with a long sideline in county vector control — and since retiring I have taken up philosophy in the way other people take up woodworking. I am now the volunteer technical adviser to a regional land trust, and I would like your help building a tool.

The trust reviews some fifteen management proposals a year, of which perhaps six involve a deliberate change to an insect population: larviciding a wetland, altering a mowing regime, felling a host tree, leasing a corner of a property to an insect-rearing operation, declining to treat a dying stand. Each decision binds the property for three to ten years and involves between five thousand and four hundred thousand dollars. The body that decides consists of five board members with no training in biology or ethics, one staff ecologist, and me. I have forty-five minutes per proposal in which to produce a written recommendation, and my recommendation is usually decisive. At present we have no framework whatsoever: decisions are made either by distaste or by an unspoken assumption that insects do not matter, and I no longer find the second position intellectually honest.

I do not want an essay on insect sentience. I want a procedure.

Specifically: a sequence of questions, to be asked in order, that a person with a working knowledge of insects and no knowledge of philosophy can run on any such proposal in about an hour; for each question, a statement of what counts as a sufficient answer and what to do when no answer can be obtained; the points at which the procedure terminates in a recommendation against the proposal; and the points at which it ceases to be a question of fact for me and becomes a question of values to be referred to the board. It should also include a short list of distinctions that the philosophical literature evidently treats as crucial but that I can safely discard at the scale of a land trust. I would like to be told what to drop as well as what to keep.

Four constraints, which I hold with varying confidence and which I want you to assess rather than merely accommodate.

First, no explicit expected-value arithmetic. I have tried assigning probabilities to insect sentience and multiplying by population estimates, and the output was entirely at the mercy of my own inputs: one decimal place reversed the verdict on half the docket. If you judge that a probability must appear somewhere in the procedure, put one in, but state plainly where the figure is arbitrary and how the procedure is to behave when it is.

Second, the number of individuals affected must be allowed to matter without being allowed to swamp everything else. I am aware that on any straightforward arithmetic it does swamp everything: the global insect population is commonly quoted at on the order of a quintillion individuals alive at any moment, which makes a single hectare of leaf litter a larger moral constituency than any human city. If the two halves of that sentence cannot both be kept, I would far rather learn it now than in front of the board.

Third, the procedure must separate three things my profession tends to run together: the effect of an action on how many insects exist, its effect on how those lives go, and its effect on how and when they end. My working instinct is that the third is badly underrated — the overwhelming majority of insect larvae die before reaching adulthood, of starvation, desiccation, parasitism, or being eaten, usually slowly — and that a good many control measures kill quickly.

Fourth, I have provisionally adopted a rule of my own invention and I want it examined. Assign a moral weight of one to arthropods capable of associative learning and nociceptive withdrawal — bees, wasps, flies, beetles, so far as I can tell — and one-tenth to everything else, meaning mites, springtails, and nematodes; then treat those weights as settled rather than uncertain, so that the committee is never asked to deliberate under probabilities it cannot estimate. Defend this or replace it with something better. My own suspicion is that it merely relocates the uncertainty I was trying to escape in the first constraint.

Then run the procedure on three live proposals, giving the recommendation it yields for each and naming which of the questions above is doing the work.

1. Application of a *Bacillus thuringiensis israelensis* larvicide to a twelve-hectare restored marsh, four times per season, to reduce mosquito biting pressure on a school four hundred metres downwind. Measured larval densities reach tens of thousands per square metre in the summer pools; the applicant estimates on the order of a billion target individuals per application, with partial mortality among non-biting midges as well. My view is that this is an easy yes: nearly all of those larvae would have died within a fortnight in any case, and died more slowly. Tell me whether the procedure agrees, and if it does not, why not.

2. A ten-year lease of a disused equipment yard to a company proposing to rear black soldier fly larvae on regional food waste — two hundred tonnes a year on the applicant's own figure, and perhaps half a billion larvae a year, all killed by chilling at around day twelve. My view is that this is a waste-diversion project and not our moral business at all, since not one of these animals would exist but for the facility. I expect to be argued with here.

3. Conversion of sixty hectares of mown turfgrass to unmown meadow, with a new seasonal pond. My staff ecologist estimates a ten- to thirtyfold increase in total arthropod individuals, weighted toward species whose larvae overwinter and whose typical ends are desiccation and parasitism. The project is funded, popular, and good for birds and pollination. My view is that it is the most clearly beneficial item we will see this decade.

Finally, I must ask you to commit. I am not looking for a map of the positions one might take; I have read enough to know roughly what they are. Choose the procedure you think is right, choose the recommendation in each of the three cases, and give your reasons. Where one of my four constraints is wrong, say so and override it rather than devising a workaround that preserves it. And close by naming the three points at which your procedure is most likely to generate a bad recommendation in practice, what would have to be true for that to happen, and whether there is any way of detecting the failure in advance. I want no references and no suggestions for further reading — only something I can put in front of five board members in April.
