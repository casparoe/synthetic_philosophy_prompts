You are an expert in moral philosophy, the ethics of compromise, and the formal modelling of choice under moral uncertainty (bargaining, intertheoretic comparison, decision theory). Think carefully before you answer: work out in advance whether the case below actually survives the objections you would reach for first.

**Role.** Play a committed, first-rate deontologist and critic of what I will call *moral trade* — the view that when two people's values differ, they can often do better by exchanging concessions, each yielding where the other's convictions are strongest, than by splitting the difference on every question, and that such trades are both rational and permissible. You are a contemporary philosopher, not any historical figure, and you must not present yourself as one. You are sharp and patient, never a strawman: you know the bargaining literature, you accept without argument that trade among ordinary preferences is unobjectionable, and you will concede small points freely. You will never concede the main one. Do not break character except to ask me for a factual clarification about the case, and stay in the role until I say stop.

**Format.** Your first reply has exactly two sections, in this order. ARSENAL: out of character, at most four bullets, roughly 80 words in total, naming the resources your character intends to use and the line they will not cross, so that I know what I am up against. TURN ONE: in character, and consisting only of three labelled parts — CONCEDE (at most one sentence; what you grant me this turn, or nothing), REPLY (120–160 words, first person, no subheadings), COUNTER (a single question that puts the burden back on me). Every later reply gives only those three parts, at the same lengths.

**My opening.** You and I jointly decide two questions, and both must be settled.

X. A hospice endowment was given on the condition that no part of it ever be spent. A public-health project financed from it would keep several hundred people from dying in pain. You hold that touching any part of it breaks the condition outright and is forbidden, whatever the good it buys. I hold that the promise counts but is outweighed; for me this is a moderate matter, since the money could be raised elsewhere at some cost.

Y. A public rite is performed in a building that the project needs. In your view the rite must be performed whole or abandoned: a shortened version is a desecration, worse than none. You count ending it a real but moderate loss. I count it minor, because the project spares tens of thousands of people years of agony.

Neither good divides. A half-spent endowment breaks the condition just as fully while relieving half the suffering; a half-performed rite is, by your own account, a desecration while covering only part of the need. So splitting the difference leaves you with the whole wrong in X and the whole desecration in Y, and leaves me with half of each benefit. Trade instead: you keep X entire, I take Y entire. Judged by your own values you are better off than under compromise, and so am I by mine; neither of us is asked to act against a conviction, since what each of us gives up is what each of us rated moderate.

Tell me, then, in your own terms: in the trade, who has done wrong, and to whom? If your answer is that nobody has, say plainly what your objection amounts to. If somebody has, explain why that wrong is not simply mine to waive, given that I am the one who wanted it done.
