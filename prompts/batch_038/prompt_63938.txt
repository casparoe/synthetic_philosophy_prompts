You are a professional philosopher whose specialty is the logic and pragmatics of conditional claims in ordinary reasoning — the sort of person who gets asked to untangle disputes about evidence, causation, and who is responsible for what. Work through my situation carefully before you write anything: in particular, work out separately what would make each of the two formulations below acceptable to print, and whether the confidence that licenses the first one also licenses the second. Do that working-out silently; what you hand me should begin with the verdict.

Here is the situation.

I am a reporter at a regional paper. Six weeks ago we published a story based on an internal memo from the city's housing authority documenting structural defects in three tower blocks. Someone inside the authority gave us the document. The authority has opened an inquiry into the leak, and I have good reason — call it about eighty-five percent confidence — to think the source was one particular engineer, a mid-level structural analyst. I have not published his name.

Yesterday the inquiry's chair told me, off the record, that if that engineer did not hand over the memo, somebody else in the authority did. That struck me as close to a truism: the memo reached us, so whoever it was not, it was someone. I wrote the sentence into a follow-up piece.

My editor cut it and substituted this: if the engineer had not handed over the memo, someone else would have. She says the two come to the same thing, and that the second is the one worth printing, because it carries the point we actually want to make — that the leak was inevitable given how much anger there was inside the authority, and that the engineer should not be turned into a scapegoat. She also says I am being inconsistent: if I am confident enough to print the first sentence, I have to be confident enough to print the second, and if I am not confident about the second, I should pull the first.

I do not think I am being inconsistent, but I cannot say why. I would print the first sentence without hesitation. I would not put my byline under the second one, even though the second appears to say less than the first rather than more.

Some further facts you should have. Three other authority staff have separately told me they had been thinking about going public. Two of them say that once they learned the memo was out they dropped the idea entirely — one because she concluded it no longer mattered, one because he was afraid the inquiry would widen. A fourth person, a contractor, told me he would have sent us the memo in the spring if the engineer had not got there first; he also said he had been waiting on a contract renewal that was finally settled in June, two months after we published. Separately, there is a version of this story that would have surfaced anyway in the autumn, when the authority's own inspection cycle is due to report.

Answer these, in this order:

1. Is my editor right that the two sentences stand or fall together?
2. What exactly would I have to establish about the authority before I could print the second sentence responsibly — and do the facts above establish it, or does one of them quietly pull against it?
3. Is there a formulation of the inevitability point that survives, one I could defend on the record if the engineer's union called the newsroom?

Lead with your bottom line in your very first sentence — a direct verdict on the editor's claim — and only then give the reasoning. No preamble, no restating my situation or my questions, and no general introduction to how these sentences work; assume I want the analysis itself. Where a fact of mine is doing more work than I realized, say so explicitly rather than working around it, and where your conclusion depends on something I have not told you, name what you would need to know.
