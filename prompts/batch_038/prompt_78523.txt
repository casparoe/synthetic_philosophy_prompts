In a seminar on early modern political philosophy last week, our leader explained why Hobbes believed his civil philosophy could be demonstrative rather than merely persuasive. His account ran as follows, and I have tried to reproduce it faithfully:

"For Hobbes the model of all science was the clockmaker. You come to understand a clock by taking it apart, inspecting each wheel and spring, and reassembling it — resolution followed by composition. Hobbes then held that geometry is the one genuine science, and the reason is that the geometer makes his own object: he draws the triangle, and he generates magnitudes by imagining a body in motion, so he is the cause of the figure and therefore knows it from the inside. In physics we are in the opposite position: we work backwards from observed effects to causes we did not make, and we can only ever reach plausible hypotheses. Civil philosophy acquires the same standing as geometry by exactly the same route. We make the commonwealth — the covenanters assemble it out of themselves — so the political philosopher is entitled to resolve it into its parts, which are the passions and appetites of individual men, and to recompose it into a demonstration. That is all the famous opening of Leviathan amounts to: the commonwealth as an artificial animal, sovereignty standing in for the soul, magistrates and officers for the joints, reward and punishment for the nerves. Politics is geometry with a different subject matter. Which is why the thirty years Hobbes spent trying to square the circle, and his savage pamphlet war with Wallis over infinitesimals and algebra, are biographical noise — a failed mathematician's vanity, with no bearing whatever on the political theory."

Three things about this unsettled me, and I cannot tell whether my unease is justified or whether I am simply missing something. First, he runs two distinct analogies together — the clockmaker's disassembly of a mechanism, and the maker's privileged knowledge of what he has made — and it is not obvious that they support the same step. Second, Hobbes elsewhere seems to claim knowledge of the passions on quite different grounds, namely that we find them in ourselves; if that is the real warrant for the elements of politics, then maker's knowledge may not be doing any work in the political case at all. Third, the identity of the maker is unclear: the multitude constructs the commonwealth, but Hobbes the philosopher is not among its constructors, and the parts being assembled are not passive wheels but creatures who can refuse the definitions they are being given.

Could you audit the whole thing for me? Specifically:

(a) Give an explicit correspondence table for each of the two analogies separately — what in the clockmaker case and what in the geometer case is supposed to stand for what in civil philosophy — and say whether the two are compatible or whether one of them is really a substitute for the other.

(b) List the disanalogies, sorted into those that genuinely threaten the inference from "we know geometry because we construct its objects" to "we know politics for the same reason," and those that are merely local embarrassments which a sympathetic reader can absorb.

(c) Say what each analogy quietly imports from its source into the target — for instance, about the passivity of parts, the repeatability of construction, the stipulativeness of definitions, the possibility of inspecting the assembled whole from outside — and which of those imports the commonwealth actually resists.

(d) Judge whether the slogan survives: is Hobbes's civil philosophy, on his own premises, geometry with a different subject matter, or is the difference in subject matter the difference between a demonstration and something weaker? If weaker, does Hobbes's substantive political theory — the necessity of an undivided sovereign, the non-obligatoriness of covenants without a common power — depend on the strong claim, or can it stand on the weaker one?

(e) Assess the "biographical noise" line. Do Hobbes's nominalism, his view of what reasoning amounts to, his account of magnitude as generated by motion, and his rejection of indivisibles and symbolic algebra have any consequences for what he thinks a demonstration must be about, and hence for the status he claims for politics? Or could he have lost every argument with Wallis and retained the political theory intact?

(f) Finally, if the clockmaker and the geometer both fail at the point where the work is needed, is there a better analogy — ideally one Hobbes himself reached for elsewhere, such as the theatrical or legal machinery of personation and authorization, or a game constituted by rules its players must accept — that would carry the conclusion the seminar leader wanted?
