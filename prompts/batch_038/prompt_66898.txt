I teach an upper-level interdisciplinary seminar whose enrollment is roughly half philosophy majors and half molecular biology majors, and week four is devoted to the claim that DNA is a code. Rather than lecture, I want a sorting instrument the students run on themselves in twenty minutes of class time and then argue about. Please write a decision tree that takes a student from a short list of molecular facts everybody in the room already accepts to a named position on whether the genome carries information in a literal sense.

The trunk of the tree should be a shared starting point, not a question: that translation proceeds by a codon-to-amino-acid mapping which is near-universal but has documented variants; that the same sequence is read to different effect in different cell types and environments; that changing a sequence reliably changes downstream products, while changing other things changes them too; and that a great deal of sequence in most genomes is never read at all. From there, six to eight questions, each a concrete case with two or three answer options, and each answerable by a student who has never encountered the literature. No question may ask whether DNA is a code, whether it carries information, or any near-paraphrase of either. No question may be otiose: every question must send at least two of the eventual leaves down different branches, and no leaf should be reachable by two different paths unless you flag the collision deliberately.

The tree must route on a distinction I use in the seminar but do not announce to the students, because I want them to arrive at it: three candidate relations between a sequence and what it produces. Correspondence by resemblance, on which the sequence is a template or mould whose parts match their product part-for-part in virtue of physical complementarity. Correspondence by convention, on which the mapping is arbitrary and could have been otherwise, as with a symbol. Correspondence by use, on which the sequence is a signal whose content is fixed by what some receiver does with it and by what the arrangement was selected for. A coherent view may affirm more than one, so these belong at separate nodes, not as three mutually exclusive exits. If you think one of my three relations is redundant or badly drawn, say so and give me the replacement, but the delivered tree must still contain a node for each.

The cases must include at least: an engineered organism in which a synthetic biologist has reassigned a stop codon to a non-natural amino acid and the machinery reads it faithfully for generations; a sequence that produces one protein in the liver and another in the eye purely by context-dependent reading; a single-base frameshift; a synonymous substitution that changes nothing in the protein but changes expression level; a pseudogene that is never transcribed in any tissue of any individual; a sequence assembled by ancestral reconstruction that no organism has ever contained but which works when synthesized; and the genome of an RNA virus that can only be read by machinery it did not build.

The leaves must include, at minimum: strict code realism; a code-without-content view on which the mapping is arbitrary and receiver-relative but nothing is represented, so the technical sense of the genetic code is not the semantic sense of a message; a causal-specificity view on which information is nothing over and above fine-grained counterfactual dependence of product on sequence; a sender–receiver or teleosemantic view on which DNA genuinely represents, mutations can misrepresent, and content is fixed by selection on the reading machinery; a truth-requiring view in the Dretskean tradition, together with what it makes of mutation as supposed false information; a developmental-systems deflationism on which DNA is one causally privileged input among equals and code talk smuggles in an asymmetry the causal facts do not support; a code-biology view on which the genetic code is only the first of many genuine biological codes — splicing, signal transduction, cell adhesion, and the rest — so the live question is how many codes there are rather than whether there is one; a pure Shannon view on which bit counts are measurable and meaning-talk is not; and a template view on which replication by complementarity makes DNA a mould rather than a code, which I suspect is a historical or unoccupied cell. Name each leaf where a name exists and write unnamed where no one defends it.

After the tree, lay out the logical space explicitly as combinations of the three relations with two further switches — whether misrepresentation is possible at all, and whether the causal-parity objection is accepted — and locate every leaf in it. Then list the unoccupied combinations with a one-line diagnosis of each: is nobody there because the view is incoherent, because it collapses into a neighbouring leaf under pressure, because it is simply unattractive, or because it is coherent and interesting and nobody has written it down. Flag every pair of leaves your questions cannot actually separate, and say what further case would separate them, if any exists. Finally give a compact key mapping answer strings to leaves, so I can run it on paper.

Please do not include citations, a bibliography, or a reading list; the handout carries those separately, and I want the instrument to stand on the cases alone.

End with a single line in the form \answer{leaves=N; unoccupied=M; inseparable=K}, where N is the number of distinct reachable leaves, M is the number of unoccupied cells you listed, and K is the number of leaf pairs you flagged as inseparable.
