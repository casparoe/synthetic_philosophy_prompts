So I just finished a year-long data migration and I've ended up with a question I can't figure out how to google. For context I'm a backend engineer, never taken a philosophy class, so this may be a completely well-known thing with an obvious name that I just don't have.

We moved ~20-some years of operational history (tickets, incident reports, deploy notes, old wiki pages) into a new system, and at the end I got asked to write up a short history of how the platform evolved. Trying to do that, I kept hitting the same wall: the record isn't a sample of what happened, it's a sample of what the recording system happened to notice, and the recording system has its own history that has nothing to do with the thing I'm studying.

Concretely:

- Ticket volume roughly triples between 2014 and 2016. Anyone who was there will tell you that was actually one of the calmest stretches in the company's life. It's just that a new ops manager started requiring a ticket for everything, including stuff that used to be a hallway conversation.
- Everything before 2009 is nearly blank. Not because nothing happened — a wiki got lost in a migration that nobody wrote down, because writing it down wasn't anybody's job at the time.
- The two people who actually understood the build system kept their notes in personal text files and left in 2013. The largest single body of operational knowledge in the company's history is a hole with a couple of names attached to it.
- Incidents get documented in agonizing detail. The hundreds of times someone quietly papered over a problem so it never became an incident leave no trace whatsoever.

And there's a pattern in who shows up at all. Anyone with standing to open a ticket exists in the record. Contractors, support staff, the offshore team — they did a big fraction of the actual work and appear mostly as initials in a comment thread, if even that.

So the history I can reconstruct is basically a portrait of the tooling and the management fashions that produced it, wearing the costume of a history of the platform.

Nearest things I know of, and none of them feel right: survivorship bias (that's about which units make it through a filter, not about the shape of the filter itself), the streetlight effect (that's about where I choose to look, and my problem is upstream of looking), and "absence of evidence is not evidence of absence" (true, but useless here — my problem is that the absence has a *shape* and that shape is informative about the recorder, not the recorded). I'm fairly sure statisticians have a formal notion for missingness that carries information about the missing values, and I'd bet historians and archivists have something too since they must run into this on every single project. But I don't know whether any of those is the same phenomenon or just one mechanism that produces it.

What I want isn't really a label. I want to know what the thing I'm describing *is*: what's the right unit of analysis, how is it different from selection bias in general, and where are its edges. Like — is a photograph that only exists because someone was paid to take it an instance, or is that too weak? Is a metric that stops being collected once it looks bad the same thing, or a different thing? If there's an established concept that covers it, tell me which and be honest about how well it actually fits versus how much of my description it leaves out. If nothing covers the whole thing, say so plainly and coin a term with a definition that would let me tell instances from non-instances. And tell me whether the who-has-standing-to-create-a-record part is a seperate phenomenon or the same one seen from a different angle, because that's the part I have the least handle on.
