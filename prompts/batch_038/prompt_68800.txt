I'm a second-year grad student and for the last few months I've been pretty convinced by disjunctivism about hallucination — roughly the Hinton / McDowell / Martin line: there's no interesting mental kind that a veridical perception and a subjectively matching hallucination both fall under, and the most you can say positively about the bad case is negative, i.e. that it's a state the subject can't tell apart from a perception of the relevant sort. I'd like you to play a philosopher of perception who's also genuinely read the vision-science and clinical literature, and stress-test the view for me. Please think carefully and brainstorm the candidate defeaters first, before ranking anything. Assume I won't be moved by generic complaints ("it ignores the science", "it's not naturalistic") — I want the specific states of the world that would make my view false, and the ones that would only make it expensive.

Here's what I've noticed on my own, and I want you to tell me which of these are real exposure and which I've misdiagnosed. (1) The screening-off worry: if indiscriminability really does all the explanatory work in the bad case, why doesn't it screen off the good case too, so that the common factor is back through the side door — my view would then just be a redescription of the highest-common-factor picture rather than a rival to it. (2) A friend in a vision lab tells me that a lot of Charles Bonnet patients keep partial insight, and some pick up on cues — the figures being cartoonishly small, or dressed absurdly — so they can learn to tell the hallucinations from real people in the room. But if that's right, then on a negative epistemic account the state stops being what I say it is once the subject's discriminatory powers improve, which makes the fundamental nature of a hallucination ability-relative in a way I find hard to swallow. (3) The argument from illusion proper: I'm happy to be a disjunctivist about hallucination while saying something else entirely about the bent stick in the water and the elliptical-looking coin. But is that stable? If I'm a hybrid, do I owe an account of what the stick-in-water case consists in, and does conceding a shared "way things look" there undercut the response I want to give to the illusion argument as a whole? (4) The perfect-match case: a neuroscientist stimulates cortex in a way that's molecule-for-molecule and signal-for-signal identical to the process that a veridical perception of a matching scene would produce. I want to say the resulting state is still not a perception, but its not being one now seems to be doing zero work — so what would have to be true for me to be wrong here, and is there anything that could confirm I'm right? (5) General psychological laws — adaptation, crowding, priming, the Müller-Lyer persisting after you've formed the belief that the lines are equal — look like they apply across both cases with no disjunctive qualification, which suggests a real common kind at some level of description even if not the level I care about.

For the deliverable: rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, and for each say what the world or the argument would have to look like. Then separate out what a committed disjunctivist would herself concede counts against her from what she'd dismiss as a category error, and say why the two lists differ. Tell me what my view predicts that a common-factor view doesn't — anything at all, including from the empirical side — and whether the absence of such predictions is itself a defeater. Finally, give me an honest verdict on unfalsifiability: it seems like I can always absorb a counterexample by saying the subject just can't tell the difference, which sounds like a move that could eat any evidence whatsoever. If that's right, is it a fatal problem, or is it what you'd expect from a thesis about which kinds are metaphysically fundamental? Please also flag any of my five worries above that you think isn't actually a worry, and where I've got the dialectic wrong. Don't pad it out, and don't give me a bibliography — I care about the reasoning, not the references.
