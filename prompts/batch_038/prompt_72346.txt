I cover science and ideas for a national newspaper. Within a fortnight last month two complaints reached the editor about the same running story: one reader objected that our long weekend feature had reduced the loss of a town's only hospital in a flood to a single unheeded warning, and another that our explainer had listed eleven contributing factors and left them unable to say what had actually happened. Both are fair. The paper has asked me to run a two-hour workshop for this year's graduate intake, plus two data journalists, on writing about how things came to pass — inquests, inquiries, disaster retrospectives, anniversary features. None has studied philosophy; all will be filing copy that makes causal claims within weeks. I have a whiteboard and no handouts.

I want a teaching plan: the order the components must go in, what genuinely depends on what, a rough timing skeleton, and where the conventional order of presentation would be wrong for this audience. Two bodies of material need placing. First the machinery — the difference between a factor being necessary, being sufficient, and making a difference on the occasion; Mackie's INUS formulation, which I suspect earns its acronym here; and the point that overdetermination in the strict sense, two or more factors each of which would have sufficed, is not the same thing as a long causal list and is much rarer than reporters assume. Second, selection — the distinction legal scholars draw between an abnormal intervention and a background of normal conditions, which is roughly how we single out "the cause" from a set of factors that are all real; and the sufficient-component-cause model epidemiologists draw as pies, which I think is the only picture a non-specialist will retain. Tell me whether both belong, and whether the pies deserve whiteboard time.

The plan should also test a hypothesis of mine rather than flatter it: that our trainees' errors are not conceptual confusions but artefacts of the form, since a narrative wants a protagonist and an English sentence wants a subject, so "the cause" gets written whether or not the evidence supports one. If that is right, the session should be taught at the level of sentence construction and story architecture. If it is wrong or only half right, say so, and say where the conceptual work has to carry the weight instead.

For the carrying case, state what a single scenario must have and must lack to do this job, then test my candidate against those criteria: an invented flood, deliberately not a real one, in which the defences were built to a forty-year-old standard, the maintenance backlog was genuine, the warning was issued but not acted on, the hospital's siting was approved by a different authority decades earlier, and the rainfall was exceptional. If it fails, propose a better case and say what it repairs.

Map the misconceptions and fix the point in the sequence at which each is headed off; some will need pre-empting before they become available. Mine are: that the cause is whatever made the largest difference to the death toll; that responsibility can be parcelled out in percentages summing to a hundred; that standing conditions are context rather than causes; that the root cause is simply the earliest thing in the chain; that genuine overdetermination leaves nobody to hold to account; and that naming more factors is automatically more honest than naming one. Add the ones I have missed.

For the check on understanding I do not want a quiz. Give me one written exercise to set in the room, what a passing answer looks like, and what the answer that sounds right but has quietly re-smuggled the single-cause frame looks like — I expect verb choice will be where that happens. Tell me finally what I should refuse to cover at this level, and how to declare that openly rather than let them discover the gaps.

Write in British English. Close with a brief note on how confident you are in the plan's load-bearing claims, especially any claim about causation the sequence quietly rests on, and single out the one thing you are least sure of — whether a point about the material or a prediction about the room — together with what would settle it.
