I'm two-thirds through a novel whose back half is six people sitting in a dying man's kitchen arguing about whether death is bad for the person who dies — the old Epicurus point, that whenever it's there, you aren't. Trouble is my drafts have all six saying some version of "it depends what you'd have missed," which reads like one voice with six haircuts.

So build me a decision tree of the real positions. Every branch node has to be phrased as something a person could answer mid-argument — does a harm have to be felt, does it have to happen at a particular time, is never-having-existed relevantly the same thing as no-longer-existing, is dying worse the earlier it comes or worse once you've built something up — no jargon, and no names or book titles, none of them have read any of it. Say six or seven nodes. The leaves should include at minimum: death is no harm to the dead at all; death is a misfortune nobody experiences; death is bad only as cancelled plans and desires; death is bad because it ruins the shape of a life that was going somewhere; and death is less of a catastrophe than we assume because the self isn't what we take it to be — plus whatever else falls out of the branches.

Then tell me which combinations of answers nobody actually holds, and for each whether that's because it's incoherent, unappealing, or just unexplored, because I want to hand one character a view no philosopher would sign up to and I need to know if I can write it without it falling apart. And flag any pair of leaves your tree cant really seperate — if two of my six are stuck in the same leaf I'd rather find out before the next draft than after.
