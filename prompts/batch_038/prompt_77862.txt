**The script (approximately 850 words)**

> Bayesian confirmation theory holds that the support which a body of evidence E lends to a hypothesis T is to be understood in terms of the conditional probability of T on E: E confirms T just in case P(T|E) exceeds P(T), and the degree of confirmation is measured by the size of that increment. Updating proceeds by conditionalisation: on acquiring E as evidence, an agent should move from her prior function P to the posterior P*(·) = P(·|E).
>
> The problem of old evidence, raised by Glymour, is that this machinery cannot accommodate cases in which the confirming evidence is already known before the hypothesis it confirms is formulated. The standard example is Einstein's general theory of relativity and the anomalous perihelion advance of Mercury. Astronomers had known since the middle of the nineteenth century that Mercury's perihelion advances by roughly 43 arc seconds per century more than Newtonian mechanics together with the perturbations due to the known planets allows. When Einstein showed in 1915 that his field equations entail precisely that figure, this was received as a striking confirmation of the theory. But the evidence was old: at the time the anomaly was measured, general relativity had not yet been formulated, so it could not have featured in anyone's probability space, and there was accordingly nothing on which an agent could conditionalise when the datum was first recorded.
>
> Formally, suppose the agent is certain of E, so that P(E) = 1. Then P(T|E) = P(T&E)/P(E) = P(T&E) = P(T), since P(T&E) cannot exceed P(T), and P(T&E) is at least P(T) + P(E) − 1, which here equals P(T). Conditionalising on the perihelion datum therefore leaves the agent's credence in relativity exactly where it was, even though intuitively the derivation of the 43 arc seconds ought to raise it substantially. The Bayesian appears unable to represent a confirmation that everyone agrees took place. That is worrying not merely as an isolated puzzle, because the hypothetico-deductive pattern — deriving an observed consequence from a hypothesis and thereby gaining support for it — is among the commonest forms of scientific reasoning there is. The underlying difficulty is that confirmation seems to depend on the surprisingness of the evidence: the lower P(E) was antecedently, the greater the boost to T. Where P(E) = 1 there is no room for surprise, and hence none for confirmation.
>
> Two responses suggest themselves.
>
> The first is Jeffrey conditionalisation. This is the generalisation of ordinary conditionalisation which applies when an agent's evidence is not certain: where a partition {E₁, …, Eₙ} shifts from prior probabilities P(Eᵢ) to new probabilities P*(Eᵢ), the agent should set P*(A) = Σᵢ P(A|Eᵢ) P*(Eᵢ) for every proposition A. This is ordinarily presented as the standard Bayesian solution to the problem of old evidence, because it permits the probability of the perihelion datum itself to be revised rather than held at 1, and so restores the connection between the surprisingness of E and the increment in P(T).
>
> The second is Howson's proposal, which attacks a different assumption: the requirement of total evidence, according to which an agent must conditionalise on everything she knows. Howson suggests that confirmation by E be assessed relative to a counterfactual background in which E has itself been excised, so that the relevant quantity is P(T | E, K − E) rather than P(T | E, K). Against that reduced background E is no longer certain, the surprise intuition is restored, and the derivation of the 43 arc seconds can register as a genuine boost. The difficulty is that this leaves the agent holding two sets of probabilities at once: her actual credences, in which P(E) = 1, and the counterfactual ones used for the assessment. The requirement of total evidence is not an optional extra but follows from the coherence constraints which make credences probabilistic at all, and an agent who assigns to E a probability below the certainty her own evidence warrants has an incoherent credence function and is exposed to a Dutch book. Howson's proposal cannot therefore succeed, since it is available only to an agent prepared to be irrational.
>
> A third line, due to Garber, treats the newly discovered entailment relation itself — the proposition that T entails E — as the new information on which the agent conditionalises. This handles cases such as Einstein's, where the derivation was a non-trivial achievement in its own right, but it says nothing about cases in which the agent already knew that T entailed E and yet still regards the old datum as confirming.
