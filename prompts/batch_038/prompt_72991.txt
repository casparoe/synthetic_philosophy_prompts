QUESTION 3 (25 marks, about 45 minutes)

Sen's liberal paradox. Consider a society of two individuals, 1 and 2, and three alternatives: x, y and z. Concretely, there is one indivisible item — a single copy of a book, say — that either individual may take up or that may be left alone. x is 1 taking it up, y is 2 taking it up, z is its being left alone. A social welfare function assigns a social ordering over {x, y, z} to each profile of individual orderings; an ordering is a complete and transitive ranking. The following conditions are imposed on the SWF.

U (unrestricted domain): the SWF is defined for every logically possible profile of individual orderings over {x, y, z}.

WP (weak Pareto): if both individuals strictly prefer a to b, the social ordering strictly prefers a to b.

ML (minimal liberalism): 1 is decisive over the pair (x, z), and 2 is decisive over the pair (y, z). That is, the social ordering ranks x above z if and only if 1 does, and ranks y above z if and only if 2 does.

Now suppose the actual profile is: 1 ranks y above x above z; 2 ranks z above y above x.

(a) [8] Show that on this profile no social ordering satisfies U, WP and ML together, and say precisely which step generates the contradiction.

(b) [7] Does the result establish that individual liberty and Pareto efficiency are incompatible? Explain what the theorem does and does not license.

(c) [10] Set out two responses to the paradox, and say which you find more convincing and why.

CANDIDATE'S ANSWER

(a) 1 is decisive over (x, z) and 1 ranks x above z, so the social ordering must rank x above z. 2 is decisive over (y, z) and 2 ranks z above y, so the social ordering must rank z above y. A social ordering is transitive, so x above z above y gives x above y. But both individuals rank y above x, so Pareto requires the social ordering to rank y above x. That is a straight contradiction: the social ordering would have to put x above y and y above x at once. So no ordering satisfies all three conditions on this profile, and since unrestricted domain requires the SWF to be defined on every profile, one such profile is enough to kill it — there is no SWF at all.

Where the contradiction actually comes from is worth being careful about, because neither right says anything about the pair (x, y). That pair lies outside both personal spheres. What carries the social ranking into (x, y) is transitivity: two local decisiveness claims get chained into a global statement about a pair nobody has any right over. So the damage is done by insisting that liberty be written as a constraint on a ranking of social states, which then has to be transitive. Also, the Pareto step is doing more work than it looks: y comes out above x because both individuals rank y at least as high as x with at least one of them strict, which is the strong Pareto principle, not the weak one. So the theorem as proved needs strong Pareto; on a version that assumed only weak Pareto the contradiction wouldnt go through, which matters because weak Pareto is the condition everyone assumes is beyond dispute.

(b) Not in general, and its worth stating the scope precisely. The contradiction needs a profile in which each person's preferences reach into the other's protected sphere — 1 cares about whether 2 reads, 2 cares about whether 1 reads — plus unrestricted domain to guarantee such a profile is admissible. Nothing in the theorem says that a society which has rights and is Pareto-efficient is impossible for every profile; it says no SWF can be defined on all profiles. So the honest statement is that the three conditions are jointly unsatisfiable, not that liberty and efficiency conflict as such.

That said, the result is normally read as an attack on rights as trumps, and I think rightly. Minimal liberalism is just the social-choice version of a side constraint: there are pairs over which the planner may not override the individual, whatever the balance of welfare turns out to be. Sen has shown that a planner who respects constraints of that kind cannot also rank states by Pareto, so the theorem is a substantive problem for the trumps picture rather than a technical curiosity — you cannot have rights-as-constraints and efficiency together, and the liberal has to say which of the two she is giving up.

(c) First response: waiving. The paradox bites only because each individual's decisiveness is unconditional — 1's preference settles (x, z) no matter what anyone else prefers about anything. If individuals can waive their rights, that is, decline to exercise them precisely in those cases where exercising them would generate the conflict, then rights can be assigned in a way that is consistent with Pareto. Sen's reply is that the waiving has to be built in as a restriction on which preference profiles are admissible, and then you owe an account of why the excluded preferences are inadmissible; ruling out meddlesome preferences by fiat looks like exactly the preference-shaping a liberal should be embarrassed by. I find this response the more convincing of the two, mainly because it treats unrestricted domain as a real assumption to be argued about rather than one to be assumed away, tho it does leave the liberal needing a positive story about which preferences count as non-meddlesome.

Second response: drop the ranking formulation. If rights are described as game forms — 1 chooses within his sphere, 2 within hers, and the social outcome is whatever the two choices produce — then there is no social ordering around to be intransitive, and the contradiction simply fails to arise. The paradox on this reading was an artefact of trying to represent liberty as a constraint on a social preference ordering. That is attractive but it changes the subject a bit, because a lot of what we want from a theory of justice is a way of ranking states of affairs, and the game-form account does not obviously supply one.

On the attribution: the title of the result tells you which condition Sen himself thinks has to go. The theorem is the impossibility of a Paretian liberal — the liberal survives and the Paretian is what gets dropped — and since Sen plainly wants to keep liberalism, the natural reading is that he takes the paradox to show Pareto-efficiency cannot serve as a condition on social evaluation at all.

On balance Id say the paradox is best read as a lesson about how not to formalise rights rather than as a refutation of liberalism as a political ideal.
