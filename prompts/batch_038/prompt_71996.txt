I lead a small reading group at my local library — mostly retirees, plus one guy who used to be a prosecutor — and in a couple weeks we're doing Herodotus on Helen, Book 2, chapters 116 and 120, in Godley's old translation. I've been going around in circles on this for like a week and I could really use some help understanding what he's actually doing argumentatively.

Here's the first bit, 2.116:

— "This, the priests said, was how Helen came to Proteus. And, in my opinion, Homer knew this story, too; but seeing that it was not so well suited to epic poetry as the tale of which he made use, he rejected it, showing that he knew it. This is apparent from the passage in the Iliad (and nowhere else does he return to the story) where he relates the wanderings of Alexander, and shows how he and Helen were carried off course, and wandered to, among other places, Sidon in Phoenicia. This is in the story of the Prowess of Diomedes, where the verses run as follows: 'There were the robes, all embroidered, / The work of women of Sidon, whom godlike Alexandrus himself / Brought from Sidon, crossing the broad sea, / The same voyage on which he brought back Helen of noble descent.'"

—he also tosses in two bits from the Odyssey, one about Helen's drugs coming from an Egyptian woman and one where Menelaus says the gods held him up in Egypt — and then wraps up:

— "In these verses the poet shows that he knew of Alexander's wanderings to Egypt; for Syria borders on Egypt, and the Phoenicians, to whom Sidon belongs, dwell in Syria."

Then four chapters later, 2.120:

— "The Egyptians' priests said this, and I myself believe their story about Helen, for I reason thus: had Helen been in Ilion, then with or without the will of Alexandrus she would have been given back to the Greeks. ... Even if it were conceded that they were so inclined in the first days, yet when not only many of the Trojans were slain in fighting against the Greeks, but Priam himself lost to death two or three or even more of his sons in every battle (if the poets are to be believed), in this turn of events, had Helen been Priam's own wife, I cannot but think that he would have restored her to the Greeks, if by so doing he could escape from the evils besetting him."

So, where I'm stuck:

1. In 116 the logic seems to be that Homer left the Egypt story out on purpose, and deliberately leaving something out proves you'd heard of it. Fine. But doesn't that only establish that the story was already circulating in Greece — not that it's true? Does Herodotus keep those two separate, or does he slide from "Homer knew the tale" to "the tale happened"?

2. Which of the quoted lines is doing the actual work? The Iliad bit only gets Paris as far as Sidon, and then he needs the geography claim (Syria borders Egypt, Sidon is in Syria) to reach Egypt at all. That feels like a huge jump, since Sidon is also just on the way to Troy. Are the two Odyssey bits carrying the argument instead, and if so why lead with the Iliad one? And what about the throwaway "nowhere else does he return to the story" — is that an argument from silence, and is it a different kind of silence argument than usual, since it's about distribution inside one poem rather than about a missing source?

3. Then in 120 he switches completely: no textual evidence at all, just "Priam woudn't have been that stupid." My prosecutor friend is going to say this is the stronger of the two arguments by a mile and the Homer stuff is decoration. I don't buy it but I can't say why — can you steelman both sides for me?

4. The part that really has me stuck: in 116 Homer is a witness you can mine for what he knew, and in 120 he's hedged with "if the poets are to be believed." Is that just a double standard, or is there a principled line — something like, a poet is unreliable about the things he has a reason to inflate but reliable about details that got in on their own, incidentally, like where the embroidered robes came from? If there's a line like that, do historians still trade on it, and roughly what would you call it?
