I need help with something that isn't philosophy but is turning out to be considerably harder than philosophy.

A former student of mine is getting married in ten days and her mother has asked me to give the toast at the recieption. About 140 people: her family, which is large and churchgoing and has known her since she was born; his family, which is large and not churchgoing; four of my colleagues, two of whom work on Russell and will notice everything; and one great-aunt who will be asleep by the time I stand up but must not be offended by anything I've said before that.

Here's the problem. I've spent twenty years on Russell's book on marriage and morals and on the fights it caused in the late twenties and thirties. So I know better than most what he'd make of a wedding toast, and it turns out that almost every stock move in the genre is something I think is simply false. I don't want to spend four minutes saying things I'd have to retract in my own seminar on Monday.

Things I can't say, and won't: that these two were destined or made for each other or that the universe arranged it; that marriage is by nature a permanent arrangement and the promise merely reports a fact about the next fifty years; that jealousy is the gauge of love (Russell thought the opposite and I agree with him, and I also agree with him that a passing attraction to someone else is entirely compatible with a serious love still being intact); that two people are half-creatures completing each other; and I can't lean on children, because they decided years ago not to have any, and at least three people at that reception will make a joke about it before the cake is cut. I don't want the toast to argue with those three people. I just refuse to hand them an opening.

And yet I have to make 140 people feel the weight of what those two did this afternoon. They stood up in front of everybody and promised each other permanence and exclusivity. I think Russell was right that you can't honestly promise a feeling, and right that a lot of what conventional marriage morality demands is a demand for a lie. But I don't think he was right that the promise is therefore empty or that it should be dropped — and I want the toast to actually hold that distinction, whatever it turns out to be, between promising a feeling and promising something else that a person can genuinely stand behind for decades. So: work out what that something else is, and build the whole speech on it. That's the load-bearing wall. I don't want a general celebration of love with a philosophical garnish.

Constraints, and they're real ones:

- Four minutes spoken, so roughly 550 to 600 words.
- Funny at least twice, warm throughout, and it has to land on people who have never read anything.
- Russell gets named at most once, and in such a way that her grandmother hears it as a compliment to the couple while my two colleagues hear an actual reference to the view rather than a bumper-sticker version of it. Ideally that one line does both jobs simultaneously.
- No lecturing, no "as a philosopher," no mention of trial marriage under any circumstances, and don't turn the decision about children into a cause or a defence of it. It should just be a fact about them that the speech is comfortable with.
- It should include something specific and a bit particular about them. Invent two or three such details (how they met, something one of them does, an object in their flat) and mark them clearly at the end so I can swap in the true versions.
- It must be deliverable by me without wincing at any sentence.

Then, separately and briefly, give me a note on the two or three places where you came closest to asserting something I don't actually believe — the places where the genre is quietly smuggling in destiny, permanence-as-fact, or the children thing — so I can check them myself before I print it out. Be blunt about those; I'd rather catch it now than hear it from a colleague in the car park.
