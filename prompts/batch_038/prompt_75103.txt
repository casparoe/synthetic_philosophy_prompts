I cover AI and the philosophy of science for a general-interest magazine and I'm working on a piece about what happens when the thing doing the modelling is inside the system it models. Can you write me a dialogue, maybe 900 words, set at a workshop, between two researchers arguing over one concrete case?

The case: a forecasting system publishes a single number, its estimate of next year's growth for a mid-sized economy. The number is front page news. Central banks, firms and households all react to it, and their reactions push actual growth toward whatever the system said — so the forecast partly causes the thing it forecasts.

One side is a control-theory type: this is just a feedback loop, put your own influence into the causal model, solve for the fixed point, publish the fixed point, done. Give them real answers about multiple or unstable fixed points, about approximate self-models being good enough for engineering purposes, and about why diagonalisation worries don't bite once you only need predictions that are right within some tolerance.

The other side thinks the case is different in kind: an agent can't hold a complete model of a world that already contains a complete model of the agent; predicting and intervening stop being cleanly seperable when the forecast is itself one of the levers; and publishing a number can function as a commitment device, which means the right decision theory here isn't the causal one.

No strawmen and no alternating monologues — they should pick up each other's actual moves and push back. And I want them to end up disagreeing about something sharp: whether the system can ever publish a number that's both accurate and not self-undermining.
