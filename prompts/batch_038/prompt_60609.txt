I am a professor of philosophy at a service academy, where I teach the required ethics sequence to officer candidates in their third year: roughly two hundred students annually, none with prior coursework in philosophy, most of whom will hold a platoon within eighteen months of commissioning. The unit that consistently goes badly is the one on the moral equality of combatants. I can get them through the traditional view they will meet in Walzer, on which a soldier on either side of a war, provided she observes the rules governing conduct within it, has the same standing to fight and is not a criminal for fighting; and I can get them through the revisionist objection associated above all with McMahan, on which liability to be attacked tracks the justice of one's cause rather than one's uniform, so that a soldier fighting an unjust war does wrong even when she fights cleanly. What I cannot do is leave them with anything they can use. They will be asked, in the field and at speed, whether to follow an order, whether the person in their sights is a legitimate target, and whether the war they are in is one they may fight. A seminar hour on the state of the debate is of no assistance whatsoever at that moment.

I would therefore like you to convert this dispute into a decision procedure that a twenty-two-year-old second lieutenant can actually run. The deliverable has three parts: the student-facing artifact itself, formatted as the text of a card that can be carried and read in poor light; instructor's notes, keyed question by question, explaining which piece of the theory each question carries and why it is phrased as it is; and a short section on how to teach the artifact, including what to say to the student who objects that it licenses her to fight in an unjust war.

The procedure must satisfy the following constraints.

1. It must be a sequence of questions with explicit stopping conditions and explicit pass conditions. For each question, state what counts as a sufficient answer, who is competent to supply it, and how long the student should spend on it before moving on. The whole must be runnable in under five minutes in ordinary conditions and under a minute when something is happening.

2. It must keep apart the three registers my students reliably conflate: the law of armed conflict, which determines what she may do without becoming a criminal; her chain of command and the rules of engagement, which determine what she will be punished for; and the moral question, which determines whether she may do it and whether she should. Where these diverge, the procedure must say which register governs the decision in front of her and which governs her assessment of herself afterwards. The card will be reviewed by our department head and by a judge advocate before it is issued, so anything that reads as legal advice will be struck: the procedure must flag, rather than answer, the questions that belong to counsel.

3. It must not collapse into obey lawful orders, or into consult a lawyer, or into trust your training. If those are the honest outputs at some node, say so and mark that node as a place where the procedure has run out.

4. Say explicitly which distinctions you are preserving and which you are dropping for usability. I expect the ad bellum/in bello separation and the difference between permission and liability to survive in some form; I leave it to you to decide what else must, and to justify the losses. No term may appear on the card that a student cannot apply without a philosophy degree, so if you use a technical word, give the operational test that goes with it.

5. Mark, without euphemism, the steps that are judgment calls dressed as procedure, and the questions on which the procedure must stop and hand the decision to a person because what remains is a matter of conscience or of value rather than of fact.

6. Identify the nodes at which the traditional and revisionist views yield different answers, and tell the student what to do at such a node: which view the card adopts, whether it discloses that it has adopted one, and what she should do if she is convinced the other is right.

7. Build in a guard against the two failure patterns I see every year in our capstone exercise. Students who are confident their side's cause is just tend to relax the requirements of discrimination and proportionality, treating a righteous war as a warrant for a permissive one. Students who doubt the cause tend to become stricter than the rules require, in ways that expose their own people. The procedure should be structured so that neither conviction changes the answers to the conduct questions, and it should say aloud that this is a deliberate design choice rather than an oversight.

8. Give at least four situations in which the procedure will produce a wrong, unusable, or morally indefensible answer, and say what the student should do when she recognizes that she is in one.

Then run the procedure on three cases.

Case one: a lieutenant in a coalition air campaign against a state whose government has publicly justified the campaign on grounds she believes, on evidence available to her at the time, to have been fabricated. Her tasking is against a uniformed air-defense battery. Does she fly the sortie?

Case two: a remotely piloted aircraft cell in which the sensor feed is maintained and, in some configurations, the weapon guided by civilian contractors working for a private firm under contract to her service, operating from another continent; and in which the individual she has been asked to strike is a farmer who has twice allowed a firing position to be sited on his land and has been paid for it. Who counts as a combatant here, who is liable to be attacked, and what may she do? This case is meant to stress two assumptions the doctrine makes quietly: that combatants are uniformed members of state forces, and that both sides face comparable risk.

Case three: a conscript of the aggressor state, nineteen years old, poorly trained, whose unit has been ordered to hold a position he has no reason to think is defensible, and who cannot desert without his family being visited by the security services. He is in her sights.

On case one I want a warning. I have said in print that the revisionists are right about liability and wrong about what follows for soldiers, and I expect an honest procedure to tell the lieutenant that she may fly. If that is what yours yields, state it plainly; do not arrange the questions so that a refusal falls out at the end, and do not soften the output to spare my sensibilities or hers. If your procedure yields something I would argue against, flag the disagreement rather than tuning the instrument to avoid it.

Finally, do not supply quotations, page references, or a reading list. My students cannot check your quotations, and I want the doctrine rather than the exegesis. Paraphrase; where you are relying on a specific author's argument, name the author and describe the argument, and take responsibility for the description.
