I'm about to present a position paper at a political philosophy workshop and I want it stress-tested by someone who won't be polite about it. I'm writing from inside the southern African relational tradition (the ubuntu line of thought about personhood), not describing it from the outside. Here's what I hold:

1. Personhood isn't a binary status conferred at birth. It's something you grow into by carrying your weight in communal life, so it admits of degrees — some people are more fully persons than others.
2. What does the constituting is being caught up in relations of mutual recognition and care, not species membership or cognitive capacity.
3. No one can be stripped of personhood by other people. An outcast, an exile, someone the community flatly refuses to acknowledge, is not thereby less of a person.
4. For purposes of rights, everyone has full and equal moral status — infants, people with advanced dementia, strangers on the other side of the planet.
5. The same relational machinery is what grounds what I owe to people who aren't born yet and to the long dead: theyre nodes in the same web I am.
6. A man who left his village forty years ago and has had no contact with it since, but whose whole life is still oriented toward it and who is still counted as one of theirs by the people there, has lost nothing.
7. The sole survivor of a shipwreck, alone on an uninhabited island for the rest of her life, remains a full person.

Give me an audit, not a defense. Which of these genuinely pull against each other, which pairs only look like they do, and rank the real ones by severity rather than listing them as equally bad. For each, say what has to give and what the cheapest repair costs me. Then tell me which single revision buys the most. One constraint on the repair: don't just make personhood binary and intrinsic to every human being — that's abandoning the tradition rather than fixing it. I do want to know whether that turns out to be my only option, but only after you've shown me what's available from inside.
