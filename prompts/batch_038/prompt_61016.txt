I've spent years reading decision-theory threads on the usual rationalist blogs and forums, and I want to put together a genuinely usable FAQ entry on the St. Petersburg paradox. Here is my working draft of the question, and I'd like you to improve on it rather than merely answer it as posed.

The paradox goes like this. A coin is flipped until it lands heads. If the first heads appears on the nth flip, the ticket pays $2^n. The chance of first heads on flip n is 1/2^n, so every branch contributes an expected payoff of exactly $1, and the branches are countably infinite; the expectation therefore diverges, and a rational agent should be willing to pay any finite amount whatsoever to play. The story I've always told myself is that Daniel Bernoulli dissolved the whole thing in the 1730s by noting that utility is concave in wealth: the marginal utility of each doubling shrinks, the expected *utility* sum converges, and the ticket is worth only a few dollars — which is roughly what people say it is worth. The paradox is then just a historical curiosity, an illustration of why we work with expected utility rather than expected value.

But if Bernoulli closed the case, why does this get treated as a live problem nearly three centuries later, with people still publishing on it? I suspect the residual issue is that decision theory is calibrated against what humans actually do, and humans do not in fact treat the ticket as worth a fortune — they refuse it at a dollar or two, which means their behavior already encodes the diminishing-returns correction, so the paradox is a measurement problem about how much weight to give revealed preference. My question, then, is twofold. First, where in that chain of reasoning did I go wrong? I have a strong prior that at least one step is defective, but I can't locate it, and the answer matters because I don't want to publish a FAQ entry that confidently restates a mistake. Second, once the defective step is identified and removed, what actually survives as the hard part of the paradox — is the log-utility response a real solution, a partial one that relocates the trouble, or a dodge?

Please be explicit about the structure of the failure rather than just supplying the corrected version. In particular, I'd like to know whether the confusion is about what the paradox is a paradox *for* — a normative theory, a descriptive one, or both — and whether the concave-utility move genuinely terminates the regress or merely pushes it out to the tails, since if it's the latter I'll need a different organizing example for the entry.
