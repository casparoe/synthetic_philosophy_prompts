I am in the sixth form at a selective state school and, as part of my prefect duties, I run the homework club for Years 7 and 8 three evenings a week. Behaviour in the club is currently handled by a system the school has used for as long as anyone can remember: your name goes on the whiteboard if you talk or mess about, three names in a term means you lose your Saturday morning games, and a fourth means detention. Our Head of Year has asked the prefects to put forward a recommendation before half term on a proposal to replace all of this with a token scheme, in which good behaviour and completed prep earn points that can be exchanged for privileges — front of the dinner queue, extra time in the games hall, small items from a reward box. The prefect drafting the proposal is a friend of mine, and his argument is that children of that age respond to incentives and to nothing else, that expecting them to behave because it is the right thing to do is sentimental, and that a well-designed token economy simply makes the incentives legible. I have an objection to this, but at present it amounts to a feeling rather than an argument, and the Head of Year will not be moved by a feeling. I would like help turning it into one.

What I have actually observed is this. The club is quiet while I am standing at the front and disintegrates within about ninety seconds if I step out into the corridor. The Year 8s have learned to price their own misbehaviour: they ask each other how many names they can afford before Saturday goes, and one boy keeps a running tally on the back of his exercise book. Meanwhile, a pupil who is regularly in detention has become something of a celebrity within his house, so the sanction that was meant to disgrace him now advertises him. And several of the quieter ones, who do all the work set, have told me separately that they no longer read anything unless it is set — one said that books smell of being told off. I should add that the headmaster opens every assembly by saying that the point of the school is not to produce compliant children but people who do the right thing when nobody is looking. I have to write two sides of A4, and I keep returning to a question I cannot settle: whether there is any real difference between a boy who refrains because the price is too high and one who has come not to want to, and whether that difference is worth anything if the outward behaviour is identical. My friend would say the behaviour is all there is. My EPQ is on whether schools can teach character at all, which is part of why I have taken this so seriously, and I would rather not simply concede the point to him.

So: is his case as strong as it sounds, and if not, precisely where does it go wrong? I am not after behaviour-management tips, and I am not especially attached to the view that the token scheme must be scrapped — if you think some version of it could be defended, say so, and say on what terms. What I want is the principle. What is school discipline supposed to produce; is it the sort of thing that rewards and penalties can produce at all; and what follows for the club I run? Please also deal with one further claim of his that I find harder to answer, which is that Year 8s are not rational enough to be reasoned with, so explaining why the rules exist is a waste of everybody's evening. My instinct is that addressing them as though they were reasonable is part of what makes them so, but I cannot tell whether that is a genuine argument or merely something flattering that I would like to be true. Begin with your bottom line, stated in one sentence, and only then give the reasoning that supports it; please do not preface the answer, restate my question, or append a summary of what you have said.
