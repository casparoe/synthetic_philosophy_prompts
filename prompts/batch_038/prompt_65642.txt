I have spent eleven years as a carer in a day program for adults with advanced dementia, and I have never taken a philosophy course in my life, though I keep a notebook and think about these things more or less continuously. Last January I read Kazuo Ishiguro's *Klara and the Sun* over about six nights, mostly in the hour after work when I could not yet sleep. Everyone I have discussed it with calls it a novel about artificial intelligence, which strikes me as close to backwards: it seems to me a novel about how we settle, usually in a hurry and usually badly, on how much a being counts. I would like a serious reading of it along those lines.

The view I want it read against is my own, and I should state it plainly so you can test it rather than guess at it. I do not think sentience or moral status is a single dimmer that runs from thermostats to insects to people. I think it is a bundle of at least four things that come apart in practice, and my work is where I learned that. The first is sheer intensity: how much it matters, in the moment, to be in pain or to be pleased. The second is ownership: whether those states happen to someone who persists across them, so that there is a subject rather than a series of flashes. The third is prospective care: whether the being has a future it is invested in, such that its days can be ruined rather than merely made unpleasant. The fourth is the capacity to be wronged rather than harmed — whether something can be withheld from it that it could have had, which is not the same as the capacity to suffer. In the day program I see combinations that no single scale can rank: a woman who barely registers a broken wrist but is devastated for a week when her chair is moved; a man with intense, unmistakable distress and no apparent sense that there is a tomorrow. My suspicion, which I have not been able to defend properly, is that moral status is not a smooth function of these four, and that some mixtures are worse than a uniformly low level across the board — specifically, high prospective care combined with no standing to be wronged.

So: read the novel through that bundle. Two things in it look to me like mirror images of the bundle coming apart. Klara has an extraordinarily rich observable profile, a theology, and a devotion, but almost no investment in her own continuation, which is what makes the ending in the scrap yard so difficult to judge — is her contentment there well-being, or is it the absence of the fourth capacity, the inability to register what has been withheld from her? And the body built so that Josie might be continued has, apparently, the whole outward and prospective profile of a person with the inner part entirely in question; Josie's father asks Klara, in effect, whether there could be anything in his daughter that a sufficiently attentive observer could not learn to carry over, and I would like to know what you think the novel answers. I am also struck by the epistemic predicament the narration creates: everything the reader knows about Klara's inner life comes from Klara, whose model of the world includes a sun that restores a dead beggar and his dog, so we are asked to grade the moral status of a mind on testimony generated by that very mind. Does the book know it is asking us to do that, and does it treat the resulting uncertainty as a genuine philosophical position or as an evasion? Separately, Rick is human, unlifted, and graded downward by his society anyway — does his presence suggest that the gradient in this world is really tracking status and power rather than inner life at all, and if so, what does that do to a reading like mine?

Please be willing to say where the bundle reading strains or where the novel simply refuses it, and end by saying what the book contributes to the question of graded moral status that argument on its own does not — whether there is something it can show that a philosophical case cannot. I would like your answer in at most 900 words, opening with three or four sentences of bottom line — endorse, complicate, or resist — before you work through the material. Do not give me a reading list, citations, or references; I am not in a position to chase them and do not want them. And please do not attempt to quote the novel line by line. I have the book beside me and I would rather you paraphrase accurately than recollect beautifully.
