Two weeks from now I'm giving a talk at another department's metaphysics seminar — visiting, mid-sized group, mostly people who work on causation but from pretty different directions. I don't want a defense of my view. I want a Q&A prep sheet.

The thesis: I'm an eliminativist about omissive causation, but not the standard kind. When we say the gardener's failure to water the plant caused it to die, there's no absence in the world doing any causing. What's true is that an actual, occurrent process — water loss, wilting, cell collapse — ran to completion because a preventive mechanism that was *poised* never fired. And "poised" is meant to be a natural property, not a normative or pragmatic one: roughly, the mechanism was assembled, energized, and one small trigger away from intervening at the relevant time. Hose attached, tap already on, he's standing in the yard = poised. Me, on another continent, failing to water your plant = not poised, so my omission is no cause of anything, even though something like the counterfactual comes out true once you fill in the plane ticket. Bottle of water in the passerby's pocket = borderline, and I'm willing to bite that. So the view promises to kill negative facts entirely, to explain why omission talk is so selective without appealing to norms or speaker interest, and to still get a real difference between the gardner and me. I know the familiar dilemma is in the room's head already — McGrath's version, either there's no causation by omission or there's vastly more of it than common sense accepts — and poisedness is supposed to be my exit from the second horn, so expect them to test whether it actually gets me out.

The room. The host is a counterfactual theorist who is completely unbothered by absences; she thinks the whole problem is manufactured and there's nothing metaphysically special about positive causation. Two people work in the causal-models tradition: one thinks variable choice and the relativity of causal claims dissolve these puzzles, the other is more formal, works with default-valued models, and will want to know why I don't just take defaults as primitive. There's a process/conserved-quantity theorist who is also an eliminativist about omissions and will read my account as a surrender dressed up as a solution. A philosopher of action cares about the doing/allowing distinction and will want to know what's left of it if omissions are never causes. Someone from the experimental side of causal selection — norms, blame, that literature — will be there. A visitor from the law school works on factual causation in tort, especially liability for negligent omissions. Plus a couple of grad students, one of whom will just keep asking what the truthmakers are.

Give me the eight-ish objections I'll actually get, ordered by how much damage they'd do, each phrased the way that person would phrase it — the process theorist and the tort lawyer are not going to use the same words — with a reply I can deliver in about two minutes, what I should concede up front so I don't look like I'm digging in, and a note on where the reply is soft. Flag any objection on which the honest answer is that my view is just contextualism with extra steps. Then tell me the one reply that has to land, the thing that if I fumble it the whole talk is a loss. And end with the question I'm hoping nobody asks.
