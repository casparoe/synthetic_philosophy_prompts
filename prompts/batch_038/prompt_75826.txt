I assembled the following argument for a seminar on statistical fairness, and I am now convinced the conclusion is false without being able to locate the failure. The setting: a binary outcome Y (say, rearrest within two years), a risk score S taking values in [0,1], a decision D that flags an individual exactly when S exceeds a threshold, and two groups, a and b, where group a has the higher base rate of Y.

1. Sufficiency, or calibration within groups, holds when for every score value s and every group the proportion of people at s who have Y = 1 is s itself.
2. Separation, or equalized odds, holds when the decision rule's true-positive and false-positive rates are equal across the two groups.
3. The impossibility results establish that when base rates differ, no score that is less than perfectly accurate admits a threshold rule satisfying both sufficiency and separation.
4. In every dataset I actually care about the base rates differ, and they differ in part because the recorded outcome is itself a biased measure of the underlying conduct.
5. So any rule I deploy must abandon one of the two criteria.
6. If I keep calibration and abandon equalized odds, then a common threshold flags group a more often, and since calibration ties each score to the local rate of Y, the surplus flags land disproportionately on members of group a who would not have had Y = 1: their false-positive rate is the higher one.
7. That is exactly the harm that made the dispute over a widely used recidivism instrument a public controversy, namely that people from an already over-policed group are detained more often for conduct they did not commit. Insisting on calibration is therefore not a neutral technical preference but a decision to impose that harm.
8. If instead I keep equalized odds and abandon calibration, the scores cease to estimate probabilities, so I can no longer set thresholds by weighing the cost of a false positive against the cost of a false negative. My rule is not expected-cost-minimizing, and a decision-maker who cannot say what quantity his rule minimizes is not making a decision at all.
9. Therefore, whenever base rates are unequal, every available rule is either discriminatory, by 6 and 7, or irrational, by 8. Statistical fairness theory does not describe a hard trade-off; it shows that there is nothing acceptable left to choose.

I want to reject 9, and if I must sacrifice a premise I would rather give up 8 than 4, since the observation about the outcome variable being a biased proxy strikes me as both true and independent of the rest of the argument. Tell me which step actually fails and why it seemed acceptable in context. Then price the alternatives: for each other premise I might have dropped instead, what do I lose, and is the loss technical, legal, or moral? If 3 is a theorem rather than a premise, state what it rules out and what it leaves open, and say what has to be true of my setting for it to apply here. Finally, tell me whether there is a version of the argument that survives, and if so how much weaker its conclusion has to become.
