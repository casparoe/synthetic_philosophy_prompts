For the past two years I've been part of a small reading group that meets fortnightly in a member's living room: eight of us, two people with theology degrees, a retired actuary, and assorted amateurs, none of us professional philosophers. Last month we took up divine providence, and the session resolved almost entirely into a running argument between two members, whom I'll call Miriam and Paul, about the middle-knowledge solution — the claim that between God's knowledge of everything that could happen and his knowledge of the world that does happen there stands a third tier, his knowledge of what every possible free creature would freely do in any complete set of circumstances in which it might be placed, so that God governs history not by determining choices but by choosing which circumstances to create. I kept quiet and took notes, and I've reconstructed the exchange below as faithfully as I can. Neither of them is a pushover, which is exactly why I couldn't referee it.

(1) Miriam opened by saying the view buys something no rival does: providence is meticulous, since nothing falls outside the plan, yet no free act is caused by God, because the counterfactuals of creaturely freedom are true in advance of any decree and God simply reads them off and works around them. (2) Paul's first objection was that such counterfactuals cannot be true before anything decides them: there is no actual creature, no actual choice, and no actual set of circumstances, hence nothing in virtue of which it is the case that a merely possible person would freely refuse a merely possible offer; if they are true regardless, they are brute facts, and a brute, contingent, ungrounded fact is precisely what the position cannot afford. (3) Miriam replied that they are grounded in the creatures' own free choices, only counterfactually rather than actually, and that this constrains God no more than arithmetic does — God does not determine that seven is prime, yet nobody supposes his sovereignty is threatened by it — adding that God is perfectly free to create nothing at all, so he is not being handed a world he must swallow. (4) Paul said the analogy ruins the case instead of rescuing it: arithmetical truths are necessary, whereas she has insisted these counterfactuals are contingent, and a contingent truth with no actual ground is the very thing under suspicion; and as for declining to create, that concedes the deeper point, since whether God can bring about, say, a world of universal redemption depends on how the counterfactuals happen to fall, and they do not fall in any pattern he selects — the meticulous providence advertised in move (1) has quietly become a lottery God enters without controlling. (5) Miriam answered that this is simply the price of taking freedom seriously, and that it compares well with the rival on which God determines every act, for there God becomes the author of sin, whereas on her account he causes no evil and only permits what he infallibly knows will occur, selecting the best feasible balance of goods. (6) Paul's last move was that 'permit' is doing no work at all: if God knowingly actualizes circumstances in which he is certain, not merely confident, that particular people will sin and be lost, when other circumstances lay open to him in which they would not have, then the gap between causing and knowingly selecting collapses and the exculpation she wanted has evaporated. They agreed to disagree and went to tea.

Please adjudicate this move by move rather than in the aggregate. For each of the six numbered moves, tell me whether it is clearly right, partly right, or clearly mistaken, and justify the rating in a few sentences; in particular, say whether Paul's objection in (2) is best read as an attack on the coherence of middle knowledge or only on its providential usefulness, since I suspect Miriam answered a different question from the one he asked. Then identify which specific sub-claims each of them gets right and which they get wrong, say who has the better of the exchange overall and by how much, and name the options neither of them put on the table — the third and fourth positions a fuller treatment would have to weigh — along with what would have to be true for the disagreement in (5) and (6) to dissolve rather than merely persist.
