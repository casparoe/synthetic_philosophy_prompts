I am a postgraduate teaching assistant on a second-year ethics module, and on Thursday I run a fifty-minute seminar for fourteen students on a problem I have now twice fumbled when attempting to explain it in my own supervision hours: the heterogeneity problem for hedonism about well-being. As I understand it, and as I shall put it to the group, the worry is that the mental states hedonism nominates as the sole bearer of value — pleasures — do not appear to constitute a single kind, so that the theory's central claim may not, in the end, be a claim about anything determinate at all. My first constraint is tonal rather than doctrinal. We covered the experience machine last week, and roughly half the seminar left convinced that hedonism is straightforwardly false and mildly pleased with themselves about it; I do not want Thursday to register as a second refutation. I want the heterogeneity problem presented as a difficulty internal to the view, one that bites precisely because the theory is otherwise attractive and because a monistic account of what makes a life go well needs its single currency to be a genuine kind. The students have done a year of philosophy. They know that hedonism here is a theory of welfare rather than of right action; they have encountered Bentham's remark that, prejudice aside, a child's game is worth as much as poetry if it yields as much pleasure; and they have met Mill's distinction between higher and lower pleasures, which they will almost certainly arrive believing to be the answer to my question. What I want from you is a plan for the explanation, not the explanation itself. Please set out the order in which the components must be introduced and what makes each depend on the one before it. In particular, tell me what the group has to be holding in mind about the structure of a monistic welfare theory before a list of disparate pleasures can possibly trouble them, because my strong suspicion is that the standard opening move — ten pleasures up on the board, what do they have in common? — fails for exactly this reason: the question has not yet been made precise enough for there to be such a thing as a right answer to it, and so the exercise generates a list of synonyms rather than a problem.

Secondly, the carrying case. I have been considering the pleasure of a hot bath after coming in from the cold, but I fear it is a trap, since it invites the group to assimilate everything to a diffuse bodily feeling and thereby concedes the sensory picture I most want to resist. Assess that worry rather than merely noting it, and propose the example you would run the hour on instead or alongside it, saying explicitly what it must have and what it must lack. It has to survive a student who will insist, with some confidence, that the satisfaction of finishing a difficult proof is not a feeling at all; it must not be so exotic that we lose five minutes to the exoticism; and ideally it should let me generate at least two further pleasures in front of them rather than being a lone exhibit. Relatedly, give me a board plan — what is written up, in what sequence, and what is deliberately erased and why. I have one whiteboard, no slides, and a group that copies down whatever is visible, which means that anything left standing at the end will be treated by them as the conclusion. There is one further complication. Two of the fourteen have done introductory neuroscience and will want to announce that the question is already settled empirically, on the grounds that the systems underlying wanting are dissociable from those underlying liking. I need to know whether to admit that, and if so at which precise point in the sequence, so that it illuminates the issue rather than converting the seminar into a lecture on reward pathways; I am entirely willing to be told to refuse it, and if so I should like the sentence with which to do it without appearing defensive.

Thirdly, the misconception map, with the point in the sequence at which each item must be headed off rather than corrected afterwards. I can predict three. That the unifying property is simply that pleasures all feel good, which seems to me to restate the problem in the hedonist's own vocabulary rather than to answer it. That Mill's higher and lower distinction dissolves the difficulty, when my own view is that it buys unity by importing a value judgement that is not itself hedonic — a judgement, that is, about the worth of the activities in which the pleasures are taken. And that if no single feel unifies the class, hedonism has simply collapsed into the claim that what matters is getting what one wants, which appears to be a different theory with a different explanatory structure. Please tell me frankly whether any of these three diagnoses is mistaken, since I would rather be corrected now than in the room, and add the misconceptions I have not thought of, especially any that arise from the students having just spent a week on the experience machine. Then give me a single question to put to the group in the last five minutes that will distinguish those who have grasped the problem from those who have merely accepted my framing of it, together with a description of the answer that would reveal the second set; a short description of what a student who has genuinely understood should be able to do with a case I have not used in the seminar; and an explicit statement of what I should decline to engage with at this level, with a reason I can give out loud for declining it.
