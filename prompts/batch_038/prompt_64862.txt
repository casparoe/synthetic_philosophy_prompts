ok, I've been going in circles on this for a week and my study group is no help at all.

The view I'm trying to get a handle on is "lexical threshold" negative utilitarianism — roughly: suffering comes in kinds, and once it passes some threshold of intensity or duration it can't be outweighed by pleasure at all, so the first duty is to relieve threshold-level suffering, and promoting happiness only gets any weight once that's handled. Not "suffering counts for more than happiness, weight-wise." Literally: no finite amount of happiness buys off one instance of threshold agony.

I wrote out an argument against it and the conclusion is so strong I'm sure I screwed something up, but every step looks fine to me.

(1) For two things to be comparable there has to be an exchange rate between them: for any amount of one, some finite amount of the other that outweighs it.
(2) So if pleasure and pain are comparable, then for any pleasure there's a big enough pain that outweighs it, and vice versa — it's the same scale read in the other direction.
(3) Which means, if pleasure and pain sit on one scale, a life of unbroken agony can be redeemed by adding enough bliss. Tack a googolplex years of the best joy anyone could have onto the end of it and the agony was worth it.
(4) The lexical threshold view is committed to denying (3). That's the whole point of having a threshold.
(5) So by (1) and (2), the threshold theorist has to deny that pleasure and pain are commensurable at all — no common scale.
(6) But with no common scale you can't rank across the two. Given a world with lots of threshold agony plus enormous bliss, and a world with no agony and only modest wellbeing, the view can't say which is better. It especially can't say that relieving the agony matters more than adding the joy, since that ranking is itself a cross-scale comparison.
(7) So the view either drops (4) and turns into ordinary trade-off utilitarianism, where everything's for sale at some price, or it can't state what it actually wants. Either way there's no there there.

I want to keep (4) more than anything else here — the googolplex business in (3) is exactly what drove me to this view in the first place, and I'm not giving it up to rescue my own arguement. And (1) feels non-negotiable to me: if there's no exchange rate, what could "better than" even mean? My hunch is the trouble has to be in (2), that "vice versa" move. But I can't see why it would fail — an exchange rate is an exchange rate, isn't it?

So: which step do you reject, why did it look fine when you first read it, and what do you have to pay by rejecting it? And separately — does the diagnosis come out the same if I run the argument on the dust-specks-versus-torture case instead of the googolplex one, where the trade is between how many people are affected and how badly, rather than between pleasure and pain?
