I am writing a novel whose protagonist paints in the manner of a dead modernist and sells the results as lost works, and the art historian who eventually exposes him keeps reaching for a single comparison: a forgery is a counterfeit. On his account a forged canvas stands to the art world exactly as a false banknote stands to a currency system — an unauthorized token of an arrangement that confers all of its value — so that when the deception is detected the value does not merely decline but evaporates, and whatever pleasure the picture gave beforehand is exposed as having been borrowed all along from a belief that happened to be untrue. He uses this to tell my protagonist that he never made anything at all, and I want to know whether the charge is sound before I let it stand in the finished book.

Please audit the comparison. Give me the mapping explicitly — what corresponds to the issuing authority, to legal tender, to the act of detection, to withdrawal from circulation, to the innocent person who passed the note along — and then separate the disanalogies that genuinely damage his conclusion from those that are merely decorative. I am especially interested in what the comparison quietly assumes about where a picture's value resides, and whether the currency case licenses any such assumption at all. Finally, tell me whether his claim survives in the weaker form that a forgery is aesthetically inferior to the original it imitates, or whether that weaker claim also depends on the counterfeit picture; and if there is a better comparison I should put in his mouth instead, describe what it would have to capture.
