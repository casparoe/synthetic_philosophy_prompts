I co-direct a graduate seminar in the metaphysics of quantum theory with a colleague in our physics department, and for several years we have opened the term by exchanging written credences on one precisely formulated question, which we then hand to the students as an exercise in locating disagreement. This year's question concerns the reality of the quantum state. For the purposes of this exercise, please adopt the standpoint of a philosopher of physics with a working command of the ψ-ontology literature: the Harrigan–Spekkens distinction between ψ-ontic and ψ-epistemic models, the Pusey–Barrett–Rudolph theorem and its preparation-independence assumption, the explicit qubit construction by Lewis, Jennings, Barrett and Rudolph showing that some such additional assumption is unavoidable if ψ-epistemic models are to be ruled out, and the single-photon experiments that place quantitative bounds on how much of the indistinguishability of non-orthogonal states a knowledge interpretation can absorb. You have no access to that literature and must not attempt to supply it: no bibliography, no page numbers, no quotations, no reference list, and no hedging along the lines of "one would have to check." Where you are uncertain about a technical detail, flag the uncertainty in one clause and reason from the structure of the argument instead. Begin, before committing to any number, with a short ungraded brainstorming pass of four to six lines sketching the escape routes open to a defender of the epistemic view, since I want to see which routes you consider live and which you consider closed before you quantify anything.

The proposition, which I shall call P, is this: *by 31 December 2045, a model will have been published — in a refereed venue or on a recognized preprint server — that (i) reproduces all operational predictions of standard quantum mechanics for finite-dimensional systems of Hilbert-space dimension at least three, (ii) is ψ-epistemic in the Harrigan–Spekkens sense, meaning that the probability distributions over ontic states corresponding to distinct pure quantum states genuinely overlap, and (iii) achieves (i) and (ii) without invoking backwards-in-time causation and without any superdeterministic conspiracy between preparation devices and measured systems.* Three features of P are deliberate and I want them respected. It is an existence claim about a construction rather than a claim about the world as such, so the truth of the wavefunction's ontic status is not identical to the falsity of P. Clause (iii) excludes the two routes that the photonic work leaves open, because I want retrocausality and superdeterminism handled as separate terms in your decomposition rather than silently absorbed into the first. And P is meant to be resolved by the judgement of the researchers who actually work on ψ-ontology theorems, not by a single enthusiast's announcement, which is itself a source of vagueness you should treat as such rather than paper over.

Your deliverable has five parts, and I would like them in this order. First, the brainstorming pass. Second, a decomposition of your credence in P into at least six named terms, each with a probability in [0,1] and one or two sentences of justification; at a minimum I expect a term for whether preparation independence fails in the actual world, a term for whether a dimension-three-or-higher ψ-epistemic model is constructible at all in principle, a term for whether the escape routes that do work are confined to the retrocausal and superdeterministic families excluded by clause (iii), a term for whether clause (ii) can be satisfied only trivially — by inflating the ontic state space, for instance letting the ontic state carry a copy of the prepared quantum state together with an independent randomiser so that overlap is purchased cheaply and the label does no explanatory work — and whether any non-gerrymandered further condition could rule such constructions out, a term for whether P is sharp enough that competent people would converge on a yes or no rather than argue about the criteria, and a sociological term for whether enough researchers remain committed to the program to produce such a model within the window. Third, the combination: state explicitly which functional form you use — plain multiplication is rarely defensible here — and say which terms you regard as approximately independent, which you regard as strongly positively or negatively correlated, and why; a weighted or noisy-OR style combination is welcome provided you defend the weights. Fourth, a sensitivity analysis: identify the single term to which your final number is most sensitive, recompute P under the two extreme assignments of that term, report the size of the swing, and say what concrete development — a theorem, an experiment, a construction — would move you most and in which direction. Fifth, a diagnosis of disagreement: my own credence in P is 0.15 and my colleague's is 0.55, and tell me which of your terms she and I most likely differ on, and whether you judge the difference to be about the physics or about what would count as an answer. Please think carefully, and do not give me "it depends on which interpretation one adopts" — the whole point of the exercise is that the dependence is supposed to be priced in. Your final line must contain nothing else but \answer{p}, where p is your credence in P expressed as a decimal to two places.
