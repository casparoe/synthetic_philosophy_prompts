I am co-teaching a graduate seminar on the philosophy of biology and its representation in cinema, and next week we are screening Kubrick's 2001: A Space Odyssey (1968). My co-instructor objects to the choice. Her argument is that the monolith is a skyhook in precisely the sense Dennett gives the term in Darwin's Dangerous Idea (1995): a lifter that delivers design — first tool use, then spaceflight, then a posthuman transformation — while withholding any account of the process that produced the beings who built it. On her view, screening the film in this course risks staging, and glamorizing, the very inference pattern the course exists to expose. My own hunch is that the objection is too quick, and that whether the film traffics in skyhooks turns on ambiguities it deliberately leaves unresolved: whether the monolith's makers are themselves products of gradual descent; whether the monolith accelerates an already-directed process rather than originating novelty; whether the film's refusal to explain is a claim about explanation or a stylistic decision with no philosophical content at all.

I would like a reading of the film through the crane/skyhook distinction — and, where it is genuinely relevant, through the related charge of greedy reductionism — that adjudicates this disagreement rather than restating it. Please take the film's architecture seriously as you go: the Dawn of Man sequence, the untitled lunar section built around the buried monolith, the Jupiter Mission section and HAL's unraveling, and the final Jupiter and Beyond the Infinite sequence ending in the Star Child, together with the famous cut from a thrown bone to an object in orbit. Which stretches of the film tell a crane story, which install a skyhook, and where does the film pass from one to the other without marking the transition? Does the bone-to-orbit cut compress an argument about continuity that the rest of the film refuses to make explicit, or does it smuggle a lift into a montage? And where does HAL sit on this ledger — is he a crane that works too well, a case the vocabulary simply was not built to handle, or something that reaches for a skyhook on his own account?

I also want you to stress-test a distinction I have been defending against my co-instructor. Call a lifter a licensed skyhook when a work openly displays the lift as unexplained and asks the audience to accept it on aesthetic rather than evidential grounds — the audience is not being deceived about the absence of a mechanism, because the absence is the point. Is that a genuine third category alongside cranes and skyhooks, with its own conditions of legitimacy, or is it a way of laundering skyhook-thinking by attaching a disclaimer to it? Say which version of the film, if any, this description fits.

Relatedly, consider the difference between the film and Clarke's companion novel, which supplies an explicit history for the monolith's builders as an ancient species that evolved and then transcended. Does the novel convert the same material into a crane narrative? What does the comparison suggest about whether withholding explanation is a philosophical merit here or an evasion?

Please be candid about where the lens strains. The film is not making an argument about the origin of design, and I would rather be told which of my questions it simply does not engage than have them answered by charitable reconstruction. In particular, say whether describing the closing transformation as a skyhook already begs the question by assuming the film owes a mechanism.

Work from plot, structure, and the concepts as you understand them; I do not need verbatim quotation from the film or from Dennett, and you should not supply any. Close by saying what the film contributes to the debate over skyhook-thinking that a purely argumentative treatment cannot deliver. My suspicion is that it has to do with why such lifts are seductive rather than merely erroneous, and with what it costs a theory of explanation to account for that fact — but I want your account developed and defended, not mine repeated back.
