There is a dispute about the temporal fine structure of experience that I want to convert into a sorting instrument rather than a lecture. The question I care about is narrow: when a subject reports something that could not have been available to them at the moment they report experiencing it — a colour change seen in transit between two lamps, a tap felt at a point on the forearm where no tap was delivered, a stationary flash that appears to trail a moving object it was physically aligned with — is there nevertheless a determinate fact about what the subject's experience was like at that moment, a fact the report merely fails to track, or is the demand for such a fact already the mistake? I am preparing a handout for a graduate summer-school session, and I want participants sorted by what they say about cases, not by which camp they announce.

**What the tree may assume.** Take it as settled, shared by every participant, that the following phenomena are real and correctly described: (i) two spatially separated lamps flashing in alternation, with subjects reporting smooth motion between them and a colour change occurring somewhere along the path; (ii) a briefly presented target followed by a mask, with subjects reporting having seen nothing at the target's location; (iii) taps delivered at the wrist and then the elbow, producing reported taps at intervening points on the forearm; (iv) a flash physically aligned with a moving object, reported as lagging behind it; (v) cortical stimulation experiments in which subjects date the onset of an evoked sensation to a time earlier than the stimulation could plausibly have produced it.

**Three paradigms of my own.** Treat these as thought experiments on the same footing as the above; they are mine, not from the literature.
1. *Cue-delayed colour phi.* On half the trials subjects are told in advance which lamp's colour to report; on the other half, a tone issued after both lamps have gone dark indicates which to report. Suppose both groups give identical reports, including the in-transit colour change.
2. *Double report.* After a flash-lag trial the subject makes an immediate keypress localisation of the flash and, two seconds later, a freehand drawing of what they saw. Suppose the keypress places the flash in alignment while the drawing places it behind the moving object.
3. *Deferred narrative.* In a masking block, no response is collected on a subset of trials. At the end of the block the subject is asked to describe, in free narrative, everything they saw. Suppose the masked target turns up in that narrative.

**Deliverable.** A decision tree of ten to twelve nodes, each a yes/no question or a choice among two or three options, and seven to nine leaves.

- Every node must be answerable by someone who has never read the literature: phrase each in the vocabulary of the cases — lamps, taps, cues, reports, memory, the moment of the flash — and not in the vocabulary of the theories. No node may contain the name of a position, a philosopher, or a term of art belonging to one side of the dispute.
- At least two nodes must probe the difference between the order in which experiences occur and an experience whose content is an order: whether a subject can undergo "A then B" without there having been an experience of A followed by an experience of B.
- Each leaf carries a label (the standard name where one exists, a coinage where none does), a two-sentence statement of the view, and one sentence naming its most awkward commitment — the thing its defender would least like to have said aloud in seminar.
- The leaves must include, at minimum: a view on which there is a finish line in the stream and nothing downstream of it can be revised; a view on which the question of when the revision happened has no answer in cases like these; a view on which experience is determinate precisely because all the integration, extrapolation and inference is finished before experience begins; a view on which experience is determinate and then overwritten in memory, leaving sincere but unfaithful reports; a view on which the specious present is a sequence of bounded windows; a view on which every experience carries its own just-past and about-to-be within it; a view on which there was never any in-transit colour experience to have, and the subject is describing what they know about the display; and a view on which the phenomenology is authoritative and the paradigms, not the theory, are what fail.
- Flag every pair of leaves that your tree routes to different branches but that the participants' answers cannot in practice distinguish. For each such pair, say whether some further case would separate them or whether the two are equivalent by construction.
- List at least four complete answer-vectors that your tree permits and that correspond to no view anyone holds. For each, diagnose the vacancy: is the combination incoherent, merely unattractive, or simply unexplored?

Two further constraints. Nothing in the handout may require a reader to look anything up: no bibliography, no citations, no page references, no author-year attributions — naming positions and people is expected and fine. And close with four to six sentences identifying which single node does the most diagnostic work in the tree, and which node comes closest to restating the question at issue rather than dividing the answers to it.
