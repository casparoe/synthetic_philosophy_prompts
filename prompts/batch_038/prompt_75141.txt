In three weeks I am giving the closing talk at a one-day interdisciplinary workshop on probability and self-locating belief, and what I need from you is a proper question-period prep sheet rather than a defense of my view. My thesis is that the self-sampling assumption, restricted to the maximally specific reference class of observer-moments subjectively indistinguishable from one's own, should be preferred to the Dieks-style self-indication assumption; and further, that SIA's supposed advantage — immunity to reference-class choice — can be recovered inside SSA by always taking that maximally specific class, so that one keeps the anti-presumptuous-philosopher result without paying SIA's costs. My argument has three moves. First, SIA treats the bare fact of there being observers at all as though it were evidence one might have lacked, and thereby licenses an unrestricted indifference over merely possible observers that no Bayesian norm supports; the presumptuous philosopher result is not an embarrassing side effect of SIA but a symptom of that confusion, since it makes the relative confirmation of two theories depend on a number that can be inflated without changing any observable consequence. Second, the standard replies to the presumptuous philosopher — the procreation-asymmetry style response, and the response that says a suitable prior over cosmological theories defuses the trouble — either concede the point or reintroduce the very arbitrariness they were meant to remove. Third, the reference class problem is a parameter-setting problem, not a structural one: fix the class by the most specific description your total evidence supports and the sampling assumption does determinate work, yielding a real but modest and easily overridden Doomsday-style shift in one's estimate of total observer count.

The audience matters, because the same thesis draws very different fire from different corners of this room. There are about forty people: (i) two or three philosophers who work on self-locating belief and indifference principles, mostly thirders about Sleeping Beauty, who suspect that any sampling assumption owes a story about what counts as one's total evidence and that SSA quietly replaces the sample space instead of conditioning on anything at all; (ii) a string cosmologist working on the multiverse measure problem, impatient with anthropic reasoning in general, who will want to know whether anything I say bears on measures, on typicality assumptions, or on whether probabilities can be extracted from an ensemble at all; (iii) a decision theorist who will press the betting question — which odds would I actually accept, and is my view exposed to a Dutch book or to diachronic inconsistency once I learn where I sit in the sequence; (iv) a statistically trained graduate student who has worked through Gott's vaguer-prior derivation and thinks the whole enterprise is a base-rate fallacy dressed up in cosmology, and who will point out that sampling language with no randomizing mechanism is doing covert work; (v) a couple of historians and generalist philosophers of science who will ask whether my position is falsifiable, whether typicality assumptions of this kind are just the cosmological principle in new clothes, and what evidence could ever distinguish SSA from SIA. Assume all of them know the standard material and none of them need it explained.

Please give me the eight or nine objections I will actually get from this audience — not the ones a general readership or an online forum would raise — ordered by how much damage they would do if I handled them badly, and give the whole thing as a single table with these columns, in this order: rank (1 = most damaging); objector (which of the five groups above, plus the standing commitment that generates the objection); objection_in_their_words (one to three sentences, first person, in the idiom that person would actually use in the room, technical vocabulary included — do not phrase all of them the same way); likelihood (high, medium, or low, with a clause saying why); damage_if_unanswered (an integer from 1 to 5, plus a sentence naming exactly which part of my thesis it costs me if the objection lands); two_minute_reply (what I should say, tight enough to deliver aloud in about two minutes and specific to that objector rather than a generic restatement of my view); soft_spot (where that reply is weakest and what the objector will say straight back); and concede_or_hold (an explicit instruction on what to give up on the spot and what to refuse to surrender). After the table, in ordinary prose: (a) the one objection I am hoping nobody raises, why it is the most dangerous thing in the room, and the best answer available to me even if that answer is only partial; (b) the two or three concessions I should volunteer in my opening five minutes to take the oxygen out of the most predictable attacks; and (c) the single reply that has to land for the talk to survive. No bibliography, no reading list, no citations, and please do not invent numerical results — keep quantities qualitative and describe the relevant thought experiments in your own words rather than quoting them.
