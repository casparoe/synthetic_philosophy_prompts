I write about science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and I've got a piece in progress on the current vogue for "just universalize it" arguments — mostly in AI ethics, where people say things like "would you accept that maxim if every system ran on it?" The wrinkle my editor wants me to chase is that a lot of this work is now being done with actual survey data: researchers hand people maxim-shaped scenarios and report what fraction would be willing to universalize them. So the live claim is that Kant's test is a procedure you can run on a sample. I did what I always do, which is skip the thinkpieces and go read the source, and I got stuck in the preface of the Groundwork before I ever reached the test itself. I'm working off a free public-domain translation on Project Gutenberg (I think it's the 19th-century Abbott one, so the English is stiff and possibly mangled in spots).

The bits I can't get past, all from the preface, in order:

"Natural and moral philosophy, on the contrary, can each have their empirical part, since the former has to determine the laws of nature as an object of experience; the latter the laws of the human will, so far as it is affected by nature: the former, however, being laws according to which everything does happen; the latter, laws according to which everything ought to happen. Ethics, however, must also consider the conditions under which what ought to happen frequently does not."

"...we should always carefully separate the empirical from the rational part, and prefix to Physics proper (or empirical physics) a metaphysic of nature, and to practical anthropology a metaphysic of morals, which must be carefully cleared of everything empirical, so that we may know how much can be accomplished by pure reason in all its parts, and from what sources it draws this its a priori teaching..."

"As my concern here is with moral philosophy, I limit the question suggested to this: Whether it is not of the utmost necessity to construct a pure thing which is only empirical and which belongs to anthropology? for that such a philosophy must be possible is evident from the common idea of duty and of the moral laws."

"Everyone must admit that if a law is to have moral force, i.e., to be the basis of an obligation, it must carry with it absolute necessity; that, for example, the precept, "Thou shalt not lie," is not valid for men alone, as if other rational beings had no need to observe it; and so with all the other moral laws properly so called; that, therefore, the basis of obligation must not be sought in the nature of man, or in the circumstances of the world in which he is placed, but a priori simply in the conception of pure reason; and although any other precept which is founded on principles of mere experience may be in certain respects universal, yet in as far as it rests even in the least degree on an empirical basis, perhaps only as to a motive, such a precept, while it may be a practical rule, can never be called a moral law."

And then the heading of what comes next: "First Section—Transition from the Common Rational Knowledge of Morality to the Philosophical."

Can you help me understand this? Six things, roughly in order of how much they matter to the piece:

1. That last clause — "perhaps only as to a motive" — is the one that's really bugging me. If a precept gets disqualified as a moral law merely because its *motive* has an empirical element in it, what's left of a test that I run on my own actual maxim? My maxim already has an empirical end baked into it (I'm making a false promise *in order to get cash*, say). So is the empirical contamination supposed to be in the content of the maxim, or in the grounds on which I adopt the principle? Walk me through what a maxim looks like on each answer, because the two readings seem to give me very different tests.

2. What's the actual difference between a precept that is "in certain respects universal" and one that carries "absolute necessity"? I'd like a worked example from inside Kant's own machinery — something that's universal-ish but only a practical rule, not a moral law — and then tell me which side of that line the universalizability test lands on. Is the universality it tests for the across-all-cases kind or the across-all-rational-beings kind? Those sound like different demands and I can't tell which one he's cashing out.

3. The Ethics sentence — "must also consider the conditions under which what ought to happen frequently does not" — looks to me like it flatly contradicts the next paragraph, where everything empirical gets shipped off to practical anthropology. Is there a reading where both survive, e.g. where a priori ethics studies obstacles as such? And does that sentence give my survey people any cover at all, or is it about something else entirely?

4. The third quote reads like nonsense as printed: "a pure thing which is only empirical." Is there any reading on which that's deliberately paradoxical, or is this translation/the scan dropping words? If it's dropping words, what has to be supplied for the sentence to hook onto the "for that such a philosophy must be possible" that follows? Tell me how confident you are and flag clearly where you're reconstructing rather than reading.

5. The First Section heading has Kant starting from the "common rational knowledge of morality" that ordinary people have — but the preface just said the basis of obligation must not be sought "in the nature of man." Is common moral knowledge a fact about the humans who happen to have it, or is it knowledge any rational being would have regardless of species? I need this one because it's basically the hinge of my article: if it's the first, then asking a sample what they'd will is at least in the right neighborhood.

6. And the big one, where I'd rather be told I'm wrong than reassured: is there anything in these preface sentences that actually gets you to a *test*? I see necessity, I see purity, I see a demotion of experience — but I don't see a criterion of universalizability anywhere. Does the test inherit these preface requirements, or does it just sit next to them? If the honest answer is that nothing here yields the test, say so plainly and tell me what the preface is doing instead.
