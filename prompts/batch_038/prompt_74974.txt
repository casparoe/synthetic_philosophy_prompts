I'm putting the last touches on a chapter for an edited volume on autonomy and the law, and before I send it off I want a proper consistency check on my own position rather than a literature recap. So: act as a philosopher of technology and criminal-law theory who has read both the responsibility-gap debate and the Strawsonian stuff on blame, and who is willing to tell me straight up that I've built a house of cards. Take your time with it. It's fine to spend a paragraph brainstorming which pairs of commitments look like candidates for trouble before you commit to anything, but what I want at the end is an audit, not a survey.

Here's roughly where I've landed.

1. There are real responsibility gaps. For some harms caused by adaptive autonomous systems there is no person who is properly blamed, and the gap isn't merely epistemic -- not a matter of our not yet having located the culprit, but of there being nobody there to find.
2. Blameworthiness requires both a control condition and an epistemic condition, a fair opportunity to see and to steer. And the epistemic condition can't be discharged by foreseeability-of-type alone; it needs the right kind of connection to *this* harm.
3. That said, foreseeability isn't strictly required, because I accept tracing. The drunk driver who never anticipated anything can be blameworthy for the collision, since it flows from a prior culpable choice.
4. I'm a compatibilist about human agency: no contra-causal freedom, no deep metaphysical desert. I'm happy to say the justification of blame is substantially practice-dependent -- it earns its keep from the role it plays in holding one another to account, not from retributive fit.
5. And yet I insist the gap has moral bite. It's a genuine injustice when the victim of an autonomous-system harm gets paid out by an insurer or a compensation fund and nobody is ever answerable *to them*. Money doesn't close the gap.
6. I'm sympathetic to filling it forward-looking: someone -- a designer, a deployment authority, an institution -- ought to carry task-responsibility, and role-responsibility plus a standing duty to investigate and to explain is most of what we should want from policy.
7. Punishing the machine makes no sense, even in principle, and I don't think the prospect of machines that are apt targets of resentment changes my analysis. The gap survives full autonomy.
8. I also want to keep strict liability in tort as a perfectly sensible institutional response, plus something in the neighborhood of respondeat superior for deploying institutions.

My worry, which I can't quite pin down, is that some of these pull against each other and I don't know which. I have a nagging sense that (2) and (3) are a flat contradiction even though I've seen them cheerfully held by the same people, and a separate sense that (4), (5) and (6) can't all be as robust as I'm stating them. I take Sparrow's general point that you can't just pin things on whoever happens to sit causally upstream, but I'm not sure that's doing the work I think it is in (1). Please don't take my hunches about where the trouble is as data -- I'd rather be surprised.

So, deliverable: go pair by pair; tell me which tensions are real and which dissolve on inspection; rank the real ones by severity rather than listing them all as equally bad; and for each say what the cheapest revision is and what it would actually cost me -- do I lose the word injustice in (5), do I lose the deflationary compatibilism in (4), do I lose the word real in (1)? Then tell me which single change cleans up the most. Flag anywhere you think I'm sliding between senses of responsibility without noticing, since I suspect that's part of what's generating the noise. And don't hedge: commit to a diagnosis and a ranking, and mark your confidence on the one you think is most severe.

One last thing. If you think the whole framing is confused -- if responsibility gap is a term of art that stops doing any work once you've carefully separated blame, liability, and answerability, or if the chapter's project can't be executed the way I'm attempting it -- say that at the top, plainly, instead of politely auditing a broken frame. I'd far rather hear it from you now than from a referee in the spring.
