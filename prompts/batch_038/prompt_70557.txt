Two things collided this summer and I cant get them apart. I'm writing my honours thesis on moral status and spent July doing intake assessments at a farm sanctuary; the same month my nana moved into memory care and I've been the one signing her paperwork. Here's the argument I built back in April that I'm now scared of.

(1) What makes harming a being bad is how much there is to it — the richness of its experience, not the species it happens to belong to.
(2) Richness of experience tracks how much neural machinery a being has for integrating information: more capacity, more there is to lose when it's destroyed.
(3) I call this the endocast condition: a criterion of moral status only counts if you could apply it to a skull, a scan, or a jarred brain on a shelf — otherwise it's just a polite word for favouring our own kind.
(4) Brain mass, encephalization quotient and neuron counts all satisfy (3), so moral status turns out to be an empirical ranking.
(5) We don't top that ranking. A sperm whale's brain outweighs mine several times over, and elephants have more neurons than we do — mostly cerebellar, but neurons are neurons.
(6) So a whale or an elephant has more moral weight than a person, and killing one is the worse harm.
(7) The same scale applies within a species over time. My nana has lost a good chunk of her brain volume since 2019, so on (2) her weight has dropped accordingly, and it now sits below that of the sanctuary's border collie.
(8) And the scale aggregates across individuals: a hundred thousand farmed shrimp outweigh one orca, and a 20-week foetus outranks an adult mouse.
(9) (6), (7) and (8) are things I can't say out loud, let alone put in a thesis.
(10) Therefore (1) is false: experience isn't what grounds status, status doesn't come in degrees at all, and only something like rational agency or membership in a moral community does the work.

I'd much rather keep (1) — it's the whole reason I picked the topic — and (3) still feels like the honest bit. But (10) reads as monstrous, and it doesn't feel like a reductio of (1) so much as a reductio of something I did somewhere between (3) and (5). Which step should I drop, why did it look safe when I wrote it, and what exactly do I lose — for the whales and for my nana — once I drop it?
