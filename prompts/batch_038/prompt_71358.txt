At a talk last week a philosopher of mind offered an image for the split-brain patient that I have not been able to shake. Severing the corpus callosum, he said, is like cutting the only telephone line between two branch offices of the same firm: each office keeps its own records, keeps making its own local decisions, and carries on pretty much as before, so the surgery does not create two agencies, it merely reveals two that the line had been masking all along. He then leaned on the picture a second time. An auditor standing outside the building can still collect reports from both offices and compare them even though neither office can reach the other; in the same way, he said, the experimenter can query each hemisphere through its own visual field and its own hand, and so nothing has genuinely been divided from the point of view that matters. Consciousness, on his telling, is unified whenever it is jointly accessible from somewhere, whether or not it is accessible from within.

I would like a check on how much weight that image can bear rather than a summary of the case. Say which element of the office picture is meant to stand for which feature of the situation — the firm, the two offices, the records, the line, the auditor — and then where the correspondence gives out, separating the failures that actually threaten his two conclusions from the ones that merely make the image look clumsy. Two questions matter most to me. Does the office picture license the claim that there were always two subjects, given that an office's records and decisions are the sort of thing that can sit in two places at once, while a single stream of experience arguably is not? And does joint third-person accessibility capture what is at stake in the unity of consciousness, or is the auditor standing in for something the experiment does not actually supply? Please also note whether any of your objections depend on assuming the hemispheres share nothing at all after the surgery, since that assumption is itself disputed, and whether it should change. End by saying whether either conclusion survives, and if the analogy is doing more damage than work, offer a better one — or explain why the phenomenon resists being pictured this way at all.

Continuous prose only, please, as if you were writing me a letter: no headings, no bullet points, no bold, no tables. Give me your verdict in the first few sentences and the reasoning after it.
