I am co-enrolled this term in a health-policy seminar on priority setting, and last week our guest — an analyst who works for a national cost-effectiveness review body — used a phrase twice in ten minutes that I have not been able to stop thinking about: "a QALY is a QALY is a QALY." The first time, a student asked whether a year of healthy life restored to a young mother supporting three children should really count for the same as a year restored to someone with no dependents, and the analyst replied that the moment you start asking that you have stopped doing health economics, because a QALY is a QALY is a QALY. The second time, someone raised the older Robbinsian objection that interpersonal comparisons of utility are value judgments rather than statements of fact, and the analyst said that this is precisely what the QALY was designed to escape: unlike a util, a QALY is anchored at zero and one by survey-based public valuations of health states, so we never have to peer into anyone's experience at all — a QALY is a QALY is a QALY. Two of us argued afterwards about whether these two uses are even consistent with one another. My own hunch, which I cannot defend, is that the slogan is being made to carry a measurement claim in the second exchange and a distributive claim in the first, and that only one of those is a technical matter.

The case that produced the argument has invented numbers, to keep it clean. Programme A delivers 0.05 QALYs to each of 20,000 people living with chronic mild arthritis; Programme B delivers 4 QALYs to each of 250 people with a progressive rare disease who would otherwise die within the year. Costs are identical and so is the total QALY gain, so the review body's arithmetic is indifferent between them. One classmate insists that anyone who wants to attach a severity weight to Programme B is flatly rejecting "a QALY is a QALY"; another insists that weighting is entirely compatible with the slogan, because the slogan only fixes the unit of account and says nothing whatever about what you may do with the units once they have been counted. I suspect both are right about something, and that is my problem.

Could you lay out the distinct precise theses that this single sentence might be expressing, as a menu rather than one interpretation defended at length? For each reading I would like to know what kind of claim it is — about the construction and semantics of the measure, about the epistemic standing of comparisons between persons, about the moral weight of equal-sized health gains, or about the division of labour between analysts and political bodies — and whether it is a platitude nobody denies, a substantive and defensible thesis, a live controversy, or simply false. Crucially, tell me which of the readings would actually license each of the two dismissals my guest made, and which of the two questions those readings leave wide open. I would also like you to say whether anchoring a scale at death and full health by population survey does anything at all to answer the Robbinsian worry, or whether it merely supplies a common label for what remain person-relative orderings; and to tell me which of my two classmates is using "rejecting the slogan" in a sense the other would not recognise. Please organise this as a numbered list I can bring to the next session, and do not trouble to be even-handed — if some readings are far stronger than others, say so plainly.
