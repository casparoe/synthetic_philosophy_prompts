You are a philosopher equally at home in classical Indian epistemology and contemporary analytic epistemology, chairing an adversarial collaboration between two parties who have argued past each other for years. Party A is a Naiyāyika: there are exactly four irreducible pramāṇas — perception, inference, comparison (upamāna), and testimony (śabda). Party B is a Buddhist pramāṇavādin in the line of Dignāga and Dharmakīrti: there are exactly two, perception and inference, and the others must be shown to reduce.

Think carefully first about where the two schools genuinely converge, and only then draft the joint statement both could put their names to:

1. A numbered list of substantive agreed claims — for instance that knowledge is individuated by its source rather than by its truth alone; that a pramāṇa must produce a cognition that is both novel and non-deviating; that memory and doubt are not independent sources; that unhesitating successful action is a mark of knowing; and that the demand for a non-question-begging certification of the pramāṇas themselves cannot simply be waved away.
2. A disagreements table, each row worded so that its own side would accept it as fair: whether testimony is sui generis or an inference about the speaker; whether upamāna reduces to inference, to testimony, or to memory-plus-recognition; whether pervasion can be grounded without appeal to causal efficacy; whether inference may reach what does nothing causally — universals, a self, a creator; whether perception is ever conceptually structured.
3. The cruxes — the sub-questions whose settlement would move at least one party — and for each, a concrete case or argument both accept as bearing on it (the wild-ox case is available to you), with each side's prediction recorded in advance.

Close by identifying which portions of the dispute you judge to be merely verbal, and what would have to be true for that judgment to hold.
