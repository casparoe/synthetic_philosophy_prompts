At the funeral of a former supervisor last month, a colleague of ours — Ruth, who is a practising Christian — said of the deceased that he was with God now. Afterwards, at the wake, another member of our reading group, Bea, remarked that she did not believe that. Ruth's response was equivocal: she said Bea's denial did not touch what she had meant, but added that if it could be shown there is nothing after death, she would want to be told. The exchange set off an argument between Bea and a third member, Adam, which ran for some weeks and which I observed without taking a side. I should like your judgement on it.

(1) Adam began from Wittgenstein's lectures on religious belief: when a believer says he believes in the Last Judgement and a sceptic says he does not, the two are not contradicting one another, because they have never agreed on what would settle the matter. On Adam's telling, Ruth's remark was not a hypothesis about the furniture of the world but a picture that regulates a life — held without evidence and not surrendered in the face of counter-evidence either. Bea's "I don't believe that" therefore misfires: it has the grammatical form of a denial but there is nothing there for it to deny.

(2) Bea replied that this is not a vindication of Ruth's belief but a demotion of it. Adam has bought immunity from criticism at the price of content, since what cannot be wrong cannot be right either. And it is false as a description of actual believers: Ruth prays for particular outcomes; she was consoled when her mother's biopsy came back clear and said she had thanked God for it; and she left one congregation over its teaching on hell. That, Bea said, is the conduct of someone who treats her doctrines as answerable to how things are.

(3) Adam said Bea had misdescribed her own examples. Thanking God for a clear biopsy is not inferring God's existence from data, and leaving a congregation over the doctrine of hell is a moral repudiation, not an experimental refutation — nobody has ever abandoned Christianity because of a laboratory result. The asymmetry, he argued, supports his position: religious beliefs are revised, if at all, by considerations internal to the practice — scriptural, moral, pastoral — and never by the external evidence that would settle an empirical hypothesis, which is just what it means to say they are not hypotheses.

(4) Bea answered that Adam's criterion is now doing far too much work. Being held unshakeably and playing a regulative role in a life describes a great many secular commitments: a physicist's confidence that nature is intelligible, someone's conviction that her partner loves her, an aid worker's belief in the equal worth of persons. None of these is tested by experiment, none would be abandoned on a lab result, and each organises conduct. Either Adam's criterion makes these non-cognitive as well, which is absurd, or he owes a further marker distinguishing religious from secular regulative commitments — and the only candidate available looks like subject matter, namely whether the claim concerns something that exists, which smuggles factual content straight back in. She added a version of the old objection associated with Kai Nielsen: if religious utterances cannot be contradicted from outside the practice, they cannot be understood from outside it either, and Adam is left describing something that is not a language at all.

(5) Adam said the secular cases were not all of one kind. The physicist's confidence in intelligibility is a methodological presupposition with an aggregate track record behind it; Ruth's belief has no such record and needs none, since it is not a premise she would cite in support of anything, and that is the difference. As for the claim that everyone present took Bea and Ruth to be disagreeing: two people at the wake took the exchange to be merely verbal, and Ruth herself said the denial did not touch what she meant. Adam insisted he was not imposing a theory upon the data but reporting them.

(6) Bea conceded the point about track records only provisionally, and pressed a consequence she said Adam had not faced. If religious claims are immune to contradiction, the problem of evil cannot be a problem for them either, which would render the entire tradition of theodicy a mistake. Yet theodicy is no marginal hobby: it is among the most sustained intellectual enterprises inside the very religion Adam is describing. Believers have evidently thought that how the world is might count against their picture, and have taken seriously the task of showing otherwise. Adam's account cannot explain why they bothered.

(7) Adam said theodicy was the clearest illustration of his account rather than an objection to it. Theodicy is not a weighing of evidence that might have gone the other way; it is a means of retaining a picture one has already resolved not to give up, by reinterpreting whatever threatens it. The believer does not approach suffering as data that might refute the doctrine but approaches the doctrine as something that must be made to accommodate the suffering — which is not evidence-responsiveness but the paradigm of its absence. As to demotion, he said Bea assumes a belief is worth holding only if it is the conclusion of an inquiry, whereas his account offers the believer something sturdier: a commitment whose authority does not depend on surviving an audit. Bea calls that insulting; Adam calls it the only description on which religious conviction is possible at all, since no religious belief has ever withstood the scrutiny Bea demands.

Neither would budge, and they have asked me to find a referee. Please adjudicate the exchange move by move: for each of the seven, say whether it succeeds, partly succeeds, or fails, and justify that rating by engaging with the argument as actually made rather than by surveying the literature. Then answer three questions directly. First, is Adam right that Bea's denial does not contradict Ruth's assertion, and does it follow that her denial is empty — or are "not a contradiction" and "not answerable to evidence" two claims he is running together? Second, does Bea's generalisation in (4) defeat Adam's criterion, or does his appeal to track records in (5) answer it? Third, who handles theodicy better in (6) and (7)? Finally, say who is more right overall, and identify what both of them are missing — including whether they are arguing about the same thing at all, since Adam often appears to be talking about what particular utterances mean while Bea is talking about the epistemic standing of a whole system of belief.
