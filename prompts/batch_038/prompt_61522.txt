I teach a philosophy of perception seminar — 16 students, one 90-minute block, and I want to spend it on Molyneux's problem. I don't want to lecture them through the old yes/no camps. I want an activity where they run into the trouble themselves before the question ever gets named.

The wrinkle I want you to build around: the obvious classroom version — blindfold a sighted student, let them handle a cube and a sphere, then uncover and ask which is which — is worthless as evidence, because that student's vision has been cross-calibrated against touch their whole life. Meanwhile theres actual data now from kids in India who had congenital cataracts removed: at first they couldn't match what they saw to what they'd felt, and then they picked the mapping up suspiciously fast. So the tidy "does he know or doesn't he" framing the whole debate ran on doesn't survive contact with the empirical case, and I want the activity to make them feel that collapse rather than hear it from me.

So design it: rules, materials (no budget to speak of, one room with movable desks), a minute-by-minute timeline, and a debrief that maps what just happened onto the real conceptual joints — discriminating two shapes visually vs. knowing which one you're looking at, and how much the "seeing" in the question has to already contain. Have them commit to a written verdict beforehand so I can show them what moved and why. Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it (somebody's bound to say something clever that derails the sequence), and be straight with me about which feature of the real case no classroom exercise can reproduce and that I'll just have to supply by talking.
