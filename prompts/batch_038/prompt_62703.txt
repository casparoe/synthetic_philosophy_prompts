I am writing the term paper for a graduate seminar on the philosophy of medicine, and I have been going in circles on the naturalism–normativism dispute about "disease" and "health" — the question, roughly, of whether those terms admit of a value-free biological analysis (typical species functioning relative to a suitably chosen reference class and situation) or are irreducibly value-laden, with hybrid accounts in between that conjoin a dysfunction condition with a harm or disvalue condition. I follow the standard dialectic well enough: the objection that the analysis must be relativized both to a reference class and to an environment, and that fixing which class and which environment is itself a discretionary, hence potentially normative, choice; the objection from contested conditions, where a departure from statistical typicality is not experienced as a harm, or is claimed by the people who have it as an identity rather than a pathology; and the objection from historical change, where classifications shift in ways that track social movements and institutional politics rather than new biological findings. What strikes me, though, is that all of these objections are ordinarily read as attacks on a claim about the extension of a predicate, when several of them look equally like claims about who is entitled to settle a case and by what procedure. My half-formed idea is to re-describe the whole dispute in those terms. On this reading the naturalist is committed to holding that novel or borderline cases are resolved by further biological evidence produced by an appropriately expert body; the normativist is committed to holding that they are resolved by a process in which the affected population, or some body answerable to it, has standing; and the hybrid is committed to a two-stage procedure with a division of labor between the two. If something like this is right, the familiar objections do double duty. The reference-class and environment-relativity objections, for instance, would not so much show that no value-free analysis is possible as show that the choice of the class and situation against which functioning is measured is a judgment that has to be lodged somewhere — so the live dispute becomes whether lodging it in a physiology panel, a specialty society, or a deliberative forum is defensible. The reframing also makes a prediction that seems to fit the practice: the two sides should converge on most paradigm cases and diverge chiefly over the procedure for new ones, which is arguably what happens when a condition is added to or withdrawn from a diagnostic classification.

Please turn this into a proposal I can hand in, of roughly 1,200 to 1,500 words, with the following parts. First, a precise statement of the question, formulated so that it has a determinate answer and so that a competent opponent could argue against my reframing rather than merely find it unilluminating. Second, an account of why the existing positions, taken on their own terms, leave that question open — and here I want the strongest case against me stated rather than buried, including the worry that procedural questions presuppose conceptual ones (you cannot ask who decides until you know what is being decided), and the worry that a dispute about who gets to classify is simply a political dispute in philosophical dress. Third, a method: what I would actually do, week by week over a semester. As part of that, select two or three concrete contested conditions — the sort that recur in this literature — and say what verdict or what diagnosis my reframing yields for each that the standard conceptual framing does not, since without cases doing that work the proposal is only a slogan. Fourth, what a successful result would look like: what I would have to show in order to have demonstrated that the reframing earns its keep rather than merely relabeling the debate. Fifth, failure conditions stated honestly, including the real possibility that the two readings turn out to be notational variants and the project simply dissolves, and what I would do with that outcome rather than pretending it away. Sixth, a scope note: what gets cut if the argument outgrows the semester, and which of the contested conditions is the cheapest to drop.

Write it in a sober grant-application register, not a breathless one, and let the intellectual work sit in the framing rather than in rhetorical escalation. Please do not include a bibliography, in-text citations, or footnote stubs — I know this literature and will supply the references myself; refer to positions, arguments, and cases by description instead of by author and date.
