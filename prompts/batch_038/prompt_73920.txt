I read a long magazine-ish essay about Bernard Williams last night and I want to know how much of it I can trust, because I was already predisposed to like it — I find the calculate-everything tone of a lot of EA-adjacent writing exhausting, so some of my enthusiasm is probably motivated and I can't tell which parts.

Here's the bit that stuck with me, roughly as the author wrote it:

"Bernard Williams is the philosopher who finally broke utilitarianism's back, and he did it with two stories. The first is Jim, a botanist who stumbles into a village where a captain has twenty Indians lined up against a wall. The captain offers him a deal: shoot one, and the other nineteen go free; refuse, and all twenty die. Any utilitarian can tell you in two seconds that Jim should shoot — nineteen beats zero. Williams says that answer is obscene, because it treats Jim's own act as just one more event in the causal soup, no different from the deaths the captain would cause. Utilitarians call this negative responsibility, and Williams thinks it is fatal: it makes you responsible for everything other people do. But the real damage is to integrity. Jim's identity is built out of the projects and commitments that make him who he is — being the sort of person who doesn't kill people — and utilitarianism asks him to trade that away for the best outcome. Which is why the second story matters even more: a man whose wife is drowning. The utilitarian has to say he saves her because it maximizes happiness, and Williams thinks that's one thought too many — you should just be moved by the fact that it's your wife. The lesson of both is that any theory demanding you think impartially is a theory demanding you stop being a person. Williams's own conclusion is that utilitarianism survives only as a kind of useful public fiction: weigh the outcomes in politics, but don't let it near your actual life. And no utilitarian has ever mounted a serious reply, which is why this argument is basically settled."

Can you go through it claim by claim and seperate out what Williams would happily sign, what's a fair simplification for a general audience, and what's actually wrong? A few specific things I couldn't figure out on my own:

- Does he really think the Jim case and the drowning-wife case are making the same argument, or has the essay fused two different lines of attack (and different targets) into one?
- Is integrity, for him, basically about not wanting to feel like a killer — or is that precisely the psychologized reading he pushes back on? Related: what happens to the objection if the utilitarian just says fine, integrity is one value among others, put it in the calculus and weigh it? Does he have an answer to that, and if so what is it?
- The drowning-wife point — is that an objection to how demanding utilitarianism is, or is it about something else entirely, like what kind of thing counts as a reason or a motive? The essay definitely reads it as the former.
- That ending about a useful public fiction. I genuinely couldn't parse it. Is he recommending a two-tier arrangement where we use utilitarian reasoning publicly and live by our projects privately, or is he doing something else with that idea — and if the latter, does the essay's version of it survive?
- Does Williams anywhere actually give a verdict on what Jim should do? The essay strongly implies he says refuse.
- And is the last sentence defensible at all? I don't want a reading list, just a sense of whether there are consequentialist responses that engage the integrity objection head-on rather than dismissing it, and whether calling the debate settled is even the right frame.

Basically: tell me what to keep from this essay and what to throw out, and whether the thing I liked about it is the thing that's actually true.
