**Two Mechanisms, One Category: Why Supererogation Isn't Really About Cost**

1. The category is supposed to pick out acts that are morally good, not required, permissible to omit, and specially praiseworthy when performed. Since Urmson's 1958 paper got the secular discussion going, nearly everyone who thinks there's a real category here explains the "not required" part by appeal to cost: the agent would have to give up enough of her own life, resources or projects that morality simply can't ask it of her. There's a prerogative to weight your own interests, and where the sacrifice crosses some threshold the demand runs out. Call this the **cost mechanism**.

2. My claim is that the cost mechanism handles the easy cases and none of the interesting ones, and that the category we actually deploy is generated by a second, independent mechanism, which I'll call the **corruption mechanism**: some goods are such that demanding them destroys them. A gift you were entitled to isn't a gift. Hospitality that can be claimed is housing provision. Forgiveness the wrongdoer has standing to insist on isn't forgiveness, it's a settlement. In these cases the act isn't beyond duty because the agent gets to keep her resources — it's beyond duty because its value depends on its not being claimable by anyone.

3. Two diagnostics tell the mechanisms apart. The first is the **compensation test**: suppose the beneficiary could pay full market value for the act. If the act would become obligatory once compensated, cost was what blocked the obligation. If the not-required-ness survives compensation, cost was never the reason. The second is the **demand test**: can the beneficiary legitimately demand the act, resent its absence, complain to third parties about it? In straightforward duty, yes. In the cases I care about, no — and the "no" is not a report about how heavy the sacrifice would be.

4. Run the compensation test on the spare room. Marta has a room she doesn't use, no dependants, and a family with nowhere to sleep. Hosting them costs her real things: privacy, control of her own home, months of adjustment. The standard story says the cost explains why she isn't obliged. But now suppose the state offers to pay her a full market rent, plus a cleaning service, plus compensation for wear. Has it become obligatory? Clearly not — nothing has changed except that she's now being paid, and paying someone doesn't create a claim on their household. So the cost was doing no work. What was doing the work is that a home is not the sort of thing that can be conscripted without turning an act of welcome into the delivery of a service. That's the corruption mechanism, and it predicts the right verdict where cost gets it wrong.

5. The corruption mechanism also explains the cheap cases, which the cost mechanism can't handle at all. Consider forgiveness. Forgiving someone who badly wronged you can cost nothing; sometimes it's straightforwardly in your interest, it can restore a relationship you want, it can end years of rumination. There is no prerogative to invoke, because there's nothing to protect. Yet we do not say the wrongdoer is owed forgiveness, and we do not say the victim acts wrongly in withholding it. What makes forgiveness non-demandable isn't its price. It's that forgiveness demanded is not forgiveness. Same structure in the small cases: the man who sits with a distressed teenager at a bus stop for twenty minutes when he has nowhere to be and nothing to lose. Zero cost, and still not something the girl's mother can demand of him as of right.

6. If the corruption mechanism is the real engine, then the four-box scheme we inherit is mis-sorted. "Supererogatory" isn't a deontic category sitting alongside obligatory, permitted and wrong. It's a fact about standing: about who is in a position to demand what. Once you see that, the puzzle about whether such acts are "really" permitted stops looking like a puzzle and starts looking like a category error in the original taxonomy — we've been sorting acts when we should have been sorting claims.

7. There's an obvious objection and I want to state it rather than hide it. If a person never once does anything beyond duty — never forgives when forgiveness is free, never sits with anyone at any bus stop, declines every spare-room request that comes with compensation — we don't just think she's within her rights. We think she's stingy, and stinginess is a criticism. So something in the neighbourhood does look required, and my account of a demand-free zone seems to license a life that is intuitively deficient. My response is that the criticism attaches to the pattern, not to any act in it, and patterns aren't the sort of thing anyone can demand either: you can't be ordered to be generous, you can only be ordered to do generous things, and that's precisely the demand that would corrupt them.

8. So: the category survives, but not as a deontic category, and not for the reason everyone gives. What's beyond duty is beyond demand, and it's beyond demand because being within demand would cost it the thing that makes it worth doing.
