I am an applied trade economist, and I want to test a suspicion about Adam Smith's case against the British colonial system by translation rather than by summary.

My working vocabulary is the standard one: comparative advantage and gains from trade; terms-of-trade effects; the sectoral allocation of capital and labour; rates of return and their tendency to equalize across employments; monopoly and preferential-market distortions measured as deadweight loss; rent-seeking and lobbying models of protection; tax incidence; and the public-finance treatment of defence and civil administration as real resource costs of empire. I also think in present-value terms about the timing and duration of returns, and I reason about counterfactuals in terms of what a general-equilibrium model would predict under alternative institutional arrangements. I read Smith's chapter on colonies in the fourth book of The Wealth of Nations once, years ago, and I retained the impression that his argument was essentially an economic one, anticipating my framework with worse tools. I want to know whether that impression survives contact with the argument itself.

Please restate his case, component by component, in my vocabulary. Cover at least the following: the claim that the colonial monopoly makes colonial produce dearer everywhere and so depresses consumption and industry even in countries that hold no colonies; the claim that the monopoly draws British capital out of nearer, faster-returning employments and into a distant, slow-returning trade, so that less productive labour is maintained at home than would otherwise be; the claim that this raises Britain's ordinary rate of profit and that a higher profit rate is a national disadvantage rather than a windfall for the owners of capital; the accounting claim that the cost of defending and governing the colonies exceeds whatever the monopoly yields, so that dominion is a net drain; the constitutional remedies he proposes, including colonial representation in proportion to contribution and a gradual, negotiated emancipation; and his diagnosis of why Britain will not adopt them. If you think his remarks on the economics of slave labour in the plantation colonies belong in this argument rather than elsewhere in his work, place them; if not, say so and leave them out.

For each component I want three things: the modern mechanism it maps onto, if there is one; a verdict of clean translation, translation with distortion, or no counterpart at all; and, where the translation is distorted, a statement of what Smith's own premises actually were, in his terms rather than mine.

Two constraints on the exercise. First, no smuggling. Do not render his claims in my language in a way that presupposes my language's conclusions — in particular, do not convert his argument about the direction of capital into an efficiency loss within a framework that already assumes profit-rate equalization and treats higher returns to capital as at worst distributionally ambiguous, and do not silently translate normative claims about justice into claims about welfare and call the result a translation. Second, correct me. If I have misstated any of these arguments above — if, say, the point about the rate of profit is not the point I have just attributed to it — say so before translating my error.

Then give me the residue report. Which of these claims has no expression whatsoever in my vocabulary, and which is merely awkward within it? My own guesses at the inexpressible set are the claim that a country can be harmed by its own elevated profit rate, the account of imperial motives in terms of vanity and grandeur rather than interest, and the proposal to give up dominion voluntarily as a matter of constitutional principle rather than of cost-benefit arithmetic. Tell me which of those guesses are right, which are wrong, and which I could rescue by adding a political-economy or institutional layer to my framework.

Finally, the verdict I actually want. Does the residue show that my vocabulary is missing something — that a language of efficiency and distribution cannot express claims about justice, national character, and the corruption of politics, which is where part of Smith's argument is really doing its work — or does it show that his argument was less rigorous than his reputation suggests, resting on assumptions about the natural course of capital and the quantity of productive labour it maintains that are false or non-standard in any modern model, so that his conclusions survive only by accident? Take a side and defend it, or explain precisely why the honest answer splits, and where the split falls.

Do not quote Smith, and give me no page numbers, no citations, and no reading list; I want the argument, not the apparatus. Flag every point at which you are reconstructing rather than reporting.

Close by telling me what would change your mind about your verdict: which argument or piece of evidence, if it turned out to be sound, would move you to the other side; and, separately, where in the translation itself you think you are most likely to have gone wrong — which component you handled with least confidence, and why.
