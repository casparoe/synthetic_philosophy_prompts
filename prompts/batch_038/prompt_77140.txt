In our philosophy-of-social-science reading group, a colleague has been defending long-range historical forecasting by analogy with climate modelling. His argument runs as follows. No model can tell you whether it will rain in Leeds on 14 March 2071, yet the same models can tell you the mean summer temperature and the changing frequency of extreme-heat days; the failure of specific forecasts therefore does not indict the science, because the science was never in the business of specific forecasts. Likewise, nobody could have foretaken the French Revolution, and that is beside the point: what a science of history would have offered is the aggregate claim that societies with those property relations and that fiscal structure were headed for a general reorganization — a claim about the shape of the trajectory, not the date of the event. When I pressed the standard Popperian objection — that what history yields are trends rather than laws, and that the course of history depends on the future growth of knowledge, which we cannot possess in advance — he replied with a second analogy, this one defensive. Social prediction, he said, is like a bank run, in which the rumour of collapse is what produces the collapse. On his telling, the failed prophecies of Marx and Comte show nothing about whether there are laws of history, because publishing the prophecy changed the system it described: the forecast was either self-fulfilling or self-defeating, and so was never a clean test. His conclusion is that historicism is no more extravagant than meteorology.

Please audit both analogies rather than adjudicating the underlying dispute in the abstract. For each, give the correspondence explicitly — a table is welcome — stating what plays the role of the forcings and boundary conditions, the ensemble of runs, the aggregate variable, the underlying laws, and the feedback loop, in the target case of historical forecasting. Then separate the disanalogies that actually bear on the use he is making of them from merely local ones, and address at least the following.

1. Does the climate case do its work by way of laws plus initial conditions, or by way of statistical aggregation over a chaotic system under slowly varying forcings? Which of those two, if either, is available to a would-be science of history, and what does the trends-versus-laws distinction cost him here?

2. Climate projections are scenario-conditional: the mean temperature in 2071 depends on emissions pathways nobody knows. Does that make them conditional rather than unconditional predictions? If so, has your colleague traded one for the other — asserting an unconditional historicist prophecy, then defending it by pointing at a conditional projection?

3. On the bank-run analogy: can reflexivity distinguish social from natural prediction at all? Consider that climate projection is itself reflexive, since the models inform policy and policy changes the forcings, which is part of why the field moved to scenarios; that medicine, public health and finance contain routine self-defeating predictions; and that Popper, who introduced the Oedipus effect for the influence of a prediction on the thing predicted, later doubted that this marked the boundary between the social and the natural sciences. Does the analogy pick out a difference in kind, or only in degree?

4. Is the defensive use of the analogy — failed prophecy explained away by feedback — an immunizing manoeuvre that leaves historicism untestable? Is the same manoeuvre available to the climate scientist? If not, spell out the asymmetry that blocks it there, since that asymmetry is presumably what your colleague needs and does not have.

5. Does either analogy answer the logical argument that we cannot predict the future course of history because it depends on knowledge we do not yet have? Say whether climate science confronts anything structurally similar when it cannot predict future technology, and whether its way of coping — bracketing the unknowable into scenarios — vindicates or undercuts the historicist.

Close with a verdict on each of his two conclusions, stated as what survives and what does not: is long-run aggregate forecasting about societies defensible, and is his explanation of past failed prophecies defensible? Concede where he has a genuine point — the blanket denial of long-range social prediction is itself arguable — but do not let the concession do unearned work. If either analogy is worth keeping, say precisely what it can carry and how far, and for whatever work it cannot do, propose a better analogy and explain why it fits. Aim for around 1,200 words, written for an audience of philosophy graduate students plus one climate scientist, with technical terms briefly defined. Reason from the arguments themselves; no bibliography or citations are needed.
