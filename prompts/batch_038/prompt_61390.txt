I want a thought experiment built to specification, in the territory of Hempel's raven paradox.

What the case is for: I want to isolate a single variable — the relative size of the subject class and of the complement of the predicate class — and see whether the familiar Bayesian verdict on the paradox, on which a white shoe does confirm the hypothesis that all ravens are black but only by a negligible amount, is a deliverance of that variable rather than of anything about evidential relevance or logical form. So the case must hold the logical form of the paradox exactly fixed while reversing the sizes. A case that reaches the target verdicts by stipulation, or that smuggles them into the way the evidence is obtained, fails: the verdicts have to fall out of the probability structure once the story's numbers and priors are on the table.

Specifications for the scenario:

1. Setting. One realistic finite investigation — an auditor, a lab technician, an inventory check, a compliance review. No urns, no balls drawn from jars, no abstract populations described purely in terms of F and G. The subject and predicate must be properties the setting makes salient on its own; no grue-like or otherwise artificially gerrymandered predicates.
2. Form. The hypothesis must be a universal generalization, "all F are G," and the two items of evidence to be compared must be, first, an object that is both F and G, and second, an object that is neither — the structural analogues of the black raven and the white shoe.
3. Sizes. The F class must be large in absolute terms and the non-G class small, small enough that a single arbitrary observation from it carries real information. State every number you need.
4. Priors. Give an explicit prior on the hypothesis and on the number of violators, and motivate both from stated facts about how the population came to be — who assembled it, what could plausibly have gone wrong, what the failure mode would look like if there were one. The motivation must be such that a reasonable investigator would hold that prior before making any observation, and it must be a claim about the production process rather than an assumption about the truth or falsity of the hypothesis.
5. Sampling. The observed object must be taken arbitrarily or at random from the whole population, with no selection effect correlated with either property, and the observer must have no independent information about that particular object.
6. Payoff. The Bayes factor for the second observation must exceed that for the first by at least an order of magnitude, and yet the second observation must still be one that an ordinary person, hearing the story, would describe as having nothing whatever to do with the question.
7. Length. The scenario itself in at most one paragraph. The analysis after it may run as long as it needs, but keep the whole response under about a thousand words.

After the scenario, do the following:

- Restate the hypothesis, the population numbers, the priors, and the sampling procedure as an explicit model.
- Compute both Bayes factors, showing the arithmetic, and report the posterior odds each observation yields. Confirm that the order-of-magnitude requirement is met, and say how close to the edge the case is — how much slack there is in the numbers before the requirement fails.
- Identify which single feature of the case does the work, and demonstrate its sensitivity by rescaling exactly that feature while leaving everything else untouched: same setting, same logical form, sizes flipped so that the complement of the predicate class becomes vast and the subject class small. Give the numbers and show that the familiar result — the contrapositive-side observation confirming negligibly — returns.
- Address both versions of the standard line: the abundance version, on which the contrapositive-side observation is swamped by how many such objects there are, and the background-knowledge version, on which the observation is negligible because we already knew the object in hand was never going to be a raven. Say whether the constructed case defeats both, or only one, and why.
- Say how a defender of the standard resolution would attack the case: what they would claim has been built in by hand, and whether that objection sticks or merely redescribes the point.
- Close with an honest verdict on what the case does and does not establish — in particular, whether it shows the raven paradox to be purely a question of magnitude, or whether something about the actual raven case resists replication inside a finite, known population with a stated prior.
