I spent thirty-eight years as an experimental petrologist, most of them at a government geological survey, and I retired three years ago with the intention of finally reading the philosophy of science I had only ever encountered second-hand. What has arrested me, rather against my expectations, is the literature on verisimilitude: the problem of saying what it is for one false theory to be closer to the truth than another. Every quantitative model I used in my working life was strictly false, yet some of them were better than their predecessors in a sense that was not merely instrumental — a high-pressure phase relation we got right in 1994 constrained a decade of subsequent work in a way that an earlier, equally false relation could not. Popper took this seriously, and then, as I understand it, Tichý, Harris, and Miller showed in 1974 that his definition could not deliver it: no false theory could be nearer the truth than any other. Later work — the content and consequence approaches on one side, the likeness approach of Hilpinen, Niiniluoto, and Oddie on the other, with its possible worlds and its choice among minimum, sum, and average ways of aggregating distance — seemed to me, as a layman, to restore something like the intuition, at the price of considerable machinery. Separately, and by inheritance from my late wife, I have for some years helped run a monthly continuing-education group of about twenty retired engineers, clinicians, and laboratory scientists who watch a film together and then argue about it. Next month's film is Orson Welles's *Citizen Kane*, and I have undertaken to introduce it. I should like to introduce it through the problem of verisimilitude, and I am writing to ask whether that lens can bear the weight.

The hooks seem obvious enough, but I want them tested rather than assumed. The newsreel that opens the film is a compressed, confident summary account of a life, produced by people who never met the subject; Thompson is then dispatched to find out what one word meant, which is an inquiry with a target it cannot specify. What follows is five testimonies that are not equally false: Thatcher's papers get the chronology exactly right and the significance exactly wrong; Bernstein is warm and precise about particulars and silent on the pattern; Leland offers the most systematic diagnosis of Kane and is also the most compromised by his own grievance; Susan Alexander is closest to the felt texture of the years and furthest from their shape; and Raymond the butler supplies what turns out to be the only detail that matters while knowing nothing whatever of the rest. Here is the private, probably amateurish idea I should like you to work on, because it is what I could not resolve myself. In the laboratory we judged a model by how much of the observed behaviour it reproduced, *weighted by how central the misfit was to the question we had asked*: a model wrong about trace elements but right about the phase boundary was a better model for our purposes than the converse, though the converse contained more true sentences. Does the film rank its testimonies in anything like this weighted way, and is that ranking what makes Thompson's closing shrug — that no single word can explain a life — strike the audience as a failure of nerve rather than a correct conclusion? Relatedly, which measure of closeness does the film's structure actually presuppose? A minimum-type measure would say that the account which nails the one decisive detail is thereby as near the truth as any other; an averaging measure would say that every account's likeness is diluted by all it gets wrong. The final scene in the sorting shed, where the audience alone learns what the sled was, seems to me to require the first; Thompson's failure seems to require the second; and I cannot tell whether Welles has noticed.

Two further things, and then some constraints on what you send back. First, the epistemological problem: even if one of these testimonies genuinely is nearer the truth about Kane, no character in the film is positioned to know it, which is precisely the gap the distinction between truthlikeness and *estimated* truthlikeness is meant to mark. Is the film's engine that distinction — the suggestion that estimated closeness is the only target available to a finite inquirer — or is it the bleaker suggestion that the target itself is inaccessible, so that the estimate is a consolation prize? Second, the vantage the film grants its audience: the furnace shot, followed by the no-trespassing sign, hands us an access to the fact of the matter that the entire truthlikeness enterprise arguably assumes and that working science never possesses. Is Welles endorsing that access, exposing our appetite for it, or simply spending it as a plot device? I would also like you to say plainly where this reading breaks. Kane's subject is a person, not a law-governed system; testimony is self-presentation shaped by the teller's stakes; and it may be that the film is not about degrees of closeness at all but about the category error of treating a life as something admitting of approximation. If the lens merely decorates, say so, and say which scenes resist it and why. Please address whether the film's structure needs Popper's original definition or survives its collapse, since that determines whether I present the movie as an illustration of a solved problem or of an open one. I do not want a bibliography or a reading list — I own the books, and I would rather you identify positions by the names of those who hold them only where the attribution does real work. Point me to particular moments by describing them, not by quoting the script. And close by telling me what the film manages to show about the business of approaching a truth one cannot reach that the formal treatments, for all their possible worlds and distance functions, cannot say — because that is the part my former colleagues, who are used to thinking of models as instruments, most need to hear.
