I convene a Michaelmas-term graduate seminar in the philosophy of the social sciences, and last week's session on the epistemology of markets produced an exchange I have not yet managed to think my way through. One of my doctoral students proposed that our faculty run a small internal prediction market — contracts on when the university's planning committee would finally rule on a contested building application — and argued that the scheme would be epistemically self-undermining: because a handful of insiders would hold private information about the committee's deliberations, the price would systematically reward precisely those people, so that the rest of us, holding no such information, could not rationally treat the resulting price as an impartial summary of what the faculty collectively believes. The visiting financial economist we had invited to comment waved this aside with the old pavement joke — if there really were a twenty-pound note lying on the pavement, it would not still be lying there when you got to it — and then drew two distinct conclusions from it: first, that any bias of the kind my student described would be competed away before anyone could act on it, and second, that the price would therefore be the best estimate available to anybody in the room who lacked private information.

My difficulty is that the quip is being made to carry a great deal of weight, and I cannot tell how much of it is licensed. I am perfectly aware of the formal backdrop — Aumann's result on agreeing to disagree, the Milgrom and Stokey no-trade theorem of 1982, and Grossman and Stiglitz's 1980 impossibility of informationally efficient markets — and I do not want a survey of that literature; I want the sentence itself taken apart. It strikes me that it is being used to point in two opposite directions at once. On the one hand it guarantees that profit opportunities are never left lying about, which presupposes a standing population of people who stoop to pick them up and who are reliably paid for doing so — which is very close to the Grossman–Stiglitz thought that a market cannot be perfectly efficient, since perfect efficiency would destroy the reward that sustains the information-gathering which makes it efficient. On the other hand, the no-trade reasoning tells a rational agent that an offer to trade from an equally rational counterpart is itself evidence against accepting, since the offeror would not have made it unless he knew something; pressed consistently, that seems to say nobody should ever try to pick the note up. One and the same sentence is thus being invoked both to guarantee that notes are always collected and to guarantee that collecting them is never rational, and I suspect my colleague is sliding between the two.

What I should like, to bring to the next session, is a numbered menu of the precise claims the pavement quip could be making rather than a single gloss on it. For each reading, please give me: a one-sentence statement that makes the quantifiers and the modal force explicit, so that I can see whether it asserts that such opportunities cannot exist at all, cannot be identified by me in particular, cannot persist for long, or cannot be exploited profitably once information costs and transaction costs are counted; a verdict on it — platitude, substantive but defensible, contentious, or simply false; the assumptions it requires (common prior, common knowledge of rationality, risk neutrality, the absence of hedging, liquidity or lifecycle motives to trade, costless information, and so forth), together with an assessment of which of those assumptions fail in the small, insider-laden, non-repeated setting my student described; and a separate judgement on whether that reading supports each of the economist's two conclusions, since I suspect one survives and the other does not. Please close by identifying which reading he was most likely deploying, and by telling me what, if anything, remains of the student's objection once the strongest defensible version of the quip has been granted.
