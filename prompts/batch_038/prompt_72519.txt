I am a novelist, and the third act of my current book is a hearing before the parks and naming commission of an unnamed mid-sized city, convened to decide what to do with an equestrian monument to an unnamed nineteenth-century founder. One of my principals is the city archivist, and she holds a position I intend to defend in print, because I expect some of my readers to work in heritage policy and to notice immediately if I have built a straw man. I would therefore like you to argue for her view, at length and on the merits, rather than to survey the state of play around it.

Her view turns on three stipulated distinctions, which I ask you to adopt as definitions for the purposes of the argument rather than to contest:

First, honorific lock-in. A commemorative act embeds a judgment in a medium whose intended lifespan vastly exceeds the lifespan of the reasons for that judgment. Bronze, inscriptions, and street names are made to outlast the constituency that authorized them, and they remain legible as praise long after the particulars of the praise have become illegible.

Second, the asymmetry of removal. Taking a monument down is not a reversion to a neutral baseline; it is a second, costlier, more conspicuous expressive act that says something new about the city. The status quo is therefore not the default position in this dispute, and "leave it where it stands" is not the option that makes no claim.

Third, reclassification. A monument's force can in principle be altered without altering the object: by transferring custody, by changing what stands around it, by an explicit declaration from the body that maintains it that the object is now held as a document rather than as an honor. Her policy conclusion is that cities should cease honorific naming altogether and name things descriptively after places, topography, and trades, while subjecting existing honorifics to a formal act of reclassification from laudatory to documentary standing.

Please make the case for that package as a whole. Four constraints. The argument must proceed from the structure of the practice rather than from the history of any particular statue or city: I want a case that holds as well in my invented jurisdiction as in a real one, so do not let weight rest on famous controversies. It must confront, at their strongest and in this order, the objection that reclassification is a dodge permitting a city to retain its honors while disclaiming them; the objection that audiences and not custodians fix what a monument does, so no declaration can change how it is received by the people who walk past it; and the objection that a polity which declines to honor anyone has abdicated the moral judgment that makes civic life legible rather than perfected it. It must then identify the single premise on which the whole package most depends, isolate it from the rest, and defend that premise separately and at greater length than anything else in the piece. Finally, it must close by stating what would count against the thesis: what a city's actual experience of reclassification would have to look like before my archivist should concede.

Continuous prose rather than bullet points, roughly fifteen hundred words, and no citations, bibliographies, or appeals to named scholars. The reasoning must stand on its own, since my archivist is a character and cannot borrow anyone's authority.
