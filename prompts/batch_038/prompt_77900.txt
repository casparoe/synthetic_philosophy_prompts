I am a science and ideas correspondent for a British daily newspaper; my beat covers economics, statistics and the philosophy of probability. Six weeks ago I published a long feature arguing that the fashionable quarrel between expected-utility theory and the "ergodicity economics" associated with Ole Peters and his collaborators has been framed badly by both sides. On the strength of that piece I have been invited to give the opening lecture of an interdisciplinary seminar series at a research university: forty-five minutes of talk, then thirty minutes of questions from a room that will not be friendly. I need a Q&A prep sheet, and I need it unsentimental — I would far rather meet the objection that sinks me now than discover it at the lectern.

My thesis, in the form I intend to defend it. The standard defence of Kelly-type betting is that maximising the time-average growth rate of wealth beats maximising the ensemble average, and that this matters because wealth dynamics are multiplicative, so a long run of individually favourable bets can still ruin an agent. The standard reply is that the whole affair is a redescription: under multiplicative dynamics, maximising the time-average growth rate and maximising expected logarithmic utility come to the same thing, so nothing new has been established, and in a genuinely one-off decision the phrase time-average has no content at all. I think the second camp is right about the redescription and wrong about the conclusion. My argument runs as follows.

First, the normative work in growth-rate reasoning is not done by compounding across repetitions but by absorbing states: outcomes after which the agent is no longer in a position to make any further bet of the relevant kind — insolvency, loss of a licence, loss of a professional reputation, death, or a career move that forecloses the next decade of choices. Second, because absorbing states operate through the closure of future option sets rather than through arithmetic compounding, the argument survives exactly the one-off case that the ergodicity literature handles badly; a single non-repeatable decision can be governed by growth-rate considerations provided that the decision changes which decisions remain available. Third, it follows that the Kelly fraction is not the answer but a ceiling: the correct stake depends on the value of the option set that would be destroyed, and that value is agent-relative and largely non-monetary, so a freelance journalist staking her savings on one favourable wager should bet well below Kelly even where the arithmetic of repeated play would say otherwise. I also concede, in the piece, that for institutions that really are close to the idealised repeat player — an endowment, a diversified insurer, a market maker — expected-value reasoning is not merely defensible but correct, and that my absorption-first account is a theory about agents with finite lives and no heirs.

The room. About twenty-five people, hosted jointly by an economics department and a centre for philosophy of science. Two Bayesian decision theorists, one of whom works on subjective probability and will regard my entire framing as a category error. A behavioural economist who studies decisions made from experience and will not accept any claim that ignores probability weighting and the description–experience gap. A philosopher of probability who will want to know what I mean by the dynamics of wealth, and whether I have any warrant for treating a personal fortune as a stochastic process at all. A retired actuary in the front row who thinks in solvency margins and ruin probabilities. Two or three finance people from the business school. Four or five graduate students, at least one of whom has written an online defence of Samuelson's old objection that many repetitions of a favourable bet do not make any single such bet safe, and another who will treat me as a journalist who has read one working paper too many. Nobody there makes a living from this debate the way the ergodicity-economics people do, and nobody is a journalist. They will be courteous and they will be thorough.

What I want is a prep sheet, not a defence of my view and not a survey of the literature. Nine objections, ordered by the product of how likely each is in this particular room and how much damage it would do if I answered it badly. Phrase each one the way the person raising it would phrase it, in the first person, in their register: the decision theorist's version of a point is not the actuary's version of the same point, and I want to be able to recognise each one as it arrives.

Deliver it as a single table with exactly these columns:

- rank — 1 to 9, in the ordering above.
- source — who raises it (decision theorist, behavioural economist, philosopher of probability, actuary, finance, graduate student), plus one clause on why this person in particular rather than someone else.
- objection — two to four sentences, first person, in their own vocabulary.
- force — high, medium or low, with one clause saying what makes it that.
- concede — what I should grant immediately, before arguing at all.
- hold — the single claim I must not surrender while answering it.
- reply — the words I would actually say, about 150 of them, deliverable in under two minutes.
- soft_spot — where that reply is weakest, and the follow-up question that would expose the weakness.

Include at least one objection that is simply correct and that I should concede outright rather than fight, and at least one that is a challenge to my standing or competence rather than to my argument; I want to know how to absorb that without either grovelling or losing the room.

After the table, three short prose items:

- The question I am hoping nobody asks: the one for which I have no good answer from where I currently stand. Say why it is fatal, give me the best partial reply available, and assess honestly how much of the position survives it.
- Two or three sentences I can place early in the talk that inoculate against the top-ranked objections without making me sound like a defence lawyer.
- Concession triage: of everything you have advised me to concede, which concessions are costless, and which one, if extracted from me under pressure, would collapse the argument.

Ground rules. British English throughout, spelling included. No bibliography, no reading list, no footnote-style references; I have the literature and I do not want invented entries — name a thinker or a result only where somebody in this room would actually name it aloud, and where you are not certain of a specific claim, flag the uncertainty in the relevant cell rather than asserting it. Do not soften the assessment on my behalf: a truthful map of the weak points is worth far more to me than encouragement.
