Here's the case as I've reconstructed it for myself, feel free to use it or fix it up if you think I've botched something. A stranger on the street offers to flip a coin for you. Let H be "the coin comes up heads" and E be "the stranger is left-handed," and suppose you're as confident as you can be that E is fifty-fifty — you've nothing to go on, but there's no reason to think otherwise. Your evidence about how the flip will go is a grab bag: maybe she's a sleight-of-hand artist who can force heads whenever she's left-handed and tails whenever she isn't; maybe she's the exact mirror image of that; maybe she's totally ordinary with a fair coin, so her handedness is irrelevant; or maybe she's anything in between — say she forces heads with probability x when left-handed and 1−x when right-handed, for x anywhere in [0,1], and you can't guess at which x. So conditional on E, and conditional on not-E, your credence in H runs the full range from 0 to 1. But check the marginals: in every one of those hypotheses, P(H) = x·(1/2) + (1−x)·(1/2) = 1/2. So before anyone tells you anything about her hands you are perfectly, exactly confident that the coin lands heads with probability 1/2, and the moment somebody mentions that she's left-handed that confidence evaporates into total ignorance. Free information makes you worse off, epistemically speaking. That's the whole objection in miniature, right?
