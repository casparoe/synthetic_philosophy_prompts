And here is the script:

> **(i)** Sen's minimal liberalism (ML) says that each individual is decisive over at least one pair of social states: for that pair, whatever the individual prefers, society prefers. Decisiveness attaches to pairs rather than to whole menus, and the pair is supposed to represent that individual's protected private sphere. The other ingredients are an unrestricted domain — every logically possible profile of individual orderings is admissible — the weak Pareto principle — if everyone prefers x to y, society ranks x above y — and the requirement that the social ranking be a complete and transitive ordering.
>
> Sen's example. One copy of *Lady Chatterley's Lover* and three social states: L (Lewd reads it), P (Prude reads it), and N (it goes unread). Lewd ranks P ≻ L ≻ N: he would enjoy reading it himself, but would enjoy Prude's being made to read it even more, and would rather read it than have nobody read it. Prude ranks N ≻ P ≻ L: he would rather the book went unread, but if somebody must read it he would rather it were himself, in disgust, than Lewd, with enjoyment.
>
> ML makes Lewd decisive over (L, N), the pair that differs only in whether Lewd reads. Lewd prefers L, so the social ranking contains L ≻ N. ML makes Prude decisive over (P, N); Prude prefers N, so the social ranking contains N ≻ P. Transitivity then yields L ≻ P. But weak Pareto also applies to (P, L): Lewd prefers P to L, and so does Prude, so P ≻ L. The social ranking is therefore cyclic — P ≻ L ≻ N ≻ P — and no ordering can satisfy U, P, and ML together.
>
> This is not Arrow's theorem with a liberal condition bolted on. Arrow's result needs independence of irrelevant alternatives; Sen's proof never invokes it. And Sen's contradiction needs only two individuals and three states, so nothing like a Condorcet cycle among voters is doing the work. The argument is also purely ordinal: no interpersonal comparisons of utility appear anywhere in it.
>
> **(ii)** The meddlesome-preference objection says that Sen's two characters are not liberals but busybodies: each has preferences about what happens inside the other's protected pair. Lewd cares whether Prude reads; Prude cares whether Lewd reads. If one admitted only profiles in which each person's ranking depends solely on the features of the state falling in his own sphere, the contradiction would not arise. The objection seems to me to succeed. What it shows is that U, P, and ML are jointly inconsistent only in a peculiar region of preference space, and it costs the liberal nothing, since it leaves all three of Sen's conditions exactly as they were and merely observes that Sen chose an unrepresentative profile to exhibit the conflict.
>
> **(iii)** Nozick's objection is that Sen's condition is too weak. On Nozick's account rights are side constraints, and a side constraint ought to cover a great deal more than a single pair of social states, so ML caricatures the liberal position by granting each person a token right. The remedy is to make each person decisive over a much larger set of pairs. With a rich enough assignment of protected pairs the cycle cannot close, because among any small set of states there will be one that everyone ranks above the rest; Sen's rejoinder that more rights simply mean more opportunities for conflict is a practical worry rather than a principled answer.
