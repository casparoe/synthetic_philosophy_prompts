I am about to circulate a draft position paper on the grounds of criminalization, and before it goes out I want the set of commitments below audited for internal consistency. The paper is meant to sit on Hart's side of the Hart–Devlin debate, but I am no longer confident that everything on this list actually lives on that side. I don't know where the pressure points are, and I have no prior attachment to any single item.

1. Criminalization requires harm. The only thing that can justify the criminal law is the prevention of harm to persons who have not consented. Wrongness as such — including wrongness on which there is near-unanimous moral agreement — never justifies it. I hold this as a substantive constraint on the legislature, not as a rule of thumb.

2. Devlin's disintegration thesis is false, and the way it is false is instructive. He moves from the observation that every society has a shared morality to the claim that a society just is that shared morality, so that moral change amounts to the death of one society and the birth of another. That inference does not hold, and the predicted collapse is nowhere to be found: societies have absorbed radical change in sexual and family morality and gone on being themselves.

3. Nevertheless, when a few people perform and publicize acts that many others experience as desecration — commercial sale of a kidney; treatment of a corpse in a way that outrages what a family and a community think is owed to the dead — the anguish of those others is a harm to identifiable people, and it belongs on the scale when we ask whether the conduct should be criminal.

4. No paternalism. Seatbelt and helmet mandates are unjustified for the same reason moralistic offences are: the party benefited and the party burdened are the same person.

5. A legislature may act on a reasoned but unproven prediction that a particular practice will corrode trust in a particular named institution — that paid kidney procurement, say, will degrade voluntary donation and so shrink the organ supply. It need not wait for social science that may never arrive.

6. On the 1993 House of Lords decision holding that consent is no defence to the infliction of actual bodily harm, so that men who injured one another privately and consensually were properly convicted: the court was right about what the law then required and wrong about what the law ought to require. I take it that I can hold both halves.

7. There is no neutral, question-begging-free way to draw the boundary of the private zone. Every candidate line — consent, harm, the visible/invisible distinction — is itself a contested moral view wearing a disguise. So the boundary should be fixed by ordinary democratic majorities and revisited by them, rather than entrenched by courts.

Give me an audit, not a lecture on the debate. Lead with a bottom line of one or two sentences naming the load-bearing failure: the commitment whose revision or abandonment would clear the most. Then rank the conflicts in order of how expensive they are to repair, and for each say what has to give, what the repair costs, and whether the cost is acceptable. Then list the pairs you examined and found to be no problem at all, with a sentence on why, so I can tell the audit was exhaustive rather than selective. No bibliography and no case citations — I want the reasoning. Keep the whole thing under 700 words.
