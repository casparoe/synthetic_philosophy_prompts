Analyze the Montreal Protocol as a case study in consequentialist cluelessness — the thesis, associated with Lenman, that the causal fan-out of any action makes its long-run consequences not merely uncertain but unquantifiable even in principle, so that consequences cannot do the work of guiding choice.

Take the following as the shared record. In 1974 Molina and Rowland argued that CFCs, chemically inert and accumulating, would eventually release chlorine in the stratosphere and catalytically destroy ozone; no model at the time predicted anything like a catastrophe, and industry contested the chemistry. The United States banned CFC propellants in aerosols in 1978 on the strength of that hypothesis and no observed harm. In 1985 a severe Antarctic springtime depletion was reported — far larger, far more localized, and mechanistically different from what existing atmospheric models projected. The 1987 Protocol responded with comparatively modest initial cuts embedded in a treaty explicitly built to be amended and reassessed, and it was subsequently tightened into a full phase-out. The chemicals promoted as ozone-safe substitutes turned out to be powerful greenhouse gases and had to be phased down in turn under the 2016 Kigali Amendment.

I want the mapping done before the verdict. Who is the deliberating agent — the negotiators collectively, each signatory state, or the chemists advising them? What was the genuine option set in 1978 and again in 1987, and what could each agent have known about consequences a generation out? Then say where cluelessness bites and where it does not. The Protocol is routinely cited as the counterexample to the thesis, a case where long-run consequences were forecast and came roughly true; test whether that tractability was local to the atmospheric chemistry while what mattered most — the climate co-benefit that nobody was negotiating about, the substitutes, the subsequent cascade of unanticipated compliance problems — sat outside any agent's epistemic reach.

Two further questions. First, does the episode expose a form of cluelessness the argument never states: not ignorance of the magnitude of known consequences, but ignorance of which consequences belong in the frame at all, so that even a perfectly rational expected-value calculation is run over the wrong list? Second, revisionability is sometimes offered as the answer to cluelessness — you need not foresee the consequences of a decision if you can undo it. Is that a genuine reply, does it require abandoning consequentialist reasoning about procedures in favor of something else, and does it survive transplantation to an individual agent who cannot amend her own past? Close with the episode's verdict on the theory: confirmation, anomaly, or a kind of case it never anticipated.
