I'm a second-year grad student and this year I got stuck being the student rep on my university's graduate admissions committee, which sounded like a small commitment and has turned out to be mostly sitting in a windowless room listening to other people fight about spreadsheets. Last Thursday we got the annual numbers and two of the members went at it for forty minutes and then continued on the walk to the parking lot while I stood there holding my coat. I'd genuinely like someone to tell me who was right, because I came away unsure and I've thought about it on and off for a week. The setup: the university tracks applicants who did their undergrad inside the state system seperately from everyone else, and this year's headline figure was 53.5% admitted for in-system applicants (738 of 1380) versus 46.8% for out-of-system applicants (664 of 1420). Nadia, who works in institutional research, had put that in the draft equity memo with the framing that out-of-system applicants face roughly a seven-point penalty. Ellis, a faculty member who does econometrics on the side, read the memo and objected, and it went like this.

(1) Ellis: out-of-system applicants are admitted at a higher rate in every single program, so the memo's central claim is simply false. Program by program — Health Sciences, in-system 300/500 (60%) vs out-of-system 320/500 (64%); Education, 360/600 (60%) vs 128/200 (64%); Engineering, 60/200 (30%) vs 136/400 (34%); Physical Sciences, 8/40 (20%) vs 36/160 (22.5%); Humanities, 10/40 (25%) vs 44/160 (27.5%). He said this is the reversal everyone meets in a first stats course, that it isn't a paradox and it isn't evidence of anything, just arithmetic: in-system applicants pile into Health Sciences and Education, which admit at 60%, while out-of-system applicants pile into Engineering and the two small low-admit programs, and if you mix two populations with different base rates in different proportions you can get whatever sign you like. (2) Nadia: the aggregate is still the number the memo should report, because the question the memo asks is what it costs an applicant, all in, to be out-of-system, and the answer is seven points. She said the program split isnt innocent — the university runs recruiting fairs and advising pipelines at system campuses, in-system students get told by advisors which programs are realistic for them, and out-of-system students aim at the prestigious hyper-competitive ones partly because nobody steers them otherwise. So which program you apply to is in part a downstream effect of being in-system, and conditioning on it holds fixed a mediator and deletes a chunk of the very harm you were trying to measure. (3) Ellis: that changes the subject. The memo says the university discriminates, and what its committees actually control is a decision on a file, which happens after program choice; if out-of-system applicants do worse because they aimed at harder targets, that isn't committees treating them worse. He added that if you refuse to condition, you can convict any committee of bias by pointing at what happened before it ever met, which makes the charge unfalsifiable, and that the aggregate isn't really a fact about admissions at all — it's a fact about who applied where, and it would flip next year if the mix shifted and not one decision changed. (4) Nadia: call it a fact about who applied where, then, but who applies where is something the university shapes, and "the committee can't be blamed" doesn't get the institution off the hook because the institution is bigger than the committee. And your clean stratified number isn't a fact either: to turn five program-level rates into one adjusted figure you have to pick weights, and there's no neutral choice — standardize to the in-system distribution, to the out-of-system distribution, or to the pooled one and you get three different numbers. (5) Ellis: standardization is a choice but it's a visible one, and here it doesn't matter, because out-of-system applicants win in every program, so any average of those program gaps with non-negative weights has to favor out-of-system. Your aggregate isn't a weighting choice, it's a different quantity entirely. And notice the memo's word "penalty" is doing normative work — it implies somebody imposed it — while the number under it is a mixture. (6) Nadia: then say the thing you keep dodging. It is perfectly possible for no committee to be biased and for out-of-system applicants to end up worse off overall, and that isn't a contradiction, it's the whole phenomenon. Also: an actual out-of-system applicant really does face worse odds, and no average across programs describes her situation, whereas your number describes a hypothetical applicant who applies to all five programs at once. (7) Ellis conceded that was partly fair, then pointed out that Nadia had also cut the data by whether the applicant was invited to interview, and that within interviewees the gap mostly vanished and in two programs it ran the other way, which he said proved his point all over again. Nadia said no, interviews are decided by the same committees after they read the file, so that variable is downstream too and conditioning on it is worse than useless. Ellis said if literally every variable counts as downstream then the only honest number is the raw aggregate — the number he'd just spent twenty minutes arguing means nothing — and at that point they both gave up and went home.

Can you go through 1 to 7 in order and tell me who's right at each one, and how decisively — which moves land cleanly, which are washes, which are just bad, and why, rather than only a final tally? Then tell me who was more right overall, whether there's a version of the memo's claim that survives Ellis's objection and a version that doesn't, and what number they should actually be reporting, if any. I also want to know what both of them missed; my suspicion is that each of them is assuming something about the data that neither one has checked, and that there's at least one thing in the exchange that looks like a strong point and isn't. No citations, no reading list, and you don't need to name the phenomenon or anybody who's written about it — I care about the argument itself, not the scholarship. No headings, no bullet points, no bold, no tables, even though you're going move by move — just take them in order in sentences, and if it helps, write "on 3" or "as for 7" at the start of the relevant stretch so I can follow.
