I'm one of seven voting members on our state fish and wildlife commission, and Thursday we take up a petition to list the Cinder Fork dace under our state endangered species act. I'm the one who has to write the opinion the rest of the commission reads beforehand, and I'm honestly stuck between two things: what our statute's test says and what the genetics actually show. Our act is broader than the federal one — it defines a protectable unit as any subspecies, variety, or population that is both (a) discrete from the remainder of the taxon and (b) significant to it, where the legislative history glosses discreteness as being a lineage substantially isolated from other populations of the taxon, and significance as contributing something to the taxon that would be lost if that population went. Everything below comes out of the petition record, so work from it rather than from anything you might think you know about this fish — I doubt you've heard of it, and you don't need to cite anything.

The record: the dace was described as a full species in a 1974 monograph on the strength of gill-raker counts, lateral-line scale counts, and a breeding coloration nothing else in the genus has. A 2019 ddRAD study of 84 fish from 11 sites found it nested inside the widespread silver dace rather than sister to it, with no reciprocal monophyly in the nuclear data and evidence of gene flow as recently as a few thousand years ago; the mitochondrial data are reciprically monophyletic, but on two fixed differences and about 0.3% divergence, and the authors say flatly that the mito signal is more consistent with a recent sweep or with drift in a small population than with long isolation. Where the two meet — the bottom six kilometers of Cinder Fork, below an irrigation return flow that only started running in the 1950s — there's a hybrid zone with fertile hybrids and asymmetric introgression, and the genomic cline work finds most of the genome moves freely across it while a handful of loci don't, and those stubborn ones overlap the outliers associated with cold tolerance and with spawning timing. Ecologically the dace is a spring-fed, cobble-bottom, winter-spawning fish that goes onto the redds about seven weeks ahead of the silver dace, confined to roughly 14 stream-kilometers in one basin plus a single population over a pass in the next basin, adult estimate around 12,000. The threat is a pending groundwater withdrawal permit for snowmaking that the modelers say would cut base flows in the upper reach by 20 to 30 percent in dry years. Staff recommends denial, reasoning that the discreteness language was meant to capture lineage independence and the 2019 study shows there isn't any. The petitioners — a watershed group and the tribal natural resources department — say staff is reading a 1988 statute with a genomic-era magnifying glass, and that if low-level gene flow defeats discreteness then half the fish in this state stop being protectable.

Here's the wrinkle. One of my colleagues, who's friendly with the ranchers holding the water rights, has a line he keeps repeating: species are a filing system we invented, the boundary in this creek was drawn by us and not by the fish, and once you admit that the only honest question is where the line does the least damage — and here the line costs something like $40 million. I don't think that's just bad faith and I'm not willing to swat it down with one paragraph about how science is objective, so your memo has to take it seriously and then tell me where it goes wrong, or where it doesn't. So: draft the written opinion I'll circulate Tuesday morning. It needs to land on an actual motion — list the unit as petitioned, deny, list some narrower unit (the hybrid zone is the obvious question, but so is whether the over-the-pass population belongs in or out), or continue for one specific piece of additional work with a deadline, since the act gives us 90 days from the petition and we're at day 61. It needs to state the finding we should enter on discreteness and the finding on significance, separately, and it has to be worded so the same reasoning would decide the two petitions behind this one in the queue (a springsnail complex and a lupine, both with hybrid zones, both with fresh genomic data), because I'm not writing a one-off. I don't want a survey of how biologists define species; if you invoke a concept, make it do work on these numbers. And give the strongest case against whatever you end up recommending its full due — my colleague's point included — not a strawman I could knock over in a sentence.
