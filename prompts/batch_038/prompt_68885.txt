I take minutes for a donor collaborative — about $2M a year, and for the last five it's been split into two blocs that don't much like each other. One funds global health stuff (nets, vitamin A supplementation, cash transfers), the other funds farmed-animal campaigns (cage-free corporate pressure, alt-protein outreach). Every year they fight over the split and the health bloc wins on headcount. Last month Sam (animal bloc) put a three-year package on the table, Nora (health bloc) shot at it, and they went around for like ninety minutes while the rest of us watched. Roughly in order:

1. **Sam:** Under the way we do it now, you win every year and we lose every year. Under my deal you get your 75% locked in for three years plus the extra $200k that two donors said they'd give if the public fighting stopped, and we get a fixed 25% set-aside. Both blocs are better off than under the annual vote we'd actually have. That's not selling anything out, it's just what trade looks like: each side gives up something it values less, at the margin, for something it values more.

2. **Nora:** Better off on whose scale? To call that a Pareto improvement you need one common measure, and the whole deal rests on pretending there's an exchange rate between a child's death averted and a hen's years out of a battery cage. The moment you accept any rate at all you've conceded the thing you claim to deny, and the rate you land on gets set by who has more donors, not by what's true. So this doesn't reveal a compromise, it launders a power imbalance and hands it a three-year contract.

3. **Sam:** You don't need a rate. Nobody in my bloc thinks 25% is the morally correct share — we think it's the share we can get. And you don't have to think it's correct either, you just have to prefer the package to the annual votes, which you do. Preferring a package doesn't commit you to an exchange rate any more than a DA taking a plea deal is committed to a view about the moral worth of the defendant.

4. **Nora:** In practice it does commit you. Next year a new funder asks why a quarter of the budget goes to chickens, and the answer is "because we agreed," and now the agreement is the precedent for the rate. I'll compromise on process all day — scheduling, staffing, what evidence standard programs have to meet, who evaluates. I won't compromise on ends, because then I'm spending money on things I think are worth less than the deaths I'm declining to prevent, and doing it on purpose. That's a different thing from just losing a vote.

5. **Sam:** That's backwards. Under the annual vote you're also spending money on things you think are worth less — you're spending it on nothing, because every year the fight restarts we burn something like 15% of the budget on re-litigating, donor churn, and re-hiring evaluators. And "on purpose" is the improvement, not the problem. At least then the number is chosen instead of falling out of a procedural stalemate.

6. **Nora:** You have never costed that 15%, it's a number you made up on the spot. And notice you just switched. A minute ago you were arguing the deal doesn't require me to share your values; now you're arguing it produces better outcomes. Pick one. If it's outcomes, then the deal has to be compared against every other way of spending the same $500k, and it loses that comparison.

7. **Sam:** Both, and they aren't in tension — the first answers your laundering-power objection and the second answers your spending-on-worse-things objection. Those were two different claims you made. Also, the draft has a reopen clause: either bloc can force renegotiation if a funded program's evaluation comes back badly, so it isn't a fixed rate at all, it's a rate conditional on the evidence holding up.

8. **Nora:** The reopen clause makes it worse. If I can reopen whenever the evidence turns, I'll reopen exactly when my side is losing, and you'll do the same. You've written a contract that only survives as long as neither party needs it, which is to say it's not a contract.
