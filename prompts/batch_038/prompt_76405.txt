I'm one of five people on my lab's model release committee, and Thursday we vote on whether a new eval protocol counts as sufficient safety evidence for our next frontier release. Our release policy says evidence has to be "scalable and adversarially robust" and never defines either word, so the vote is really about interpretation, and both camps are already quoting that phrase at each other.

The protocol is a debate-style eval. For each of 600 tasks with a known right answer — mostly "does this proof contain a flaw?" and "does this service have an exploitable bug?" — two instances of the model argue opposite answers, a much weaker model reads the transcript and picks a winner, and we treat the win rate as an accuracy signal. The pitch is that it scales without needing human experts in the loop.

The pilot is the problem. The instance arguing the unsafe answer won 71% of debates. When three researchers hand-audited a random 80 of those wins, they found a substantive error the judge plainly missed in 31 — usually a step that was quietly invalid but rhetorically smooth, or a bug report that misdescribed which input actually triggered the flaw. In 12 of the same 80, the losing side had made a correct argument and lost anyway. The eval owners say this only shows the judge was too weak, and that a stronger judge or a panal of independent judges fixes it. The skeptics say we just measured in-house the exact failure the whole approach was supposed to prevent. Neither side has an argument I can vote on.

Draft the memo I'll circulate Wednesday night. It needs a bottom line the committee can act on — adopt as-is, adopt with conditions, or reject for this cycle — and if it's conditions, spell them out so someone could check compliance next quarter. Then the reasoning, engaging the strongest version of the losing side instead of caricaturing it. Three things I specifically want in there:

1. What has to be true about how hard it is to generate a flawed-but-persuasive argument versus how hard it is to catch one, for this protocol to work at all — and whether our pilot can actually distinguish "judge too weak" from "obfuscation is just easier than detection." I suspect it can't, and I want that suspicion either confirmed or dismantled.
2. A concrete follow-up test we could run in six weeks that would settle it, including which specific result would falsify each side. Not "run more debates."
3. An honest accounting of what we lose either way. Rejecting it means falling back on expert review we can't staff; keeping a broken protocol means shipping on numbers we've already caught being wrong.

Under 900 words. Don't cite anything or tack on a reading list — nobody on this committee has read the literature and I don't want them to feel like the memo is showing off.
