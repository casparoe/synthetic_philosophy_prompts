I cover science and ideas for a national outlet, and for most of this year I have been reporting on the creator economy — the small number of novelists, musicians, and independent developers who now earn directly from audiences, without a publisher or label in between. The column I am drafting has stalled for a reason I find embarrassing: my own settled judgments about who is entitled to what will not fit together. I would like you to tell me what principle, if any, they add up to, and to tell me plainly which of them I have to give up. I would rather you name and locate the family of position my verdicts put me in than assume I already know it.

Here are the verdicts, in the order I collected them.

1. A novelist serializes her work chapter by chapter. Roughly four million readers each pay a small amount; she ends the decade with twenty million dollars and a readership thousands of times larger than any of her competitors. I think she is entitled to every dollar of it, and that no consideration of how lopsided the resulting distribution is can justify taking any of it from her.

2. A man buys a scrub lot on the edge of a city. Thirty years later the municipality builds a transit hub beside it and the plot is worth forty times what he paid. He did nothing to bring this about. I think the community may recapture most of the increase, and I feel no pull at all toward the view that taking it would be theft.

3. A developer's modest game is picked up by an influential streamer and becomes a phenomenon; his income rises a thousandfold overnight through purchases every one of which was voluntary. I think he is entitled to all of it, exactly as in case 1.

4. A firm acquires the rights to a drug that has been off patent for years and raises the price fiftyfold. Every transaction in the chain was voluntary on both sides. I nonetheless think the patients retain some claim to supply at something like the old price — that the firm's new entitlement does not fully extinguish theirs.

5. During a drought, a company buys the only water source serving a farming town and charges what the market will bear, again with no coercion anywhere in the transaction. Here my reaction is stronger: I do not want the gain merely recaptured, I want the sale unwound. I cannot tell you whether that is a difference in degree from case 4 or a difference in kind.

6. The novelist of case 1 gives her fortune to her children. The giving seems to me entirely her business, yet the children's claim on what they receive feels weaker than hers did, and a substantial tax on the transfer does not strike me as the violation that taxing her earnings would have been. I have no account of why a right should lose force in the moment it is exercised by being handed on.

7. In a town dominated by one employer, the first round of contracts was voluntary and, I think, unobjectionable. Later rounds were signed by people whose alternatives had been narrowed by those very contracts. I want to say the later arrangements are tainted without saying the earlier ones were, and I have no idea where the line falls. I also notice something that worries me more: if each step is unobjectionable and holdings keep accumulating anyway, the voluntariness that justified the steps may quietly disappear, which would mean a principle that looks only at how holdings arose can undermine the conditions of its own application over time.

My own candidate for the principle behind all this is a distinction between what a holder created and what a holder was merely adjacent to. Gains that track something the holder brought into existence are, on this view, inviolable; gains that track value which accrued to an asset the holder happened to be sitting next to are recapturable. Case 1 is created, case 2 is adjacent. But the distinction is already in trouble, and I want it tested rather than flattered. Case 3 looks adjacent in exactly the way case 2 does — the developer sat next to an audience's tastes and a platform's recommendation system — yet I refuse to treat it as recapturable. The landowner in case 2 paid property taxes and bore illiquidity risk for three decades, which starts to look like a contribution. And the novelist's earnings track the existence of a literate public, an enforcement apparatus for contracts, and an algorithm that happened to favor her, as much as they track her prose.

So, four things. First, state the principle — or the small set of rival principles — that best captures these verdicts, clause by clause, saying for each clause which case motivated it. Second, say which of my verdicts the principle cannot accommodate and which one I should drop first; I would most like to keep 1 and 4, and if something has to go I would sacrifice 6, but tell me what I lose by that sacrifice, since 6 may be doing more work than I think. Third, tell me whether a purely historical account of justice in holdings can be saved here or must be supplemented by something sensitive to the shape of the outcome, and what the supplement costs — in particular whether case 7 forces a threshold, and whether any threshold you propose collapses the historical view into the patterned view it was meant to replace. Fourth, give me the principle's verdict on cases I have not listed, beginning with royalties earned on a drug developed in a publicly funded laboratory and patented by a university spin-off; then supply two further cases of your own where it bites hard, including one in which it delivers a verdict I would want to reject.

Finally, say whether my created-versus-adjacent distinction can be repaired, and if it cannot, whether it was doing any work at all or only marking a difference in how much effort I could see.
