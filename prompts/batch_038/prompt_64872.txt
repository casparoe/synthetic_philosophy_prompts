I did low-temperature physics for thirty-eight years and only got properly into philosophy after retiring, so forgive the naivety. In the reading group I've joined, whenever Leibniz's question comes up — why is there something rather than nothing — somebody waves it away with what I've started calling the vacuum analogy: the quantum vacuum isn't nothing at all, it's a restless state with fields and zero-point energy, particle-antiparticle pairs blink in and out of it all the time, and a whole universe can tunnel out of it; and anyway the account balances, because the positive energy of matter is offset by the negative energy of gravity, so the cosmos is a free lunch that costs nothing to produce. My former colleague reads this stuff too and pushes it hard, and he says asking why there are quantum laws in the first place is like asking why water is wet — a category error dressed up as profundity. The conclusion being licensed is that science has already answered the question and philosophy can go home.

Could you take the analogy apart for me? I'd want the correspondence laid out plainly: what stands in for the nothing, what for the something, what the fluctuation maps onto, what the balancing ledger maps onto. Then where the mapping fails. Two things from my own field keep nagging: total energy in general relativity isn't the clean additive conserved quantity we used to measure at the bench, and virtual particles aren't really little objects appearing and vanishing. Do those kill it or just dent it? Sort the disanalogies into two seperate piles — the ones that actually matter for the conclusion my colleague wants, and the ones that are just pedantry — tell me what the analogy is quietly importing from a laboratory setting that the cosmos as a whole doesnt have, say whether the water-is-wet move is any good, and if there's a better analogy that doesn't oversell, what it'd be and where it too would break.
