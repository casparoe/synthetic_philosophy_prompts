I teach a decision-theory seminar to a mixed group of philosophy and economics graduate students, and every year the same thing happens in the week we reach Allais. I put the two choice pairs on the board, take a live poll, and roughly four-fifths of the room takes the certain million in the first pair and then the ten-percent shot at five million in the second. When I show them that the second pair is nothing but the first with the eighty-nine-percent branch struck out — a million in the first pair, nothing in the second — they are not embarrassed; they insist they must have misread the options. This year I would like to open the session with a poem instead of the usual table, something that makes the substitution visible in the shape of the verse rather than in the algebra.

Here is what the poem has to get right. First pair: option A pays a million with certainty; option B pays five million with probability one in ten, a million with probability eighty-nine in a hundred, and nothing with probability one in a hundred. Second pair: option C pays a million with probability eleven in a hundred and nothing otherwise; option D pays five million with probability one in ten and nothing otherwise. C and D are what you get from A and B when the branch that both options of a pair share — the eighty-nine-percent chance, a million in the first pair and nothing in the second — is replaced simultaneously in both. The chooser in the poem takes A over B and then D over C, knows it, and should not have the flip presented as a slip or a miscalculation; the poem should stage it. I want the closing to make plain what the flip costs — the principle that a component shared by both options can be set aside, on the grounds that what both have in common cannot be what settles the ranking between them — and I want the poem to stop there rather than adjudicate whether that principle or the chooser is the thing that has to go.

For the form I would like a strict pantoum: four quatrains, sixteen lines, with lines 2 and 4 of each stanza recurring verbatim as lines 1 and 3 of the next, and with lines 2 and 4 of the final stanza being lines 1 and 3 of the first. Please give the recurring lines the job of carrying what genuinely stays fixed across the two pairs — the one-in-ten shot at the larger prize, which is in B and in D alike, and the fact that eighty-nine chances in a hundred go the same way for both options whichever pair one is in — and let the other lines carry what changes: the prize attached to those eighty-nine, and the certainty. The effect I am after is that a reader can point at the two lines that keep coming back and say that those are precisely the ones that ought to have cancelled, and that the final stanza's return of the opening lines lands as the reversal rather than as a decorative echo. Two further constraints: the name of the axiom, or of Allais, may appear at most once and only in the last quatrain, and the figures should appear in the poem as figures, so that the class can check them against the board. First person, in the chooser's own voice, no casino imagery and no limerick bounce. Afterward, in three sentences at most, tell me which two lines are doing the work of the common consequence, and where you think the poem risks overstating the case against the axiom.
