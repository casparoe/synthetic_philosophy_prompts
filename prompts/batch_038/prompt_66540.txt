I'm writing the part of our org's handbook that says when a spoken or typed thing actually counts as a commitment, and I've realised my intuitions about this are a mess. Verdicts below. I want to know what principle, if any, they add up to. You don't need to cite anything or give me sources — I just want the principle stated precisely enough that I could hand it to a lawyer or a first-year student and they could apply it.

Where I think the words DO the thing:
- A judge pronounces the sentence out loud in open court, having quietly taken a bribe the week before. Still a real sentence, still binding on the defendant. Bad act, successful act.
- A head of state reads out a declaration of war on television. War declared, and I think it's declared even if nobody on the other side is watching.
- At the office party, in front of witnesses, someone furious says "I quit" to their manager's face and walks out. Next morning they say they didn't mean it and want their desk back. I think they quit.
- Two friends: "fifty quid it rains Saturday" — "you're on." Bet exists.
- Someone types their name in the box beside "I accept these terms" without reading a single word of them. Bound, I'd say.

Where I think they DON'T:
- The same declaration-of-war sentence posted by a private citizen on their own feed. Nothing happens, nobody's declared anything.
- An actor saying the promise lines in a play, eight shows a week. No promises made, obviously.
- Someone alone in their kitchen on Thursday rehearsing the vows they'll say on Saturday.
- A launch where a bystander who isn't the appointed sponsor grabs the bottle, smashes it, and names the ship. I want to say the ship isn't named by him — but I can't tell you why, and I half suspect I should give this one up.
- A promise made with a gun at your head. My feeling: the promise was genuinely made, and you're still not held to it. That's a different kind of failure from the actor's, and I have no way of marking the difference.

The pair thats killing me:
- Betting needs uptake. If I say "fifty quid it rains" and you just change the subject, I feel strongly there's no bet. So the other person taking it up is doing real work in my verdicts.
- But forgiveness: standing at a grave and saying I forgive you, or writing it in a letter I never send — I want to say the forgiving happened. Nobody took it up. So uptake can't be necessary.
- And then, worse: apology. Saying sorry under your breath where nobody can hear, or in a note you burn — I don't think that's an apology that got made. Forgiveness without a hearer works, apology without a hearer doesn't, and both look to me like the speaker changing their own standing rather than creating anything between two people. That's the asymmetry I can't explain at all.

My own guess at a principle is roughly: the words do the thing when there's a convention covering them, the speaker is the right person in the right circumstances, and everyone who has to be party to it knows the convention's in play. But that seems to get the gun case wrong (or right, I can't tell which), and I don't see how it separates the grave-side forgiveness from the burnt apology.

Can you give me:

1. "What my verdicts commit me to" — 150–200 words on the structural features my answers are already leaning on, including any place they're inconsistent in a way I haven't spotted.
2. "Candidate principles" — 400–500 words, two or three, each stated as actual conditions rather than vibes, each with a line saying which of my cases motivated each clause.
3. "The uptake problem" — 250–300 words on the bet / grave-side forgiveness / burnt apology triangle. If one of those three verdicts has to go, say which and argue for it rather than just noting the tension.
4. "Off my list" — about 200 words giving your verdicts on three cases I didn't think of: a promise made aloud to a voice assistant that logs it; a vow made to yourself, alone, and then broken; a resignation emailed at 11pm to a boss who never opens it, found by HR a week later.
5. "What I should give up" — 150 words, the verdicts of mine no decent principle can save, ranked by how much it'd hurt to lose them.
