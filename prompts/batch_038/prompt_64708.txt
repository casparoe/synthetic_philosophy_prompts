Think carefully before answering, and where two defensible designs conflict, pick one and say what you gave up.

I run a one-person review function at a company that ships autonomous agents into enterprise workflows: procurement approvals, ticket triage, calendar and inventory coordination. Roughly twice a week someone from engineering, incident response, or the commercial team sends me a sentence they intend to put in an outward-facing document — a customer-facing system card, a postmortem that will go to a regulator, a procurement response, a due-diligence questionnaire — and the sentence ascribes a mental state to one of our models. Typical submissions: the agent believed the invoice was already approved; the model wants to keep the session open; the system thought it was speaking to an administrator; the model does not care whether it is shut down. My job is to return one of three answers — pass, block, or rewrite — usually within thirty minutes.

The constraints are as follows. The people who will actually run the procedure have no philosophy training and cannot be asked to reason about interpretive holism or rationality presuppositions from first principles; they need questions to ask in a fixed order. The evidence I typically have on hand is transcripts, behavioral test results and reruns, red-team logs, and occasionally a small probe or activation study — I do not have an interpretability team, and I often have less than that. Someone senior signs off and will read at most one page. The costs are asymmetric and both are real: a false positive means we have put a claim about a system's internal states into a legal document we may not be able to support, and a false negative means our postmortems read as evasive, because a document that scrubs all mentalistic language is widely read as concealment.

Please write me a reusable decision procedure for this. It should contain: an ordered list of tests, each with a plain-language question, what would count as a sufficient answer, what kinds of artifact can and cannot supply it, and what to do when the relevant evidence is simply missing; explicit gates where the answer is an outright no; a short rewrite playbook showing how to convert a blocked ascription into a claim we can stand behind — behavioral, dispositional, or design-level — with a sample rewrite for each blocked verdict; and a clearly marked set of steps that are irreducibly judgment calls rather than lookups, which I will have to escalate.

I want the procedure to preserve what actually does the work in the theory rather than borrow its vocabulary. At a minimum it should engage with four things. First, that the intentional stance is a strategy for generating predictions, and whether a license earned by predictive success transfers to a document that asserts what the system's states are; if it does not transfer, the procedure should say so and show what does. Second, that ordinary belief attribution presupposes a mostly coherent and mostly true web of attitudes, and that our agents routinely violate that presupposition — they avow one thing under direct probing, do another in behavior, and do a third under a differently worded probe — so the procedure needs a rule for what to do when the evidence for an ascription contradicts itself rather than merely running thin. Third, that the design stance can sometimes support a mentalistic sentence that behavior alone cannot, and can sometimes undercut one that behavior seems to support; the procedure should say which direction applies and under what conditions. Fourth, that some ascriptions are doing explanatory or predictive work and others are doing rhetorical work — making the system sound competent to a buyer, or making it sound blameless to a regulator — and that the same sentence can shift categories depending on the document. If you judge that any of these four can be dropped without loss from a thirty-minute procedure, say which and why.
