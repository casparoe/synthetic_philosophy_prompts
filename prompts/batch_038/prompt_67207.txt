So here's my situation. I run a little reading group on vagueness — five of us, Tuesdays, mostly ex-philosophy people who now do data work — and we've hit a stalemate that I'm pretty sure is about the scoreboard rather than the players. One of us is an epistemicist about "bald"-type predicates, one is a supervaluationist, and every time somebody raises an objection the answer is "well, that isn't a real requirement, it's just your view wearing a trenchcoat." Which is sometimes probably right! So before we read any more I want the neutral spec: what does an account of vague predicates actually have to deliver to count as adequate, stated in terms both of them could accept as the conditions of the argument.

What I want per item: the requirement, the case or consideration behind it (a real puzzle, not a slogan), and — this is the part I care about most — what failing it would look like. Give me a test, not a vibe. E.g. if the list says an account has to explain why the tolerence premise feels compelling even though it has to reject it, what concrete datum shows an account failing to explain that rather than just waving at it? Same deal for the higher-order stuff, for our ignorance of any cutoff (and if the explanation is a safety/margins-for-error style story, say what would falsify it), for the problem of the many, for whether an account lets a legislature or a court sharpen a term by stipulation without changing the subject, and for the learnability point — kids pick these predicates up from finite examples and then agree with adults, which seems to constrain things.

Also, I ran a small pilot over the summer: 40 subjects, a forced-choice series of 600 color patches running from unambiguous red to unambiguous orange, yes/no on "is this red?" What came out was a smooth sigmoid spread across maybe 150 patches, not a step function with noise around one patch. I don't know how much weight that carries — could be response noise, could be an artifact of how I ordered the trials — but I want it on the list as a candidate requirement, and I want you to say honestly whether it is one, and what each camp would owe us if it is. Then sort the whole thing for me: which requirements are genuinely neutral, which are one camp's commitments in disguise, and whether some subset can't all be satisfied at once — if there is such a subset, show the derivation, that's the payof I'm after. Last, name the one requirement that does the most exclusionary work, the one that already rules out most of the field if you take it seriously, and then run epistemicism and supervaluationism against the full list, briefly and only briefly. Don't pick a winner and don't hand me a reading list — I'll get to the primary material once I know what I'm supposed to be looking for in it.
