I am three years into my fourth novel, and for the last fourteen months I have been living inside the working documents of American fact-checking: the published rationales, the style guides, the appeals, the internal memos about how to word a ruling. My protagonist runs a small nonprofit verification outfit in a mid-size river city, and she has contracted with one of the large platforms to rate viral posts. Her shop grades each eligible statement on a six-step ladder that runs from "True" through mostly true, half true, mostly false and false, and ends in a sixth category, "Pants on Fire," reserved for claims that are not merely wrong but outlandish. Before a statement can enter the ladder at all it must clear a gate: it has to be checkable, so overt opinion, prediction, value judgment and obvious hyperbole are ruled out of scope and never graded. Alongside the ladder the shop maintains a public tracker of campaign promises, scored on whether each has been kept, stalled or broken, and once a year it names a single "Lie of the Year." On the platform side, the mechanics are different but the logic looks continuous: a statement rated false gets a warning overlay and reduced distribution, and pages that repeatedly circulate rated-false material lose reach and monetization. My editor keeps telling me that the crisis at the center of the book cannot be generic burnout or a facile both-sides epiphany — she wants my protagonist's breakdown to be intellectual, and exact, and I want to be sure I have actually understood the thing she has devoted her career to before I write her losing faith in it.

So I would like you to reconstruct the implicit epistemology of this whole apparatus — the ladder, the checkability gate, the promise tracker, the overlay-and-demote regime — as a single philosophy of knowledge and correction, and then tell me how much of it is load-bearing. What view of truth does a graduated verdict presuppose? Truth is not usually thought to come in degrees, yet the ladder grades it six ways, so what is the quantity actually being measured: the proportion of a claim's components that survive checking, the strength of the evidence available, the care the speaker took, or the impression a reasonable listener would carry away? The shop's own practice seems to slide among these, since it insists on rating the speaker's exact words while also allowing that a technically defensible statement can still leave a misleading overall impression. And what is going on in the sixth category and in the "Lie of the Year," which appear to smuggle in something about the speaker's character — absurdity, bad faith, culpability — into an instrument that everywhere else claims to measure only accuracy? I want to know whether that is an inconsistency she should feel guilty about or a tacit admission that the ladder was never really about truth-conditions. Similarly, what does the checkability gate commit her to? It seems to assume that public discourse divides cleanly into truth-apt propositions on one side and non-cognitive expression on the other, and that the second side can be set aside without loss — but the claims that most move belief in her city are precisely the ones the gate excludes. Tell me which rival pictures of knowledge the gate and the ladder quietly rule out: views on which a claim's content cannot be assessed apart from the surrounding web of commitments that gives it meaning, or on which the interesting question about a public assertion is never its isolated truth-value but what it does to the audience's trust.

Here is the part I most need help with, and the part my protagonist cannot articulate for herself, which is why she is my protagonist and not my mouthpiece. The whole apparatus, in both its newsroom and its platform forms, appears to assume that the pathology of a misled public is a shortage of accurate information arriving through a channel the public already accepts: identify the false proposition, publish the correction, and belief will follow the correction, with the platform's demotion merely adjusting the odds that people encounter it. But there is a rival diagnosis available, which her own reporting keeps stumbling across and which she has no vocabulary for — that some of her audience is not uninformed but enclosed, embedded in a closed circuit of trust in which the institutions outside the circuit are precisely the ones already discredited, so that her ruling arrives not as information but as a marker of which side she is on, and can even strengthen the belief it was meant to dislodge. On that diagnosis the trouble is not propositional but relational, and the corrective is not a better verdict but the reconstruction of channels of credibility — endorsement across camps rather than by majority, credentialing of sources rather than scoring of sentences, preemptive exposure to weakened versions of a coming falsehood rather than post-hoc refutation. I want you to say whether the first diagnosis is genuinely presupposed by the practice as it exists, or only the most natural way to rationalize it — that is, whether some second-order epistemology about deference, testimony, and who is entitled to speak for the informed could license exactly the same ladder and the same overlays. And then: does the institution's own structure — a standing panel of unelected adjudicators whose authority depends on being accepted in advance by the people it corrects — sit well or badly with each of the two diagnoses? Finally, spell out what would have to change in the day-to-day mechanics if her shop adopted the rival view wholesale: which parts of the ladder and the checkability gate survive, which are abolished, what replaces the annual prize, and what happens to the platform contract. Distinguish throughout between what the practice strictly cannot do without and what it merely happens to fit comfortably, flag the places where several incompatible theories would license the identical procedure, and give me the argument rather than a list of thinkers or titles to chase down; I have no time to verify references and no interest in a bibliography, only in whether my protagonist is wrong, and if so, about what exactly.
