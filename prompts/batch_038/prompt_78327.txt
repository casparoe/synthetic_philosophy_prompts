For a decade I've read the rationalist-blog treatment of the frame problem, and I've come away with a distinction my day job refuses to make. The narrow logical version — writing frame axioms for every fluent an action leaves untouched — is more or less a solved engineering matter, absorbed into successor-state axioms and fluent occlusion, with the Yale shooting problem as a museum piece. What is not solved, and what the philosophical discussion was really worried about, is which facts are relevant to an action at all. My day job consists entirely of the second problem, and nobody has handed me a way to review it.

I'm the technical lead on a twelve-person team building an autonomous mobile robot that replenishes picking slots in an operating warehouse. Most of the team are strong software engineers with no formal AI training and no philosophy; a hardware partner signs off the safety case, and I sign off the specs. Roughly twice a month I have to approve a behaviour spec — resume after a pallet has been relocated, recover from a blocked aisle, act on a slot last observed nine minutes ago — and each one silently commits us to an answer about which facts the planner assumes persist, which it re-senses, and which it is permitted to ignore. The failures come in two flavours: an unmaintainable thicket of preconditions and frame clauses nobody understands, or a robot acting with total confidence on a stale belief about the world.

Please think carefully, and start by brainstorming the space of decisions a reviewer in this position actually faces before you write anything normative. Then give me a spec-review procedure: an ordered set of questions; for each, what counts as a sufficient answer from the author and what evidence I should demand; the points at which the procedure should return "not ready"; and the points at which the question is one of risk appetite or of values and must be escalated to me or to the safety sign-off rather than settled by the checklist. It has to be runnable in about an hour by engineers who will never read McCarthy and Hayes, and it should say explicitly which of the frame problem proper, the ramification problem, and the qualification problem a team can dissolve by stipulation and which it cannot.

Then run it on three cases: a pallet a human relocates while the robot is mid-task; a chilled slot whose contents degrade without any action of ours; and a proposal from a senior engineer to re-sense the whole relevant world before every action, which I currently favour. I expect the procedure to overrule me on the third. If it does, say so plainly and price the cheap-looking fix.

Close with what the procedure cannot do — where it will misfire, and which decisions it merely relocates rather than resolves.
