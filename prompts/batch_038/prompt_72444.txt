I should like a sestina on metaphysical grounding and fundamentality in which the form, rather than the diction, does the philosophical work.

Take these six words as your end-words, in this order for the opening stanza: ground, because, whole, nothing, below, rest. Then follow the traditional permutation throughout, so that the six stanzas end as follows:

1. ground / because / whole / nothing / below / rest
2. rest / ground / below / because / nothing / whole
3. whole / rest / nothing / ground / because / below
5. nothing / below / ground / whole / rest / because
4. below / whole / because / rest / ground / nothing
6. because / nothing / rest / below / whole / ground

and close with a three-line envoi whose line-endings are below, whole, ground, carrying rest, nothing and because inside the lines. Write in unrhymed lines of roughly ten syllables.

Please exploit the range of each end-word rather than repeating a single sense of it: ground as floor, as basis, as the sediment settled at the bottom of a cup; because as the connective of a causal story and, quite differently, of a metaphysical one; rest as coming to rest, as remainder, as what is simply left over; nothing as absence and also as the negative way of picking out whatever owes its character to no further thing. The rotation must be made to mean something. No stanza introduces an item that has not already appeared, so the reader should come to feel that the descent is not an endless supply of fresh candidates for the fundamental but a finite stock returning in a new order each time: not turtles all the way down, but the same six turtles, re-shuffled.

Set the descent in something ordinary and handleable — a house and its floors, a well, a stack of objects, a cup on a table. Let the vertical imagery do its seductive work, and then let the poem notice that it is only an image: the direction in which one thing holds in virtue of another is not a direction in space. At some point, and the envoi is the natural place, make room for the inverted picture on which the whole is prior to its parts rather than assembled from them, since the envoi is the one moment in which the finished poem holds all six words at once.

Two further things should be audible without being announced as doctrine. First, that the relevant because is supposed to explain, and not merely to report that two things always vary together; a stanza in which it threatens to collapse into the second thing would be welcome. Second, that there is a deflationary suspicion abroad according to which, once the base is fixed, everything else comes for free and the because is doing no further work at all. Someone who works on this material should recognise the dialectic — the demand for an unconditioned floor, the possibility of a descent that never terminates, the possibility that dependence runs in a circle — and should not wince at any line; someone who has never met the topic should simply find it a good poem about looking for the one thing that does not lean.

After the poem, add a note of no more than 250 words saying where it takes liberties. Does it treat grounding as a single unified relation, or as a family of smaller ones, and which stanzas depend on that choice? Is it committed to well-foundedness, or does it merely entertain the failure of well-foundedness while secretly preferring it? Which stanza is most likely to strike a specialist as getting things wrong, and what exactly is wrong with it? Finally, say what would change your mind about the poem's verdict — which argument or consideration would make you end the descent differently, in the envoi or elsewhere — and where you think your own reasoning about the material is most likely to fail. Please write throughout in British English. I do not want references, a bibliography, or a reading list.
