**The question as assigned** (25 points; students were told to stay near 500 words):

In *Making Up People* (1986) and in the chapter titled *The Looping Effects of Human Kinds*, Ian Hacking argues that classifications of people differ in kind from classifications of, say, quarks.

(a) Explain the distinction Hacking draws between the two sorts of classification, and explain what a looping effect is.
(b) Explain why, on Hacking's account, looping effects pose a distinctive problem for a psychiatry that models itself on the natural sciences.
(c) A working group proposes that the next revision of the diagnostic manual include a new category, dysregulated online engagement (DOE), defined solely by a checklist of observable items — hours of daily use, number of nocturnal awakenings to check a device, self-reported inability to stop, neglect of previously routine obligations — and explicitly silent about causes, mechanism, and whether the pattern counts as a disorder at all. The group's stated rationale is that by declining to assert anything about what DOE is, they immunise the category against looping effects. Assess this rationale using Hacking's framework.

**The student's answer:**

Hacking's claim is that the classifications of the human sciences differ in kind from those of the natural sciences. A classification of quarks is what he calls an indifferent kind: nothing about quarks depends on how we sort them, and the classification is neutral, carrying no political or moral weight. A classification of people is an interactive kind, and it is loaded, because to be labelled is to be placed in a social position.

Once an interactive kind is in circulation, the people who fall under it can come to know the description, and they either grow into it or resist it. Their behaviour changes accordingly, and the changed behaviour no longer matches the description that produced it, so the experts must revise the category. Hacking calls this a moving target, and it is why psychiatry cannot be a science on the natural-science model: it wants generalisations that support prediction and intervention, but every successful generalisation alters the population it generalises over, so the evidence base for one edition of the manual is partly destroyed by the manual's own publication.

Two inferences must be handled carefully here. First, looping effects are a version of Merton's self-fulfilling prophecy, generalised from single predictions to whole taxonomies: a description of a kind of person becomes true because it is believed, and looping is that process happening repeatedly rather than once. Second, looping does not license debunking. Hacking is not arguing that looping kinds are unreal or that the people who come to inhabit them are faking. That reported alternate personalities multiplied once multiple personality became an available diagnosis does not show that the earlier patients were mistaken about themselves; it shows that a new way to be a person was created. Looping is a thesis about the epistemology of the human sciences, not a verdict on the reality of the disorder.

On (c), the working group is wrong, though not for the reason it might seem. Hacking's account does not require a causal theory in order for a category to loop; it requires only that the classification be known to those classified and usable as a self-description. A checklist is still a name, and names travel: once DOE is invoked in schools, in workplace accommodations, and in online communities, people will describe themselves in its terms, the distribution of the checked behaviours will shift, and the committee will have to move its cut-offs. Silence about etiology therefore removes nothing essential. Hacking himself treats the descriptive turn in the third edition of the manual as evidence that the strategy can work, since its authors hoped that by listing symptoms and abstaining from theories of causation they would obtain stable categories and reliable diagnosis. But this could only help if the categories never reached the people they describe, which no diagnostic category achieves, and in any case a purely descriptive checklist is not really free of theory, since choosing which behaviours to list already asserts what hangs together with what. The rationale fails because a classification cannot opt out of being known.
