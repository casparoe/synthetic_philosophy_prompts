**Student's answer:**

> Moore tried to refute the sceptic by holding up his hands and claiming to know that here is one hand and here is another. Wittgenstein's response in his final notes is that Moore has got the grammar of 'know' wrong. To say that you know something implies that you could be mistaken, implies that you have grounds, and implies that there is some way of finding out. None of that applies to Moore's propositions. He has no evidence for them, he could not say how he came to know them, and in the circumstances it would make no sense to doubt them. So they are not items of knowledge at all, and Moore has not proved anything against the sceptic; he has only made a noise.
>
> What Wittgenstein offers instead is a different role for such propositions. Things like 'the earth existed long before my birth' or 'objects do not vanish when nobody is looking at them' work as hinges: the door turns on them, but they themselves stay put, and it is only against a background of such propositions that testing, justifying and doubting can get going at all. Doubt presupposes certainty. A doubt that doubted everything would not be a doubt, because it would have destroyed the rules that determine what counts as a reason, a test or a mistake. Hinges are therefore not justified; they are what justification is measured against, and demanding grounds for them gets the order of explanation backwards. Nor are they beliefs we merely hold very strongly. The certainty involved shows itself in how we act — in the fact that certain possibilities simply do not occur to us as things to investigate — rather than in any inner feeling of conviction.
>
> The consequences for scepticism are the interesting part. Because hinges are groundless, Wittgenstein's position amounts to a kind of relativism about knowledge. Each of us inherits a world-picture, and inside it some things stand fast while others are open to question. The sceptic is working with a different set of hinges: for him, 'there is an external world' is not something that stands fast but something up for debate. Since there is no framework-neutral standpoint from which to compare the two world-pictures, the sceptic cannot be refuted — but neither can he be shown to be mistaken, since 'mistake' only has a use within a framework. The two sides are simply playing different games.
>
> This is also why the closure principle has to be abandoned. We can know that we have hands, and we can know that having hands entails that there is an external world, but we cannot know that there is an external world; knowledge is not closed under known entailment, and once you see that the sceptical argument loses its bite, because it depends on transferring warrant from the everyday proposition to the sceptical hypothesis. Contemporary hinge epistemologists make the same point by talking about entitlement: we are entitled to accept our hinge commitments without evidence and without doing anything wrong. Wittgenstein's riverbed image says the same thing in a picture. The water of ordinary empirical propositions flows along a bed made of hardened ones, and although the bed can be reshaped over time, what lies in the bed is not itself up for revision on the basis of experience.
