I am finalizing the position chapter of a monograph on political ethics — the chapter in which I set out my own account of the problem of dirty hands — and before circulating it I would like a rigorous consistency audit of the package. Below are my commitments, numbered for reference. They are stated as I actually hold them, not as I would defend them in print, so please treat the wording as load-bearing.

1. There are agent-relative moral constraints — paradigmatically against torture and against the intentional killing of non-combatants — which are not rules of thumb and which no aggregation of benefits to others can override.

2. The distinction between what an agent intends and what she merely foresees is real and does substantive normative work. I am unmoved by the standard closeness objections to it.

3. The obligations attaching to political office are genuine moral obligations, generated by the one morality I accept together with the institutional facts of the office. I reject the thesis that politics is an autonomous normative domain with first principles of its own.

4. There is a narrow exemption, which I prefer to define by the kind of threat rather than by numbers — the imminent destruction of a rights-respecting political community, in the spirit of Walzer's supreme emergency — under which the duty described in 3 overrides the constraint described in 1. I mean overrides robustly: all things considered, the officeholder should authorize the otherwise prohibited act, and a critic who says that she acted wrongly is simply mistaken.

5. There are no genuine moral dilemmas. For any agent at any time there is exactly one thing she ought to do all things considered, and that ought implies can. I regard this as a constraint on any adequate moral theory rather than as a stipulation of mine.

6. The dirt in dirty hands is real, and it is guilt rather than mere regret. The officeholder who does the prohibited act has done wrong, and theories that leave her with nothing more than the distinctive sorrow an agent feels at harm her own agency has caused — the attitude Williams describes — have sanitized the phenomenon and missed the point of the literature.

7. The citizens on whose behalf the prohibited act was done owe the officeholder gratitude, and she deserves admiration rather than blame. The abstainer who keeps his hands clean when the override was required is a moral free-rider, and I hold him to be worse, not better, than the one who bears the dirt.

8. My own account, which I call the ledger view: every violation of a constraint opens a debt owed to the particular persons violated — not to the community, not to morality at large — which cannot be cancelled by benefits conferred on anyone else, including the very community for whose sake the violation occurred. Such debts can only be paid, and only to their creditors. The officeholder should therefore afterwards do what the ledger requires — reparations, public naming of the victims, acceptance of prosecution where the law provides for it — while remaining convinced that she did what she should have done.

9. The principal practical upshot of this whole debate is institutional: procedures, committees, statutory authorizations, and distributed chains of command arranged so that no single agent's agency is decisive and hence so that no individual has to bear the dirt. I regard such diffusion as a moral improvement, not as an evasion.

10. A verdict on a case I use to test the account. Call it the Courtyard Shot. A governor faces a prison riot; one rioter holds a nurse at knifepoint in the yard, and verified intelligence indicates that he will kill her within minutes. The governor authorizes a marksman to shoot to kill. The shot is clean: the rioter dies, the nurse is unharmed, and no one else is touched. A diary that surfaces years later records that she gave the order with elation, that she had been looking for an occasion to see this particular man dead because he had once testified against her administration, and that she was relieved the hostage situation had supplied one. My verdict: she acted wrongly; she is properly blamed; no one owes her gratitude; and this is not a dirty hands case at all, since nothing prohibited was done.

Do not argue for consequentialism, for the autonomy of politics, or against any of these views on the merits; I am asking what my own set commits me to, not which of the standard positions is true.
