For forty-one years I worked in vegetation science, and a large part of that time was spent doing something philosophers seem never to do: identifying specimens with a dichotomous key. A key is a severe instrument. Every couplet must turn on a character that can actually be observed in the thing in front of you, and a key that asks whether a sedge is "rather grasslike" is worthless, because the user cannot answer it without already knowing which species it is. Since retiring I have taken up political philosophy, and I find that its discussions of empire are conducted almost entirely in that useless register: people announce that they are for or against a given author's position without ever having been shown a case on which the positions genuinely come apart.

I now run a small reading group of seven — retired engineers mostly, one physician, two former schoolteachers. We have read the opening chapters of On Liberty together with Mill's discussion, in his book on representative government, of how a free state ought to govern a dependency, and we have got thoroughly stuck. My own paraphrase of the difficulty is this. Mill restricts his liberty principle to human beings in the maturity of their faculties; he says that it does not cover children, and he extends the exclusion to societies he takes to be incapable of being improved by free and equal discussion; and he holds that rule by a more advanced people over such a society can be legitimate provided the end is that society's improvement and the means really do effect it. Elsewhere he casts the governing country as a trustee, denies the governed any share in the government, and locates the restraint on abuse in the institutions and public opinion of the governing country itself. Two members of my group say this exception swallows the principle whole. A third says it is nothing more than the principle applied to a collective child. I should like an instrument that tells me whether those two verdicts describe genuinely different positions or one position in two costumes.

Please write that instrument as a dichotomous key to positions on the permissibility of ruling another people without their consent. I am not asking for a history of British administration in India, and I do not want Mill either defended or condemned; I want the key. Specifically:

1. Begin with the character list rather than the tree. Enumerate the separable commitments — consent, competence, the improvement condition, the identity of the judge, revisability, the standing of the governed population's own preferences, and whatever else belongs — so that we see the logical space before we see the paths cut through it.
2. Then give the key itself as indented text I can photocopy and hand out: no more than nine couplets, no more than eight leaves.
3. Every couplet must be a concrete administrative case. Use at least six of the following: a famine in which the local grain trade has collapsed and a foreign administration commandeers distribution; a civil code drawn up by foreign jurists in a language most of the governed cannot read; recruitment to the administration by open competitive examination conducted in the ruler's language; annexation of a territory whose ruler has died without an heir recognised by the ruler's own customs; a school system taught wholly in the ruler's language; an irrigation or railway works that raises revenue for the ruler and incomes for the governed; deportation of subjects who criticise the administration. No couplet may name a philosopher, an ism, or a century, and none may be answerable only by someone who already knows where it leads.
4. Tag every couplet as threshold or scalar — threshold meaning it admits a plain yes or no on the facts as given, scalar meaning it is a matter of degree. Where a scalar character is unavoidable, state the cut-point you are imposing and give one sentence in its defence. A key built only from scalar characters never terminates, and I will not accept one.
5. For each couplet, name the false positive: the answer that sends a reader to the wrong leaf, together with the single follow-up question that would catch it.
6. Name each leaf first in a descriptive coinage of your own. Attach a historical label only where you are confident it is correct; where you are not, leave the leaf unnamed and characterise the view in one sentence instead. Four leaves are mandatory: Mill's own position as I have paraphrased it above; the position that admits no competence-based exception to consent whatever; the position that foreign rule is justified by the ruler's power or achievement with no improvement condition attached; and the position that permits temporary trusteeship only where the exit criterion is fixed in advance and verified by someone other than the trustee.
7. One branch must turn on who judges whether the governed people has become capable of governing itself, on what evidence, and whether that judgment can be reversed by anyone other than the judge. Put this as a case, not as a name.
8. Report the unoccupied combinations in your logical space and diagnose each: is nobody there because the view is incoherent, because it is unattractive, or merely because it has not been explored?
9. Flag explicitly any pair of leaves your key cannot separate, and state what question would separate them. In particular, tell me where the "swallows the principle" verdict and the "collective child" verdict land.
10. Close with a consistency audit: a short table listing, for the three answer patterns you expect to be most common in a room of well-meaning liberals, the tension each pattern leaves the reader holding.

No bibliography, no citations, no page references, and no attributions you have not actually checked. If you are unsure who held a view, say so in the leaf rather than guessing at it.
