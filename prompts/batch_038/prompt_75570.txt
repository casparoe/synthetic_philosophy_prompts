For the examination paper in our graduate seminar on the semantics of conditionals, I need one question worth 25 marks, to be answered in 45 minutes. It must not be answerable by reciting the familiar story that Lewis showed Stalnaker's Thesis — that the probability of an indicative conditional equals the conditional probability of its consequent given its antecedent — to be incompatible with conditionals denoting propositions. Build the question instead around the following toy model, which should be stated for the candidates in the paper itself:

An algebra generated by four atoms w1–w4, each with prior probability 1/4; let A = {w1, w2} and C = {w1, w3}. A centered selection function f assigns, for antecedent A: f(A, w1) = w1, f(A, w2) = w2, f(A, w3) = w1, f(A, w4) = w2; so the conditional A→C denotes the proposition {w1, w3}.

Part (a) should require candidates to verify that this assignment satisfies the thesis for the prior, and then to exhibit a conditionalization of that prior — updating on evidence that rules out exactly one atom, with f held fixed — on which the thesis fails, computing both sides explicitly. Part (b) should ask what this tells us about where the real trouble lies: whether it comes from the demand that the thesis hold for every probability function, or from the assumption that a conditional denotes a single proposition settled independently of one's credences; and whether the escape of letting "if A, C" denote different propositions at different credal states preserves anything worth having — in particular, whether accepting a conditional can still amount to believing a proposition, and whether testimony could ever inform someone of a conditional's content.

Please supply three things. First, the question exactly as it would appear on the paper, including a statement of the thesis at issue and of Adams's contrasting thesis about assertability, since candidates should not be asked to supply citations or to remember formulations. Second, a model answer of about 800 words, showing the arithmetic for part (a). Third, a marking scheme that distributes all 25 marks across named moves and separately lists errors that should cost marks — for instance, identifying Adams's thesis with Stalnaker's, reporting Lewis's result as showing the thesis false for a given credence function, treating the failure of closure under conditionalization as a merely technical wrinkle, or reading "triviality" as the conclusion that conditionals are material conditionals. End by stating what separates an A from a B. Write everything in American English.
