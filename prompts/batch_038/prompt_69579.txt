I am a junior at a public high school and for the past two years I have run our philosophy club, which has fourteen members who meet every Thursday; only two of them have had any exposure to logic, and none has taken a philosophy course. For our next meeting I want to hand out a single sheet, printable on one page front and back, that sorts each of us according to what we actually believe about vagueness — the heap problem and its relatives — rather than according to which label we find appealing. Please write it as a decision tree of ten to twelve branch nodes, each node a short question with two or three answer options and each path ending at a named position, with a scoring key on the reverse for anyone who wants to check which leaf they reached and why. Two constraints on the questions. None of them may use technical vocabulary or name a theory — no talk of truth-value gaps, precisifications, epistemic restrictions on knowledge, or the like — and none may simply ask us to choose between labels; every option should read as a first-person commitment a fifteen-year-old can accept or reject on the spot, for instance: I would still insist there is a right answer here even if nobody could ever find out what it is. The single exception is that one node may refer to an inference rule by name, provided you explain that rule in a subordinate clause, because two of our members would enjoy catching the tree in a modus tollens.

Please build the tree out of a fixed battery of cases rather than out of a list of views, and use these cases and no others: a heap of sand with a counter that reports the exact grain count as grains are removed one at a time; a printed strip of two hundred colour patches, each differing from its neighbour by an amount nobody can see, running from obviously red at one end to obviously orange at the other; a student whose height is measured every morning for a year; a bell that is rung more and more faintly until it is silent; and a town ordinance banning vehicles in the park, applied in turn to a car, a moped, an electric wheelchair, a child's push scooter, and a working replica of a tank built for a parade. Before the tree itself, list the cruxes you take to genuinely divide the positions — at a minimum: whether the argument fails at its first premise, at the step from one case to the next, or nowhere; whether there is a fact about where the cutoff lies and whether that fact is in principle discoverable; whether a phrase like clearly a heap is itself as vague as heap; whether the extension of a vague word can shift with the situation of the person judging; and whether a borderline sentence might be both true and false. Then locate the leaves in that space, and these must be among them: the view that there is a sharp cutoff we can never know, the view that borderline sentences are neither true nor false because our language has not been made precise enough, the view that truth comes in degrees, the view that the extension shifts with context, the view that some borderline claims are both true and false, and the revisionary view that no vague word ever really applies. If you think one or two further positions belong on the sheet — a deflationary option, say, or a view on which vagueness is a fact about our psychology rather than about meaning — add them and say why.

Three further things, all of which matter to me more than the labels do. First, flag any pair of leaves your tree cannot really separate: I suspect several of these views will yield identical answers to every one of my cases, and I would rather the sheet concede that than pretend to a precision it does not have. Second, mark at least three combinations of answers that no one defends, and say for each whether the cell is empty because the combination is incoherent, because it is coherent but unattractive, or simply because nobody has thought to occupy it. Third, several of my members will answer inconsistently, which is the point of the exercise rather than a failure of it, so end the scoring key with a short repair menu for each unstable pattern — most importantly for anyone who insists both that every such statement is determinately true or false and that removing a single grain can never make the difference between a heap and a non-heap — naming which commitment each leaf forces them to abandon and roughly what the abandonment costs. Please do not include a bibliography, a list of recommended readings, or references to particular books or papers; the names of positions should appear in the key only, never in the questions.
