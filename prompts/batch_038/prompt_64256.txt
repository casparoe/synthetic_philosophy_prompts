I work on the internals of a query language — think typed graphs, where nodes are entities and predicates and edges carry the structure, and the whole thing gets compiled down to something a planner can chew on. We've been stuck for two weeks on a design argument that has slowly turned into a philosophy argument and I genuinely can't tell whether there's a real problem here or we're just confusing ourselves with notation.

The setup: our IR represents a clause as an ordered tuple, like (loves, romeo, juliet). One of us claimed that's "just a heap of parts plus a convention about which slot is which" — that nothing in the data structure makes it one thing rather than three things and an ordering discipline we happen to enforce in code. Another one of us said if you add some explicit glue node to fix that, you've just built the thing that needs glueing to the tuple, and then that needs glueing, forever — he called it Bradley's regress, in a code review, unironically. Third position, from me: this is a fake problem, tuples are primitive, ship it.

Before I burn a weekend on metaphysics papers, I want the spec rather than the solutions. Write out what any account of how a proposition — or a structured graph, or a fact, whatever the right unit is — manages to be a single thing rather than a collection of constituents has to deliver. Be concrete about the cases; I think better from counterexamples than from abstractions.
