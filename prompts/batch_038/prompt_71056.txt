**the question (20 marks):**
> State the multiple realizability argument against the type-identity theory of mind, using at least two of the standard examples. Then explain what a functionalist says a mental state like pain *is*, and say whether functionalism keeps or gives up the claim that minds are physical. Finally, explain what work a thought experiment like Block's Chinese Nation is doing in this debate, and whether it points the same direction as Putnam's examples.

**the answer i got:**
> The type-identity theory says mental states just are brain states — pain is identical to C-fiber firing, in the same strict sense that water is identical to H2O, so if it's true it's true necessarily and not just as a matter of what happens to be going on in our heads. Putnam's argument against it is that the very same mental state can show up in wildly different physical systems. Humans have C-fibers, but an octopus has a completely different neural setup spread through its arms and it plausibly feels pain, and a silicon-based Martian or a sufficiently advanced robot would too. If pain = C-fiber firing were true, none of those creatures could be in pain, which is obviously absurd, so the identity is false — or at best it's only contingently true of us, pain happens to be C-fiber firing in humans but could be something else elsewhere. That's the whole point of calling it a *type* identity problem: the identity theorist is claiming a general law-level match between two kinds, and Putnam only needs one mental kind that comes in more than one physical kind to break it.
>
> Functionalism is the fix. On functionalism a mental state isn't picked out by what it's made of but by the causal role it plays: pain is the state typically caused by bodily damage, which typically causes the belief that something's wrong, the desire to be out of it, and wincing or groaning, and which is partly defined by its relations to those other mental states and not just to input and output. So functionalism isn't behaviorism — it lets mental states cause each other, it doesn't reduce them to dispositions to behave. And since only the role matters, pain can be realized in neurons, silicon, or even a whole population: Block's Chinese Nation is the case where a billion people each do the job of one neuron by passing messages around, so the nation as a whole instantiates one person's functional organization. Block's point there is to show that mental states really can be multiply realized on an enormous scale, which is just Putnam's octopus lesson scaled up.
>
> Functionalism does give up the claim that minds are physical, though. If a nation of people, a computer, and a brain can all be in the same mental state, then there's no physical property common to all of them, so mental states can't be physical states — you've traded a reduction to neuroscience for something that isn't a physical thing at all. What functionalism keeps is that mental states are real and causally effective. And Searle's Chinese Room supports this: the man follows the rules and produces the right answers, so the system is functionally correct, but there's no understanding in there, which shows getting the functional organization right isn't enough and that minds aren't just physical processes arranged in the right pattern.
