For my constitutional interpretation seminar we're doing three weeks on abolitionist readings, and I ended up writing about four times what the assignment asked for, so before I gut it I'd really like some outside eyes on the draft. It's about Douglass's break with Garrison. Pasting what I have, then some specific questions at the end — please read the questions first, actually, they tell you where I'm least confident.

THE DRAFT

Everyone tells the story the same way. Douglass spends the 1840s as a Garrisonian, denouncing the Constitution as a covenant with slavery; then somewhere around 1851 he changes his mind, partly from his own study of the document and its history, partly under the influence of Spooner's 1845 treatise; and he spends the rest of his life arguing that the charter is anti-slavery. My draft says the standard story gets the shape of the change wrong. What changed, I claim, was not mainly Douglass's verdict about particular clauses. On the three provisions everyone pointed at — the one counting three-fifths of enslaved people for representation, the one barring Congress from ending the Atlantic trade before 1808, and the one about people who escape into another state while owing service or labour — the textual facts did not budge between 1847 and 1851. What changed was his theory of what fixes the meaning of a written instrument, and specifically his view about how much evidential weight the fact of a polity's non-compliance carries.

Garrison's case, as I read it, leans hard on the makers: the correspondence, the convention debates, the compromises actually struck, the government that got built out of them. That is a method on which a document inherits the moral character of the bargain that produced it, and on which a community's subsequent conduct under an instrument is evidence about what the instrument means. After 1851 Douglass refuses both moves. He insists the text be judged by its own words and by the purposes it announces in its own preamble, and he makes much of the fact that the document nowhere names slavery or the slave, speaking instead of persons.

The center of the draft is that this is not a semantic quibble but a claim about what violation can prove. A promise's content is not settled by how the promisor behaves afterward: a broken promise is still the promise it was. If sustained breach counted as evidence about meaning, Garrison's method would make slavery self-certifying at the level of interpretation — the more consistently the republic actually protected slavery, the stronger the evidence that its charter licensed slavery, and the more hopeless the project of holding the country to its own words. I call this the self-certifying vice and argue it's why Douglass's turn matters for interpretive theory generally and not just for the politics of the 1850s.

I then try to be fair to Garrison and to mark where I think Douglass overreaches. The negative claim — no pro-slavery clause — is one thing. Douglass also needs an affirmative claim: that Congress has power to abolish slavery in the states, which he gets out of the guarantee-and-protection clause and the welfare and common-defence powers. There, reading in the best light is doing much heavier lifting than reading. Relatedly, he leans on the old maxim that law favors liberty, but a maxim like that is a tie-breaker for genuine doubt, and he seems to be promoting it into a general canon of construction; the escape clause and the twenty-year clause do not look all that doubtful to us. And if purposes control, whose purposes? The obvious escape route, that the Declaration supplies them, turns constitutional interpretation into natural-rights argument, which may be fine but means the Constitution isn't settling the question. I close by wondering whether all this is close to a modern interpretivist approach — construing law in its best moral light — and whether that comparison is doing work or is just a crutch that makes my draft feel smarter than it is.

QUESTIONS

First, is my reconstruction of Garrison a strawman? My worry is that Garrison's position was political rather than semantic — that his point was this is a slaveholding bargain and the honest response is to dissolve it, not that he had a theory of meaning at all. If he and Douglass aren't answering the same question, the whole draft collapses and I'd rather know now.

Second, is the self-certifying vice objection sound, or does it prove too much? Drafting history and subsequent practice get used in ordinary interpretation all the time and nobody wants to abandon them, so I need a principled line, and I don't think I have one.

Third, accuracy check on the factual bits: the three clauses and Douglass's handling of each, the 1851 dating, how much credit Spooner deserves, and whether Douglass really did rest a congressional abolition power on those clauses. If any of that is wrong or oversimplified, tell me which and how badly.

Fourth, should I cut the interpretivist comparison in the last paragraph? I keep going back and forth.

And finally, tell me what you think the strongest single objection to the draft is, even if it isn't one I listed.
