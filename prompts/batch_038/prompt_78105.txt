I sit on our county's transport-access advisory board — unpaid, mostly listening — and last Thursday two of the members got into it over how to spend a fixed pot, about $600k a year, for residents who can't use the regular bus network. The question was whether to give every eligible person the same monthly voucher or to assess people individually and fund whatever they turn out to need. Here's roughly how it went, in order:

(1) Nadia argued for individual assessment because identical cash buys radically different freedom. A deafblind woman in the county seat needs a support worker to go anywhere and a single voucher week eats the whole thing, while a guy with a bad hip three blocks from a bus line gets by on nothing. Worse, the voucher only works with licensed paratransit vendors and there aren't any in the north end, so for some people it's close to unspendable. Equal money is a sham; what matters is what people can actually do and be.

(2) Ross said he accepted the diagnosis but not the cure: the moment a board decides what each person needs, it's ranking people's ends, and the expensive-tastes door swings open. The resident who says he can't face a shared van, he needs a private car and driver, gets more public money than the guy who's fine with a van, and the board has no principled way to refuse him.

(3) Nadia said that's a confusion. The capability approach isn't about satisfying preferences, it's about getting everyone over a threshold on a list of central functionings — being able to get to a doctor, to a job, to see other people. Private car and driver isn't on the list, so there's no slide.

(4) Ross said fine, but then the board is the one writing the list, and he wanted to know who she thought should write it and how. He added that the list will be contested forever and every revision is a political fight, whereas the flat number gets decided once and everyone can plan around it.

(5) Nadia said that's not a cost, that's the point: the fight is about what people are entitled to be able to do, and burying it inside a dollar figure doesn't end the argument, it just stops anyone from having it.

(6) Ross said that a lot of the people who report being content can't be taken at their word, because people adjust their expectations to what's on offer — he named a woman with advanced MS who told the board shed rather they gave her share to someone else. He said that's the fatal flaw: the theory licenses you to overrule people's own account of their own lives and gives no test for when. Nadia said sometimes you should overrule reported contentment, that's exactly what adaptive preferences means, and a metric that never does so just rewards people for having given up.

(7) Ross replied that "adaptive" is its doing unfalsifiable work in her hands — contentment that supports your allocation counts as genuine, contentment that doesn't gets called adaptation.

(8) Nadia's compromise was to publish a conversion-factor table — condition X, living in area Y, needs roughly $Z to reach the baseline — and fund off the table. Ross said that's capabilities in a costume, since the Z column is made entirely out of judgments about what people should be able to do, and if she admits that she should drop the resource vocabulary. She said no, it's still resources, because the table is denominated in money and checkable against what people can actually buy with it.

(9) Ross's closer: if what you care about is freedom rather than achieved outcomes, then two residents with identical options are identical on your own metric even if one uses them and one doesn't, so in every case you can observe your allocations will come out exactly like resource allocations. Your metric goes invisible at precisely the point where it was supposed to do work.

Nobody conceded anything and the vote got pushed to next month. Please go through 1–9 one at a time and tell me who's right at each step, who's right for a bad reason, and where a move is just false or doesn't follow from what came before. Then say who was more right overall, and what the two of them were jointly missing. In particular: was Nadia sliding between two different versions of the capability approach without noticing, and if so does Ross's objection in (2) and (4) work against both of them or only one? And is there anything in Ross's (9) that Nadia could actually answer?
