I am auditing a philosophy-of-mathematics seminar this term, and in the week on Skolem's paradox our reading group tried to reconstruct the relativism worry as a single deductive argument rather than as a mood. One of us wrote the following on the board; we all agreed the conclusion was unacceptable, and we then spent an hour failing to agree about where to stop it.

(1) Downward Löwenheim–Skolem: if a first-order theory in a countable vocabulary has an infinite model, it has a countable model. (2) Set theory as ordinarily axiomatized is a first-order theory in a countable vocabulary; suppose it has an infinite model. (3) So there is a model M of set theory whose domain is countable. Grant M as well behaved as the discussion needs — say, that the object M takes to be the set of natural numbers really is the set of natural numbers, so that M's quantifiers over numbers are not somehow off. (4) Cantor's theorem is a theorem of set theory, so M satisfies it; hence there is an object R in M's domain such that M satisfies "R is uncountable," namely M's surrogate for the power set of ω. (5) Uncountability is not a perspectival property. R is uncountable just in case there is no bijection from ω onto R, and a bijection is nothing over and above a set of ordered pairs: either such a set exists or it does not, and no choice of model can make that come out otherwise. (6) The members of R are among the members of M's domain, and there are only countably many of those, so a bijection from ω onto R exists and R is countable. The sentence M satisfies is therefore simply false, and M is not the intended interpretation of our language. (7) But we have no way of singling out the intended interpretation. Any interpretation of the language of set theory that we can write down, describe, or grasp is itself a set-sized object, and so, by (1) through (3) applied to whatever theory describes it, is recoverable within some countable model of set theory. Every candidate meaning we can actually attach to "uncountable" is thus a meaning on which the reals come out countable. (8) If a sentence comes out false under every interpretation we can so much as formulate, then it is not true. So "there are uncountably many reals" is not true, and Cantor's theorem, correctly understood, fails.

I can see that (6) is where an objection will first be aimed, by saying that the bijection (6) invokes is not itself an element of M, so that M's satisfying "R is uncountable" is perfectly compatible with R's being countable from outside — the textbook dissolution. I have written that reply down and I find it unsatisfying rather than incorrect: it licenses us to stand outside M and grade M's sentences against a richer universe, while (7) insists that we cannot characterize any such richer universe without immediately drawing it back inside a countable model. It feels as though the argument borrows a vantage point at (6) that it abolishes at (7), but I cannot tell whether that is a genuine diagnosis or only a restatement of my discomfort, and I cannot rank (5), (6), (7), and (8) by how cheap they are to surrender. If forced, I would rather give up the assumption in (2) that set theory must be axiomatized in first-order terms than accept (8). Which step should be rejected, why did it look unimpeachable when we wrote it down, and what does the rejection cost — in particular, what obligations do I incur if I follow through on my stated preference and abandon first-order axiomatization, and would doing so actually resolve the difficulty or merely move it somewhere I would rather not look?
