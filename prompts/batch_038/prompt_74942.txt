I'm three years into a PhD in early modern European history and I've been conscripted into something awkward. Our department's history-and-theory workshop has two regulars who have been at each other for the better part of a decade: one is a document-first social historian who thinks the craft's standards — source criticism, corroboration, being explicit about what would falsify your claim — genuinely track how things were, in something like Ranke's sense; the other is a narrativist who thinks the same documents underdetermine any account and that the plot the historian imposes (tragic, comic, satiric, whatever) is doing real work that no amount of footnoting can discipline. It's mostly been the same argument every year: the first says the second is a sophist with no way to tell a good account from a clever one, the second says the first is naive about how "what happened" is already a narrative selection before anybody writes a sentence.

I've been asked to draft the thing they should have produced years ago, a joint statement from an adversarial collaboration, and I'd rather hand them something they could both actually sign than a plea for civility. To keep it from floating off into abstractions I told them to work from one shared case, which I made up as a composite so nobody has to go look anything up: a mid-sized market town, c. 1740, where a run of poor harvests is followed by three days of crowd action against a grain dealer. Surviving evidence is (i) the parish burial and poor-relief registers, (ii) a grain-price series kept by the market clerk, (iii) a magistrate's deposition from the dealer's servant, (iv) two letters from the mayor to a regional official, (v) a pamphlet published three weeks later by an outside printer. One account treats the three days as a spontaneous subsistence riot driven by price shock and reads the pamphlet as post-hoc justification; the other treats it as organized action by a faction of the town elite using the crowd, reads the mayor's letters as the crucial document and the price series as a lagging indicator. Same corpus, opposite conclusions, both internally coherent, both citing their sources honestly.

So draft the statement. Open with a numbered list of claims both camps can sign — including claims about this case specifically: what the corpus does and doesn't establish, what each account gets right, what "the sources" are actually doing in each argument. Then give the disagreements as a table, and be careful here: for each row state the disagreement twice, once in wording the document-first historian would accept as a fair statement of their own position and once in wording the narrativist would accept for theirs, so neither can say they've been strawman'd. Then identify the cruxes, meaning the sub-questions where settling the issue would actually move at least one side, and for each crux name the test, argument, or piece of evidence both agree bears on it with each side's prediction written down in advance — e.g. what the document-first person expects a third, independent corpus from a comparable town to show about the spontaneity reading, and what the narrativist expects a comparison of the two accounts' plot structures to show. Close with a short section saying which parts of the decade-long fight were verbal, i.e. where the two are using "what happened" or "evidence" or "bias" in ways that make them sound opposed without being opposed, and which parts are substantive and would survive perfect good faith and a recieve-and-reply email chain of infinite length.

Start the response with the statement itself — no preface, no note about what you're about to do, no restating my setup. Tone should be that of a document two working scholars put their names to, not a textbook tour of the twentieth-century historiography debate; don't attribute specific books or articles to real people and don't invent citations.
