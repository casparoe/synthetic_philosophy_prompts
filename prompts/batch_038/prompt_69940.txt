I am contributing a chapter to an edited volume on strategic decision-making, and the section assigned to me is supposed to do what the other contributors conspicuously have not: state, before any candidate theory is introduced, what an adequate theory of escalation of commitment would actually have to deliver. The obstacle is that the field does not agree about what it is trying to explain. Game theorists treat Shubik's dollar auction as a puzzle about equilibrium and about what happens when a locally sensible calculation is iterated; behavioural economists treat the same set-up as a demonstration that people respond to sunk costs; and the organisational tradition that Staw opened runs experiments in which a lone manager, with no adversary anywhere in the room, adds further funds to the underperforming division he himself picked, and then all three camps cite one another's findings as though they concerned a single phenomenon. My editor wants a specification, not a survey, and I would like you to write it. Fix the purpose first, since adequacy is relative to it: I want a theory good enough that a disinterested observer — a board, a funding agency, an outside reviewer — could use it ex ante, on information available at the time, to say whether an ongoing commitment is an escalation trap or a defensible bet in light of evidence that has genuinely arrived, and to say what institutional change would break the trap. A theory that only makes sense of the outcome after the money is gone does not meet the standard.

The deliverable is the requirements list: the phenomena an adequate account must explain, the cases it must get right, the distinctions it may not collapse, and the results it must be consistent with — each paired with the case or consideration that motivates it, and each marked either non-negotiable or a preference of one particular camp. Treat what follows as candidates to accept, reject, or reformulate, not as an outline I am supplying you: that the account explain why play continues past the point at which the next increment is dominated by withdrawing, the step from ninety-five cents to a dollar and five; that it distinguish an escalation trap from rational persistence under arriving information by some criterion internal to the theory rather than by assertion; that it handle the asymmetry between the bidder currently ahead and the bidder currently behind, including the fact that the trailing position is what generates the pressure to keep going; that it say whether the solitary, single-decision-maker cases are the same phenomenon as the interactive ones or a different one travelling under the same name; that it reproduce the familiar comparative statics — public accountability for the decision, competition for the same prize, personal responsibility for the initial outlay, the fraction of the project already completed, whether the decision-maker chose it or inherited it — and predict the direction of each rather than accommodate them after the fact; that it be consistent with the observation that de-escalation also occurs and is itself patterned; that it assign some role to the third party who collects the bids, since that is where the institutional-design question lives; that it be consistent with the fact that the dollar auction is the discrete counterpart of the war of attrition, so that whatever it says about one constrains what it may say about the other; and that it not be vacuous, meaning capable of failing.

Two harder things remain. First, tell me which single requirement does the most work in excluding candidates. Second, say whether any subset of the list is jointly unsatisfiable. My own suspicion is that the unity requirement — one mechanism covering interactive and solitary escalation alike — cannot be had together with both a rationality requirement, on which nothing is attributed to the players beyond correct reasoning given their information, and the ex ante discrimination requirement, for this reason: once bidders are uncertain about one another's budgets or valuations, sustained escalation past the value of the prize is what a fully rational player does in equilibrium, so a theory that takes rationality seriously seems unable to license an outsider's verdict, at the time, that a commitment is a trap rather than a bet. Argue that case or refute it; I do not want it restated. Close with a single paragraph, not a scorecard, saying which of the three families named above survives the list intact and what each of the others has to surrender. A numbered list with the motivating case under each item is fine, and please append no references.
