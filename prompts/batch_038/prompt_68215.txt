**QUESTION 4 (25 marks).** Read the case, then answer all four parts.

*A forester tells a villager who has never been out of the plains that there is an animal in the forest called a gavaya which resembles a cow. Some weeks later the villager is in the forest, sees an animal he does not recognise, notes that it is cow-like, and concludes that this must be the gavaya the forester described.*

(a) [6] Which pramāṇa or pramāṇas is Nyāya committed to here, and what exactly does the villager come to know? Say which components Nyāya takes to be doing the causal work in producing that knowledge.

(b) [6] A Buddhist logician holds that there are only two pramāṇas. Reconstruct their reduction of (a) to what they accept, and give Nyāya's reason for refusing the reduction.

(c) [7] Nyāya also reduces postulation (arthāpatti) and non-apprehension (anupalabdhi) to pramāṇas it already has. Explain both reductions, and say whether you think they are genuine reductions or moves in a dispute about how to count.

(d) [6] Suppose the villager afterwards tells his sister, who was not present, that the gavaya is a cow-like animal. She goes into the forest, sees one, and says that that is a gavaya. Does she come by the same knowledge by the same means? Say what turns on your answer.

Here's the script I got:

> (a) The pramāṇa here is upamāna, comparison. Nyāya admits four — perception, inference, comparison and testimony — and this is the textbook illustration of the third one. The villager perceives the animal, notices its resemblance to a cow, and so comes to know that this animal really is like a cow, i.e. that the resemblance the forester described actually holds of something. Knowing the resemblance is what upamāna delivers. The further fact that the word "gavaya" now attaches to this particular animal looks like a small piece of inference on top of it: he has to reason that the animal matching the forester's description is the animal the name was for. The causal ingredients are the present perception of the animal, the remembered similarity to cows, and arguably the memory of what the forester said, though that last one seems to be doing less work than the other two, since he could have noticed the cow-likeness without it.
>
> (b) For the Buddhist logician there are only two pramāṇas because there are only two kinds of object to be known: the particular, which perception grasps, and the universal or the general characteristic, which inference grasps. Comparison has to be folded into one of them, and it gets folded into inference. Their reduction would run something like this: whatever is cow-like in the respects the forester listed and is called "gavaya" by him, this animal is cow-like in those respects, therefore this is the animal he meant. The resemblance relation plays the part of the pervading reason, and the remembered statement is what supplies the middle term. Nyāya refuses because the cognition that results is not general in the way an inference is. It is about this word and this thing, and seeing further cow-like animals would not make it any better confirmed, whereas inference is exactly the sort of thing that repeated instances strengthen. So the deliverable has a different structure and it earns its own slot.
>
> (c) Postulation is the Mīmāṃsā addition, and the case they always give is of a man who is known to be stout and known never to eat during the day: you postulate that he eats at night in order to resolve the tension between the two. Nyāya says that is just inference with the presentation dressed up differently — from the stoutness together with the not-eating-by-day we infer the night-eating, and the only difference is that the Mīmāṃsaka reports the two known facts as jointly inexplicable rather than as a reason and a thing to be proved. I think this reduction is genuine. Anything you would postulate can be redescribed as an inference from a pair of facts that cannot both be explained otherwise, and nothing is lost in the redescription.
>
> Non-apprehension is the other addition: knowledge of an absence, that there is no pot on the floor where you are looking. The reduction here is much weaker. Nyāya treats absence as a real category in its own ontology, so it is committed to there being knowable absences, and since it refuses to multiply pramāṇas it says we know these by anupalabdhi along with everything else it has to account for. So in practice Nyāya is working with the same six as Mīmāṃsā, and the "four" is only a different way of listing them. That is the sense in which the whole quarrel about numbers is dialectical: every school has the same stock of cases and repacks them to fit the count it started with.
>
> (d) The sister gets the same knowledge by a different route. She never heard the forester, she heard her brother, so what reaches her is testimony, which for Nyāya is a pramāṇa in its own right provided the speaker is an āpta, someone who knows the thing and intends to say what he knows. But she still has to pick the animal out in the forest, and that recognition is either upamāna again or just perception working on a remembered description. What turns on the answer is whether testimony can transmit a link between a name and an object, or only descriptions. If the brother's report is enough on its own, then upamāna looks like a shortcut rather than a source, and Nyāya's reason for keeping it seperate from testimony in the first case loses some of its force.
