I'm a novelist — fourth book out next spring, currently about forty thousand words behind where I told my agent I'd be — and I also chair the programming comittee of a small regional literary festival in a town with a hospice, two bookshops and not much else going on culturally. A health foundation has offered us £30k to run a strand next October built out of bleak fiction: readings and facilitated conversations about books on bereavement, aimed squarely at people in their first year after a death, co-designed with a local bereavement charity who'd supply the peer facilitators. Our safeguarding trustee is dead set against it. Her case isn't that sadness is bad for people — she's not squeamish — it's about who's doing the choosing and who's paying: that we'd be advertising grief back to people who are already drowning in it, that bereavement isn't a genre you programme between the poetry slam and the kids' workshop, and that the funder's marketing conditions (they want it branded the Grief Season, with faces on the posters) turn somebody's worst year into our footfall numbers. Against that, the charity's own service users apparently asked for precisely this, and I have a shoebox of letters about my last book, which is about a child dying, and they run in both directions: dozens saying it kept them company through the first months, and one I've never shaken, from a woman who wrote that reading it at four months undid her and she'd have to give her copy away.

We vote in three weeks and I have to circulate something beforehand; everyone's a volunteer with a day job, so nothing longer than a couple of sides. So draft the memo I'll send round: recommend we take the money as offered, take it with conditions, or decline it, and say plainly what happens next and by when, given the funder wants an answer by the end of the month. If you land on conditions, give me ones a committee could actually apply on a Tuesday evening in a church hall — including whether you'd insist on a trained counsellor at every event, which is the number the whole argument keeps sliding back to, since it eats roughly a third of the grant; whether content notices go on the listings and what they'd have to say to be worth printing; and whether we let the funder call it the Grief Season or lose the money over it. And handle my safeguarding trustee's objection in the strongest form you can manage, not as a straw man — I want her to read the memo and feel argued with rather than managed.

Here's the bit I actually want you to think hard about, because I suspect it decides the case and nobody on the committee has put it this way yet. There are at least two different stories about why bleak art is bearable at all, maybe even wanted. One is the old release story: the book stirs the feeling up and lets it out, comfort as discharge, with aesthetic distance doing the safety work — roughly Bullough's psychical distance idea. The other is my own hunch, which I can't yet argue properly: that nothing much gets discharged, and what a bereaved reader gets instead is that their grief arrives wearing a shape it doesn't have in life — a pace, an order, sentences that know where they're going, a thing that ends — so the comfort isn't release but form, being accompanied rather than emptied. If that second story is right, a fair amount follows for how you'd run the strand: you'd curate for structure rather than intensity, you'd stop worrying about books being too sad and start worrying about ones that leave a reader in freefall, and the notices on the listings would need to flag something different entirely. So take a view on which story the committee should act on and why, tell me what would count as evidence either way from our own events next year that a non-academic volunteer could actually gather, and say how the recommendation shifts depending on the answer. No citations, no reading lists, no footnotes, please — and write the whole thing in British English, -ise spellings, programme, the lot.
