Below is a draft (~650 words) of a short paper I intend to circulate at a graduate metaphysics workshop on dispositions. I would like critical comments rather than encouragement, and I have deliberately left in the moves I am least sure about.

---

**Boundary masks: why the fink/mask distinction presupposes an answer about object boundaries**

1. The conditional analysis of disposition ascriptions holds that an object is disposed to manifest M in stimulus conditions S just in case it would manifest M were it to undergo S. Two families of case have been taken to defeat it. In a fink, an intervening condition removes the disposition after the stimulus but before the manifestation, so the conditional is false even though the object was genuinely disposed; in a mask, the disposition survives intact but something prevents it from manifesting. Lewis's reformed analysis responded by restricting the relevant counterfactual to conditions in which the object's intrinsic character is held fixed, and the subsequent consensus has been that finks are the serious threat while masks are comparatively benign — a masked disposition is still there, and can in principle be unmasked and retested. My aim is to attack that asymmetry.

2. Consider a subclass of masks, which I will call *scaffolded* masks. In a scaffolded mask, what blocks the manifestation is not a static arrangement but the ongoing exercise of a disposition belonging to a second object, aimed at the first object's disposition. A town below sea level has its disposition to flood suppressed by a pumping station that is itself disposed to fail in a storm; a bank's disposition to collapse under a run is suppressed by a standing lender of last resort; a data centre's disposition to overheat is suppressed by a cooling loop whose own failure disposition is monitored by a third system. In each case the suppression is an activity rather than a packing material, and the suppressing object has a modal life of its own.

3. In such cases, whether the first object retains its disposition appears to depend on whether the scaffold falls inside it. The town is not floodable if the pumps are constitutively part of its flood-defence system; it is floodable if the same pumps are contracted from a private firm two counties away and may be switched off at will. Yet the town's intrinsic properties, and indeed the pumps', are the same in both descriptions. The question whether the disposition is masked or simply absent therefore collapses into the question where the object's boundaries lie — which is to say that the fink/mask distinction, and Lewis's appeal to intrinsic character, both presuppose that objects have determinate boundaries, a presupposition that artifacts and institutions flagrantly fail to satisfy.

4. It follows, I suggest, that the conditional analysis debate is live only for boundary-clear cases: molecules, crystals, elementary particles, and the like, where "intrinsic" has a determinate interpretation. For artifacts and institutions the analysis cannot be refuted, because there is no fixed target for it to be refuted about; we should instead relativize such ascriptions — fragile-relative-to-a-boundary, floodable-relative-to-a-boundary — and stop treating our intuitions about masks and finks in these domains as data for theory choice. This also disposes of the more recent strategy of analyzing dispositions in terms of what an object would be disposed to do were it to get into the relevant circumstances: that strategy does not solve the problem but relocates it from the stimulus clause to the getting-into clause, since whether an object gets into the circumstances is itself boundary-dependent.

5. One difficulty I cannot resolve. On this account nearly every ordinary mask turns out to be a scaffolded mask, since the packing foam is an object too, with dispositions of its own to compress, rebound, and degrade. If so, the class of boundary-clear cases may be empty, and my restriction is either trivial or vacuous.
