So my office runs this monthly thing where a handful of us play games and argue afterwards. Usually it's board games, but last week someone brought in a game theory book and we spent an hour playing the centipede game with actual cash in a hat. Rules as we played them: you alternate turns, and on your turn you either grab the pot (you keep roughly two thirds of it and the other person gets the rest) or pass, which makes the pot bigger and hands the turn to the other person. If you both pass all the way to the final turn you split it evenly. Ten turns total. Almost nobody grabbed on turn one — most pairs passed several times before someone grabbed, and two pairs went the whole way and split.

Afterwards two guys got into it for like forty minutes and I honestly couldn't follow half of it. Devin is a backend engineer on my team; Marcus is my partner's brother, who did some logic in undergrad but works in insurance now. Here's roughly how it went, move by move:

(1) Marcus said correct play is obvious: player 1 grabs immediately. At the last node player 2 would grab rather than pass, so at the second-to-last node player 1 grabs, and so on back to the start. He said that's exactly what common knowledge of rationality delivers — both are rational, both know the other is rational, both know both know, and so on.

(2) Devin said the derivation is self-defeating. For player 1 to reason "if I pass, player 2 will grab," player 1 has to believe player 2 is rational. But if the game has actually reached player 2's turn, then player 1 already passed, which contradicts the assumption everybody started with. So the assumption set describes no possible situation, and from an assumption set that describes nothing you can prove anything. He said the whole backward induction "result" is a theorem about an empty set of worlds, which is why nobody at our table played it.

(3) Marcus said that's a cheap move — a strategy has to specify what you'd do at every node, including ones that don't get reached, otherwise the theory is silent precisely where you need it. And "anything follows from a contradiction" would destroy every reductio in mathematics, including induction itself.

(4) Devin said no, the cases are different: in a normal reductio you derive a contradiction *inside* the assumption and then discharge it to get a negation. Here the contradiction shows the premises are jointly unsatisfiable, so the conclusion isn't a prediction about anything. Vacuity, not a proof.

(5) Marcus said the machinery clearly does real work elsewhere: in the muddy children puzzle, the kids only figure it out after the parent's public announcement, and how many rounds it takes depends on exactly how many kids are muddy. So "levels of knowledge" isn't an empty abstraction, it's tracking something you can count.

(6) Devin said that supports him — the muddy children case works because there's an actual mechanism creating the common knowledge (a loud announcement, everyone in one room watching everyone). The centipede has no announcement; "common knowledge of rationality" is just something the author stipulated into existence. And anyway real people can never attain common knowledge, because you can't establish it over an unreliable channel — that's the coordinated attack / Byzantine generals impossibility we deal with at work all the time. So it's an idealization nobody ever reaches and results depending on it have no empirical content.

(7) Marcus said the infinite-regress and the channel complaints are both beside the point, because for a game with n moves you only need n levels of iterated knowledge, not infinitely many. And Devin's "nobody actually played it" objection backfires: if people don't play backward induction, that's evidence they aren't rational, or aren't commonly known to be rational — not evidence the reasoning is bad.

(8) Devin said that last part makes the whole enterprise unfalsifiable: if any observation can be absorbed by "well then they must not have been rational," the theory can never be wrong about anything, so it isn't telling you anything.

They asked me to break the tie and I just made more coffee. Can you go through (1)–(8) and rate each one, from clearly mistaken to clearly right, with a sentence or two of justification each? Then tell me who's right about what overall, and whether they're actually disagreeing about the same thing or talking past each other. Two specific things I'm curious about: is the Byzantine generals point Devin made genuinely relevant to the centipede, or was that a category error on his part? And is there some weaker or more careful way of stating the epistemic assumption — something less than full common knowledge of rationality — that dissolves the fight, and if there is, does that weaker version still get you any interesting conclusion about how the game gets played?
