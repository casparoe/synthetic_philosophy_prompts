Something's been bugging me and I cant tell whether it's obvious or whether I'm reinventing a wheel that already has a name.

Here's the pattern I keep running into with the big gloomy long-range predictions. Somebody says the world will run out of cheap oil around the turn of the century, or that hundreds of millions will starve in the 70s and 80s because mouths multiply faster than wheat, like Malthus argued and like the population-bomb crowd argued again. The date comes, the date goes, nothing remotely like it happens. By any normal standard that's a miss. But the defenders don't score it as a miss. They say the mechanism was real, the pressure was real, we just got the year wrong — and look, forty years on the aquifers are dropping and the oil business runs on rock nobody was getting oil out of back then. There's also that bet between an economist and a biologist over five metal prices that I remember reading about: the loser's fans said the bet was a bad test, ten years is way too short a window, over fifty he'd have won.

So the claim never actually dies. Miss the date → "right but early." Something bad-ish eventually happens → "told you so." Nothing ever happens → "well it's just further out than anyone expected, the math hasn't changed." Every branch of the future counts as a win, or at worst a postponement, and the postponement is always granted for a perfectly sincere reason.

I know about moving the goalposts, but that isn't it — they're not changing the claim, they're changing when it has to come true. And "ad hoc" feels too broad to mean anything.

So: is there an established term for rescuing a prediction by extending its deadline after the deadline has already been missed? What are the two or three closest concepts, and how is the thing I'm describing different from each of them? And if there genuinely isn't one, coin it — a definition plus where the edges are. Because obviously extending the date is sometimes legit: you measured the mechanism and just got the rate wrong. I want to know what separates that case from the immune one. Give me three quick concrete cases and say which side of the line each lands on, including at least one you think is genuinely hard to call.

No bibliography, I'm not doing homework — just names of ideas. Keep the whole thing under 400 words and put your answer to "what is it called" in the very first line.
