I cover science and technology policy for a general-interest magazine — mostly emerging-risk stories: gene drives, novel pathogens, carbon removal, autonomous systems. Over five years on the beat I have watched a single four-word slogan do an enormous amount of argumentative work, and I would like it taken apart before I use it in print again. The slogan is *better safe than sorry*, deployed as the folk formulation of the precautionary principle.

The episode that prompted this is a composite of several hearings I have sat through, so no real person is being quoted, but the moves are verbatim from my notebooks. A company proposes releasing an engineered organism in a contained field trial. An advocate on the panel says: "We don't know what happens if it escapes and establishes, so better safe than sorry — don't release it." A scientist on the same panel replies that the slogan is empty, because doing nothing has costs too: the disease the organism was designed to suppress keeps killing people, and "sorry" arrives either way. The advocate then says: "Fine, but the difference is that we caused one of those harms and not the other." A regulator, trying to break the deadlock, invokes the soft-law version of precaution — roughly, that where there are threats of serious or irreversible damage, the lack of full scientific certainty is not a reason to postpone cost-effective measures — and is immediately accused by the company's counsel of using a rule that no one could ever satisfy, since you can always imagine a worse outcome attached to the cautious option as well.

What I need is a disambiguation, not a defense of either side. Please lay out the distinct precise claims that *better safe than sorry* can be making in an argument like this one, as a numbered menu, from the near-tautological to the genuinely radical. For each reading, give me: what it asserts once you spell out the suppressed terms — safe for whom, compared with what alternative, judged at what time, under what standard of evidence; what would have to be true of the world, or of the case, for it to hold; whether it is a platitude, a substantive and defensible thesis, or false; and whether it would actually license the conclusion my panelist drew from it.

I want the menu to bear directly on the pressure points I keep hitting. At least one reading should turn on the question of what counts as the baseline, and on whether "safe" can be a property of an option at all when every option, including inaction, carries risk. At least one should distinguish precaution as a decision rule under uncertainty from precaution as a rule about who bears the burden of proof, and say which of the two is doing the work when the slogan is used to shut down a proposal. At least one should make sense of the advocate's asymmetry move — that harms we cause are worse than identical harms we allow — and say what has to be true for that to matter. And at least one should state the paralysis objection as sharply as it can be stated, including the version on which the principle forbids the very precaution it recommends, and say whether any reading survives it.

Finish with two things: a short diagnosis of which readings each of my three speakers was actually invoking, and where they were talking past one another rather than disagreeing; and a list of five or six clarifying questions I could put to a source on the record that would force them to say which reading they mean. Please write for an educated non-specialist reader — I will be paraphrasing this in an article — and do not give me a bibliography, a reading list, or any quotations I would need to verify. Names of principles and of their authors are welcome where they help, but the argument should stand on its own logic.
