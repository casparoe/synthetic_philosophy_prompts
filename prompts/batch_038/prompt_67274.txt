I run the investigations desk at a small nonprofit that receives roughly thirty public claims a month of purportedly miraculous events — inexplicable healings, apparitions, survivals against medical odds. My two analysts have neither philosophical nor statistical training, and each claim must receive a written verdict within five working days, which we then publish; both kinds of error are costly, endorsing a fabrication and dismissing something that deserved to stand. Only a verdict of not established goes out without a third signature.

Please convert Section X of Hume's Enquiry Concerning Human Understanding, the section on miracles, into a procedure my analysts can run on a claim file without having read it: the questions in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure terminates in not established, and where it hands the decision to a human being because the question is one of judgment rather than fact. Two points especially need operationalizing. First, Hume's central maxim, to the effect that testimony establishes a miracle only where the falsehood of that testimony would itself be more of a marvel than the event reported: tell me which of the two readings you have built in, the strict one on which no testimony could ever establish a miracle however strong or the balancing one on which it sometimes could, and what turns on that choice in practice. Second, the difference between an event that would violate a law of nature and one that is merely improbable, since many of our files concern the second and I suspect Hume's argument does not obviously reach them. I would also like his observation about rival religious traditions each producing miracles of comparable evidential weight to function as a step rather than a footnote.

Then run the procedure on two files. One is a report from 1917 of a solar phenomenon said to have been observed by tens of thousands of people gathered in a single place, among them skeptics and newspapermen, and covered in the secular press within days. The other is a present-day case in which a patient given months to live recovered completely, with three physicians attesting that no treatment received could account for it. Say plainly where the procedure delivers a verdict I will dislike.

Finally, tell me where it will misfire: the standing charge that the maxim begs the question by building the uniformity of experience into the standard of proof, any point at which Hume's text simply underdetermines a rule, and which of his distinctions I lose by making it usable for readers who have never encountered him.

Please write to me in continuous prose, as a letter would be — no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables, numbering the questions within your sentences instead of setting them out as a list.
