My girlfriend is an eligibility caseworker and I've been helping her rewrite some of the client-facing handbooks in plainer language, which means I've spent the last week actually reading the purchase rules for SNAP benefits, and something in them has lodged in my brain. The card will pay for the cold rotisserie chicken in the fridge case but not for the identical chicken sitting in the hot warmer three feet away. It will pay for a birthday cake, and for the flour and eggs to bake one, but not for the toilet paper, the bar soap, the baby wipes, or the ibuprofen you buy at the same register on the same trip. It will pay for a packet of tomato seeds and for a live bean plant, on the theory that these produce food the household will eat. It will not pay for a bottle of vitamin D even if the clinic just told you you're deficient, and the deciding fact is which panel the manufacturer printed on the box — a Nutrition Facts label means it's food and a Supplement Facts label means it isn't. So the line between what the state will buy you and what it won't is drawn in grocery-store categories, and it tracks need pretty badly: the stuff a poor household actually reports going without most often is soap, paper goods, and over-the-counter medicine, all of which are excluded, while a decorated sheet cake is fine.

Here's the part I can't reconcile. Very often the same person, in the same month, is also getting the earned income tax credit — a refund of several thousand dollars in one lump, in cash, with no conditions whatever on what it's spent on. And her mother's retirement check arrives as cash too, which she is legally free to spend entirely on cigarettes and slot machines, and nobody proposes to convert that into a card with a list of approved goods. So the same government seems to hold two views of poor people at the same time: trustworthy enough with money when the money is a reward for working, not trustworthy enough when it's a handout. What I want is a reconstruction of the theory the SNAP design presupposes, and I'd rather you be blunt about whether there is one. Is it paternalism about judgment — a claim that this particular person can't be relied on to rank her own needs? Is it a claim that food is a distinct good that money doesn't substitute for, so that the transfer is aimed at a specific commodity rather than at welfare in general, and if so what makes food special but not soap or medicine? Is it expressive, a statement about who counts as a full participant in the market, given that the restrictions are visible to everyone standing in line at the checkout? Or is it just deterrence, making the benefit annoying enough to use that fewer people claim it? And crucially: which of these does the practice actually *need*, as opposed to merely sitting comfortably with? Because I keep coming back to the fact that this program is run by the agriculture department and was built alongside farm-surplus policy, which suggests the shape might be explained by who was in the room when it was designed rather than by any theory of desert at all — in which case maybe there's no implicit philosophy to extract, just history.

So tell me how you'd sort those possibilities apart, and say what the design quietly rules out. A straightforward welfarist view — the point is to make people better off by their own lights — seems hard to square with an in-kind benefit, since cash would dominate if the recipient knew her own needs better than the legislature does; is that right, or can a welfarist defend the card on other grounds, like the thing about cash being fungible and getting spent down? Also: which features would survive under each candidate theory, and which ones look like pure artifacts that no theory predicts — the hot/cold chicken line, the label test, the seeds-and-plants inclusion? If the honest answer is that the theory predicts none of those and the artifacts are doing all the work, I want to hear that argued rather than papered over. Finally, say what would have to change in practice if the state switched to cash with no restrictions: what would it lose, not just administratively but in terms of whatever it's currently expressing, and what would that tell us about what the restrictions were really for.
