I chair the allocation panel of a municipal foundation whose charter commits us to reasoning as free and equal persons behind a veil of ignorance, applying Rawls's two principles in lexical order, identifying the least advantaged by an index of primary goods, and observing a just-savings provision for later generations. Our published rulings to date:

1. Approved an unconditional top-up for the lowest-earning tenth of households, funded by a levy costing the average household 2% and the top tenth 9%; the independent estimate was a small fall in the city's aggregate output with the worst off clearly better off.
2. Rejected a growth scheme that would have raised the bottom tenth's incomes by 4% and the top tenth's by 30%, leaving nobody worse off in absolute terms. Our minute records the objection as the widening of the ratio, which we judged corrosive to the standing of the worst off in the city.
3. Approved a subsidy reaching a group at the fortieth percentile, none of which went to the bottom tenth, on the grounds that without it that group would have fallen below a decent minimum while the bottom tenth's position was unchanged.
4. Rejected a proposal delivering a very large income gain to the bottom tenth in exchange for a curfew and registration requirement applying only to them.
5. Approved a scheme cutting the bottom tenth's current incomes by 1% to fund early-years provision for their own children, whose lifetime prospects were projected to improve substantially.

Next month we rule on a "transition dividend": a permanent fund built by running down part of the endowment, which pays the bottom tenth 6% more across their lifetimes and the top tenth 40% more, and which is projected to leave the least advantaged of the generation after next about 10% worse off than they would otherwise be. Claiming it requires an annual means test administered by a named case worker, which two of our community partners say recipients experience as stigmatising. It restricts no liberty.

Tell me how the panel should rule if it wants to be consistent with its own record, and which rule it has actually been running. Set out the competing rules that fit precedents 1–5 equally well — including at least one on which we are not a Rawlsian panel at all but have quietly adopted a rival view, and at least one on which precedent 5 and the dividend are relevantly different cases rather than similar ones — say what each predicts for the dividend, and give a best guess with reasons for preferring it. Then name the closest precedent, state honestly what principle we would be adopting if we approve and if we reject, and identify the further case that would most sharply separate your two leading candidates, noting any pair of candidates that no single case could separate. Note that precedent 2 is not a textbook difference-principle ruling and deal with what that implies.
