Our political-thought seminar spent six weeks on Douglass's constitutional writing and it ended in a stalemate that's honestly a bit demoralizing, because both sides agree on practically all the facts. Roughly: camp one says Douglass broke with Garrison around 1851, went out on his own with the Rochester paper, took money and backing from the free-soil and political-abolition crowd (Gerrit Smith most conspicuously), and the antislavery reading he then pressed — slavery is a creature of state law only, the federal government has no power to establish or protect it, the fugitive-slave provision speaks of persons held to service and never of property or of anything Congress could legislate, the three-fifths clause treats the enslaved as persons rather than chattel and clips planter power instead of adding to it, the document nowhere uses the word slave or recognizes property in people, and read together with its preamble and with the Declaration it comes out antislavery — is suspiciously exactly the reading he needed to have a usable politics, and it arrives right when the funding and the new audience do. On this camp's view, Garrison's people reading the same text as a proslavery compact were being more honest about what the framers actually intended, and Douglass's later position (including the July 1852 Rochester address, where the document comes out as a charter of liberty if you construe it correctly) is conclusions-first lawyering by a man under institutional pressure. Camp two says that's a genetic fallogy dressed up as history: sincere conversions also land on convenient conclusions, the two readings differ about interpretive method rather than about any word on the page, and whether the change was strategically useful to Douglass is a separate question from whether the reading is defensible, so evidence about motives can't settle the second.

Six weeks of that arguement, nobody moved, and the seminar paper deadlines made everyone dig in. So: what is actually going on here? Is there a buried methodological disagreement — original intent versus the text construed against implied powers — that neither camp is naming, so they keep talking past each other? Are they answering two different questions under the single heading "what did Douglass think the Constitution meant": a causal-historical one about why he changed and a normative one about whether his new reading holds up? Is it a fight about evidential standards, i.e. whether coincidence of interest and conclusion carries any weight at all, and if so under what conditions it would? Or is part of it flatly verbal, in that the two camps mean different things by the Constitution meaning something, so their claims don't actually contradict? I also have a sneaking suspicion that the real content of the fight is displaced: it's a present-day originalism quarrel with Douglass as the proxy, which would explain why no amount of 1850s detail moves anyone.

Tell me which of these is closest, or propose a better diagnosis, and ground it in what each side is actually committed to rather than in a survey of the scholarship. Then tell me what would count as progress given that diagnosis — some discriminating piece of evidence about the sequence of Douglass's change, a distinction that splits one of the questions cleanly, a way of stating the motivated-reasoning inference so it's testable — or argue that no progress should be expected here and say why.
