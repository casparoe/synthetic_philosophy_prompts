Retired four years ago from an enzyme kinetics bench, 35 years of it, and I've been reading philosophy of mind on my own since — badly, I assume, but enthusiastically. Our little group meets Tuesday in the back room of the public library and I've been asked to lay out where I've actually got to, so I'd like a reality check before I say any of it out loud. Not a tour of the field, I've got books for that. Just an audit of whether this hangs together.

Here's the list.

1. The physical world is causally closed — every physical event has a physical cause sufficient for it. I dont say this out of metaphysical conviction, I say it because in 35 years of experiments nothing ever needed anything else to make it come out, and I've seen no reason to start expecting otherwise.

2. But experience is real and it isn't just structure and function. Mary in the black-and-white room did it for me — and I was tickled to discover that the paper she comes from was arguing *for* the view I've landed in rather than against it. Something is left over after you've listed every physical fact.

3. So: brain states cause the qualia, and the qualia dont cause anything. That's where I keep arriving, wether I like it or not.

4. At the same time I treat first-person reports as data, and as unusually good data. If I say it aches, or that the afterimage looks green, I'm not making an observation error of the kind I could be making about a band on a gel. Of everything on this list, this is the item I'd least like to hand over.

5. And I've kept a working principle from the lab: if something is genuinely real, then in principle it shows up in the numbers somewhere. Maybe indirectly, maybe only statistically, maybe not in my lifetime. But not never — not "not even in principle."

6. I don't accept the illusionist way out. Saying the experience isn't really there and we're merely disposed to think it is, that explains the report and dodges the thing.

7. Something that seems to me to back up (2): everybody who thinks hard about this hits a wall. The stuckness is widespread and stubborn, and I read it as a sign there's something non-functional there to be stuck about.

Two worries of my own that I suspect are dumb. First, people tell me (3) breaks (1) — if a brain can produce a non-physical quale, hasn't physics been allowed to reach outside itself? I don't think that's right but I can't articulate why, and I'd rather not stand in the library unable to. Second, if qualia do nothing, why are they so neatly matched to what's useful — pain with tissue damage, and so on? My answer is that evolution shaped the machinery and the qualia came along for the ride, and I want to know whether that's a real answer or just a shrug in a lab coat.

So: go through it and tell me which pairs genuinely can't both stand and which only look bad, worst first. For each, say what would have to give and what giving it up costs me. I'm deliberately not telling you which of these I'm fondest of, so please don't guess at it from the order or from how long I spend on each. And I'd much rather have one clean revision that fixes the most than seven small patches. If the honest answer is that the whole thing has to be rebuilt outward from (2), say so — but say why, and tell me what I'd be signing up for on the other side of it.
