So I write novels, and the one I'm working on has a character who models fisheries — she's an economist, and there's a scene where a journalist calls her out on an assumption in her model that everybody knows is false. I gave the scene to two friends to pressure-test and they ended up arguing with each other for an hour while I sat there uselessly. Can you referee?

The setup: her model assumes boats always go where expected catch is highest. They don't, obviously — captains have habits, grudges, fuel contracts. But the model nails the year the stock collapsed to within about four months.

1. Mara (does this kind of modelling for a living): whether the assumption is true is beside the point. What matters is whether the model's numbers track the landings. If they do, the assumption has earned its keep. Insisting it be true is like insisting the map be the size of the territory.

2. Owen (philosophy, mostly of science): that's the Friedman move and it's a bad one. If the premises are false and the predictions land anyway, you have no account of *why* they landed — you've traded explanation for curve-fitting. And on your rule a good idealization and a lucky guess are indistinguishable right up until the guess runs out.

3. Mara: fine, then I'll earn it properly. I'll make the boats dumb — give them inertia, whatever — rerun it, and show the collapse year barely shifts. Now the idealization is licensed.

4. Owen: notice you just conceded. What licenses the idealization isn't the model's predictive success, it's a demonstration that the error you introduced is small and bounded. Two completely different claims. Your first one was the second all along, badly dressed.

5. Mara: only when I can run that test. Half the assumptions in my model can't be relaxed without rebuilding the thing from the keel up. So your position is that I can't use them at all.

6. Owen: my position is that they're unlicensed, not unusable. There's a difference.

Go through it move by move and tell me whose right at each step — not just who won the hour. And what are they both missing? Then, practically: what is my character allowed to say back in the scene without sounding like someone who's already lost?
