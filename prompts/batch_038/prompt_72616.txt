I work as a research coordinator for a hospital-affiliated biobank, and I have been asked to sit on the committee that rewrites our participant agreement, which is how I ended up with a knot I cannot untangle. Here is roughly what the current form does. It asks people for permission to give a blood sample and to let us pull relevant entries from their medical record, not for any particular study but for a standing research collection whose uses no one can yet describe: the material may be stored indefinitely and used for studies that have not yet been designed, by investigators we cannot name, at institutions we have not chosen. It says participation is voluntary and that a participant may withdraw at any time without penalty, then adds that withdrawal operates only going forward, that specimens whose identifiers have already been stripped or that have already been distributed to other laboratories cannot be recalled, and that once the identifying code is destroyed withdrawal becomes impossible altogether because we could no longer tell which vials belonged to whom. Children are enrolled on a parent's permission, with a promise that we will approach them for their own consent at eighteen, coupled with the statement that material already anonymized stays in the collection either way. Participants are told they will not receive individual results, though they will get an annual newsletter describing what the collection has been used for. And there is a clause I find hardest to parse: it asks people to agree in advance that the material may be used in research they might find objectionable, with the genetics of intelligence given as the example, and it states that they will not be contacted again about that decision.

My question is what picture of consent this bundle presupposes, and whether that picture survives being stated out loud. What has to be true of consent for it to keep working after the person who gave it retracts it — is it being treated as a one-time transfer of authority, more like a sale or an irrevocable gift, or as a standing condition on the permissibility of an ongoing activity, more like permission to remain in someone's house or to keep touching someone's body? The form seems to need both at once: offering withdrawal only makes sense on the second view, while limiting it to future collections only makes sense on the first. What does the anonymization clause assume makes tissue and information someone's to control — that it came from their body, or that it is about them as an identifiable person? If the second, then stripping identifiers does not merely make withdrawal impractical; it dissolves the person's standing entirely, which looks like a substantive claim dressed up as a clerical one. What does calling the permission broad concede about the requirement that it be informed: can an authorization that covers studies nobody can describe still have content the authorizer grasps, or has the concept quietly changed into something closer to trust, or to a general license? And what theory of competence sits in the clause that re-asks the eighteen-year-old for consent while leaving the sample collected at age six exactly where it was?

A colleague on the committee argues that we should stop calling any of this consent and describe it instead as a gift or a waiver of a right one cannot get back, and the committee is also piloting a participant dashboard that lets people toggle categories of future research on and off, including a delete-everything button, in the hope that this restores something worth calling authorization. Please assess both moves: is the reframing right, does the dashboard repair the problem or relocate it, and which rival views of consent does the current bundle rule out rather than merely fail to express? Above all, I would like you to separate what the practice strictly requires from what it merely fits comfortably with — how much of this is driven by a theory of consent at all, and how much by reliance interests, feasibility, cost, and the impossibility of un-publishing a paper — and to say explicitly where the practice is philosophically underdetermined, such that several incompatible theories would license it. End by telling me what would have to change in the operation, not just in the wording, if the biobank adopted a genuinely revocable model. Please write the answer in American English.
