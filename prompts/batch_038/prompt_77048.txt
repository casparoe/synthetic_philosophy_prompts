The script (17 of 23) reads as follows.

The question turns on a difficulty: if the forged canvas is indistinguishable from the Rembrandt, in what sense could the two differ aesthetically? Goodman's distinction between autographic and allographic arts is usually presented as the way out of this difficulty, and it works as follows. An art is autographic when the distinction between an original and a forgery is significant within it, that is, when even the most exact duplicate fails to be a genuine instance of the work. Painting is autographic, because the work is identical with the particular object produced by a particular history of making; a copy of it, however exact, is a different object with a different history, and so a different work. The allographic arts are those, such as music, literature and dance, in which the work is fixed by a notation: whatever complies with the score or the text is a genuine instance of the work, and so the notion of forgery loses its grip. Any score-compliant performance of a Beethoven symphony is a genuine performance of that symphony, whoever plays it and however the score was acquired.

That last clause points to what I take to be a weakness in the taxonomy. In literature the thing we call forgery is really plagiarism: someone takes an existing text and passes it off as another author's. Since plagiarism is possible in an allographic art, and since Goodman defines the autographic arts by the significance of the original-forgery distinction, it seems that forgery is possible in both branches of the distinction, which makes the distinction less sharp than it first appears.

Turning to the case in the question, the difficulty reasserts itself. Ex hypothesi the forged canvas and the Rembrandt look exactly alike, and aesthetic appreciation is a matter of how a work looks when it is properly attended to. Goodman, I think, concedes this. His point is not that we are blind to an aesthetic difference between the original and the forgery, but that the two objects are different works, and that the forgery's fault lies in the false claim attached to it rather than in anything visible in it. The difference between them is therefore a difference in identity and in provenance, not a difference in aesthetic character; the original and the forgery are aesthetically on a par.

Dutton's account is normally introduced as a reply to that conclusion, and it does go further. For Dutton a work of art is a performance, and part of what we value in it is the achievement it represents: the artist solved a problem, within a tradition and against the possibilities available at the time, in a way that had not been done before. The forger cannot be credited with that achievement, because a forgery borrows an established style rather than originating one; the forger's canvas looks like a Rembrandt precisely because Rembrandt's problems were already solved. But although Dutton puts this in aesthetic terms, it remains at bottom a moral objection. What we resent is the lie, and a lie about the history of an object does not alter the object's visible properties, which is why the claim that the forgery is aesthetically inferior has to be cashed out as the claim that we are entitled to feel cheated.

The distinction also seems to me to come apart in photography and in digital images. A print is produced from a negative, and the negative fixes the work in something like the way a score does, so any print pulled from it is genuine; photography is therefore allographic. A digital image has no negative at all and can be copied without generational loss, so it is allographic in a still purer sense. If allographic status excludes forgery, then forgery in the strict sense is impossible in digital imagery. This explains, I think, why AI-generated images passed off as photographs are disturbing in a way that does not obviously concern authenticity at all: they are not forgeries of anything, but inventions presented as records.

My conclusion, then, is that the forged canvas does not differ aesthetically from the Rembrandt. What differs is its history, and the importance we attach to that history is art-historical and moral rather than aesthetic. A viewer who knew everything there is to know about both canvases and looked at them carefully would be seeing the same thing.
