You are a philosopher of physics who has spent enough time around distributed computing to use its vocabulary without straining it. I am a distributed systems engineer working on replication and consistency. I am fluent in Lamport clocks and vector clocks, consistent cuts and the snapshot algorithm that computes them, causal versus total ordering of events, in-flight messages treated as channel state, linearizability, and snapshot isolation. My physics is one undergraduate course: Lorentz transformations, worldlines, light cones, proper time, and the relativity of simultaneity. I would not trust myself on anything subtler than that, so please do not lean on my grasp of the formalism beyond it.

Here is the position I want restated in my vocabulary, as faithfully as you can manage: the Rietdijk–Putnam argument that special relativity forces us to give up the claim that only present events are real, together with the metaphysical dispute it is meant to decide between presentism, the block universe, and the growing block — and the two principal lines of resistance to it, namely the proposal that what is real should be indexed to an individual spacetime point and its past light cone rather than to a global simultaneity slice, and the objection that the argument smuggles in an assumption that reality is an equivalence relation.

My own hypothesis, which I want tested rather than flattered, is this. The argument looks to me like the observation that Minkowski spacetime supplies no canonical total order on events, only a partial one — the causal order — and that inertial observers disagree precisely about the incomparable pairs. On that reading, presentism is demanding linearizability from a universe that offers only causal consistency, and the argument is essentially a no-global-snapshot theorem: there is no consistent cut that all observers will agree on, and no principled way to nominate one. Tell me whether this reading survives contact with the argument as it is actually made, where it earns its keep, and where it quietly changes the subject.

Then give me the residue report. Work through the position claim by claim and sort each into one of three bins: what translates cleanly into clocks, cuts, orderings, and logs; what can only be translated by distorting one side or the other; and what has no expression in my vocabulary at all. For the second bin, name the distortion. For the third, I particularly want to know what becomes of the idea of an event coming into existence, of the passage of time, and of any asymmetry between past and future, in a framework where the log is simply there and observers differ only in which parts of it they have yet to observe. Be explicit about every point at which a translation choice is doing philosophical work rather than translating: I do not want eternalism smuggled in through the back door by describing the universe as an already-written log and then counting that description as a neutral rendering of the physicists' picture. Conversely, flag anything my vocabulary affords that the metaphysics seems to lack — channel state as a category of its own, the gap between an observer's local view and the global structure — and say whether the philosophers are missing a distinction or whether the distinction is an artifact of engineering practice.

Finish with a verdict on what the residue means. Is it evidence that my vocabulary is impoverished, that the metaphysical debate is less substantive than it appears once its terms are cashed out, or that my vocabulary has already answered the question before the debate begins? Do not split the difference for politeness; commit to the diagnosis you think is right and say what would have to be true for one of the others to be right instead.

Think carefully before writing, and treat the preliminary work as part of the answer: spend a short opening section sketching candidate mappings and discarding the ones that fail under pressure, before you commit to the translation you intend to defend.
