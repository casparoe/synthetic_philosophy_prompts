I am delivering a paper at an interdisciplinary workshop on the ethics of armed conflict, and what I need from you is a preparation sheet for the question period, not a defense of the view.

My thesis has two halves. The first is revisionist, and I expect little resistance to it from most of the room: the moral equality of combatants is false, and soldiers fighting for an unjust cause act impermissibly when they kill, however excusable they may be. The second half is what the paper is actually about. The practice of treating combatants on both sides as moral equals should be preserved not only in the law of armed conflict but in the formation of soldiers: the state should continue to train and address those it sends to fight as though killing enemy combatants were morally permitted, knowing full well that it is not. My argument for this runs in three steps. (i) An in bello norm conditioned on the justice of one's own cause cannot be applied by the people who must apply it, because ad bellum facts are contested, propagandized, and inaccessible from inside a firefight. (ii) A norm that cannot be applied will not be followed, and where it is not followed, the restraints that presently limit killing in war — proportionality, discrimination, and above all the reciprocity by which each side's compliance is conditioned on the other's — come apart in predictable ways. (iii) The false norm is therefore load-bearing, and the right response to discovering that it is false is not to discard it but to keep it while acknowledging what we are doing. The claim I know to be most exposed is the second part of (iii): that a state may deliberately inculcate a moral falsehood in the people it sends to fight. I call the cost of doing so the bad-faith burden, and I contend that it is real, that it compounds over a career, and that it is still smaller than the cost of the alternative.

The audience is not a philosophy seminar. Present are two traditionalists — a political theorist who holds that the equality is a genuine moral truth because soldiers on both sides are non-culpable threats who cannot answer for the justice of their cause, and a military historian who thinks in terms of how armies have actually behaved; a serving judge advocate with two deployments; two international lawyers who will insist that IHL never asserted a moral permission in the first place, only a legal status; a revisionist trained in the liability-based account of defensive killing who accepts my moral premise and regards my conservatism about the law as a betrayal of it; and a doctoral student working on moral injury in veterans.

Draft the sheet: nine or ten objections in the order of how much damage each would do to the argument if it went unanswered, each phrased in the idiom of the person most likely to raise it — status, privileges and prisoner-of-war protections from the judge advocate, the divergence between law and morals from the lawyers, liability and degrees of responsibility from the revisionist, the lived reality of command from the historian — each with a reply I could deliver in about two minutes and a candid note on where that reply is softest. Then tell me which single objection I should concede before anyone raises it, which one reply has to land for the paper to survive, and the question I am hoping nobody asks, together with a fallback answer in case it comes anyway. I expect the charge that step (iii) merely dresses up the familiar view that law and morality should diverge here; treat that charge as one objection among the others rather than as the headline.

Begin with the first objection. No preface, no restatement of my thesis, no outline of what the sheet will contain, and no bibliography or reference list; where a named position helps identify who will object, name the person and move on.

Finally, close with a short paragraph assessing your own triage: which objection you have ranked low would in fact sink the thesis if pressed hard, and what consideration, if someone in that room supplied it, would lead you to conclude that step (iii) is not merely underdefended but false. I want your judgment about the strength of these objections, not a neutral inventory, but I want to know what would change your mind.
