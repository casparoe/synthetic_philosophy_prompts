I am in the final weeks of a history-of-ideas seminar, and last night I spent the better part of three hours arguing with a friend — a doctoral candidate in colonial Latin American literature — about Sor Juana Inés de la Cruz's *Respuesta a Sor Filotea de la Cruz* of 1691. Neither of us is willing to yield, and since our seminar paper drafts are due shortly, I would like a considered adjudication from someone with no stake in the exchange. I should say at the outset that I am the one advancing the stronger thesis, so please do not hesitate to tell me that it fails.

My reading is that the *Respuesta* is, beneath its conventions, a covert but definite argument for the institutional admission of women to universities. The textual hook I rely on is her autobiographical account of learning as a small child that there were schools and a university in Mexico City where Latin and philosophy were taught, and of begging her mother to send her there dressed in men's clothing so that she could matriculate; I take that anecdote to be doing argumentative work rather than merely coloring a life story. I also lean on her remark, in answering an objection about women teaching or expounding Scripture publicly, that the relevant test is ability rather than sex. On my account, the elaborate humility topoi, the long catalogue of learned women from pagan, Jewish, and Christian antiquity, and above all the theological justification — that God gave her the intellect she has and that refusing to use it would be a kind of ingratitude toward the giver — are largely protective scaffolding: a genre she was obliged to work within because she was writing under the shadow of the Inquisition, which is also why the letter had to be addressed to a pseudonym adopted by the bishop of Puebla, and why she was effectively silenced within a few years. My friend's objection, put bluntly, is that I am importing a modern, rights-based notion of institutional access into a text that is concerned almost entirely with private study and with defending a personal vocation, and that the Inquisition framing is anachronistic melodrama.

Could you address four specific points? First, does the childhood episode about dressing as a boy function in the argument as an institutional claim, or is it better read as part of a narrative of God-given natural inclination, in which case it would cut against my use of it? Second, how much weight can the ability-rather-than-sex passage bear, given that elsewhere she appears to favor older learned women instructing younger ones privately, and to accept the Pauline prohibition on women teaching publicly, rather than proposing that women be admitted to chairs or matriculated? Third, was there in fact any Inquisitorial proceeding against her, or should the pressure she describes be attributed to the bishop of Puebla, to her confessor, and to the unauthorized publication of her earlier theological letter? Fourth, how much of the theology is load-bearing argument rather than decoration — is it defensible to treat it as tactical? In answering, please be explicit about the boundary between what specialists regard as settled and what remains genuinely contested: I am aware that the familiar biographical narrative of persecution and of a coerced renunciation late in her life has been substantially reassessed, and I do not want a response that quietly adopts one side of that scholarly dispute as though it were established fact. Where you think one of my claims is simply mistaken rather than merely debatable, say so directly.
