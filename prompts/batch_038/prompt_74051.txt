Just got off the phone with Dana after almost three hours of argueing about Thomson's lamp and I'm still buzzing, so I figure the only way to actually settle it in my own head is to see it as a story. Quick version of the fight: I said the whole thing shows the supertask is incoherent, because at the end of the halving schedule there just isn't any fact about whether the lamp is on or off. Dana said Benacerraf buried that decades ago — nothing about what happens before the final instant forces an answer at it, so there's no contradiction, only a gap in the description, and the gap is the point. I said fine, then it's a physics problem, since no real switch can flip infinitely many times in a minute. And she said, great, then build the thing with a real filament and tell me what you get. That's the space I want the story to live in.

Here's the setup I've got in my head. Small town, an eccentric instrument-maker dies and leaves a sealed basement room containing his life's work: a lamp wired to a clock that halves its own interval each time it throws the switch, so the whole infinite schedule runs itself out at exactly noon on a named day, which the executors duly witness. The will is drafted badly on purpose, or maybe not on purpose. Whichever of his two estranged kids can show the lamp was on at noon takes the bulk of the estate; if neither state can be established, it all goes to the county. And buried in a codicil is the old man's actual obsession, which neither kid cares about at all: he maintained the rig was a computer, that a machine which finishes an infinite search by noon could settle questions no ordinary machine can touch, if only you knew how to read the answer at the limit. The narrator should be the lawyer or the executor, someone whose job is to decide what the words commit anybody to.

The bit I really want you to lean on, the thing Dana and I never resolved: the filament doesn't switch instantaneously. Its brightness lags, so the state isn't a clean 0-or-1 function but a dimming curve, and the total light thrown in those last instants converges to something perfectly well defined even though the binary question supposedly has no answer. So the lawyer is stuck between two readings of one word — "on" as the indeterminate yes/no question the old man thought he was asking, and "on" as a brightness above some threshold, which is determinate but arbitrary in where you put the threshold. Even better if someone notices that feed the same physical device a different-but-equally-legitimate halving schedule, one starting off instead of on, and the residual glow at noon comes out different, which is a weird thing to be true of a question of fact. I want that felt through people's behavior, not explained: somebody should be tempted to say the final state is like a limit that doesn't exist, and somebody else should snap back that a real lamp was never a characteristic function to begin with.

Some constraints: 1,500–2,500 words, present or past tense, whatever you like. No info-dumps about supertasks and no lecture at the end — close on the narrator doing something that commits them, signing or not signing, making a call, killing the power, whatever. Don't cite anything, real or invented, and don't put words in any philosopher's mouth; the names can come up in dialogue the way two educated people would actually say them, that's it. Keep the physics loose, but please don't fumble the halving arithmetic, because that's exactly the thing I would notice.
