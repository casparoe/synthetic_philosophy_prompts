So I'm one of nine members of my city's charter review commission (mid-sized place, about 240k people, council-manager government) and in three weeks we vote on a single recommendation that goes on the November ballot. The proposal currently on the table: expand the council from 9 to 24 seats, fill 15 of those seats by stratified lottery off the voter roll, one 30-month non-renewable term, with a civic-duty-style opt-out and a $50/day stipend for anyone actually seated; the other 9 stay elected at-large but pick up a two-consecutive-term limit. Half the commission thinks this is the fix for a council that has turned into a full-time job nobody can hold except people who already have money or a boss who lets them moonlight; the other half thinks we're about to hand a legislature to people nobody can fire.

Here's the part I want the memo to actually wrestle with instead of reciting the standard case for and against sortition. We ran a pilot last spring — 40 names drawn for a panel on a parking-pricing change. 27 of the 40 declined, and the decliners were overwhelmingly hourly-wage workers and parents of small kids even with the stipend, which means the seated panel was self-selected toward people with schedule slack. Then, of the 13 who served, the recommendation they landed on in week one was the near-opposite of what they signed off on in week four, after city staff briefed them. The sponsors call that a feature, deliberation doing its thing. One of my colleagues says it proves the sorted members are just whoever-gets-the-last-briefing, i.e. the permanent bureaucracy quietly becomes the legislature, and a body with no reelection incentive and no electoral accountability has no structural defense against capture. I don't think he's wrong, but I think he's assuming elections are the only anti-capture mechanism available, which a 13-person pilot run once can't establish either way — and neither of us can point to anything that would.

Draft the short written opinion I'll circulate to the other eight comissioners before Thursday's session. It has to come down somewhere: endorse as drafted, reject, or endorse with amendments, and if amendments, make them specific enough that someone could write them into the ballot language — stipend structure and whether it scales with lost wages, term length, who briefs the seated members and whether sorted members get agenda-setting or subpoena-ish powers over staff, mandatory-service-with-hardship-exemptions vs. free opt-out, that kind of thing. Give the capture worry its strongest form and then say honestly whether the amendments actually answer it or just dress it up; I'd much rather read a memo that concedes a residual problem than one that pretends the fix is total. Format-wise: exactly five numbered items, each one or two sentences, no introduction and no closing remarks — item 1 the bottom-line recommendation, items 2 through 4 the reasons, item 5 the strongest objection and what's left of it after the amendments. That compression is deliberate, our packet rules cap written opinions at ten sentences and I want five lines my colleagues can argue with rather than two pages they'll skim.
