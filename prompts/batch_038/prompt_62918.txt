Retired three years ago from a hydrology outfit where I'd spent my last decade running an in-house forecasting exercise — about forty of us handing in probability forecasts each month against a fixed list of questions, scored with the Brier score, rule published in advance and never touched mid-season. I hated the logarithmic score for that job: one overconfident wrong call and a forecaster's whole season was toast, while a bounded rule let you recover. Since retiring I've been sitting in on a continuing-ed philosophy course and I've wandered into this accuracy-first / epistemic utility literature, and I keep having the uneasy feeling that these people are running a forecasting competition in which nobody is permitted to ask who wrote the rules. The shape of the arguments I keep meeting: pick a measure of inaccuracy, show that any credence function failing the probability axioms is beaten by one that satisfies them no matter how the world turns out, conclude probabilism, then run something similar to get an updating rule. Reasonable enough, but before I read another one of these papers I want the spec rather than the results.

So write me the list of requirements a measure of inaccuracy — and, more broadly, a whole epistemic utility theory — has to meet to count as adequate. I want the genuinely load-bearing conditions, in plain language of your own rather than as a recital of the standard names, each with the case or consideration behind it: why would anyone insist that total inaccuracy be the simple sum over the individual propositions, and what breaks if it isn't; why the measure should be such that an honest agent expects her own credences to score best, and whether that's a virtue or a bias baked in from the start; what continuity, boundedness and symmetry are doing there and whether any of them are doing real work; what has to be true of the setup for a dominance claim that holds in every possible world to be worth anything to an actual person. Three things from my old life I especially want addressed: whether the rule may be chosen by the person it's applied to (in our competition it was fixed publicly and in advance, and I can't see what the analogue is over here); whether some questions can matter more than others depending on how things turn out, since a flood warning and a wager on whether the office picnic got rained out were never worth the same to us; and whether the score shifts when you re-carve the same possibilities into more or fewer questions, which caused an actual shouting match at our 2016 awards lunch. Also, does the theory have to be able to move an agent who already holds incoherent credences, judging by her own lights, or is world-by-world dominance enough? And one case I'm stuck on: I know that tomorrow a junior colleague will hand me a dataset that I will, through sheer stubborness, misread — if the accuracy-first calculation says it's better not to look, is that a reductio of the theory or a correct prediction it should own?

Give me exactly five numbered desiderata — no introduction, no conclusion, and skip the scorecard of which existing view passes, I'll do that myself. Two or three sentences per item: the requirement in plain language, the case or consideration motivating it, and whether you count it as non-negotiable or as a prejudice of one particular camp. If two of your five can't both be satisfied, say so right there in the item where the collision shows up rather than in a section of its own — that's the part I most want to see.
