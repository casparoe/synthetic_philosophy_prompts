I have published four novels and am at work on a fifth. Over a decade of interviews, reviews, and correspondence with academic readers, I have kept encountering a pattern for which I have no vocabulary, and I should like to know whether one exists.

The pattern has three stages, and it is the third that interests me.

Stage one. For reasons that are purely logistical, a contingent detail goes into the manuscript. In my second book a minor character whistles whenever he is lying, because I needed a way for the reader to pick him out of a crowded chapter and something for him to do with his hands during long stretches of dialogue. In the third, a pivotal scene falls on a Tuesday, because the plot required the market to be open and Tuesday was the day I had settled on for the market. Neither detail carried meaning for me; both were scaffolding.

Stage two. Readers find meaning anyway. The whistling was written up as the book's central figure for a man who cannot speak plainly. The Tuesday was praised for its insistence on the ordinary. I have made the correction in print more than once — said outright, in one interview, that Tuesday was chosen for the market and for nothing else — and the correction has never stuck. The reading survives the denial, is restated in the next survey, and ends up in the summary of the book that most readers will ever meet.

Stage three, the part I cannot name. The accident becomes binding, and binding on me. The whistling character returns in the fifth book, and I have found that I cannot write him without the whistle; to omit it now would feel like a betrayal of the character, though nothing in my own conception of him ever required it. Worse — and here I want to be scrupulous, since it is easy for a writer to flatter himself — when I try now to remember why I gave him the whistle, I retrieve something like a reason. I cannot tell whether I am recovering an intention that was present all along in a form too inarticulate to report at the time, or manufacturing one on demand; and I notice that the second possibility does not feel like the second possibility from the inside.

So the thing I want a name for is not the familiar complaint that readers find meanings an author never meant. It is the direction of the authority. An unintended feature of a finished work acquires, through being taken up by readers, the normative standing of an intention — standing it did not have when the work was made — and then governs what the author may subsequently write and even what the author can honestly remember about having written. Authority appears to run backwards, from reception to composition. And the author's explicit disavowal proves powerless to revoke it, while the author's later confession that he meant it all along appears to add nothing: neither the denial nor the retroactive avowal alters the work's standing, which suggests that whatever is doing the binding is not the author's mind at all.

Two questions, then.

First: is there an established concept, in the philosophy of interpretation or in literary theory, for this backward flow — for the post hoc conferral on an accident of the status of an intention, and for the failure of the author's denial to undo it? I know the general neighbourhood. I know there is a mid-century argument that consulting an author's intentions is a mistake, and I know there are later positions on which what matters is not the intention the author actually had but the intention a competent audience would postulate. I am asking whether any of those already covers my stage three, and if so, exactly where the coverage runs out. In law, as I understand it, a principal can become bound by an act performed in the principal's name without authority, simply by later going along with it; I do not know whether that structure has been borrowed into aesthetics, and I would want to be told why it cannot be borrowed, if it cannot.

Second, if there is no name: give me one. But I am not after a label. A bare name would be worse than useless to me, because I would not know how far it reaches. I want the carving. State what the phenomenon is and what it is not. Distinguish it from the mere fact that interpretations outrun intentions; from an ordinary misattribution of meaning by a reader; from the case in which an author genuinely did intend something and simply could not articulate it for years; and from the mundane way a series constrains its later installments through consistency alone. Tell me where the edges lie. Does it apply to a detail the author intended but never disclosed? Does it apply when the uptake comes from one influential critic rather than from readers generally? Does the author's sincere later confabulation convert the accident into a real intention, so that stage three terminates in an intention rather than in a fiction wearing the author's own face? And must any of this be true for the constraint I feel to be rational — or is my inability to write the character without his whistle a piece of professional superstition that the concept, once named, would dissolve?

I am not asking for sources, references, or a reading list; I am asking for the concepts and their boundaries, from whatever you already have.

One request about candour. If you think my description rests on a confusion — if, for instance, what I call a purely logistical detail was never free of intention and the whole problem dissolves on that ground, or if I have bundled together two or three distinct phenomena that ought to be prised apart — I would much rather be told so plainly than have my framing honoured out of politeness. And since you may be reconstructing a literature from a handful of landmarks, mark your confidence as you go: say where you are confident that no established term exists, where you are merely unable to think of one, and which existing position comes closest even if it comes closest only loosely.

Finish with a single line, and nothing else on it, in the form: Verdict: [the established term, or, if there is none, the term you propose].
