I retired four years ago after thirty-odd years in marine geophysics, and I've fallen into philosophy of time pretty much by accident — mostly by reading around the edges of relativity, which I thought I understood. I've now talked myself into a position I can't seem to shake, and I want you to go after it rather than nod along with me.

The position: Gödel's rotating universe settles the question of whether time objectively passes, and settles it in the negative.

Here's my reasoning, such as it is. The field equations of GR admit an exact, singularity-free solution containing dust and a cosmological constant in which the whole cosmos rotates and, as I understand it, closed timelike curves run through every event, not just out past some radius (please correct me if I've got that wrong). A spacetime with curves like that can't carry a global time function — there's no way to slice it into a stack of nows ordered by an earlier/later relation that the geometry itself picks out rather than a coordinate choice. And the moral Gödel draws, as I read it, isn't the science-fiction one about shooting your grandfather. It's that if the laws of gravity permit a world in which nothing at all counts as coming into being, then coming-into-being can't be part of physical reality, becuase what's physically real is fixed by the laws and not by which solution happens to be ours.

That last step is where I want the pressure applied. I keep running into the reply that our universe doesn't rotate, the microwave background is isotropic, therefore case closed — and I think that reply misses. Mine is an argument about the solution space of the theory, not about our particular corner of it. Telling me the actual cosmos has negligible vorticity feels beside the point, like objecting to a reductio on the grounds that the counterexample isn't in the room.

So tell me what would count against me. Specifically:

- Rank the defeaters, fatal ones first and merely awkward ones last. I want to know which I'd have to fold on and which I could shrug off.
- My argument has at least three load-bearing joints: curves of that kind rule out a global time function; the absence of a global time function rules out objective becoming; and permissibility by the field equations tells us something about reality rather than about the theory's permissiveness. Which of those can fail on its own? Can I lose the second and keep the rest?
- Does the argument actually need closed timelike curves, or would something weaker — a failure of stable causality, or of global hyperbolicity — do the same work? If I'm committed to more geometry than I need, where does the extra commitment expose me?
- What would a defender of the ideality-of-time line themselves concede as counting against them, as opposed to what critics say? I'm thinking about the stability of such solutions under perturbation, energy conditions, whether classical GR is the right place to read metaphysics off at all, and what quantum gravity might do here.
- Does my position predict or explain anything that a rival doesn't — including a rival that also denies objective becoming but wants nothing to do with rotating universes? If it risks nothing, say so.
- Is there a point where I've qualified it into safety? If I retreat to something like the laws don't privilege a becoming, am I still saying anything that could come out false, and is that a problem for a claim that started life as a refutation?

And at the end, tell me the single thing that ought to make me give this up. Not a reading list, I just want the reasoning laid out. Sorry if the setup is long — I'm still learning to ask these questions.
