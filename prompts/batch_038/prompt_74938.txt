I would like a credence together with the arithmetic behind it. Take two principles for updating on self-locating evidence, stipulated as follows so that the usual terminological quarrels are set aside. The sampling principle (the self-sampling assumption) says: assign credences in proportion to a hypothesis's prior probability times the fraction of the observers it postulates who share your total evidence. The counting principle (the self-indication assumption) says: assign them in proportion to the prior times the sheer number of observers it postulates who share your total evidence. The two differ by exactly one factor — the total number of observers a hypothesis says exist.

Now a case of my own devising. Two cosmologies, Ω and Σ, equally simple, otherwise empirically equivalent, and equally probable a priori. Ω says the cosmos contains 10^30 observers, of whom only about 10^5 have evidence like yours. Σ says it contains 10^12 observers, essentially all of them with evidence like yours. The sampling principle favours Σ over Ω by roughly 10^25 to 1; the counting principle favours Σ by only about 10^7 to 1. Both point the same way, yet they are eighteen orders of magnitude apart, and it is the vastly more populous cosmology that the counting principle here demotes — the opposite of what its critics allege.

What is your credence that the counting principle, and not the sampling principle, gives the correct update?
