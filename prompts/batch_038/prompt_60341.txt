I am preparing a short position statement for a reading group on decision theory under ambiguity, and before circulating it I would like an honest consistency audit. What follows are the commitments I intend to defend, stated as plainly as I can. They are a mixture of theses, verdicts on particular cases, and methodological preferences. I have not attempted to reconcile them in advance, and I do not know where the difficulties lie; locating them is what I am asking you to do.

1. The Ellsberg preferences are rational as they stand, and are not a cognitive error to be explained away. Concretely, in an urn containing thirty red balls and sixty further balls that are either black or yellow in an unknown proportion, I prefer a bet on red to a bet on black, and I likewise prefer a bet on "not red" to a bet on "not black."

2. I therefore do not accept the sure-thing principle as a general condition of rationality. I treat its failure in the case above as the datum a theory must accommodate, rather than as a symptom of confused preferences.

3. I nevertheless hold expected utility theory strictly for objective lotteries. Where a randomizing device with a publicly known physical chance is involved — a wheel, a die, a coin — I follow expected utility and separability without exception, including the reduction of compound lotteries.

4. My doxastic state is a single, sharp probability function. My credence that the next ball drawn from the urn in (1) is red is one third, and my credence that it is black is also one third. I deny that ambiguity aversion requires imprecise credences, and I deny that it is a matter of second-order uncertainty, or of having beliefs about one's own beliefs.

5. I also reject the deflationary suggestion that what is really going on is unfamiliarity with a domain, or a lack of competence in assessing it. How practiced I am at a task cannot, in my view, do the normative work.

6. The correct formal treatment nonetheless evaluates an act by its worst case over a family of probability measures. I maintain that this family is a feature of the decision problem as it is posed to me, not a feature of my doxastic state: it records what the situation leaves open, not what I believe.

7. Ambiguity is a function of evidence and of nothing else. Two agents with the same evidence face the same ambiguity, and an agent who learns the composition of the urn no longer faces any.

8. I require immunity to a money pump. Across any finite sequence of decision problems, with free choice at each node and no possibility of binding prior commitment, my choices must not be exploitable into a sure loss. I accept the dynamic reasoning that goes with this: at each node I choose what I prefer at that node, in the light of what I know then.

9. Consider a variant of my own construction. A machine fills an urn with exactly thirty red balls and sixty further balls, each of which is independently made black or yellow by the toss of a fair coin; the machine records every toss and the record is then destroyed. The composition is therefore fixed, and in principle recoverable from the discarded record, but nobody has recovered it. My verdict: there is no ambiguity here, and ambiguity aversion is unwarranted. At even stakes I should be indifferent between the bet on red and the bet on black, and indifferent between the corresponding "not red" and "not black" bets, because a known generator stands behind the composition and ordinary calculation applies.

10. Consider a second variant. The composition is chosen by a person who has been told precisely which bets I will be offered, and who wants me to lose. My verdict: reasoning to the worst-case composition here is not ambiguity aversion at all. It is simply correct strategic reasoning about an adversary, and I would carry it out with complete confidence rather than as a hedge against ignorance.

What I want from you is an audit, not a lecture. Specifically:

- Identify the genuine tensions among these commitments and rank them by severity. Please do not present every friction as equally serious, and do not pad the list.
- For each genuine tension, say exactly which commitment or commitments must be surrendered, what is lost by surrendering them, and whether more than one exit is available. Where a commitment looks revisable but is in fact load-bearing for something else I hold, say so.
- Distinguish these from the pairs that merely look like tensions and dissolve under inspection, and explain why they dissolve.
- Note any commitment that turns out to be doing no work at all once the others are in place.
- Finish with the single revision that would clear the most difficulty at the least cost, and describe briefly what the resulting position would look like.

Please engage with (9) and (10) as cases in their own right, and not merely as illustrations of my theses.
