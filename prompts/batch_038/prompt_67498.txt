You are a social epistemologist who works on the philosophy of disagreement and who also advises real deliberative bodies — expert review panels, consensus conferences, structured elicitations — on how they handle internal dissent. Please think carefully and work through this in stages rather than answering on the first pass.

I chair a technical review committee at a public health agency, and I read epistemology on the side, including the long argument between those who think you should move substantially toward an equally well-informed person who disagrees with you and those who think you may hold your ground. I have run into a pattern in my own reasoning that I cannot name, and I want to know whether it has already been recognized.

Here is the pattern. When my committee splits on a question I have no stake in — whether a given survey instrument measures what it claims to measure, say — I revise cheerfully. We have read the same submissions, we are all competent, so the fact of disagreement is itself evidence that I have gone wrong somewhere, and I move most of the way across. But when the question touches work I have built, something different happens, and it does not feel like resistance from the inside. I find myself asking not whether the dissenting member is well informed in general, but whether she is well informed *on this*: has she run this particular assay, has she handled data of this shape, has she read the literature on the subgroup in question, does she know why the field abandoned the earlier method. Every one of those questions is one I would happily grant in the low-stakes case too. Yet by the time I have asked all of them, the set of people who count as equally well placed has contracted to people who already agree with me — sometimes to me alone. And then I revise, fully and sincerely, toward a set that contains no dissenters.

The same thing happens outside work. On a historical question I care about I halve my confidence without difficulty. On a live political question tangled up with my own identity, I discover that being equally well placed must be assessed sub-question by sub-question — knowledge of the region, access to sources in the language, whether their community has an interest in a particular answer — and again each consideration is genuine, and again the upshot is that nobody who disagrees with me is quite in a position to.

The feature that puzzles me is that the standard never changes. I am not applying a harsher test to the people I disagree with than to those I agree with; if anything I am applying a more detailed one, and the detail is warranted. What changes is the size of the units the test gets applied to. Any competence can be subdivided indefinitely, and at each level the further subdivision looks reasonable, so there is no moment at which I could catch myself doing anything wrong. The obligation to revise is never violated — it is dissolved. And it survives intact only in the cases where it costs me nothing.

Candidates I have considered and find unsatisfying: a double standard or special pleading (but it is the same standard, not a different one); the move of excluding a counterexample by redefining membership (closer, except that no membership is being protected by fiat — each exclusion is backed by a real fact); moving the goalposts (the goalposts do not move; the field is subdivided); motivated reasoning or bias (true but too blunt — it does not explain why the move is invisible from inside and why each step is independently defensible); and the phenomenon of experts pronouncing confidently outside their own field, which is about the trespasser rather than about me.

What I would like from you:

1. Before committing to an answer, brainstorm broadly. Generate candidate labels and near-miss concepts from at least four directions — the philosophical literature on disagreement, the fallacy taxonomy of informal logic, the study of expertise and professional judgment including how panels are designed to prevent distortions like this one, and anywhere else you think relevant. Rank them by fit and say why each ranks where it does.

2. Then tell me which established concept this actually is, if any, and be precise about how what I described differs from each of the closest candidates. In particular, address the familiar worry that determining who is equally well placed may itself require settling the disputed question. Is what I have described a version of that worry, a way of exploiting it, or something independent of it?

3. If nothing established fits, say so plainly and coin a term, with a definition stated as conditions rather than as a slogan, plus boundary cases. The boundary I most need drawn: there are real situations in which insisting on finer subdivisions is exactly right — a distinguished cardiologist is not thereby well placed to judge a claim about a drug's renal clearance. What separates legitimate specialization from the failure I described? Give me a test I could apply to my own reasoning in the moment, and tell me honestly whether such a test is possible for someone standing inside the pattern.

4. Finally, what does this do to the two positions in the debate I mentioned? Is it a new objection to the conciliating view, an objection to the steadfast one, or an argument that both need a rule fixing the level of detail in advance of any particular disagreement?

Please flag your uncertainty rather than papering over it: if you are not sure whether a term is standard, say that it may not be instead of assigning it to an author or a work. I am not asking for references or a reading list — I would rather have your reasoned judgment about the conceptual geography than citations I cannot check. A name alone will not satisfy me; the point is the carving — what this phenomenon is, what it is not, and exactly where its edges lie. Aim for roughly 900 to 1200 words, with short headings marking the four parts.
