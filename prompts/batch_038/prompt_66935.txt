I am trying to settle what to demand of an account of higher-order evidence before committing to any particular one, and I want the specification written out rather than reverse-engineered from the usual parade of cases. Fix the target of adequacy as follows: the account is meant to guide a bounded reasoner that can choose whether to check its own work, not an ideally self-transparent one. It has to issue usable instructions to an agent whose self-monitoring is itself fallible, partial, and expensive, and whose higher-order evidence typically arrives in a form that does not tell it what to do next.

Set out the desiderata as a numbered list. For each entry give: the requirement, stated precisely; the case or consideration that motivates it; and a label marking it non-negotiable, negotiable, or a prejudice of a particular camp. Then tell me which single requirement does the most to exclude contenders, and — the part I most want — identify any subset of the requirements that cannot be jointly satisfied, with the argument. If the list turns out to be an impossibility result wearing the costume of a shopping list, say so plainly.

Among the things I expect to see, and want pressed on rather than merely itemized:

- Which principles the account must accept or explicitly refuse: the enkratic requirement, whatever it is that licenses splitting one's levels, and the question whether higher-order evidence changes what your evidence supports or only whether you may rely on that support. Draw out what each choice implies about whether rational epistemic akrasia is possible.
- Which distinctions it may not collapse: evidence about your evidence versus evidence about whether your evidence supports the proposition; report-directed evidence about this particular episode of reasoning versus base-rate evidence about reasoners of your kind in situations of your kind; a state's being irrational versus its being merely excusable; and the three separate questions of knowledge, justification, and rational belief, which accounts in this area routinely treat as one question.
- Whether it survives iteration — higher-order evidence about the reasoner's response to the first report, and then about that response. Either the account is stable under iteration or it owes an explanation of why stability is not a requirement.

It must also handle the following three cases, which I have constructed so that no appeal to ideal reasoning dissolves them.

1. Unknown scope. You are reliably informed that some unidentified fraction of your conclusions from the past week were reached under conditions that degrade the sort of reasoning those conclusions depend on. The degradation has no known direction: it does not push you toward or away from any particular proposition. Nothing tells you which conclusions are affected. Say what the account instructs you to do with that set, and with your confidence in your ability to pick out its members.
2. The impaired instrument. Your only means of detecting the degradation is the very capacity suspected of being degraded: your own ability to evaluate your own reasoning. Can the deliverances of that capacity legitimately raise or lower confidence in the capacity itself? If not, on what non-circular basis are you to respond to the report at all?
3. The steadfast akratic. Having taken the report seriously, you continue to believe p while also believing that, given the report, believing p is irrational. Is that state rational, irrational but excusable, or irrational outright — and should the answer differ depending on whether p concerns the ordinary world or your own reliability?

Close with a short assessment of the live positions: the view that higher-order evidence never genuinely defeats, the level-splitting view, and the view that rationality requires coherence between your first-order and higher-order attitudes. Two or three sentences each, scored against the list rather than against one another. Argue from the content of the positions; I want no reference list and no bibliography.
