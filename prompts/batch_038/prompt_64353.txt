You are a philosopher of interpretation who has worked at the border between analytic aesthetics and literary theory, and you are known for building diagnostic instruments that sort people by what they actually believe rather than by the labels they claim. Please think carefully about the structure of the problem before you write a single question.

**Setting and purpose.** I convene a small interdisciplinary reading group — philosophy graduate students, two literary scholars, a translator, and a working editor — that has been arguing in circles for weeks about whether what a text means is settled by what its author meant. The dispute has turned tribal: members announce allegiances (intentionalist, anti-intentionalist, Barthesian) that they have never tested against a case. I want a private sorting instrument that each member can complete in about twenty minutes and then compare with the others, so that the group discovers where its members actually diverge — including divergences they have been papering over with shared vocabulary.

**Part 1 — the logical space. Do this first, as explicit brainstorming, and show your reasoning.** Identify three or four independent axes along which positions on authorial intention genuinely come apart. Distinctions you should consider, though you may reject or supplement them: whether intentions of any kind are meaning-constitutive or merely evidence about meaning; whether a text means what its author successfully realized in it, or what she intended whether or not she succeeded; whose intention governs when the actual author's private evidence (letters, diaries, later interviews) conflicts with what the original audience could have recovered from the public text; whether the author is a historical person or a figure constructed by the interpreter; whether there is one correct interpretation or several compatible or incompatible ones; and whether the question about meaning is even the same question as the question about how the work should be evaluated, or how it should be read now. For each axis, give its range of possible values, then say which combinations of values are occupied, which are incoherent, and which are merely unexplored.

**Part 2 — the instrument.** Ten to twelve numbered nodes arranged as a decision tree, with the branching made explicit at every step (a flat numbered list that says where each answer leads is acceptable; an ASCII diagram at the end would be a bonus). Every node must be a concrete micro-case on which the positions genuinely differ, with two or three answer options in plain, non-technical language. Strict rules:

1. No node may name a theorist, a school, or any "-ism." A member must be able to answer it without knowing which leaf each answer leads to.
2. At least half the cases must be ones on which the familiar stock examples give no traction. Build several around non-standard authorship and non-standard texts, drawing on material such as: a manuscript found with no author, no date, and no context; a work written by a committee with no single mind behind it; a hoax in which the named author is a persona invented by someone else; a text whose author, now living with advanced dementia, can no longer say what she meant; a poem the author publicly disavowed decades later, insisting it was meant ironically and that everyone read it straight; a misprint that two centuries of readers have taken as meaningful and that most now defend as belonging to the work; a private letter showing the author intended an ambiguous pronoun to pick out one referent, where the public conventions of the language at the time permit two; a translation of a work whose original is lost, so that the only recoverable intentions are the translator's; a machine-generated text with no author at all. You may substitute or add cases you consider sharper, but at least two nodes must be ones on which the standard intention debate loses its grip entirely.
3. At least two nodes must admit a well-formed answer of the form "none of these fits me," so that the instrument can detect people who fall outside the tree instead of forcing them into a leaf.
4. End with a completion guide or scoring key: for each leaf, the exact answer path that leads there.

**Part 3 — the leaves.** Among the positions that must appear as leaves are: a Hirsch-style actual intentionalism on which meaning is fixed by the author's will; a Beardsley-style view on which the work's meaning is constituted by what is publicly available in the text; a Levinson-style hypothetical intentionalism on which the work means what an ideally informed audience would be best justified in attributing to the author on the basis of the text and its public context; a Nehamas-style view on which interpretation constructs a postulated author who is not identical with the historical person; a Knapp-and-Michaels-style view on which there is no gap between intention and meaning to close, so that the whole debate is a pseudo-problem; a moderate actual intentionalism of the kind Davies and Stecker defend, on which failed intentions can still partly determine meaning; a pluralist or textualist view of the kind Margolis defends, on which several incompatible interpretations can each be defensible; and a Barthes-style author-effacement view. Add any further leaves the space from Part 1 demands. For each leaf, give:

- the position's name, or "unnamed" if nobody holds it;
- one sentence stating its most awkward commitment — the thing its defender least wants to say out loud;
- one sentence on what that position is obliged to say about at least one of the non-standard cases from Part 2.

Then mark explicitly any combination of answers from Part 1 that corresponds to no leaf, and say briefly why the slot is empty: incoherent, unattractive, or simply unexplored.

**Finally, flag every pair of leaves your tree cannot separate,** rather than papering over the failure. I expect at least two near-collapses: between the view that interpretation recovers the actual author's intention and the view that intention is only ever recovered through the text, which may never diverge in practice; and between hypothetical intentionalism and the postulated-author view, whose difference may be metaphysical rather than operational. For each pair, say whether some further case could in principle separate them and sketch that case if so; if not, say so plainly.

Two housekeeping constraints. If you are not confident about the fine print of any named position, describe it in terms of its commitments rather than leaning on the label — I care about the commitments, not the doxography. And do not include a bibliography, reading list, or citations; the instrument has to stand on its own.
