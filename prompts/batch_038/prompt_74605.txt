Right, so I'm drafting the requirements spec for a piece of work on giving a moderately capable deployed system hard deontic lines it won't cross — the sort of thing where you can't just shove a big negative weight on "killing" into the objective and call it a constraint. Before I write any of that up, though, I want to know what philosophy actually owes me here. So: write the desiderata for a theory of agent-relative moral constraints, i.e. what any account has to deliver to be usable for that purpose at all. For each one, give me the motivating case or the consideration that makes it a requirement rather than a hobby horse.

The things I want the list to cover, roughly:

- the paradox of deontology itself. A theory has to say something about the situation where the only way to stop five constraint-violations is to commit one yourself. Does it dissolve that, explain it away, or bite the bullet — and can I tell in advance which it's going to do?
- which distinctions it must not collapse. Minimising violations vs respecting constraints; doing vs allowing; intending a harm vs merely foreseeing it; and — this one matters most to me — a constraint binding the agent's own choices vs a constraint that's imposed on it from outside by somebody else. Those last two get blurred constantly in the engineering literature and I suspect they can't both do the work people want.
- the scope puzzles. Can a constraint bind an entity that never has a default state of inaction? A machine is always doing something; the nearest thing to forbearance available to it is being switched off, which is also a decision somebody makes. What does a constraint against killing even prohibit in that setting, and does the theory have resources for it or does it just quietly assume a human-shaped agent?
- tractability and epistemics. Deciding in real time, under uncertainty, whether an act of mine would cross the line — including the messy bit where I'm unsure whether the line is crossed at all. What does a theory owe you when the constraint's application conditions are vague or contested?
- the trade-off worry, stated properly. A cost function with a large penalty on violation is still a trade-off: given big enough stakes the system violates the constraint and that's the correct output. What would a theory have to say about why that's a failure rather than a feature, and is that intuition a genuine desideratum or just a prejudice of the anti-consequentialist camp? I'd like you to be honest about which side of that line each requirement falls on.
- what happens to the constraint when the agent could get someone else to do the dirty work — delegation, and also the softer version where it just doesn't prevent the violation.

Mark clearly which requirements are non-negotiable for the purpose and which are preferences of particular camps, and tell me which single requirement knocks out the largest number of otherwise attractive views. If you think some subset of them is jointly unsatisfiable, say so plainly and show the argument — I'd much rather have that as a result up front than discover it after the thing is built. And flag the requirements you suspect I've only put on the list because they're fashionable, not because they're load-bearing.

Then, briefly and only briefly, a paragraph each on how the main contenders do against the list: threshold deontology, an absolutist line, and whatever contractualist or rule-consequentialist story you'd reach for. I'm not after a ranking, and no references please — I want the reasoning, not a bibliography.
