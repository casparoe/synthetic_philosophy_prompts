I run a two-person integrity unit at a mid-sized development agency. We fund local partner organisations in about eight countries and our own program officers send us referrals — the district health officer's brother just got hired onto the project, our country director was given a cow, someone had to slip 20 dollars to get a permit stamped — roughly five or six a month. At the moment we answer these off vibes and the results are embarrasing. If we flag something, a partner's funding can get suspended and a relationship we spent years building dies; if we don't, we're signing off on it and the board sees the pattern in aggregate at the quarterly review. There's no lawyer on call and I have maybe half a day a week for this.

We do have one piece of house doctrine, a heuristic a previous director made up, and I want you to build a real procedure out of it while telling me where it breaks. The heuristic: a benefit is presumptively corrupt only if it's convertible, meaning whoever received it could turn it into cash, hand it to somebody else, or use it outside the relationship it was given in. A shared meal, a public ceremony honouring an official, a contribution that stays inside the community — not convertible, therefore not corrupt, no matter how large. Two riders. A convertible benefit is still cleared if the decision would have come out the same anyway. And anything gets flagged if neither party would want the arrangement described accurately in the local paper.

What I need back is an ordered procedure a program officer with no legal or philosophical training can run in about thirty minutes per referral: the questions to ask in sequence, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where it stops at no flag, what has to be written down for me, who signs off on what, and — importantly — the points where it hands the decision to a human being because the question is one of values rather than facts. Also tell me what to do when we simply can't find out a relevant fact, since half the time we can't.

It has to say something about the relativism fight rather than ducking it. Half our board thinks we're exporting Western standards and should defer to local norms of gift-giving and obligation, which in a lot of these places are genuinely load-bearing, not a cover story. The other half thinks corruption is corruption everywhere and saying otherwise is a slur on the local staff who refuse to play. The definition the big international bodies use — the abuse of power someone has been entrusted with for private gain — doesn't settle it, and US anti-bribery law carves out small payments that merely speed up routine government action, which is a precedent I can't tell whether it helps us or undercuts us. So I want each step of your procedure labelled as to whether its answer is allowed to vary from country to country, and why.

Then run it on three referrals. One: our country director accepted a cash contribution toward his daughter's wedding from a chief whose district we fund, it was announced publicly, and everyone in the district expected it of him — declining would have been the insult. Two: a partner put a project officer's brother on the payroll at a salary well above whatever work he actually does. Three: a training where every participant gets a per diem that everyone involved knows is the real reason they turn up.

Be blunt about where the convertibility idea gets things backwards — my suspicion is that a job isn't liquid at all, but a brother on the payroll is worse than a cow — and about which steps are judgment calls dressed up as procedure. At least one of the three should come out with an answer I won't like, and I'd rather you say so than soften it.
