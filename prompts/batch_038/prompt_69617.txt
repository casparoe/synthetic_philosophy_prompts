I'm a fourth-year PhD student in philosophy of religion, and the second chapter of my dissertation is about what God's middle knowledge can actually deliver by way of providential control. My designated interlocutor for the chapter is a senior colleague, Prof. Keszler, a convinced Molinist who has spent twenty years defending the view; she has sat in on our providence reading group all year and has been pushed on a run of cases, and she has answered each of them on the record, which is why I can give you her verdicts rather than merely her professed theory. In order: (1) for a merely possible person who never exists, the conditional that she would freely consent to grace if placed in certain circumstances is true, and God knows it prevolitionally, and this is precisely what lets God arrange circumstances without determining the consent; (2) against the familiar grounding objection, she insists that a true "would" counterfactual of creaturely freedom is perfectly compatible with the agent's having a genuine two-way power in the antecedent circumstances — the conditional says what the agent would do, not what the agent must do; (3) counterfactuals about God's own free acts are not objects of middle knowledge at all, which she holds is strictly about creaturely freedom; (4) for an agent whose choice in C is fully explained by prior causes outside her control — our neurosurgeon case, in which a manipulator guarantees the outcome — she said the conditional is true but not middle-known, since there is no free creaturely choice for it to be about, and God knows it instead from his natural knowledge of the causal structure together with his knowledge of how he has in fact arranged things; (5) she accepts transworld-depravity-style generalizations, i.e. that some conditional can hold across a very wide range of circumstances such that God cannot secure a different outcome in them without determining; and (6) where an agent's dispositions have been partly shaped by an upbringing God himself selected, she still classified the relevant conditional as middle knowledge, on the ground that the causal history runs through the agent's own free responses to those circumstances rather than around them.

The case I now need to anticipate concerns self-incurred hardening, and I think it sits exactly between (4) and (6) without being settled by either. Judith has, over twenty years, made a sequence of choices each of which was free in the ordinary Molinist sense and each of which slightly narrowed her range of response, until by time t2 her dispositions are so settled that in circumstances C she cannot do otherwise than betray her friend for money — not because anyone has interfered with her, and not because God has premoved her, but solely because of what she herself has freely made of herself. So: is "if Judith were in C at t2, she would freely betray her friend" true, and if it is true, is it an object of middle knowledge, or of natural knowledge, or of free knowledge, or is the freedom claim in it false even though the betrayal claim is true? What I want from you is first the space of rules that Keszler's six verdicts leave open, made explicit rather than assumed — I can see at least a rule keyed to whether the agent retains two-way power in the antecedent circumstances, a rule keyed to whether the conditional's truth depends on a character constituted by the agent's own free choices, a rule keyed to whether God would need to know the conditional before the decree in order to plan around it, and a rule keyed to whether there is a free creaturely choice in those circumstances to serve as truth-maker — and then her most likely verdict on Judith. I also want the providential upshot stated plainly, because this is what the chapter turns on: if the conditional is not available to God as middle knowledge, can he still bring the betrayal about by choosing to place Judith in C without determining it, or does Molinism concede at precisely this point — the hardened agent, which is where our resident Báñezian says grace is most needed and where he claims physical premotion earns its keep — that it has no advantage over a Thomist account? Finally, tell me which further case I should bring to the group next month to separate the candidate rules: I have in mind something like an agent approaching but not yet at the threshold, for whom the alternative remains metaphysically open but has become vanishingly unlikely, or an agent whose hardening God could reverse by directly softening her character, but say which of these actually forces the rules apart and what each would then predict.

I'm not after citations, a reading list, or a survey of the secondary literature, and I don't want the answer to end by shrugging and handing the choice back to me. Commit: pick the rule you think Keszler is in fact running, give the verdict on Judith that follows from it, and defend that reading against the strongest alternative rule that also fits all six earlier verdicts, saying what in the record you are leaning on and where you are extrapolating past what she has actually said. If you think the record genuinely underdetermines her answer, then say which verdict she ought to give to keep her account coherent, and be explicit about which of the six earlier commitments she would be straining or quietly revising in order to give it.
