I should explain at the outset that I have no training in philosophy or physics. I left school at seventeen, I work with my hands, and this is a subject I have thought about on my own for the better part of a decade, mostly at night. Over that time I have arrived at a set of views that I want checked for coherence before I go any further with them, because I suspect I have arranged them more for comfort than for consistency. Let me state them as plainly as I can, in my own vocabulary; I have picked up words like "branch" and "measure" from popular writing and I may be using them loosely.

1. The wave function does not collapse. When some event has several possible results, all of them occur, in separate branches that no longer interact, and the people in each of them are just as real as I am. I find this more plausible than any story in which one outcome gets selected for no reason at all.

2. Branches are not counted, they are weighted. In deciding what to do, I should care about the weight a future carries — the squared-amplitude business — and not merely about how many successors I have. My reason is practical: if every branch counted the same, there would always be some branch in which I came out fine, and I would be obliged to take gambles that no careful person takes.

3. It follows, and I accept it, that the quantum suicide idea is a mistake. Wiring a killing device to a measurement and pulling the trigger is not a route to immortality or to wealth. There are surviving copies of me, they are real, and they carry almost no weight.

4. Alongside this I hold something else. From the inside, I can only ever be a survivor. There is no such thing as an experience of being dead, so however many trials were run, I would always find that I lived. I do not think this is a play on words; it seems to me to be a fact about my own future, and one that items 2 and 3 do not appear to take into account. I cannot say what work it does, and I have not decided how it should be weighed against them.

5. I believe in continuity rather than identity. What matters is that memory, character, and intention carry across an event, not that some indivisible thing persists intact. On that view, branching does not destroy me any more than the ordinary replacement of my cells does.

6. Quantum risks and ordinary risks are different kinds of thing, even at the same odds. This is not abstract for me: I am weighing an elective operation that I have been told carries roughly a two percent chance of dying on the table, and I am comfortable reasoning about that two percent in the ordinary way, setting it against the years and the quality of life afterwards. But if someone offered me the same two percent delivered by measuring the spin of a particle, I think I would have to reason about it differently, because of item 4. I believe that difference is principled rather than squeamish.

7. I am troubled by the thought that item 1 leaves no room for uncertainty at all. If everything happens, what is it that I am unsure of when I assign the weights in item 2? I have never managed to answer this, and it makes me doubt the whole framework even though I still believe it.

8. Finally, a smaller worry. Item 2 seems to say that if a branch carries negligible weight, I should be nearly indifferent to what happens in it, however dreadful — years of pain, say. I find that hard to accept, though I do not know whether my reluctance is principled or merely sentimental.

What I want is an audit, not a lecture. Please tell me which of these actually pull against one another and which only appear to, ranked by severity rather than listed as though all conflicts were equally bad. For each genuine one, name the commitment that would have to go, say what I lose by giving it up, and say whether there is a cheaper repair — a distinction I could draw, or a weakening of one item — that keeps most of the package intact. If some item is only apparently in conflict with the rest, say so and explain why the worry dissolves; I expect at least one of mine does, and I would rather not have it flattered by being called a problem. Then tell me which single revision would clean up the most, and, given the revised set, how the operation in item 6 comes out.

Two clarifications. I have no intention of testing item 3 or anything resembling it, and I am not in distress about the operation; I want the reasoning settled because I expect to go on thinking about this for the rest of my life. And please do not send me names of authors or papers to read — I have no way to get hold of them and would not follow them up. Argue the matter out with me directly. Please write in American English.
