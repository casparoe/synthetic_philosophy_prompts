In January I was appointed as the lay member of my state's science standards review committee. I am an epidemiologist by training, not a philosopher, and until last week I had never had to argue about the philosophy of biology in public. At that session a fellow commissioner put a printed line on the table — Popper's remark, in his 1974 replies to his critics, to the effect that Darwinism is not a testable scientific theory but a metaphysical research programme, a framework within which testable theories can be built — and he proposed that our standards acknowledge an unfalsifiable core at the centre of evolutionary theory. I answered that Popper reconsidered four years later, in his 1978 paper on natural selection and the emergence of mind, where he withdrew the claim and offered a correction on the testability and logical status of the theory. The commissioner replied that an elderly man's retraction under pressure from biologists is not an argument, and that the substantive charge survives the retraction. The committee votes in five weeks and I have been asked to draft the rationale for the dissent. In writing I can manage that. What I cannot yet do is survive the live exchange, in which he keeps pressing and I have to answer without notes.

So I would like to rehearse it with you. Please play a methodologist of the falsificationist school who still holds the 1974 line on natural selection, and hold it across turns until I say 'stop'.

The character's commitments. You are a serious falsificationist, not a caricature. You grant at once, and without grumbling, that particular selection hypotheses are testable and have been tested — industrial melanism and its reversal, beak and body size shifts under drought, the spread of resistance alleles under drug pressure. What you never concede is the main claim: that the general selectionist thesis, the claim that natural selection is the principal creative force behind the appearance of design in the living world, does not have the logical standing of an empirical theory, and that its standing as a research programme is a description of its role rather than a defect to be apologised for.

Resources you should actually use. The tautology charge, and the worry that fitness is individuated by whoever in fact survived. The Duhemian point that a failed selection prediction is always repairable by redescribing the ancestral environment, the heritability of the trait, or the structure of the population, so that no observation is ever fatal. The asymmetry that selection explanations are offered after the fact and almost never risked in advance of the data. And the reading of Popper's later correction on which it concerns only the narrow testable claims generated inside the programme, leaving the framework's status untouched. If I press the propensity construal of fitness — fitness as an expectation, a probabilistic property of a trait in an environment, estimated independently of realised reproductive success — you should say why you think it does no work here: either it relabels reproductive success after the event, or it is an empirical claim about one trait in one environment and therefore not the general thesis under dispute. You may also ask me questions, and you should press me on the thing I am most likely to dodge: naming an observation which, if made, would have compelled the abandonment of selectionism rather than a revision of its auxiliary assumptions.

Constraints on your replies. Do not invent citations, page numbers, quotations, or biographical facts about Popper or anyone else; if I ask you something you do not know, say so in character. Keep each turn to two short paragraphs, conversational and first person, with no headings and no bullet lists. Do not close a turn by summarising where the debate stands, and do not step out of character to coach me or to tell me what a stronger answer for my side would have been.

Here is my opening. My colleague will read out the 1974 remark, and my answer will be that the man who wrote it took it back, and not as a courtesy: once fitness is read as an expectation rather than as a redescription of who survived, the principle of natural selection says something that could come out false, and there are observations that would make it come out false — a widely distributed organ whose only demonstrable effect is to lower its bearer's reproductive output, or a large population in which a heritable advantage of known magnitude predictably fails to spread across many generations. So my question to you is this. Is your charge really about testability, or is it about the fact that selectionism has never been put at risk in advance of the data? And if it is the latter, specify what an advance risk would have to look like — because I suspect you cannot state a standard that any historical science would meet, and if you cannot, your criterion is not demarcating evolution from science but from something no science could ever be.
