I am co-convening a semester-long seminar that sits deliberately between two audiences: law students who have read the corporate anti-bribery statutes cover to cover, and anthropology students who have read the literature on gift exchange, reciprocity, and clientelism and who think the first group is smuggling in a very particular folk ontology of what an "office" is. For the opening session I want a piece of satire that both camps will recognize as aimed at them, and I would like you to write it. The target is not a person, a firm, or a country — it is a discourse: the confident assumption that corruption is a natural kind with a bright line through it, that the line is drawn by monetary value and by the public/private boundary, and that a taxonomy built out of those two instruments can be exported to every jurisdiction without the taxonomy itself being the thing under examination.

The form I want is a product-and-onboarding page for a fictional enterprise SaaS platform — invented vendor, invented product name, no real company anywhere in it — running to roughly 800 words in the deadpan register of compliance marketing copy that has merged with compliance engineering. It should include: a tagline and a short feature list; the platform's flagship instrument, a gift-and-hospitality decision matrix with escalating tiers keyed to declared value, a "relationship loading" multiplier for repeat transfers, and a jurisdiction selector containing a toggle for small payments that merely expedite routine administrative action, so that the identical act is or is not corrupt depending on the state of a checkbox; a kinship and relationship-hiring module that treats a cousin, a nephew, or a wedding invitation as a channel of value transfer with a measurable bandwidth; three to five FAQ entries; a short log of flagged transactions from a sample audit; and a footer disclaimer with a testimonial from an invented chief ethics officer. I would like the legal scaffolding to be handled accurately rather than gestured at — the US statute with its carve-out for expediting payments, the UK statute that makes a commercial organization criminally liable when an associated person bribes unless it can show adequate preventive procedures, the multilateral convention that harmonized all of this — and the anthropological scaffolding to be equally accurate: the obligations to give, to receive, and to reciprocate; the gift whose refusal is a severing and whose return at a lesser value is a demotion; patronage as a durable bond of mutual obligation rather than a discrete transfer that can be dated and priced; and, underneath everything, the premise that a public role exists as something separable from the person who holds it, with a private sphere waiting on the far side of the boundary.

Two constraints matter more than any individual joke. First, the platform must never fail: every anomaly you feed it — the goat presented to a visiting inspector, the surgery paid for, the pen with the logo on it, the supplier who insists and will be offended by refusal — must be absorbed by the generation of one more clause, one more field, one more risk tier, and the comedy should come from the fact that the taxonomy is unfalsifiable and therefore useless as a description of anything. Its cultural-sensitivity feature should be the sharpest bit: a genuinely well-intentioned localization module that lets the user select from a dropdown which local norms apply and then encodes every practice on that list in the same categories, so that the pluralism is in the interface and nowhere else. Somewhere in the flagged-transaction log there should be an output in which the engine, asked to assess a polity where the officeholder's household and treasury are not distinguished, cannot locate the public role at all and reports accordingly, with a recommended remediation — and the reader should be able to see that the machine has just declared a social order non-compliant for failing to instantiate a distinction the machine was built out of. Second, keep it fair and legible: nothing is stated as criticism, no winking, no authorial aside, no named living person, no real firm, no fabricated quotations from statutes or from anthropologists, and no bibliography, footnotes, or citation apparatus of any kind. A specialist in either literature should recognize that the author understands exactly what is being exaggerated and should find the exaggeration defensible; a reader with no background in either should still find the FAQ funny.
