Two weeks ago the governance committee of the research institute where I work — thirty-odd people, five of us on the committee — voted down a proposal to replace our founding-era hiring rule (no offer may be extended without the unanimous approval of every existing team lead) with a four-fifths supermajority. The proposal came after two years of deadlock and one strong candidate withdrawing when the search ran past seven months. The committee's most senior member voted it down by invoking Chesterton's fence: you must not tear down a fence until you know why it was put up. Ten minutes later he invoked the same slogan against a motion to move the weekly all-hands from Tuesday to Thursday, a change for which nobody could plausibly claim hidden systemic function, and I found myself unable to tell whether he had used one principle twice or two different principles wearing one name. The case has since acquired two complications that make the slogan harder to apply rather than easier. The institute's founder is still alive and sits on the board; when I asked him why the unanimity rule exists he said he honestly could not remember, and offered two mutually inconsistent guesses — that it had been lifted wholesale from another organisation's bylaws, and that it had been a concession to one sceptical early signatory whose support was needed to launch — adding that it may originally have been a temporary measure tied to a funder's condition that lapsed years ago. And when I proposed a compromise, namely running supermajority hiring inside one team for six months as a pilot, the same member rejected it on the ground that you cannot experiment with a fence, since its purpose is precisely what removing it would reveal. I have spent something like fifteen years reading rationalist blogs and forums, where this proverb is close to canonical, and I still cannot say whether that objection is a legitimate consequence of the principle or a shell game.

What I want is a disambiguation rather than a defence. Please lay out, as a numbered menu running from weakest to strongest, the distinct precise claims that "don't tear down a fence until you know why it was put up" could be making. For each reading, state it with its quantifiers and its epistemic operator explicit — what kind of knowing is required (grasping the founders' intentions, identifying a function the practice still serves, ruling out every possible function, or possessing any causal explanation of its origin whatsoever), who must know it, and whether the requirement concerns knowledge, justified belief, or merely having looked — then give a verdict on whether that reading is a platitude, a substantive and defensible claim, a merely contentious one, or flatly false or self-defeating, and say whether it licenses a veto on the hiring rule, on the all-hands motion, and on the pilot. I am most interested in the line between the epistemic constraint the slogan states and the normative presumption people slide out of it: the gap between "you may not remove it until you know why" and "the fence enjoys a presumption in its favour," and the awkward fact that discovering the reason can strengthen rather than weaken the case for removal, since a rule adopted to placate one long-departed signatory, or a temporary measure whose triggering condition lapsed, is arguably a fence whose purpose, once known, is a reason to take it down.

Please also handle the scope questions. Is the principle symmetric — keeping a fence is itself a decision with costs, and the slogan says nothing about putting fences up, nor about modifying one rather than removing it? Does it apply alike to deliberately designed institutions, to norms that emerged without design, and to kludges and rules cargo-culted from elsewhere? And is persistence evidence of function at all, given that practices can survive by drift, by lock-in, by serving whoever holds power, or by being copied by people who never knew why? Flag any pair of readings that cannot both be true, or that come apart in my case the way the two vetoes did, and close with your view of which reading people in my circles usually have in mind when they deploy the slogan, together with the strongest core that survives once the false and self-defeating readings are stripped away. Two administrative notes. First, I have no web access and neither do you, so no bibliography and no reading list — the menu and the verdicts are the deliverable. Second, do not supply Chesterton's original wording. I believe his own version is stronger than the popular slogan, distinguishing reforming from deforming and casting the principle as a question of who is permitted to clear the fence away until the would-be reformer has gone off and thought about it, but I cannot vouch for the phrasing, so work from my paraphrase — and say so if you think that paraphrase is doing work the slogan itself does not.
