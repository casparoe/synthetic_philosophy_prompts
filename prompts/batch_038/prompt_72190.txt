I'm in a Stoicism reading group and we've hit a wall on the dichotomy of control, mostly because two people keep reaching for two different pictures and drawing opposite conclusions out of them. One guy, who runs Stoic-flavoured resilience workshops for managers, says the whole doctrine is the dog tied behind the cart: the cart goes where it goes, and the only real choice is whether you trot along or get dragged. He used it last week to argue nobody should burn energy on the local housing campaign — just work on your own reactions. The other person pushes back with the archer: your job is to aim well, hitting the target isn't the end, so Stoicism demands maximum effort, it just relocates what you're aiming at. She also likes the "internalise your goals" version, the trichotomy where partly-controlled things get converted into a goal that's fully up to you.

Can you audit both? For each, give me the correspondance table — what plays the cart, the leash, the dog's own legs, the target, the arrow in flight, the score — and then where the mapping breaks. I mainly want to know which disanalogies actually matter for the conclusion each one is being used to license and which are decoration, and what each picture quietly imports about agency that Stoic theory doesn't grant. Does the dog-and-cart survive once you ask whose leash it is and whether the dog's pulling is itself part of what moves the cart? Does the archer survive the fact that an archer can correct mid-shot and that hitting genuinely matters to an archer in a way that, on the Stoic account, nothing external is supposed to matter to me? And if both are load-bearing in bad ways, is there a better image — the ball game, a sailor trimming sail, a doctor treating someone whose recovery isn't guaranteed — that carries the same point without smuggling in either fatalism or a scoreboard?
