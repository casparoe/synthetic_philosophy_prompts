I'm the political director of a public employees union, about 30,000 members — county clerks, 911 dispatchers, road crew, courthouse security. Every cycle I write an endorsement recommendation for our executive board, and every cycle it turns into the same fight. One faction says that if we endorse anyone we own everything they do afterwards, so we should sit elections out entirely and just lobby whoever wins. The other faction says sitting them out is how you get the worse candidate elected, and then you get nothing from them either. Honestly both of them have a point and I can't adjudicate between them, mostly because I don't have any framework. I took one political theory class in college and I remember there being stuff about whether a single vote can even matter, voting as a duty, voting as a statement of who you are, and the whole lesser evil problem — but as far as I can tell none of that was written with a 30,000-person organization's endorsement in mind, and an endorsement is obviously not the same thing as one vote, so I don't know which parts carry over.

Can you build me an actual procedure I can run on a race? The questions in order, what counts as a good enough answer to each one, where it lands (endorse / no endorsement / endorse but publish a criticism), and where it has to just stop and hand the thing back to the board because it's a values call rather than a facts call. Be upfront about which pieces of the voting ethics stuff genuinely transfer to an organization and which ones quietly break, and about where the procedure will give a bad answer or get gamed by whoever writes the memo — which is usually me, so I want to know my own failure modes.

Constraints worth knowing: state rules mean we file 30 days out to make the coalition sample ballot, so "wait and see how it develops" is never actually available. Endorsement takes 60% of the board. A yes commits about $15k and a weekend of member canvassing. Nobody on staff has philosophy training and I need something a board member can follow from a two page handout.

Then run it on three real-ish races:

1. Safe seat, our incumbent is fine but a first-time challenger is way better on our core issue (we're fighting to keep 911 dispatch in-house instead of contracting it out) and has never held anything.
2. Open seat. Candidate A is strong for us on paper but there's a credible, well-sourced story that he took money from a private prison contractor. Candidate B voted against collective bargaining rights twice but is clean, competent, and probably winnable on everything else.
3. A race where both candidates will vote to privatize dispatch no matter what we do, and the only real difference is that one of them is a decent person and the other is a liar. This is the one where I suspect the procedure is going to tell me to do something half the board will hate, and I want to see how it handles that and what I'm supposed to say when someone asks "so you're telling us we just let them win."

Finally, tell me what the procedure flat out cannot decide, so I stop pretending it can.
