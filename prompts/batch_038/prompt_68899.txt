So I'm running a grad seminar session on promissory obligation next week and I've volunteered — nobody made me — to defend the Hume/Rawls practice view against the voluntarists. Trouble is I think the practice view is in worse shape than the standard objection suggests, and I'd rather land the strong version of the worry myself than get fobbed off with the easy answers in the room. Help me build it.

Here's the setup I'll be using. Call it an orphan promise: you're alone with a friend who's dying, she makes you promise to burn the letters in her desk, she dies an hour later, and nobody else alive has any idea the promise was made. Six months on you find out the letters are of enormous scholarly interest and publishing them would make your career. Everyone named in them is dead too, so stipulate no feelings get hurt. Breaking the promise disappoints no expectation, defeats no reliance, and leaves nobody worse off — the only cost is, on the practice view, one more invisible dent in a social institution, which is exactly the kind of thing a practice theorist thinks they can tally up.

The claim I want you to argue for isn't the stock "the practice view gets the wrong verdict here." It's this: every repair open to the practice theorist either fails extensionally or works only by conceding the voluntarist's central thesis — that what binds me is my own act of taking on an obligation, with the practice demoted to merely enabling or causal work. So take the two repairs I expect from my students and break both. First, the rule-utilitarian tally: my defection slightly weakens the institution, so it's still wrong. Second, the solitaire-chess reply: the practice is there whether or not anyone's watching, the rules of chess bind me in a game I play alone. For the first, show why the dent is either negligible in precisely the cases where intuition says the obligation is strongest, or has to be inflated until it stops being a fact about the practice and starts being a fact about this particular act. For the second, show why it still owes an answer to "what makes my invocation of the rule normatively loaded, rather than just a description of what I happen to be doing" — and note that the answer it gives is going to be about my intention, not the institution.

Then as a coda tell me whether the collapse is a genuine reductio or just the discovery that practice views were voluntarism in a sociologists hat all along. Pick a side and say why, I want an actual commitment at the end rather than a both-sides shrug.

Argument only please, no reading list or bibliography, I know the literature — I just want the moves, laid out tight enough that I could deliver them from a set of index cards.
