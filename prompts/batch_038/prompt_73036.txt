I lead a small infrastructure team at a mid-sized B2B software company, and over the past year I have been keeping an informal log of every decision where we followed what everyone else was doing instead of investigating the question ourselves. I have no formal training in epistemology or economics; I have read the standard introductory accounts of informational cascades — the ones where each agent receives a private signal, observes the actions of everyone who moved before them, and then acts, so that beyond some point the public history outweighs any individual's private evidence — but what I actually care about is the less tidy question of when copying the crowd counts as a genuine inference and when it is an abdication dressed up as one. I want to build this into a one-page checklist for our design review template, which means I need something statable rather than something merely true. Below are my own verdicts on a set of concrete cases. Please treat those verdicts as data to be explained, not as errors to be corrected, at least until you have a candidate principle on the table; only then tell me which of them it cannot accommodate.

The cases, in the order I wrote them down.

1. We adopted Postgres for a new service without anyone on the team benchmarking alternatives or reading a line of its source. Nearly every company in our ecosystem uses it. Verdict: rational. My private information about databases is weak, and thousands of comparable teams have already revealed theirs.

2. During a 2 a.m. incident, three on-call engineers before me each marked the same paging alert as a flapping sensor and cleared it. I did the same without opening the dashboard. It was not a flapping sensor, and we lost four hours. Verdict: not rational. This is the one that bothers me, because informationally it looks exactly like case 1 — three predecessors, each with a private read on the situation, all pointing the same way.

3. A conference had roughly forty exhibitors selling observability tooling. From that I inferred the category was real and had real budgets attached to it, and I was right. I did not infer that any particular one of the forty products was good. Verdict: rational at the first level, would have been irrational at the second.

4. Everyone in our engineering org cites the same third-party benchmark post in support of a performance claim. I refuse to credit it, because when I traced four separate internal threads that looked like independent agreement, all four led back to that single post. Verdict: my refusal was rational. This is where my rule of thumb comes from, and it is the rule I most want tested: count one vote per independent source of evidence, not one vote per person expressing a view — so if the first three adopters were all reading from the same artifact, they are one vote and not three.

5. In hiring I filter heavily on referrals from people whose judgment I respect. Verdict: rational in general, but I am uneasy here, because my referrers all came from the same two companies and roughly the same handful of schools.

6. I copied the syntax and idioms of the most widely used language in our stack without evaluating alternatives, purely so that new hires could read our code on day one. Verdict: rational — though I suspect that in calling it rational I am smuggling in something that is not an epistemic reason at all.

7. The case I keep going back and forth on. Our organization migrated its systems work to Rust over eighteen months; I supported it, wrote the migration plan, and argued for it in review. I never ran a single load test. What I had was three staff engineers I rate highly saying it was the right call, plus a general sense that the broader industry was drifting that way. I alternate between thinking this was clearly rational, because deferring to people who know more than you is what a reasonable person does, and thinking I was simply vote four in a chain of four.

My tentative principle is the one from case 4: one vote per independent source. Does it deliver all seven verdicts, or only most of them? In particular, does it separate case 1 from case 2, or do I have to bolt on extra terms to get that separation — the cost of checking for yourself, the reversibility of the decision, the asymmetry between the two kinds of error, whether the predecessors knew they were being watched and therefore had reason to be careful, whether the question is about what to believe or about what convention to join? If those terms have to be in there, tell me honestly whether the result is still one principle about evidence or two principles stapled together, and which of the two is doing more work. I would also like the principle's verdict on cases I have not listed: specifically, one where the crowd turns out to be right but no member of it could state why, and one where I hold a strong private signal that contradicts a large and genuinely independent public consensus. And if the honest answer is that some of my verdicts have to go, argue for dropping them rather than merely asserting it — I can be persuaded on 6 and 7, but I want to hold onto 2 unless you can show me it was case 1 wearing a disguise all along.

I am not asking for references or a reading list, and I would not be able to chase them if you gave me one; I want the analysis itself, in plain language. If there is established terminology for the distinctions you end up relying on, name it and define it in a sentence, but do not assume I will recognize it.
