I have no academic training in philosophy. I spent thirty-one years in a county records office, and for the last several I have been reading Kierkegaard on my own in the evenings — whatever I can find secondhand, plus the introductions my library happens to hold. What has lodged in me is not the religious writing but his account of the press, above all in Two Ages, the short book he published in 1846 in the form of a review of someone else's novel.

As I understand him, his complaint is not that the newspapers print falsehoods. It is stranger than that. He seems to think the daily press does not address a public so much as manufacture one. Before it, he suggests, there were assemblies and factions and particular men with particular commitments — people who could be answered, contradicted, shamed, and held to what they had said under their own names. The press dissolves all of that into an abstraction with no location and no owner, something like a phantom that everyone speaks on behalf of and that no one is. What unsettles him most, I think, is that this is not a corruption of opinions but a dissolution of the person holding them: the same view stated by a single individual who will sign it and pay for it, and the same view uttered by the public, are not morally equivalent, and the difference has nothing to do with whether the view is true. He has a word for the process, levelling, and he appears to regard it as worse than any particular error, because an error at least still belongs to somebody.

I would like a poem that carries that idea, and I want the form to carry it rather than the argument. It is for a monthly discussion group I attend; I mean to read it aloud at the end of the evening.

Please write twenty-four lines in six quatrains, in whatever meter you judge best, with rhyme occasional rather than regular if you rhyme at all. The opening quatrain should be as concrete as possible: named people, particular grievances, a woman who will stand up at a meeting and be contradicted to her face, a man who will sign a letter to the editor and be recognized in the street for it. Each subsequent quatrain should give something up — first the names, then the particulars, then the singular first person — until the grammar itself has thinned out. The turn should fall exactly where the singular pronoun disappears, and by the final quatrain the only voice left should be a collective first person that belongs to no one, so that the last line reads as an opinion that has no holder. A reader who knows the book should be able to put a finger on the line where the individual is replaced by the crowd.

Two further requests. Set it among the objects of his own century — the daily paper, the reading room, the subscription list, the town meeting, the compositor setting type — and do not let it slide into a lament about screens and feeds; the machinery should be old enough to feel permanent. And keep it from sounding like a smug man sneering at the masses, because the book was written just after a satirical weekly had made him a figure people laughed at in the street, and part of its force is that he is not simply standing outside the crowd he describes. No jargon inside the poem itself, please: do not use the word levelling in a line, and do not name him. It should work first as a poem for someone who has never read a word of him.
