I cover science and ideas for a magazine and I keep running into the same puzzle: when is going along with the crowd smart, and when is it a failure? Here are my gut verdicts on a bunch of cases, and I want to know what principle, if any, actually generates them.

Tourist in an unfamiliar city eats at the restaurant with the queue rather than the identical-looking empty one next door: smart. eBay bidder who raises her ceiling because the bidding tells her others see value she doesn't: also fine. Doctor who orders a test she privately thinks is useless because every colleague orders it: not fine. Fund manager who keeps buying an asset he's sure is overpriced because everyone else is buying it: not fine — even though the reasoning looks like the eBay case with the numbers turned up. Juror who changes her vote after hearing eleven others: fine, that's literally what a jury is for. Scientist who abandons her own research program because nobody else has picked it up: not fine. Someone who deletes her two-star review after seeing fifty five-star ones: fine, sort of, mildly embarrassing.

And the one I can't settle: an epidemiologist who revises her forecast after reading the other modelling groups' forecasts. Structurally that's just the juror again, but I want to call it bad.

So: is there one principle that gets all of these, or nearly all? State it precisely enough that a stranger could apply it, show me which case motivates each clause of it, tell me what it predicts for two I've been dodging — a journalist who adopts the consensus framing of a story she hasn't reported herself, and a founder who prices her round off other term sheets she thinks are insane — and be straight with me about which of my verdicts it can't save. If the eBay/fund manager pair forces a choice between a complicated principle that keeps both and a simple one that flips one of them, say which you'd pick and why.
