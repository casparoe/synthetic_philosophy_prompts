Forty years of growing synthetic sapphire and quartz have left me with a professional prejudice I cannot quite shake. In my trade a flux-grown crystal can be chemically, optically, and structurally the equal of a natural one; the entire difference is its history of formation, legible only in growth inclusions and strain. Since retiring I have been reading aesthetics, and I find that the gemmologist's insistence and the philosopher's argument come to much the same place: Goodman's contention that a forgery no one can tell from the original is nonetheless aesthetically significant, because it bears on what we might be trained to perceive and on the history of style; and Dutton's division between nominal authenticity, a matter of correct attribution of origin, and expressive authenticity, a matter of fidelity to the maker's own values and tradition.

I should like a reading of Donna Tartt's *The Goldfinch* pressed against those two ideas. The novel offers a case I do not think either account handles cleanly. Hobie builds furniture out of genuine period parts, salvaged old timber, and new work into objects that never historically existed, and he discloses what he has done; it is Theo who moves these pieces as antiques, and the customer who is not taken in, Lucius Reeve, turns the deception into leverage. Meanwhile the one object whose nominal authenticity is beyond dispute — the small Fabritius panel, which was in fact recovered intact from the rubble of the Delft powder-magazine explosion of 1654 that killed its painter, and which in the novel is carried out of another explosion — spends most of the book wrapped in a pillowcase, in a school locker, in a storage bin, and finally as collateral among criminals, seen by almost nobody.

So: which of these objects is the fake, and by what criterion does the novel decide — origin, disclosure, or the custody chain that carries a thing forward? Does Theo's theft simply remove the panel from view, or does it change what kind of object it is? And where does the Goodman line actually fail here, given that his argument seems to require that someone be able to look and learn to discriminate, while this panel is discriminated by no one for a decade? I am equally interested in the places where the novel resists the reading; please say so plainly rather than forcing it. Note that I am working from memory and cannot check passages, so describe scenes rather than quoting them, point me to the ones that carry the weight, and finish by telling me what Tartt contributes to this debate that argument alone would not have supplied.
