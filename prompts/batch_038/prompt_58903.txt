ok so I was up way too late arguing with my roommate about consciousness and I sort of need you to help me settle it, or at least write the thing that makes my side of it vivid, becuase I'm pretty sure I lost the argument even though I don't think I was wrong.

Here's the fight. He's one of those people who thinks the whole notion of qualia — raw feels, intrinsic qualitative character, the "what it's like" stuff — is a philosophical posit that just dissolves when you look at it hard. His best move was the coffee case: picture someone who drank coffee every day for years and loved it, and now can't stand it. Did the taste quale change, or did only their reactions change — their grimace, their discriminations between cups, their running commentary about how it tastes? He says there's no way to get leverage on the question because there is nothing left over to check the reactions against. Then he piles on: the supposed intrinsic properties are ineffable in a way that makes them permanently uncheckable, they do no explanatory work, and every time you actually try to point at one you end up pointing at a comparison, a memory, or a disposition to report. So it gets quined rather than denied — he keeps insisting he isn't saying nobody has experiences, just that the concept is broken and we should stop shipping it. My counter, which he thought was cute but not compelling: fine, swap in dispositions and reports, but at the end you've just relabeled the thing, and the relabeling is doing 100% of the work. Something is still doing the seeming. If the whole method is "we redefine experience until nothing is left over," then whether anything is left over depends entirely on how aggressive the redefinition was — and that's not a discovery about the world, it's a choice of bookkeeping.

So what I want is a piece of satire that stages that fight without staging it as a fight. Write it as a software product deprecation notice — you know the genre: a vendor's "End of Life announcement" page for a legacy component, with an "action required" banner, a deprecation timeline table (dates, versions, what breaks when), a rationale section, a migration guide mapping old concepts onto new ones, and a short FAQ for confused customers. The deprecated product is Qualia. The vendor's replacement is something like a dispositions-and-reports module. Around 750–900 words, with real headings and the little boilerplate tics of that genre ("no-op," "won't fix," "not reproducible," "backwards compatible," "we recommend migrating at your earliest convenience"). Invent all of it — the company name, the version numbers, the ticket IDs. Don't quote any real text and don't name any real philosophers, books, or papers; the target is the argumentative move, not a person.

The constraints I care about: (1) it has to be fair. My roommate should be able to read it and laugh rather than feel strawmanned, which means the vendor's reasoning needs to be the strongest version of the reasoning — genuinely good, genuinely tempting — not a cartoon of a materialist. (2) The vendor never once concedes that anything was removed. Every single user complaint gets handled as a documentation misunderstanding, a misconfigured expectation, or a duplicate ticket. That refusal is where the comedy lives, and it has to be perfectly consistent start to finish. (3) The coffee case should show up as a known issue and/or a migration case study, with the ticket thread ending in something like "cannot reproduce, closing." (4) The ineffability complaint should appear as a whole category of support tickets that are auto-closed, and there should be at least one where the customer says they can't describe the problem in terms the form accepts — which is, obviously, exactly why the form doesn't accept it. (5) My objection — that there's still something doing the seeming, and the residue only vanishes if you define hard enough — should surface once, near the end, as a user report, and get classified in the driest possible way as a customer misreading the changelog. Don't argue it. Don't wink. A reader should feel it land, not be told it landed. Keep the register deadpan the whole way through: the joke is that this is just normal corporate lifecycle documentation, and that the tone is perfectly appropriate to what is being announced.
