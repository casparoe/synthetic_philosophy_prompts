**Question (10 points; 4 for the exposition, 6 for the second part):**

> Naiyāyikas hold that there are exactly four pramāṇas. Explain what cognitive work each of the four is supposed to do, and then explain the argumentative strategy a Naiyāyika would use to defend the claim that the list is complete — i.e. that nothing else deserves to be added as a fifth.

**Student's answer:**

> Gautama's sutras open with epistemology rather than metaphysics, and the first thing they do is list four pramanas: perception (*pratyakṣa*), inference (*anumāna*), analogical reasoning (*upamāna*), and testimony (*śabda*). A pramāṇa is the instrument that produces a cognition which matches its object, so this is a list of ways of getting things right, not a list of subject matters. That's also why the pramāṇas function as the final court of appeal in a philosophical dispute: any argument about whether something counts as a knowledge-source has to be conducted using some knowledge-source, so you can't get outside the list to adjudicate it.
>
> Perception comes first and is basic in the sense that the others depend on it. It arises from a connection between a sense faculty and its object, it is non-deviating, and on the standard reading it is determinate, i.e. it involves concepts, which puts Nyāya against Dignāga on whether genuine perception is conceptual. It's basic because inference requires remembered cases of the reason occurring with what it's a reason for, and you can only have those by perceiving them.
>
> Inference is a cognition that depends on a prior cognition of that sort. Nyāya lays it out in five members — thesis, reason, example, application, conclusion — rather than Aristotle's three, and the difference isn't just presentational. The third member is a concrete, agreed case (the kitchen, where there is smoke and there is fire) and it's that case that carries the pervasion between smoke and fire, instead of the pervasion being asserted as a bare universal. This is also why Nyāya sidesteps the problem of induction altogether: the example does the justificatory work that a Humean thinks requires an extra assumption about the uniformity of nature, so there's no gap left to close.
>
> *Upamāna* is how you learn what a word picks out by means of a similarity. Someone tells me a *gavaya* is like a cow; later, in the forest, I see something and recall the similarity, and now I know what 'gavaya' refers to. *Śabda* is the utterance of an *āpta*, a reliable speaker. Nyāya refuses to fold testimony into inference, which the Buddhists do — they only keep two pramāṇas — and refusing is the right call, since a lot of what we know about dharma and about the past could never be inferred from anything we've perceived.
>
> For completeness, I think the strategy has two halves. The first is irreducibility: for each of the four, the Naiyāyika has to show it can't be reconstructed out of the others. *Upamāna* is the hard test, because the opponent says you can rebuild the *gavaya* case out of testimony plus perception plus memory. The reply is that what does the actual work is grasping the similarity itself, and similarity isn't delivered by any of those three on their own, so *upamāna* stays separate.
>
> The second half is that the Naiyāyika doesn't have to deny that there are other things people want to call pramāṇas — she absorbs them. Awareness of absence is the classic candidate: I come to know there's no pot on the floor precisely by not apprehending a pot there. Nyāya takes this seriously enough to admit non-apprehension (*anupalabdhi*) as a fifth instrument, but only treats it as a limiting case of the other four, so the fourfold list survives because *anupalabdhi* is already inside it doing work the four cover. And that's a general pattern: the Buddhists say two, the Cārvākas say one, Kumārila says six, Nyāya says four, but everybody is counting the same kind of thing and arguing about how many of them there are. So the completeness claim is mostly bookkeeping — it just amounts to showing that no candidate anybody has proposed does work the four can't already do.
