I am a novelist rather than a philosopher, and in six weeks I am giving the annual colloquium at an interdisciplinary institute for the study of philosophy and the literary imagination: forty-five minutes of talk, thirty of questions, roughly forty people in the room. I would like a preparation sheet for that question period. You should assume I can deliver at most two minutes of answer per objection, that I will be standing up without notes in front of people who do this for a living, and that I would rather know now where I am exposed than discover it there.

The talk is about my third novel and the method behind it. The method is as follows. There is no interior monologue anywhere in the book and no free indirect discourse; I do not believe there is a single psychological verb in it. No character is ever named outright: each figure is referred to throughout only by the relational or official term that whoever is momentarily at the center of the scene would use for them, so the same man is called third uncle in four chapters, the assessor in two, and old man in one, and the reader has to construct his identity from the drift in the terms alone. Every principal character's first appearance is an inventory rather than a description — what they owe, to whom, in what capacity, and what is owed to them. Two of the central figures hold the same role at different points in the book and are deliberately near-interchangeable; the plot turns on a vacancy in a role being filled, not on anyone's inner life being disclosed. The one scene in which a character refuses a role is written as a catastrophe and not as an emancipation.

The thesis I intend to defend is that these are consequences rather than mannerisms. My claim is that the person is not a self who subsequently enters relations and takes up roles, but a locus of roles; that humanness in the Confucian sense is an achievement rather than an endowment, and can therefore only be shown in the exercise of particular obligations; that what the Western novel calls interiority is a set of techniques for manufacturing the appearance of a residue beneath the relations; and that when one stops manufacturing it, one finds there was never a residue to lose. I take the vocabulary for this from the role-ethics reconstruction of early Chinese thought that has been argued over in Anglophone scholarship for some decades, and I will say so openly, including that I came to it as a working novelist looking for a license to leave things out.

The room is specific, and the objections will be too. There are two narratologists: one works on theory of mind in fiction and holds that readers mentalize compulsively and involuntarily, so that any textual refusal of interiority is simply a different recipe for producing depth; the other is a formalist about point of view and reference and will treat my naming scheme as a technical breakdown rather than a metaphysics. There is one analytic philosopher of personal identity and moral psychology, sympathetic to the idea that a practical identity is constituted by commitments, who nevertheless holds that roles presuppose an agent able to step outside them and endorse or repudiate them, and who will want to know why my own refusal scene does not prove his point. There are three people who work on early Chinese thought: a philologist who is impatient with what she regards as the projection of a modern sociological vocabulary of roles onto texts that speak of ritual, of names, and of family feeling; a comparative philosopher who reads the tradition in virtue-ethical terms and thinks the relational reading underplays the Mencian claim that the sprouts of the virtues are inner; and a graduate student who has read the entire role-ethics debate, disagrees with it, and is looking for a chance to say so. The rest are literary people, including two novelists who will ask the craft question in its bluntest form, and an MFA student who suspects the whole method is a gimmick and will ask me to admit it.

Please give me the eight or ten objections I will actually get, in the order they will come and weighted by how much damage each would do. Phrase each one the way that particular questioner would phrase it — the philologist does not sound like the analytic philosopher, and the novelist in the third row does not sound like either — rather than in a neutral summary voice. For each, give me a reply I could say aloud in two minutes without hedging into mush, and then a candid note on where that reply is soft: what it costs me to give it, and what the questioner will say next if they are quick. Tell me separately which two or three concessions I should make in the first five minutes to take the wind out of the exegesis objections, and how to make a concession without abandoning the thesis.

Then tell me the one question I am hoping nobody asks. I have my own suspicion about what it is — something about whether a novelist in my position is entitled to the material at all, or about the fact that the refusal scene seems to smuggle back exactly the self I claim not to believe in — but I want your judgment, not my fear, and I want to know honestly whether there is any answer to it or whether the correct strategy is to concede it gracefully and move on.

One further requirement, and it matters more to me than the rest. I have built a book on a reading that specialists dispute. So for each objection, and especially for the exegetical ones, mark explicitly which underlying claims are settled among people who work on these texts and which are live controversies — for instance, whether role vocabulary is a faithful reconstruction or a late imposition, whether the early Confucians had anything like a notion of interiority, whether the famous episodes about a son shielding his sheep-stealing father and about the sage-king fleeing with his father on his back license the conclusions I want from them, and whether the sprouts doctrine commits the tradition to an inner psychology. Do not let me walk into that room treating one contested reconstruction as though it were the consensus; where the field is genuinely split, say that it is split and say which side I would be aligning myself with by answering as you suggest. And please do not send me to the literature — no reading list, no citations, nothing to look up. I want your reconstruction of what these people will say and what I can say back.
