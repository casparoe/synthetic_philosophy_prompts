Two years ago I was appointed director of clinical governance for a group of four district hospitals, and the first initiative I drove through was a mandatory numeric deterioration score: every inpatient has a fixed set of observations recorded at set intervals, the values are fed into a scoring rule, and any patient crossing a threshold triggers an automatic escalation to a rapid-response team. Previously, escalation had depended largely on a nurse deciding to pick up a telephone. Documentation compliance rose from roughly half to near-universal, escalations tripled, and the board was delighted. I have spent the last eight months trying to explain to that same board why I think we are worse off, and I have not found the words.

Here is the pattern, stripped of our particulars. Nothing about the score is falsified and nothing is gamed. Observations are recorded honestly, and the score still predicts what it was built to predict for the patients to whom it is applied; on its own terms it has never performed better. What has changed is the ward around it. Our longest-serving nurses used to be able to say, many hours before any single observation left the normal range, that a patient was not right, and they were usually correct. Those nurses have retired or moved on. The juniors who replaced them have never had to form that judgement: the protocol dictates when to look and what to record, escalation is triggered by the number rather than by an impression, and an impression that contradicts the number now carries no standing at all — it is not even a category of report. Two patients this year deteriorated severely while their scores stayed below threshold throughout, and in both cases nobody noticed, because noticing had stopped being anyone's job. And there is nothing to revert to. The skill was never written down, the people who held it have gone, the habit of forming an independent impression is no longer taught, and even a nurse who did form one would have no recognised channel for acting on it.

Colleagues assure me this is Goodhart's law, or Campbell's, and that is exactly where I get stuck. The familiar formulation concerns a measure ceasing to be a good measure once it is made a target, and the mechanism is people distorting or optimising toward it. In my case nobody is optimising, nothing is distorted, and the measure is being obeyed with real fidelity; the damage lies in the unmeasured judgement that it displaced and then made unlearnable.

So I would like to know whether this has an established name. I expect there are several neighbouring concepts scattered across different literatures — I have come across talk of deskilling and of complacency induced by automation, and neither seems to capture the whole thing — and what I want is the precise boundary between each of them and what I have described. The feature I most need captured is the irreversibility: the institution loses not merely the skill but the standing to appeal to anything besides the measure, so that the substitution cannot be undone even after the measure has been discredited. If no term fits, I would like you to coin one, with a definition rigorous enough that I could state observable criteria for it in a governance report, together with the boundary cases — what would genuinely count as an instance, and what would merely resemble one.
