Two weeks from now I have to stand up in my epistemology seminar and defend the paper I've been writing, and I'm already dreading the Q&A. Twelve-ish people, nobody will have read more than the abstract, 15 minutes of questions. I want a prep sheet, not a defense of my view.

The view: Williamson's anti-luminosity argument — the cold one, where the guy wakes up feeling cold and the feeling fades gradually through the morning — is usually read as killing the KK principle, since if knowing were luminous then KK would hold for free, and he shows no nontrivial condition is luminous. My thesis is that the argument leaves a restricted KK standing, and that the restriction isn't a dodge. Sketch of what I'll say:

1. I grant the trivial step. Luminosity of knowing entails KK, so the whole fight is over what does the work in the argument.
2. What does the work is a margin-for-error principle, and margin-for-error only falls out of a safety condition on knowledge. But safety is method-relative: it's about the same belief formed the same way in nearby cases. So margin-for-error is licensed only relative to a method.
3. For a class of cases the method is what I call self-presenting — the state is the basis of the belief in a way that doesn't involve discrimination against alternatives, so there's no threshold to sit near and no margin to preserve. In those cases the inductive step of the sorites doesn't merely lack support, it's false.
4. So: KK holds when the inner knowledge is had by a self-presenting method, fails otherwise. I call it KK-SM. I claim it's not ad hoc, that it explains why KK seems compelling for your own beliefs and your own felt states and hopeless for stuff like counting cars in a lot from a window, and that it predicts failure in exactly the threshold cases.

Honest soft spots I already know about: I assert (2) in a footnote with basically no argument, and I don't have a crisp non-circular characterisation of "self-presenting" yet.
