I have spent something like two decades reading Overcoming Bias, the Sequences, and their successors, and the habit I have taken from that literature is less any particular conclusion than a reflex: when someone produces an argument that feels overwhelmingly persuasive, ask which features of the presentation are doing the work, and which are merely making it pleasant to be persuaded. The vocabulary for this is second nature to me — true rejections, fully general counterarguments, applause lights, semantic stopsigns. I want to turn it on thought experiments, and I am drafting the results as a talk for a local rationalist meetup, so I need the output to be quantitative rather than discursive.

Score the following eleven intuition pumps on three axes I am defining for this exercise. Do not substitute your own axes; the point is comparability.

- **Ornament load (0–5).** How much of the scenario's concrete content is not required by the argument the scenario is offered to support. 0 means every detail in the description maps onto a premise, so that deleting anything changes the argument. 5 means you could cut most of the story and leave the inference intact, and the cut portion is where nearly all the persuasive force lived.
- **Framing asymmetry (0–5).** Whether the setup quietly presupposes one side of the dispute it is supposed to adjudicate. 0 means the description is neutral between the rival positions. 5 means the scenario embeds a premise that no partisan of the other side would grant, and the pump is routinely deployed as though that grant had been made.
- **Yield (0–5).** What you get from the pump that you could not get from a bare argument. 0 means it is a restatement in costume. 5 means it genuinely isolates a variable, forces a distinction that was previously unavailable, or shows that a widely accepted principle has consequences its proponents would disavow.

Then compute a composite, C = Yield − (Ornament load + Framing asymmetry) / 2, and place each pump in a tier: S for C ≥ 2.5, A for 1 ≤ C < 2.5, B for −0.5 ≤ C < 1, C for −2 ≤ C < −0.5, D for C < −2.

The eleven: Searle's Chinese Room; Thomson's violinist; Nozick's experience machine; Jackson's Mary, the color scientist; the footbridge variant of the trolley problem, in which you push a heavy man onto the tracks; Parfit's teletransporter; Chalmers's philosophical zombies; Nozick's utility monster; Putnam's Twin Earth; Singer's child in the shallow pond; and Newcomb's problem.

For each, after the three numbers, give me four to six sentences total covering:

1. The single most load-bearing illegitimate detail — the one you would cut first, and the one whose removal the pump's partisans would most resent. Add a clause on roughly what their objection to that cut would be.
2. A de-pumped restatement: the same argument rendered as drily as you can manage, all structure and variables and no scenery. Two or three sentences. This is where I will be checking whether you have grasped the argument rather than the story, so no rhetorical flourishes.
3. A verdict on your restatement, choosing exactly one: the target intuition survives intact, meaning the pump was ornament; it survives but weakened in a way that matters to the conclusion, meaning the pump was doing real but unearned work; or it dies, meaning the pump was the argument. Defend the choice in one or two sentences.
4. If the pump is standardly read in more than one way, state which reading you scored. Do not average across readings.

After the eleven, answer four cross-cutting questions: which pump shows the largest gap between how often it is deployed and what it earns; if exactly one were permanently retired from the literature, which retirement would cost the most, and what specifically becomes harder to establish without it; which de-pumped restatement was hardest for you to write, and whether that difficulty reflects a subtle argument or an absent one; and whether the tiers sort by subject matter — normative pumps against descriptive ones — and if so, what that pattern suggests about how much work vividness does in each.

Constraints. No citations, bibliography, page numbers, or publication dates; I cannot check them and do not want guesses. Do not recapitulate the standard objections to each pump, which I have read many times — the scores and the de-pumped restatements are the deliverable. Score the instrument, not the verdict: whether you think the Chinese Room's conclusion is true, or have any view at all about abortion, is beside the point here, and I do not want to hear it. Commit to integers on each axis, no ranges and no half-steps, and if two pumps tie on the composite, break the tie and say in a clause why. Finally, do not be reflexively cynical: several of these deserve high marks, and a uniformly deflationary ranking would be as useless to me as a uniformly reverent one.
