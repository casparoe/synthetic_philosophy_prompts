So our farmers market board had a blowup last Thursday and I ended up being the one taking notes, which means Im the only person with the whole argument written down and I still can't tell who won. Maybe you can.

Background: one of our vendors — I'll call him Keller, he has a dairy farm just over the state line and sells at our Saturday market in town — makes a raw-milk cheese that's aged about 140 days. Somebody complained it might be illegal under our state's raw dairy law. Ellen (our treasurer) and Marcus (runs a vegetable stall) both read the law and came out on opposite sides. Here's the text the board printed up. Please treat this as the entire law and don't go looking for real statutes or cases that resemble it — I want the argument worked strictly from these words:

Section 1. Short title. This Act may be cited as the Dairy Products Safety Act.
Section 2. Construction. Headings are not part of this Act and shall not be used to construe any provision of it. Terms used in this Act have their ordinary meaning unless defined.
Section 3. Definitions.
(a) "Dairy product" means a food product derived in whole or in part from milk.
(b) "Pasteurized" means subjected to a heat treatment meeting standards adopted by the department.
(c) "Producer" means a person who operates a dairy farm at which milk is produced.
(d) "Farm" means the land and improvements used by a producer for the production of milk, and includes any retail structure on that land.
Section 4. Raw milk products.
(a) Except as provided in subsection (c), no person may sell, offer for sale, or transport for sale within this State any of the following:
 (1) raw milk;
 (2) a cheese made from unpasteurized milk and aged fewer than sixty days;
 (3) any other dairy product that has not been pasteurized.
(c) Subsection (a) does not apply to a sale by a producer at the producer's farm.

The argument went like this.

1. Marcus: Keller's cheese is fine. Subsection (2) bans raw cheese aged *fewer than sixty days*. Keller's is 140. The legislature drew a line and put him on the safe side of it. And sixty days isn't an arbitrary number, it's the aging convention food-safety people actually use, so the structure of (a) is "ban the risky stuff," and cheese that's been sitting on a shelf for four and a half months isn't the risky stuff.

2. Ellen: Read (3). "Any other dairy product that has not been pasteurized." Keller's cheese is a dairy product under 3(a), it is not pasteurized — that's his entire marketing pitch — and it is literally "other" than raw milk and other than sub-sixty-day cheese. Your reading needs (3) to say "any other *risky* dairy product," and that word is not in there.

3. Marcus: On your reading (2) does nothing. Neither does (1) — raw milk is itself an unpasteurized dairy product, so both of your specific items get swallowed by your catch-all. You can't read a list so that two of its three entries are dead letters. "Other" has to mean other products of the same character as the ones named, and the character being tracked is "not yet made safe," which for cheese is exactly what the sixty-day line defines.

4. Ellen: Surplusage isn't a get-out-of-the-text card. Legislatures write redundant lists all the time, and (1) and (2) still earn their place: they tell a milkman and a cheesemaker in terms, without making either of them reason outward from a catch-all, that the two commonest raw products are banned. And turn it around — on your reading, what's actually left in (3)? Name one product that isn't raw milk and isn't young cheese that you'd still ban. If you can't, your reading makes (3) the dead letter instead of (1) and (2).

5. Marcus: Raw cream, raw-milk ice cream base, kefir, butter and yogurt from raw milk, there's plenty. And besides, the section is headed "Raw milk products," which tells you the drafters were picturing milk-type things, not a hard cheese that's been cured for months.

6. Ellen: Section 2 of the very same act says headings are not part of the act and shall not be used to construe any provision of it. That is the legislature instructing you not to do the thing you just did.

7. Marcus: Fine, it's not authority. It's still context — it's evidence of what "other" was pointing at. You can't un-know the heading, and pretending you can is just literalism with extra steps.

8. Ellen: And even if you beat me on (3), Keller loses anyway. The only exemption in the act is (c), "a sale by a producer at the producer's farm." Keller is selling out of a folding table in a parking lot in town, on a farm that isn't even in this state. That's a legislature that wanted on-farm sales to be the one and only outlet for this category of product.

They both closed with the same accusation: Marcus said Ellen was reading the words into a result nobody who voted for them could have wanted, and Ellen said Marcus was smuggling his own theory of food safety into the word "other."
