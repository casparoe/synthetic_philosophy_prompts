Standard speed-reading instruction trains students to eliminate subvocalization — the faint inner voicing of the words on the page — on the theory that it caps reading at speaking pace and contributes nothing to comprehension. School reading instruction in the same culture spends years building something like the opposite skill: phonics, sounding words out, reading aloud, treating phonological recoding as the way printed marks become meaning. What picture of the relationship between language and thought does each practice presuppose? Does suppressing the inner voice commit you to something like a language of thought — an innate code that is not any natural language, in which understanding proceeds while speech is switched off — and does phonics commit you to the view that thinking in words is part of what understanding words consists in? Reconstruct both implicit theories as precisely as you can; separate what each practice strictly requires from what it merely fits comfortably with; and identify where the practices are philosophically underdetermined, such that a single theory of mind could license both. Then say what would have to change in how reading is taught — and in how ordinary readers describe their own experience of reading — if we adopted the strong claim that all propositional thought occurs in a non-natural-language code, versus the claim that conscious verbal thinking is carried out in a natural language itself. Please write your answer in American English.
