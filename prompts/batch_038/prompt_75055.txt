I'm in the middle of a seminar paper on weakness of will and I've gotten stuck on a chunk of Plato's *Protagoras* — the bit where Socrates is arguing that what gets called being "overcome by pleasure" is really an error of measurement. Here's the passage (356c–356e, in an old public-domain translation; I've stitched two pages together but it runs continuously):

> Does not the same size appear larger to your sight when near, and smaller when distant? They will admit this. And it is the same with thickness and number? And sounds of equal strength are greater when near, and smaller when distant? They would agree to this. Now if our welfare consisted in doing and choosing things of large dimensions, and avoiding and not doing those of small, what would be our salvation in life? Would it be the art of measurement, or the power of appearance? Is it not the latter that leads us astray, as we saw, and many a time causes us to take things topsy-turvy and to have to change our minds both in our conduct and in our choice of great or small? Whereas the art of measurement would have made this appearance ineffective, and by showing us the truth would have brought our soul into the repose of abiding by the truth, and so would have saved our life.

The reason I care is that everyone in my seminar immediately jumped on the near/distant bit and said "look, Plato invented hyperbolic discounting, temporal proximity inflates apparent magnitude just like spatial proximity inflates apparent size." I want to push back on that, but I can't quite articulate why, and I think the problem is that I don't actually understand what the passage is committing Socrates to. So can you walk me through it carefully? A few things I specifically want unpacked:

1. What is the "power of appearance" doing here as an explanation? A spatial-size illusion is a *cold* error — the stick in the water still looks bent after you've measured it, and I think somewhere in the *Republic* Plato says outright that calculation can't dissolve the appearance, only override it. Does that fit what's said here, that measurement "would have made this appearance ineffective"? There's a difference between an illusion being corrected and an illusion being rendered inert, and I suspect that difference is doing real work in the argument.

2. Is "near or distant" in this stretch spatial, temporal, or deliberately both? Because it makes a big difference. If it's purely spatial the analogy is just an analogy and Socrates hasn't yet claimed anything about time; if it's temporal he's claiming a systematic, directional distortion. And that's where I think the hyperbolic-discounting comparison actually breaks: a size-constancy illusion is symmetric and non-motivated — sometimes things look too big, sometimes too small, and there's no built-in gradient — whereas a discount function is systematically biased toward the near and monotonically decreasing in delay. Can Socrates get from "appearances mislead about magnitude" to "appearances mislead about magnitude in a way that reliably favors the nearer option"? What would he need for that step, and does the passage supply it?

3. The odd/even and greater/less stuff that comes right after, and the slide from "things of large dimensions" to "pleasure and pain" — is that a substantive move or just him filling in the template? Relatedly: my own hunch, which I want you to stress-test rather than flatter, is that the standard framing of this as Socrates needing a *cardinal scale of pleasure* is a red herring. I think the measuring art here is better read as a **correction procedure** rather than a common currency: you don't need pleasures to be commensurable in units, you only need the appearance-inflating factor to be learnable and removable, the way knowing about perspective lets you un-foreshorten a seen object without ever assigning it a number. Call it the calibration reading. Does the text support that, or does something in the passage — the "repose of abiding by the truth" line, say, or the fact that he keeps talking about choosing the greater over the lesser — force you back onto a scale with a single unit? I'd want to know what specifically in the wording would sink the calibration reading.

4. And what is that "repose" phrase doing at all? It's the one part that doesn't sound like optics. Why does he describe the successful outcome as the soul settling down rather than just getting the right answer? Is that an ethical payoff bolted onto a cognitive argument, or is it smuggling in a claim that akrasia essentially involves inner turmoil — which would be a completely different model of the mind from the size-constancy one, since illusions don't come with anguish?

Feel free to tell me where you think I'm overreading a translation and where the Greek concept underneath is doing something the English can't show, and flag anything in my three questions that rests on a mistranslation rather than a real interpretive problem. Don't bother pointing me at the secondary literature or giving me a bibliography — I'm working offline this week and can't get at any of it, so I need the reasoning spelled out in your own words rather than referred out to other people's.
