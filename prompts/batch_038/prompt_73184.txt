I'm the director of residence life at a small college, and a couple dozen times a semester I get the same fight: one student says something is their own business because it happens inside their own room, and their suitemates file a petition saying they all unanimously want it stopped. Both sides usually have a real point, and right now I decide on vibes, which isnt fair and takes me all week. Stakes aren't life and death — mostly people threatening to break their housing contract or complaining to the dean, who signs off on anything I can't settle.

Can you write me a decision procedure I could actually run on one of these cases in about twenty minutes, built out of the social choice work on Sen's liberal paradox? I want it explicit about the moving parts of the theorem — the personal-sphere condition, Pareto, unrestricted domain — and about which one I'm giving up when I rule a particular way, plus Sen's own distinction between protecting a right and exercising it, and the objection that the whole paradox only bites when preferences are meddlesome. Tell me which of those distinctions have to survive the translation into a checklist and which ones I can safely drop.

Then run it on three cases: a student reading aloud behind a closed door from a text the rest of the suite finds deeply offensive; two suitemates who each claim a personal sphere over overlapping things; and a student who offers to trade their prerogative away for something the others want. At least one of them should come out the way I won't like. Finish by saying where the procedure has to stop and hand the call to a person, and what it just can't do. American English please.
