I want a map of the exposure of a historical thesis I have come to accept, not a defense of it.

The thesis: revolutions devour their children as a matter of principle rather than of circumstance. On my reading of the French Revolution, the Terror was not a derailment produced by invasion, civil war, and subsistence crisis; those supplied the occasion but not the explanation. The mechanism I have in mind is roughly this — if sovereignty is vested in an indivisible general will, then organized opposition is not a legitimate competitor but a usurpation, and dissent becomes criminal without anyone having to decide that it should. On this view the criminalization of opposition was already licensed at the Revolution's outset, and the emergencies of the following years merely removed the inhibitions against acting on it.

Grant that I know the stock objections: that the intensity of repression tracks military news and bread prices rather than doctrine; that the bulk of those prosecuted were not nobles or priests but ordinary people denounced by neighbours for ordinary, local, personal reasons; that identical principles were invoked for incompatible policies by factions in the act of killing one another; that the whole apparatus came apart within weeks of Thermidor, which looks more like a contingent coup than the exhaustion of a logic. I want to know how much weight these actually carry against the thesis as stated, rather than against a strawman of it.

First, sort the available defeaters into those that would be decisive and those that would be merely awkward, and say why each falls where it does. Include findings a historian could in principle produce — about timing, about variation across the departments, about the social profile of denouncers and denounced, about what the revolutionaries said and did in periods when they were not under threat — and specify what shape such a finding would have to have in order to be genuinely fatal rather than absorbable into a more subtle retelling.

Second, address the comparative dimension. What would count as a real test case: a revolution committed to the doctrine of an indivisible popular will that did not criminalize opposition, or one that criminalized opposition without any such doctrine? Say what such cases would have to look like for the comparison to carry evidential weight, and whether the thesis can be tested this way at all given how it is formulated.

Third, confront the worry I cannot shake, which is that an interpretive thesis of this shape is insulated rather than confirmed by its explanatory reach: almost any outcome can be derived after the fact from principles as capacious as unity, virtue, and the general will, and the mechanism can simply be swapped when the first candidate mechanism is refuted. Tell me whether the thesis as I have stated it risks anything at all — whether there is a possible history of the Revolution that would oblige me to abandon it, as opposed to one that would only oblige me to state it more carefully. If it is insulated, say plainly whether that is a defect in a claim of this kind or simply what interpretive historical claims are, and whether the insulation is worse here than it is for the rival readings.

Fourth, state what the thesis predicts that the circumstantialist and the class-conflict accounts do not, and identify the points at which a serious historian who holds it would themselves concede that the evidence runs against them — not merely the points at which its critics say so.

Treat this throughout as a question about the direction of explanation. Do not spend time on whether the Terror was justified, and do not give me a survey of the historiography or a list of works to consult. I am asking what would have to be true for my position to be false.
