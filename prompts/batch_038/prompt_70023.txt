Third novel, and I'm stuck on a chapter that's basically an argument about constitutions wearing a costume, so I need to talk to someone who isn't a character.

The book is set in a country writing its first constitution after a bad decade. My drafter — a woman named Ilse — has slipped in a device I keep calling a slow knot in my notes: repealing certain clauses needs a two-thirds vote AND the repeal has to be opened in one parliament and closed, by a second vote, five years later. So you can untie it, but only slower than the panic that made you want to untie it. Her rival calls this a coward's clause: a knot you can cut isn't a commitment, and a knot you can't cut is just a cage.

That argument sent me back to Dr. Strangelove, which I now think is definately the purest precommitment film ever made and which almost nobody seems to read that way. The Doomsday Machine is a binding device: it exists to take the option of backing down off the table so that neither side has to trust anybody's nerve. And it fails for two reasons that strike me as seperate failures, though I can't tell whether the film knows they're separate — it's irrevocable, by design, so no one can recall it once triggered; and nobody knew it existed, so it couldn't frighten anyone, which was the entire point of building it.

So: read the film against the precommitment literature, properly. Start from Elster — Ulysses lashed to the mast, the wax in the sailors' ears, the asymmetry of who gets the experience and who pays for it — and then whatever he went on to say about why self-binding so often fails, including his point that the hard cases are the ones where it's your judgment that gets corrupted rather than your willpower. Then bring in Holmes's objection, as you remember it, that the whole Ulysses picture mis-describes constitutions: a constitution isn't one person tying down a later version of himself, it's a lot of people binding a lot of other people, and it empowers at least as much as it disables. And Jefferson's generational argument, the one where he calculates that no generation can bind the next beyond roughly nineteen years.

What I want out of it:

- Is the Doomsday Machine actually a Ulysses contract, or a deterrent borrowing the grammar of one? Who's the binding self and who's the bound self, and does that question even survive when the parties are states rather than people?
- Which conditions for successful self-binding does the machine satisfy and which does it blow up? Is irrevocability what makes it work, or is irrevocability precisely the defect — and does the film seem aware that it's the same feature doing both jobs?
- Does it dramatize the dead-hand problem literally: a decision made by people who are mostly dead by the time it executes, which cannot be renegotiated by anyone? And is that what makes it comic rather than merely bleak — Strangelove visibly more in love with the machine than with surviving?
- Where does the reading break? Be honest with me. What does any real constitution have that the machine lacks — courts, amendment procedure, the fact that somebody has to say what the text means on an ordinary Tuesday — and does that missing interpretive layer mean the film isn't really about precommitment at all?
- Does Ilse's slow knot survive the comparison? It's reversible-but-slow, neither mast nor machine, and I need to know whether that's a genuine third category or a fudge I invented to keep my plot moving.
