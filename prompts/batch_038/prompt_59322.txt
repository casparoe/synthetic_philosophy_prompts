I am several years into a novel about the July crisis of 1914, told largely from inside the Wilhelmstrasse — an under-secretary, a cipher clerk, a junior military attaché, people who help make a world war without ever intending one. Two of my readers have now fallen out over a single sentence, and I cannot settle my author's note until I know what the sentence is actually claiming.

The sentence is Lloyd George's retrospective one: "The nations slithered over the brink into the boiling cauldron of war without any trace of apprehension or dismay." My consulting historian quotes it at me whenever I worry that a book populated by sympathetic German officials will read as an apologia. His position is that the line licenses a novel without villains and that the diplomatic record of July 1914 bears it out; he pairs it with Bethmann Hollweg's remark at the height of the crisis that "All the governments, including that of Russia, and the great majority of the peoples are pacific, but things are out of control." A doctoral student who read the draft takes the opposite view: that this is the oldest exculpatory formula in the historiography, kin to the "war guilt" article of the Versailles settlement and to the German campaign against it, that Fritz Fischer's *Germany's Aims in the First World War* was written precisely to destroy it, and that its recent revival in the sleepwalking idiom is a retreat from the question of who wanted what.

They cannot both be right about one sentence, and I suspect they are arguing about different sentences wearing the same clothes. So I would like the sentence disassembled.

Lay out, as a menu rather than a single interpretation, the distinct propositions it could be carrying — separating at minimum the claims about what the actors *expected* (a short war, a local war, no general war at all), what they *wanted*, what the *mechanism* was (mobilization timetables, alliance commitments, railway logistics, the delegation of decision to staff officers), whether the descent was *continuous* or whether there was a moment at which someone could have stopped it, who the grammatical subject "the nations" picks out and whether that entity is capable of slithering, apprehension, or dismay at all, and finally what follows about *responsibility* — culpability, reparations, the rightness of assigning guilt to a state.

For each candidate reading, tell me what would have to be true for it to hold, whether it is a platitude that no historian disputes, a substantive and defensible thesis, or flatly false on any reasonable account of the July record; and whether Fischer's argument — in its strongest form, resting on war-aims documents such as the September Programme and the continuity thesis rather than on any single incriminating telegram — actually contradicts it. Some of these readings, I suspect, Fischer leaves untouched, and it matters to me which.

Then answer the two questions that are mine rather than the historians'. First: which reading, if any, does my consulting historian need in order to get his "novel without villains," and does that reading in fact deliver it — or is the exculpation coming from somewhere else, for instance from a subject that has no intentions to begin with, so that nothing could count as culpable? And is Bethmann's sentence the same claim as Lloyd George's, or a different and more damaging one, given that it is a first-person admission of lost control offered in the moment rather than a collective denial of intent offered in retrospect? Second: which of these readings may I build scenes on without lying to my reader? Dramatic irony requires that the reader know what the characters do not; if genuinely nobody foresaw the general war, is there irony left to deploy, or only hindsight that the novel would be smuggling in through its narration?

End by saying which reading my historian was actually invoking when he quoted the line at me — the one he thinks he was using, if the two differ.
