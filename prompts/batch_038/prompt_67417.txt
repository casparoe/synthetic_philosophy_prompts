I want to be sure I am neither marking it too generously nor too harshly.

**Question (10 marks):** An engineering team proposes to encode an explicit rule set, modeled on the doctrine of double effect, in a hospital triage-support system. Their rationale is that a system which learns its moral policy from recorded human decisions merely absorbs whatever regularities happen to be present in the data, whereas a system that explicitly represents moral principles derives its behavior from those principles instead. (a) Does representing principles explicitly answer the objection the team raises about learning from data? (b) Would a system built this way be an ethical agent in the fullest sense?

**Student's answer:**

(a) Partly. The team is right that a model fitted to past triage decisions has no internal standard by which to criticize those decisions. If the record reflects a historical tendency to under-treat a particular patient group, the model will reproduce that tendency and register it as normal, because nothing in the training signal marks it as a departure from what ought to happen. An explicit rule set fixes the standard in advance, so behavior can be checked against a stated principle rather than against further data, and the principle itself can be revised by an ethics committee without any retraining. That is a genuine advantage.

The team overstates it, though. Making the rules explicit does not show that the system's behavior is derived from the principles rather than from the choices of the people who wrote them. The rules encode whatever the designers took double effect to require, so explicitness makes that choice easier to locate and dispute but does not justify it. The worry that the system's moral outlook is inherited rather than grounded is therefore answered only in the weaker sense that the inheritance is now visible.

(b) Double effect permits an action with both good and bad effects when the bad effect is not intended but merely foreseen, and when allowing the bad effect minimizes overall harm. A system that represents the intended/foreseen distinction, applies it reliably across cases, and reports which clause of the rule decided the case is doing more than pattern matching: it is operating with moral concepts and can give its reasons in terms clinicians can evaluate. On that basis it qualifies as an ethical agent in the fullest sense, and the outstanding difficulties concern the content of the rules rather than the standing of the system.
