Six weeks ago I became chair of a small nonprofit board, which means that when something falls through — a treasurer who stops filing, a program lead who misses a deadline others depend on — the unpleasant conversation is mine to have. What I had not anticipated is how unevenly those conversations land. Nothing in my own record resembles what I am condemning; the pressures that undid these people never came anywhere near me, partly because my circumstances have always had more slack in them and partly, I will admit, because I have never much wanted what they wanted. So the usual retort is simply unavailable: nobody can say I did the same thing last year, nobody can point to a fault of my own. And yet the sharpest resistance I meet is not an accusation of double standards but something closer to what would you know — a sense that condemnation from me is weightless, or faintly indecent, precisely because it comes from someone the relevant test was never administered to, and who therefore cannot tell from the inside whether what he is condemning is a defect of character or a run of bad luck.

I keep seeing the same shape outside my own life: the comfortably employed condemning the person who took money from the till, the childless condemning a parent, a nation founded in violence condemning another nation's. In each case the objector is clean, and the cleanliness is what people resent.

Is there an established concept for this — for a person's blame losing its force not because of anything they did but because of what they were never asked to face? I am aware of the neighbouring territory: hypocrisy, the retort that turns a charge back on the accuser, and the general philosophical question of who is entitled to blame at all. I want the specific thing that sits alongside those. Please tell me which nearby notions come closest and how mine differs from each — in particular, how it differs both from the case of the pot calling the kettle black and from the observation that whether one ever resisted a temptation may be a matter of luck rather than virtue — and, if nothing in the literature covers it, propose a term with a definition and mark its edges. Where does it stop applying: to a judge sentencing a thief, to a parent, to a stranger writing online, to someone who was tested and passed? A name alone will not help me; what I need is the carving.
