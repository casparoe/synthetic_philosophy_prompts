Three weeks ago I was appointed to a four-person technical working group inside a national agency that runs the geological disposal programme for high-level radioactive waste. My assignment is to redraft the section of our cost-benefit guidance dealing with the ethical parameters of discounting, because the current guidance applies a single real rate — three percent, inherited wholesale from the treasury's general appraisal rules — uniformly out to ten thousand years, and nobody on the staff can defend that choice in public consultation any longer. I am an applied economist rather than a philosopher, and I have been reading the intergenerational ethics literature at night. What I put in my first draft has produced an internal dispute that I now have to resolve in writing, and I would like a calibrated outside view on the central question before I revise it.

The draft introduces what I have been calling a two-clock rule. The first clock is monetary: cash flows are discounted at the long-run real return on the safest instrument that could finance the programme, which handles opportunity cost without entailing any ethical commitment. The second clock is ethical: a pure time preference parameter, applied to wellbeing rather than to money, defaulted to zero, and — this is the provision my colleagues object to most — a positive value may be entered only if the analyst attaches what the draft calls a conversion certificate, meaning an explicit written statement of whose interests are being down-weighted and on what ground. In effect the ethical parameter becomes a documented exception rather than an inherited number. The proposition that licenses this default is the one I want your credence on. Call it P: for two states of affairs identical in the identity and number of persons, in the wellbeing each person attains, and in everyone's consumption level at each date, but differing only in that some given benefit falls at time t rather than at t+k, the correct axiology assigns them equal value. Put differently, the bare fact that wellbeing is located later in time is not itself a reason to count it for less, once declining marginal utility of consumption, uncertainty about whether the benefit will ever be received, and the opportunity cost of capital have all been handled separately.

Give me a number for P and show me the derivation. I would like at least the following candidate terms, each with a probability to one decimal place and one to three sentences of justification: the probability that the correct axiology is impersonal and additive in a way that makes temporal location irrelevant, as against person-affecting or prioritarian alternatives; the probability that all the intuitive support for a positive ethical parameter is exhausted by the non-ethical factors listed above, including the possibility that future people will simply be richer and that what presents itself as impatience is really an empirical survival-or-uptake probability wearing normative clothes; the probability that a genuinely non-arbitrary normative defence of pure time preference exists — something like the thought that obligations of sacrifice weaken where reciprocity and shared membership are impossible; the probability that the non-identity problem leaves P intact, since in real long-horizon cases the timing of our choices determines who will exist at all, so that the ceteris paribus clause may be unsatisfiable in practice even where P is true; and the probability that the dispute is at least partly verbal, there being no determinate fact about the correct axiology. State your combination rule explicitly — product, weighted average, something else — flag any of these terms you regard as non-independent and say how that dependence changes the result, and then give the final credence. Finally, tell me which term the number is most sensitive to, what evidence or argument would move that term the most, and whether your figure should be read as the probability that P is true or as the probability that informed philosophical opinion would endorse P; if those come apart, give me both. Two constraints. You have no web access, and I do not want references, a bibliography, or named citations of any kind — I want the reasoning, and I will do the reading myself. And please do not dissolve the question into a survey of positions: I already know that the descriptivist-versus-prescriptivist dispute over social discount rates is decades old, that the near-zero ethical parameter used in a widely publicised climate review attracted precisely the criticism my colleagues are now making, and that a number of national appraisal frameworks already taper their rates at long horizons. I need a single number with its weak points marked, because a number is what has to go into the guidance document.
