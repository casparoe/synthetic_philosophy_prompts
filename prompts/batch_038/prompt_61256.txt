I retired four years ago after 33 years running a trace-metals lab, and to stop my head going soft I've taken up reading philosophy, badly and out of order. At the moment I'm stuck in Plato's Charmides, in an ancient translation somebody left at the recycling centre — the pages are practically translucent. It's a dialogue about temperance, and partway through the definition on the table turns into something like "knowledge of knowledge", and I've hit a wall at the bit marked 169b in the margin. Here it is, starting mid-sentence because that's where the page broke:

"...that there should be a science of science, nor willing, let it be ever so true, to acknowledge this to be temperance until I have made out whether such a thing as this would benefit us or not. For, you see, I have a presentiment that temperance is something beneficial and good; and you, therefore, son of Callaeschrus—since you lay it down that temperance is this very science of science, and moreover of the lack of science—shall first indicate the possibility, as I put it just now, and then the benefit added to the possibility, of such a thing;"

I don't want a summary of the dialogue, I want help getting the machinery of this bit into focus. Specifically:

1. Socrates looks like he's splitting the problem into two seperate jobs — (a) show that such a science is possible at all, (b) show it would do us any good — and refusing to accept the definition until both are done. Is that the right read? And which of the two does he think he can actually win? My impression from reading on is that he presses (b) much harder than (a), which strikes me as odd, since (a) is the more interesting question to a scientist.

2. What's the logical status of "let it be ever so true"? He sounds like he's granting the possibility for the sake of argument. Is this a conditional refutation — even if such self-knowledge were possible it wouldn't be the virtue — or does he separately think it's flat impossible? Earlier on there's something about sight and hearing and about a magnitude bigger than itself, and I can't tell whether those are meant to establish impossibility or just to raise a worry. I may be mangling that part.

3. "And moreover of the lack of science" throws me every time. Is knowing what you don't know part of the definition, or a bonus feature tacked on? Does any of the later argument lean on it?

4. Here's my own prejudice, and I'd like you to attack it. I spent my working life on calibration: instruments that measure things, then a layer above that checks the instruments against standards, then standards traceable to further standards. "Science of science" reads to me like metrology — a second-order discipline that doesn't measure the world but audits the measuring. If that analogy is worth anything, tell me exactly where Socrates' objection bites. Is it that in my lab the audit layer has a product — a certificate, a corrected reading, an uncertainty budget — while his second-order science turns out to have no product of its own? Or is it that my traceability chain bottoms out in something external (a reference mass, a caesium clock) whereas his has nothing outside itself to bottom out in? I genuinely want to know whether my instinct is illuminating or just a comfortable way of not understanding him.

5. Down a Wikipedia rabbit hole I learned that modern epistemologists argue about a principle called KK — roughly, if you know p then you're in a position to know that you know p — and about "luminosity", the idea that for some mental states you can always tell you're in them. So: is Socrates here arguing against KK, against luminosity, or against neither? My guess is that his worry isn't whether such self-knowledge is available but whether it would be worth anything to have, which would make it a completely different argument from the modern ones. Tell me if I'm right, and tell me whether anything in his line on this page actually bears on the modern debate. Please don't point me at papers or chapters — I can't get hold of them and I don't want a reading list, I want the thinking.

6. Lastly, does this passage and where it leads actually refute the claim that temperance is self-knowledge, or only Critias's particular way of putting it? The dialogue seems to end with everybody baffled and Socrates admitting he's done the boy no good. Is that just aporia, or is there a positive result buried in there that I'm supposed to dig out?

Numbered answers would be great. And if I've got something about the text flat wrong, say so bluntly — I spent enough years telling postdocs that their data doesn't mean what they think it means.
