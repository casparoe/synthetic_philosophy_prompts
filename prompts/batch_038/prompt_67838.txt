I'm a data engineer, not a philosopher — I mostly read this stuff on the bus, which is about 40 minutes each way, and my partner has made fun of me for it for years. A while back I was trying to understand how you represent an agent's beliefs in the possible-worlds style, and I hit the wall everybody hits: if a belief state is just a set of worlds, then anyone who believes anything at all believes every logical truth and everything that follows from it. I learned the problem has a name (logical omniscience) and that real people work on it, but what I actually got stuck on is a more basic question, and I'd rather work through it by going case by case on my own gut verdicts than by reading more machinery. Here's what I think:

- Me believing it's raining, believing that if it's raining the basement window is open, being asked about the window out loud, saying oh yeah, and still not counting myself as believing the window is open: irrational. It's one step and I had the pieces in front of me.
- A mathematician who has checked forty of forty-one steps of a long proof and won't accept the theorem until she checks the last one: not irrational, even though the theorem follows from what she already believes. I'd go further and say she's doing her job well.
- But now the case I can't fit with the previous one: someone who believes p, believes that if p then q, is handed a two-line derivation connecting them, reads it carefully, and says they still don't believe q, something just feels off about it. That one I do call irrational. Same shape as the mathematician, different verdict, and the only difference I can point to is how long the chain is and whether I trust the chain.
- Me having no view on Goldbach's conjecture: totally fine. Me believing 2+2=5: not fine at all. Both are logical matters.
- Believing a bunch of things that are jointly contradictory, where the contradiction only surfaces after two weeks of careful work: not irrational, at least not yet. The very same person, same beliefs, after someone hands her a one-page derivation: irrational if she keeps everything. Nothing about her changed except what she was shown, and that's the part that bugs me.
- Believing p and believing if p then q while driving home and thinking about dinner, so the connection never crosses my mind: not irrational, surely. But there's something wrong with an agent who could never be brought to notice no matter how you put it to them, and I don't know how to say what that something is without saying they're irrational right now, which I just denied.
- My friend does competitive programming and spots consequences basically instantly. Part of me wants to hold her to a higher standard than me — missing an inference she could do in her sleep is a failing in her that isn't one in me. But then rationality becomes a tax on being smart: get better at deduction and you become newly criticizable for beliefs you were happily holding last year, and you can't go back. That feels perverse. On the other hand, a bright eight-year-old who accepts both premises of a trivial syllogism and shrugs at the conclusion isn't irrational, while an adult doing the same thing is, so capacity has to matter somehow.

My rough attempt at a principle is: you're required to believe the obvious consequences of what you believe, where obviousness depends on how much work the derivation takes and how good you are at that kind of work. That's near-circular — obvious is doing all the labor — and it says nothing about the before/after business, or about whether the requirement only switches on when you actually consider the question. I also can't tell whether the complaint in these cases is that I'm missing a belief, or only that I'm holding an incoherent combination, and those feel like genuinely different accusations that would give different verdicts in the distracted-driving case. So: what principle, or small set of candidates, comes closest to capturing all of this? For each candidate I'd want every clause tied to the case that motivates it, its verdict on cases I haven't listed — someone who's asleep, say, or a knowledge base of mine that stores flatly inconsistent facts, where I don't even know if irrational is the right word — and a straight list of which of my verdicts it can't accommodate. If the honest answer is that the mathematician case and the two-line-derivation case can't both survive, pick one, argue for it, and don't paper over the split with an ad hoc clause about chain length that isn't motivated by anything else.
