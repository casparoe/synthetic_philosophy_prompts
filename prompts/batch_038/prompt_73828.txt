I'm a reporter working on a piece about the distance between what AI labs say about safety and what they actually build, and the phrase doing most of the work in my interviews is the alignment tax: the claim that making a system safer makes it less competitive, so the lab that pays it loses ground to rivals that don't. Before I write another word, I want to know what I should be demanding of any account of that claim. Write the desiderata — what a theory of the competitiveness constraint on alignment work has to deliver to be more than a slogan. It has to say what is being taxed and in what currency the tax is denominated, since lost capability, lost calendar time, lost dollars, and lost market share do not trade off cleanly against one another; it has to identify the counterfactual rival against whom the tax is measured, given that a cost borne by every lab simultaneously is not a competitive cost to any of them; it has to distinguish a recurring burden paid at deployment from a one-off burden paid at training; and it has to leave room for the possibility that some safety work is capability-positive rather than costly at all. Give the consideration behind each requirement, mark which are non-negotiable and which are the preferences of a particular camp — the people who model this as race dynamics versus the people who think the operative constraint is regulatory legitimacy and public trust — and tell me which single requirement, taken seriously, would falsify the most confident things I have been told on the record. If some subset of the desiderata proves jointly unsatisfiable, so that no account can be at once externally checkable by people like me and decision-relevant to the people actually making the tradeoff, say so plainly; that may be the story. Then, briefly, how the two accounts I keep hearing — that coordination dissolves the tax, and that the tax is too small to matter — fare against the list.
