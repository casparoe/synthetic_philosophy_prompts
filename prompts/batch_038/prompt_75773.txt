I have followed the functions debate in philosophy of biology on and off for years, and I have become convinced that neither side is losing. On one side are the etiological accounts, on which a trait's function is fixed by the history of selection that explains the trait's presence; on the other, the causal-role accounts, on which a function is a contribution the trait now makes to some capacity of the containing system. Each camp concedes the other's paradigm cases. Each has a well-known set of cases it handles badly — malfunction and vestigial organs for the causal-role side, novel traits and the first heart in a lineage for the etiological side — and each responds to those cases by tightening its account rather than by retreating. The field's own summary of the situation goes back at least to Godfrey-Smith's argument in 1993 that there is consensus without unity here, that working biologists use the term pluralistically and the philosophy is an argument over which usage to privilege. Add the more recent organizational account, which claims to deliver normativity without a selection history, plus the parallel literature comparing biological and artifactual function, and there are now several live theories with heavily overlapping extensions and no visible attrition.

My hypothesis, offered strictly as a hypothesis: this is a disguised query of the standard kind. Two camps are using one word to stand in for two different questions — a historical-explanatory question about why this trait is there at all, and a systemic question about what this trait currently does for the organism — and the appearance of substantive dispute is generated by the fact that both questions matter, both get asked in the same register, and both are answered with the same word. On that reading, the live issue is not which account is true but which concept deserves the name for which downstream job: content and teleosemantics, norms of malfunction, physiological explanation, engineering and design reasoning. Progress would then look like an explicit division of labor, not a refutation.

Before you accept or reject that, I want it tested against a case where the usual obstacles are removed. I work on interpretability for a small recurrent policy network trained by gradient descent on a control task. Two things are true of this system that are never true of an organism: its entire selection history is logged at the level of individual parameter updates, and its causal organization is fully measurable. There is no inferential gap between evidence and etiology, and no gap between counterfactual reasoning and actual contribution.

Consider three components. Component A was shaped early in training, gradients consistently pushed on it in the direction of the task, and ablating it collapses performance; both accounts agree it has a function. Component B arose late as a side effect of an architectural constraint — an interaction between weight decay and a normalization layer — and throughout training the objective was effectively insensitive to it; nothing in the reward ever selected for what it does. But after training, in the deployment environment, B turns out to respond to out-of-distribution inputs in a way that makes the network degrade conservatively, and ablating B removes that behavior. So: no etiology for the effect in question, maximal causal role. Then a third stage — we fine-tuned for the behavior, so B now has a selection history for it as well, except that the history is bimodal, a long unselected phase followed by a short selected one, which is precisely the configuration where a modern-history account keyed to the most recent shaping and an account keyed to cumulative lineage contribution come apart. Meanwhile the engineers have started writing tests around B and describing it in documentation as the anomaly detector, importing an intentionalist notion nobody ever trained for.

In this system the two theories' inputs are both known to arbitrary precision. If they still deliver different verdicts about whether B has a function, then the disagreement cannot be about any fact that further investigation could settle — which is what my hypothesis predicts. If they converge, then the classic biological cases were doing something else, and my diagnosis is wrong.

What I want from you:

1. A diagnosis, not a survey. Is this a terminological dispute, a genuine substantive disagreement about which facts are function-constituting, two groups answering different questions under one heading, an argument about which concept should hold the name because of the work it does, or an ill-posed question? Commit to one primary diagnosis; do not distribute your confidence evenly across the menu to be safe.

2. The diagnosis argued from what each side actually says. Use the specific moves each camp makes when confronted with its known bad cases — malfunction, vestiges, novel traits, the first heart — and tell me which of those moves my B case reproduces, which it does not, and whether the moves are responsive or merely protective.

3. A check on my construction. Does B genuinely separate the theories, or have I built a case in which they agree while looking like they disagree — for instance because the etiological camp can claim B's function all along via the fine-tuning phase, or because the causal-role camp must relativize function to an environment that was never part of B's training distribution? If the case fails, say how, and say whether any artificial case could do the separating work I want.

4. What would count as progress given your diagnosis: a distinction, a stipulation, a formal result, a change of subject, an index of function ascriptions to disciplines or explanatory interests, or nothing at all. If the honest answer is that no progress should be expected, argue for that and explain what the persistent disagreement is doing for the people having it.

5. The stakes outside the debate itself. If this is really a contest over concept allocation, what follows for the projects that consume these accounts — teleosemantics in particular, and interpretability work of the kind I do, where I need a principled way to say which components of a trained system have functions and which merely have effects?

I want your confidence stated numerically for the primary diagnosis, your best steelman of the leading alternative, and the specific piece of evidence or argument that would move you off your first choice. Please do not flatter the framing: if my hypothesis rests on a confused premise — if the two accounts are not rivals in the way I have assumed, if my synthetic case cannot bear the weight I put on it, or if the category of a verbal dispute is itself too crude to do the work I am asking of it — say that plainly at the top and argue it out, rather than answering the question I asked. I am not looking for references; do not assemble a bibliography or a reading list, and do not cite by author, title, and year beyond what I have already named. Argue from the content of the positions.
