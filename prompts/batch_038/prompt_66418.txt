I cover neuroscience and philosophy of mind for a general-interest magazine, and I am drafting a long piece on the readiness-potential experiments — the ones in which a subject's upcoming button-press can be decoded from brain activity seconds before the subject reports having made the decision. Nearly every framing I have encountered runs through a single phrase: "could have done otherwise." The lead investigator's summary, as relayed to me by the university press office, is that the volunteers in these studies had no such ability, since the outcome was settled in their brains before they knew it. A philosopher I interviewed replied that this has nothing to do with moral responsibility, because responsibility on his view requires only that the action issue from the agent in the right way; he pointed me toward Frankfurt-style counterexamples to the principle of alternative possibilities, and when I asked him what that principle actually says, he answered that it has several formulations and that the differences matter — but our recording cut off there.

I would like to prepare properly before the next interview. Could you lay out, as a numbered menu, the distinct precise claims that "he could have done otherwise" can be used to make? I am looking for readings that differ along several axes: causal determination by prior states versus mere predictability; what the agent was able to do at the time versus what would have happened in nearby counterfactual circumstances; alternative actions versus alternative precursors, signs, or moments of decision; general abilities the agent possesses versus opportunities actually available on the occasion; and how robust or how reason-responsive an alternative must be before its absence excuses. For each reading, tell me whether it is a platitude, a substantive and defensible thesis, or something the decoding results simply do not bear on — and then say explicitly which readings those results genuinely threaten, and which reading the philosopher's objection presupposes.

Two further constraints on the answer. First, work through a case of your own construction rather than the familiar Jones-and-Black setup: an implanted monitor that stands ready to steer the agent but never actually fires, and use it to show which entries on your menu survive the case and which do not. Second, one of the cases in my reporting is a failure to act rather than an act — a bystander who did nothing while there was, on the record, time to intervene — and I was told in passing that the slogan behaves differently for omissions than for actions, without any explanation of why. Please say whether that is right and what turns on it.

Finally, because these will appear in print: give me one plain-language sentence per reading that an ordinary reader could plausibly utter, and flag any that would be actively misleading if quoted out of context.
