My roommate is in a philosophy of science reading group and last Tuesday they let me tag along — the topic was the pessimistic meta-induction, and two of the regulars, Dev and Margit, went head to head for like forty minutes while everyone else ate cold pizza and scribbled on the whiteboard. I don't have a dog in this fight, I'm not a scientist, I just like watching arguments, but I trancscribed it as best I could and I still can't tell who actually landed punches. Here's roughly the sequence:

(1) Dev opened with Laudan's list — phlogeston, caloric fluid, the crystalline spheres, vital forces, the luminiferous ether — noting that each was endorsed by the best scientists of its day, each was later thrown out along with the entities it posited, and that the 1981 version of the argument was first fully pressed by Laudan. So "it was successful" can't be evidence for "it's approximately true," because we've watched success and falsity cohabit over and over. And since the realist's main weapon is that approximate truth is the best explanation of success, an inference that has misfired repeatedly before should be expected to misfire again now. (2) Margit said the sample is rigged: nearly every theory anyone has ever proposed is false, the graveyard is enormous, and the induction is drawn from it. To get from "the false theories we remember were once thought successful" to "today's successful theories are false" you need to know how many successful theories there have been in total, not just how many died — she accused him of counting bodies without knowing the size of the population, which she called a base-rate error. (3) Dev said fine, restrict the induction to theories that were genuinely well-confirmed and made novel predictions; there are still members. Fresnel's wave theory of light implied a bright spot at the center of a circular shadow, Poisson raised that as a reductio in 1818, Arago went and looked, and there it was — a novel prediction bought with a central posit (a mechanical ether) that's now flatly rejected. (4) Margit said divide and conquer: the success rode on the mathematical structure — the wave equations, interference, boundary conditions — and that structure is still doing work in later theories, while the ether was idle decoration. Realism should attach to the working posits and drop the metaphysical ones, and the working posits were retained, not refuted. (5) Dev hit that twice over: "working posit" is only identifiable in hindsight, so the realist isn't following a policy, he's rationalizing whatever happened to survive; and more deeply, the reason you can't tell in advance is that you can't enumerate the alternatives nobody has thought of yet, so at any moment there are unconceived rivals fitting the same data, which leaves today's working posits exactly as exposed as Fresnel's ether looked in 1818. (6) Margit said that charge boomerangs — Dev also only knows the ether was idle in hindsight, and she also can't enumerate the unconceived alternatives to her own anti-realism — and that Dev is inducting from a body of historical claims that is itself current and successful, so if her induction licenses pessimism about current theories it licenses pessimism about the historiography feeding it. (7) Dev said that was tu quoque and dodged the real issue, which is that the no-miracles argument assumes truth is the only explanation of predictive success on offer; van Fraassen's point is that successful theories are survivors of a competition, we select for empirical adequacy, so finding survivors empirically adequate is about as surprising as finding that all the fish in a pond breathe underwater. (8) Margit's closer: selection-for-fit explains why survivors fit the data they were selected on, but novel prediction is the anomaly — a theory tuned to known phenomena has no reason to get the bright spot right, or the bending of starlight, or the existence of the positron — and if you want an explanation of those you need something beyond "it was the fittest."
