I'm an editorial assistant at a journal in evolutionary behavioral science, and for the past year I've been triaging desk decisions alongside our editor-in-chief, who made her name defending adaptationist hypotheses against the charge that they are just-so stories while still rejecting a great deal of adaptationist work. Her rulings so far, with the evidence profiles she cited:

- A manuscript arguing for an evolved mechanism of rapid predator detection — faster detection of snake images than of flowers or mushrooms, a conditioning asymmetry the authors predicted before running the study, samples from four continents including two foraging populations, alarm-response data from monkeys, and no behavioral-genetic evidence: accepted, "the real thing."
- A manuscript on pathogen-avoidance disgust which predicted in advance that disgust would track cues of possible infection rather than statistical dangerousness, tested across eleven societies including foragers, with historical epidemiological records offered as support for the ancestral selection pressure independent of the trait, and no non-human data: accepted.
- A manuscript on a mate-preference adaptation where a cultural-transmission account was at least as well supported by the existing data: accepted, because the study ran the single test on which the two accounts disagree and the cultural account lost. Her note doesn't mention sampling.
- A manuscript with samples from twelve societies including foragers, an appealing functional account of the trait's design, and independent archaeological evidence about ancestral conditions, but whose measures were constructed after the effects were found and whose "predictions" restate the results: rejected, unfalsifiable in practice.
- A manuscript whose preregistered predictions all came out as expected, but where the authors reported that the effect disappeared once a schooling-related confound was controlled and offered a byproduct account of the whole pattern: rejected.

Now on her desk: the claim that humans have an evolved mechanism for detecting genetic relatedness from facial features and modulating trust accordingly, on the grounds that misdirected altruism was costly in small ancestral groups. The team preregistered three predictions — a graded trust response to faces morphed toward the participant's own face, no gradient when the morph alters equally large but unrelated dimensions, and persistence of the effect with attractiveness and familiarity statistically controlled — and all three held. Every participant is an American or British undergraduate; the cross-cultural test is promised for a follow-up. There are no heritability estimates, no comparative data, no independent evidence about ancestral group composition, and no positively supported rival account, though the authors note that self-face familiarity presupposes mirror and photograph exposure.

Predict her ruling and give me your confidence. Then tell me which decision rules these verdicts are jointly consistent with — I want at least two genuinely different rules that accommodate all of them — which precedent is closest to the new case, which rule you would bet she is running and why, what she would be conceding or abandoning by ruling either way, and what further case I could put to her that would settle it.
