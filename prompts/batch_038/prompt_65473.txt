I am auditing a graduate seminar on the philosophy of artificial intelligence this term, and our unit on the frame problem has stalled in a way I would like to break open with a concrete case. We have done the usual survey: the representational puzzle of writing down what persists after an action without an endless schedule of frame axioms; McCarthy's qualification problem about enumerating preconditions; the ramification problem about indirect effects; and the later distinction between the narrow formal problem and Dennett's broader problem of relevance-realisation, where a robot keeps deriving true but useless implications. What we have not managed to settle is whether any of this describes anything that actually happens to people operating machines, or whether it is an artefact of trying to write a logic of action. So please analyse the Three Mile Island accident of 28 March 1979 as a case study in the frame problem — not the nuclear physics, and not a general verdict on human factors, but a careful theory-to-case mapping tested in both directions. Please write the answer in British English.

For convenience, the salient facts; correct me only if I have got something plainly wrong, and flag any step where your analysis leans on a detail I have not supplied. Unit 2 was a pressurised water reactor running near full power when the secondary feedwater loop failed, tripping the turbine and then the reactor, while decay heat continued. Pressure rose in the primary loop and the pilot-operated relief valve on the pressuriser opened automatically; when pressure fell it should have reseated but stuck open, venting coolant for hours. Two matters outweighed the valve itself. First, the control-room indicator for that valve showed whether the closing solenoid was energised rather than whether the valve was physically shut, so the operators read it as shut. Second, in each of the two emergency feedwater lines a block valve had been closed for maintenance and its position light was obscured by a maintenance tag, so operators who could see the emergency pumps running believed feedwater was reaching the steam generators when it was not. Meanwhile coolant inventory in the primary loop was falling even as pressuriser level rose; the operators had been trained to treat a pressuriser that filled completely with water — going solid, with no steam bubble left — as dangerous, and so they cut back on emergency injection at the very moment the core was uncovering. Part of the core melted; offsite release was small. The President's commission concluded that preventing such accidents would require fundamental changes in the organisation, procedures, practices and above all the attitudes of the regulator and the industry, and insisted that the operators' errors could not be assessed apart from the system they had been trained for.

Three movements, please. First, do the mapping explicitly: in the frame-problem schema, who or what is the agent, what are its actions, what is the frame, which axioms would have to be written down, and where does the qualification problem bite — bearing in mind that almost everything decisive went wrong before anyone in the control room acted at all, so it is not obvious that the operators are the relevant agent. Second, take seriously a suggestion one of my classmates made that the rest of us found provocative but could not evaluate: that a control room is a frozen bundle of frame axioms, in that every instrument is a standing claim that one observable variable tracks one physical state and that nothing else relevant has changed — the relief-valve light asserting that a de-energised solenoid means a seated valve, the pump lights asserting that running pumps mean flowing feedwater. On that reading, Three Mile Island was not a failure to enumerate non-changes but a failure of already-enumerated non-changes, which would make it an instance of the frame problem in a form the literature barely distinguishes from the original one. Assess whether this re-description earns its keep: does it explain anything that the standard accounts — design-induced error, misleading instrumentation, training that encoded the wrong priorities, Perrow's interactive complexity and tight coupling — leave unexplained, or does it merely relabel them and shift nothing? Third, run the case back against the theory and say which of the following it is: a confirmation of the narrow construal, a confirmation only of the broad relevance-realisation construal, an anomaly, or something the theory never anticipated because it was framed for an agent acting upon the world rather than for an agent trying to keep track of a world that is acting upon it. Be explicit about where the frame problem falls silent — in particular, whether the operators' central mistake is better described as a failure of belief revision under misleading evidence, which is not obviously a framing problem at all — rather than stretching the theory until it covers everything.
