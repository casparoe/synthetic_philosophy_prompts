For the past year I have been co-facilitating a lunchtime reading group on Stoic practice for staff at the public hospital where I work — it runs out of the employee wellness office rather than the chaplaincy, and most participants are nurses and respiratory therapists on rotating shifts. The group's unofficial motto, repeated at nearly every session and printed at the top of our handout, is "focus on what you can control." Last month the phrase did some work I found troubling. A night-shift nurse asked whether she should keep filing formal escalations about chronic understaffing, given that she had been told three times that ratios are set well above her level and that her filings were "noted." The facilitator answered that the staffing level is not up to her, so the costly thing is to keep fighting it; she should instead focus on what she can control, namely her own conduct during the shift. She looked satisfied and I did not, and I have been unable to say why. Two weeks later a different participant used the same sentence in almost the opposite direction: because his adult son's choices are not up to him, he said, nothing his son does can be a genuine harm to him, and he is therefore under no obligation to feel or do anything about it.

My own attempt at a taxonomy has three readings, and I suspect it is incomplete and that the categories overlap. First, a causal claim about which outcomes an agent's actions suffice to bring about. Second, a normative or prudential claim about where to direct effort and attention. Third, an axiological claim about what can count as good or bad for a person, on which externals are merely preferred or dispreferred rather than genuinely good or bad. I also suspect a fourth reading is doing quiet work about responsibility and blame, and I worry the first reading is simply false as stated, since almost nothing is wholly up to anyone — the middle ground of things only partly under one's control is where actual working life happens.

Could you lay out, as a menu rather than a single favored interpretation, the distinct precise theses that "focus on what you can control" might be expressing in a setting like this? For each, state it as explicitly as you can — what kind of control is meant (guarantee, contribution, sole available lever, something else), whose control, over what sort of object (token events, types of outcome, one's own judgments and actions), and assessed at what time — and then give a verdict on whether that thesis is a platitude, a substantive and defensible claim, or false. Then tell me which readings would actually license the facilitator's dismissal of the nurse's escalations, and whether any of them licenses the second participant's conclusion, flagging it if the two uses depend on readings that cannot both be held at once. I would also like the menu to engage three complications rather than bracket them: whether the dichotomy survives the observation that our own characters and judgments are themselves products of temperament and upbringing; how the ancient practice of acting with a reservation, and the related image of the archer whose end is to do everything within his power to hit the target rather than to hit it, bear on which reading a speaker is invoking; and whether replacing the dichotomy with a trichotomy — things wholly up to us, things not up to us, and things partly up to us — changes any of the verdicts. I am not after references or a reading list, just the disambiguation and your assessment of each reading; I want to bring it to the next session and possibly argue that the handout's motto should be retired, or at least rewritten.
