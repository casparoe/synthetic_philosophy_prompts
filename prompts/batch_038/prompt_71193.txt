I am preparing a session of my graduate seminar on the foundations of decision theory, in which we will discuss a chapter from a well-regarded trade-press volume on rationality that I assigned as background. The author is an accomplished popularizer rather than a specialist in the area, and the chapter contains the following summary of the coherence-theoretic tradition, which several of my students appear to have absorbed without resistance:

> Ever since Ramsey and de Finetti, decision theorists have been able to show that rationality is a matter of internal consistency. If your degrees of belief do not obey the probability axioms, a clever bookmaker can assemble a package of bets that you will accept one by one, each of them individually attractive to you, but which together guarantee you a net loss whatever way the world turns out; to hold incoherent beliefs is therefore not merely imprudent but strictly dominated. The same goes for preferences: if your choices violate transitivity or the independence axiom, you can be money-pumped, walked around a circle of trades that returns you to your original holdings while leaving you poorer. Taken together, these results show that any agent that resists such exploitation — and any agent that can be described as having beliefs and desires at all — must maximize expected utility. Note also what has not been shown: nothing here guarantees that the agent's beliefs are true or its values good. A perfectly coherent agent may be systematically mistaken about the world and monstrous in its aims, provided everything fits together. What the coherence theorems rule out is incoherence, and incoherence is the only real form of irrationality. The mathematics, in any case, is not in dispute; only its interpretation is.

Before I address this in class, I would like a careful accuracy audit, because I do not want to spend the hour correcting errors I have misidentified or, worse, denouncing claims that are in fact unobjectionable. Please work through the passage claim by claim and, for each, tell me whether a specialist in the foundations of decision theory would accept it, accept it as harmless shorthand for something more qualified, or reject it outright. In particular, I would like the following points taken up:

1. Whether the guaranteed loss described in the second sentence is unconditional, or whether it depends on premises the passage leaves implicit — the link between credences and betting dispositions, the additivity of the value of stakes, the bookmaker's access to the agent's degrees of belief — and whether "strictly dominated" is being used in anything like its technical sense.
2. Whether the sentence beginning "The same goes for preferences" treats two presentations of a single result as though they were interchangeable, and whether the exploitation of a violation of the independence axiom is as straightforward as the exploitation of intransitivity.
3. What a representation theorem in this tradition does and does not deliver about an agent's psychology, including the reading on which such a theorem establishes only that an agent behaves *as if* it maximized, and the standing worry that representation is cheap enough to be uninformative.
4. Whether the closing claim that incoherence is the only real form of irrationality follows from anything established earlier in the passage, and what it suppresses — logical omniscience, responsiveness to evidence, and those versions of the dominance arguments that are framed in terms of accuracy rather than consistency, which the passage's dichotomy between fitting together and getting the world right seems to miss entirely.
5. Whether the final sentence is fair: is it true that only the interpretation is contestable, or is there substantive, arguable content sitting in the premises of the theorems themselves?

Then tell me which single correction is most damaging — if the passage were revised to fix only one thing, which revision would most change what follows from it. My own inclination is to think that the author is broadly fair about the mathematics and unfair about what the mathematics licenses, but I would rather be told plainly if that instinct is itself too generous, in either direction.

Finally, please supply a corrected version of the passage, of comparable length and register, that a specialist could sign without wincing, so that I may hand it to the students alongside the original.
