I am applying for a six-month, full-time fellowship at an institute that funds conceptual work on the governance of emerging technologies, and I need a two-page proposal of roughly 1,200 words built around an idea I have not managed to get past a sketch.

The argument I want to work from is the one often called the unilateralist's curse. Suppose that *n* actors each independently consider undertaking an activity that is risky but potentially very beneficial, and that each forms a noisy estimate of whether the activity is on balance worth pursuing. Because the estimates are noisy, and because it takes only one actor erring in the optimistic direction for the activity to be carried out, the probability of premature or reckless action rises with the number of capable actors instead of falling, as an appeal to the wisdom of crowds would lead us to expect. The remedy usually proposed is coordination of some kind: a norm against acting alone, deference to a supermajority of informed peers, or a veto that lets any single participant halt the work.

My suspicion is that the standard remedy does not dissolve this statistical structure so much as mirror it. Under a rule on which any one of *n* actors may block, the probability that at least one blocks a genuinely worthwhile project rises with *n* by exactly the same logic, with the sign of the error reversed. Call this the veto's curse. If that is right, the quantity that matters is not how many actors there are but which error type the distribution of outcomes makes more expensive, and the practical question becomes which aggregation rule loads the selection bias onto the mistake a given field can afford to make.
