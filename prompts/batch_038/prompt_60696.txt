I cover mathematics and its discontents for the ideas desk of a national Sunday magazine, and I am currently four months into a long feature about a puzzle that I have not been able to explain to my editor in a single sentence: how working number theorists extract genuine, dependable knowledge from models they know to be false. The clearest case is the heuristic for pairs of primes. Take the prime number theorem's crude estimate that an integer near n is prime with probability about one over the natural log of n, multiply that estimate by itself to get a chance for two nearby numbers both being prime, and then repair the result with one small correction factor for every prime p at which the two numbers' residues are demonstrably not free of one another — an infinite product over the odd primes, converging to something near two-thirds, which is exactly the constant that appears in the Hardy–Littlewood conjecture. The whole edifice rests on what is effectively a presumption of independence: absent a named obstruction, treat the events as severable and multiply. And the presumption is known to be a fiction — Cramér's random model of the primes, which is the presumption made flesh, gives provably wrong answers about primes in short intervals, and sieve methods suffer a parity obstruction that no finite list of local corrections can ever repair, because the obstruction is not attached to any particular prime at all. My editor has agreed that the piece should open not with this material but with a short story that carries the same conceptual structure, and that is what I am commissioning from you.

Here is the setting I would like you to build in, though you should feel free to furnish it as you see fit. A delta city of canals, in no particular century, maintains a Bureau of Reckoning whose clerks issue likelihoods that the city's grain lenders, toll farmers, and marriage contract writers then act upon. When asked what chance two households will both be ruined in the same season, a clerk's first move is prescribed by statute: assume their fates are severable, take each household's own recorded misfortune rate, and multiply. The multiplication is licensed by a standing presumption of severance, which, like a presumption in a court, places the burden on whoever would rebut it — and rebuttal takes a fixed form, a kinship entry in the Ledger: two households draw from the same sluice, share a mill, intermarried, owe one granary. Each named kinship carries its own correction factor, derived by a clerk from a small counting argument about the shared thing, and the Bureau's estimate is the naive product times every correction on the ledger. The trouble, and the story, is a young actuary's discovery of a residual pattern that no entry can produce: along one arm of the delta, ruined and spared households alternate with a regularity that survives every kinship anyone can name, and that the Bureau's own calibration instrument — a miniature model city of lots, in which each household's fate is drawn from its own urn and is therefore severable by construction — says should be impossible.

Please write this as a story of roughly 1,600 to 2,200 words, with a title, and give me at least three clerks of genuinely opposed temperament: one for whom the ledger's estimates are true beliefs about the city, one for whom they are merely licenses to act, and one who suspects the alternating pattern is an artifact of the method's own blindness rather than a fact about the delta. Two requirements matter more to me than any other. First, the arithmetic must be shown at least once on the page, in the fiction's own terms — a clerk deriving one correction factor from a concrete counting argument about a shared sluice or a shared granary, so that a reader who knows the twin-prime heuristic recognizes exactly what is being done, and a reader who has never heard of it simply sees a person doing careful work. Second, no character may deliver an explanatory lecture, and no real mathematician, theorem, conjecture, or constant may be named anywhere in the story; I will supply the apparatus in the main text of the feature myself. End without resolving whether the alternation is an unnamed common cause, a structural limit of the presumption, or noise from too few households — but make it vivid what each of your three clerks would have to surrender in order to settle the question, and let the reader feel that the Bureau has been issuing confident numbers for a century on the strength of a default nobody ever chose.
