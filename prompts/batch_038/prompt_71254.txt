I am assembling material for a graduate workshop on the ethics of dramatizing real people, and I would like a piece of deadpan bureaucratic satire to hand out at the start of the session.

Please write a certification standard — in the house style of an ISO-type or fair-trade-type scheme, with numbered clauses, italicized defined terms, cross-references, and a scoring annex — issued by an invented industry body that audits film and television productions for their treatment of the real people they portray. Target length: roughly 800–900 words.

The document should include the following furniture:

- A Scope clause establishing that the scheme's requirements attach to a "Subject," and quietly revealing that its jurisdiction thins out the further back in time the Subject died. This gap must be handled in bureaucratic language — tiers, sunset periods, presumptions of no surviving interest — and never admitted as a gap.
- A short clause noting that Subjects who are still alive fall outside the scheme because other remedies are available to them, written so that the reader can see protection declining as the Subject recedes into the past.
- A Definitions clause attempting to separate *dramatization* from *documentary* and from *inspired by*, with those distinctions doing real work in the audit criteria and not quite holding up.
- A Requirements section containing at least one clause that awards positive credit for disclosure — a title card, an end-credit disclaimer, a note in the press kit — and at least one clause that treats the composite character as a recommended corrective measure rather than as a defect.
- Annex B: a points table with no fewer than twelve line items, each one a specific drafting choice a screenwriter actually makes (attributed speech where no record of the words survives; a death relocated to a different act or a different year; two attested persons merged into one role; interior motive supplied to a person who left no diary; a scene staged that no character present could have reported; a real person's known objection to something removed because it slowed the second act; and the like). Each item needs a point value and a severity classification, and at least two items should be scored as *credits* rather than deductions.
- A Complaints, Standing and Appeals clause specifying who may lodge a complaint on behalf of a deceased Subject, and what the scheme does when the answer is that nobody can.
- A closing notice announcing the first production to achieve the scheme's highest rating. It must be possible for an attentive reader, using Annex B and the arithmetic alone, to work out that this production earned its rating largely by inventing nearly everything, and the document must not remark on this in any way.

Three constraints on execution.

First, stay entirely in register. No authorial asides, no winking, no sentence in which the scheme doubts itself. The comedy has to come from a competent bureaucrat writing sincerely and correctly.

Second, make the satire fair and technically literate. A reader who has actually worked in conformance assessment should find the vocabulary used properly rather than as costume — nonconformity, observation, corrective action plan, surveillance audit, scope extension, grandfathering, provisional and suspended status, accreditation of the auditor by the body being audited's own membership.

Third, the clause structure should be grappling with genuine questions without ever naming them: whether the dead can be wronged at all; whether someone who died last year and someone who died four centuries ago are owed different treatment; whether descendants inherit a right of complaint and what happens when the line runs out; whether telling the audience you are taking liberties discharges the debt or merely prices it; whether merging two real people into one role is a way of being careful or a way of becoming unaccountable. None of these may be stated, and no philosophical vocabulary from these debates may appear anywhere in the piece. The reader should reach them from the shape of the requirements.

Do not use real films, real productions, real certification bodies, real standards numbers, or real people as Subjects; invent everything, and describe any invented production in generic terms. Quotation marks inside the document should appear only where the document quotes itself, its own defined terms, or a title card it is prescribing — no quoted language from any real source.
