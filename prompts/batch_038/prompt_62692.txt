I retired four years ago after thirty-five years in applied mathematics, most of it spent on the stability of large coupled systems — oscillators, control loops, that sort of thing — and I have been working my way through the history of political thought in a rather amateur fashion, partly with a reading group of former colleagues who are considerably better read than I am. I have just reached Book VI of Polybius, in the Loeb translation by W. R. Paton (1923), where he breaks off his narrative to explain the Roman constitution, and I am finding the opening pages much harder than I expected. I have set out below, with the section numbers, the chunk I am stuck on.

[6.2.8–10] "What chiefly attracts and chiefly benefits students of history is just this — the study of causes and the consequent power of choosing what is best in each case. Now the chief cause of success or the reverse in all matters is the form of a state's constitution; for springing from this, as from a fountain-head, all designs and plans of action not only originate, but reach their consummation." [6.3.1–3] "In the case of those Greek states which have often risen to greatness and have often experienced a complete change of fortune, it is an easy matter both to describe their past and to pronounce as to their future. For there is no difficulty in reporting the known facts, and it is not hard to foretell the future by inference from the past. But about the Roman state it is neither at all easy to explain the present situation owing to the complicated character of the constitution, nor to foretell the future owing to our ignorance of the peculiar features of public and private life at Rome in the past." [6.3.7] "For it is evident that we must regard as the best constitution a combination of all these three varieties, since we have had proof of this not only theoretically but by actual experience, Lycurgus having been the first to draw up a constitution — that of Sparta — on this principle." [6.4.2] "It is by no means every monarchy which we can call straight off a kingship, but only that which is voluntarily accepted by the subjects and where they are governed rather by an appeal to their reason than by fear and force." [6.4.5] "Similarly that is no true democracy in which the whole crowd of citizens is free to do whatever they wish or purpose, but when, in a community where it is traditional and customary to reverence the gods, to honour our parents, to respect our elders, and to obey the laws, the will of the greater number prevails, this is to be called a democracy." [6.4.6–9] "We should therefore assert that there are six kinds of governments, the three above mentioned which are in everyone's mouth and the three which are naturally allied to them, I mean monarchy, oligarchy, and mob-rule. Now the first of these to come into being is monarchy, its growth being natural and unaided; and next arises kingship derived from monarchy by the aid of art and by the correction of defects. Monarchy first changes into its vicious allied form, tyranny; and next, the abolishment of both gives birth to aristocracy. Aristocracy by its very nature degenerates into oligarchy;"

My difficulties are three, and they are all about the argumentative structure rather than the vocabulary. First, the foretelling claim at 6.3.1–3 strikes me as odd. If the succession of constitutions really is a regular process, one would expect the mixed constitution to be the most predictable case of all, since it is the one whose parts are supposed to hold each other in check; yet Polybius says precisely that Rome is the case where the future cannot be foretold, while the small Greek states — which have "often risen to greatness and have often experienced a complete change of fortune" — are easy to project forward. Is he making a point about evidence, namely that projection is licensed only where one has watched the same process iterate many times, and that Rome has not iterated? Or is he saying something stronger, that the mixed constitution is not governed by the same transition rule as the simple ones, and is therefore a different kind of object rather than a better-sampled one? I should like to know which reading the wording supports, and what turns on the difference. Second, at 6.4.7 he says monarchy grows "naturally and unaided" while kingship arises "by the aid of art and by the correction of defects," and at 6.3.7 he says the combination of the three is proved "not only theoretically but by actual experience," instancing Lycurgus. I cannot tell what epistemic work Lycurgus is doing there — an existence proof, a causal validation, or merely an illustration — and I cannot tell whether "natural" versus "artificial" here marks a difference in stability. That is the question my reading group thinks I am importing from my own field: whether a natural form is one that arises when nothing intervenes and an artificial one is a state that must be continuously maintained against drift, so that the mixed constitution would be a maintained equilibrium rather than a genuinely stable one, and the cycle would be slowed rather than arrested. Tell me plainly whether the passage licenses that distinction or whether I am reading a modern idea into "art." Third, the criteria at 6.4.2 and 6.4.5 look to me less like descriptions than like definitions: a kingship is rule accepted voluntarily and by appeal to reason rather than fear and force; a democracy is majority rule in a community that reverences the gods, honours parents and elders, and obeys the laws. If those are constitutive, then any regime that decays into tyranny or mob-rule was, strictly, never a kingship or a democracy at all — which would seem to make the classification immune to counterexample, since the bad cases get re-labelled rather than counted against the good ones. Are these definitions or diagnostic tests, does the sequence at 6.4.7–9 follow from them or from observation, and is the vice in each pair a matter of the same features intensified or of a wholly different mechanism, consent being replaced by fear? I should also note an impression of mine that may be wrong: I believe Polybius elsewhere assigns a good deal of causal work to chance, and I cannot see how that sits with the "fountain-head" language at 6.2.9–10, where the constitution is called the chief cause of everything a state designs and does.
