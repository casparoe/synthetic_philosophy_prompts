Last spring the engineering firm I work for converted to full employee ownership, and since January I have sat on its governing board. I have been asked to write the foreword to our second annual ownership report; what began as a foreword has grown into a 3,000-word essay for a general-interest outlet, arguing through Mill's late writing on socialism. Below are my notes and the drafted sections. I would like substantive comments rather than encouragement.

**Section 1 — The shift usually read as softening is in fact a hardening.** Mill's late treatment of property (the chapters drafted in 1869 and published after his death in 1879, together with his two-part 1869 review of Thornton on labor, in which he defended trade union combinations and looked favorably on cooperative production, and his chairmanship of the Land Tenure Reform Association, whose programme he drafted) is standardly described as a retreat from principle to expediency: he stops asking whether existing holdings are just and asks only whether they are useful. I think this conflates two things Mill separates — the *grounds* on which property is defended and the *presumption* attached to those grounds. Once property is defended on public-welfare grounds alone — he writes in the 1869 drafts that for the newly enfranchised "the laws of property have to depend for support upon considerations of a public nature, upon the estimate made of their conduciveness to the general welfare" — the conservative's advantage disappears. The status quo holds no default entitlement; it must re-earn its title whenever the evidence about consequences changes. What looks like a weakening of the argument is a symmetrizing of the burden of proof.

**Section 2 — The draft discount.** Almost everything we call Mill's late socialism is unpublished draft material. Helen Taylor's preliminary notice to the 1879 chapters says plainly that they are "the first rough drafts thrown down towards the foundation of that work," a work Mill never lived to write. Scholars nevertheless quote the drafts as settled doctrine and then charge Mill with contradicting the published *Principles*. I propose an explicit weighting rule: (i) drafts can establish the direction of Mill's thinking but not its settled content; (ii) where a draft conflicts with a passage Mill revised across editions, the revision wins unless corroborated by an independently dated item — letters, speeches, association programmes; (iii) the discount is not uniform: it is steepest for institutional claims (how a commune would be governed) and shallowest for evaluative claims (what property is for).

**Section 3 — The pedagogical reading, and why it cuts against state socialism.** The distributive reading of late Mill strikes me as anachronistic. His recurring complaint about the wages relation is that it makes adults dependent on the will of an employer; his recurring hope for cooperative production is that running an enterprise together is a school in the capacities self-government requires — deliberation, foresight, mutual accounting. Institutions are character-formation devices first and allocation devices second. That is what makes Mill a socialist about workshops and a skeptic about ministries: both replace many masters with one, but only one hands the worker the syllabus. The stationary state is where this converges — with accumulation no longer the engine of progress, the question becomes what people do with themselves, and Mill's answer is that they had better be people who have practiced governing something.

**Section 4 — The tension I cannot resolve.** If the point is character formation, trial by experiment follows. Yet the notice describes his ambition as applying tested theories "without prolongation of suffering on the one hand, or unnecessary disturbance on the other," which sounds like a brake on precisely those experiments. I currently argue that Mill's line between permissible experiments and impermissible disturbances is temperamental rather than principled, and I suspect that is unfair to him.

**Section 5 — Contemporary application.** Whether the draft discount plus the pedagogical reading predicts anything: conversion schemes that transfer equity but leave governance intact — shares without a syllabus — should deliver Mill's benefits only weakly, which would fit the mixed productivity evidence for employee ownership in firms without real participation mechanisms.

Please comment on the following, in American English:

1. Accuracy. Flag misattributions, misdatings, and overstatements about Mill, especially in Sections 1 and 3. Am I right about what the Thornton review and the Land Tenure Reform Association programme actually commit him to? Where would a Mill scholar say I have leaned on a phrase whose context undercuts my use of it?
2. Novelty. Are the burden-of-proof symmetrizing claim and the draft discount worth the space, or well-worn? If well-worn, say what nearby claim is not.
3. Clause (iii) of Section 2. Does weighting institutional claims more steeply than evaluative ones smuggle in the conclusion I want — that Mill's late value theory is stable while his institutional proposals are provisional?
4. Section 4. Which way should I resolve the tension, and what is the strongest available case that Mill's gradualism is principled?
5. Cuts. What goes, assuming a general audience and 3,000 words? I am inclined to cut Section 5, and would like to know whether you agree.
