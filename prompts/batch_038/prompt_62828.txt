ok so my friend and I had a three hour argument last night about Memento and I still haven't cooled down, so help me settle it — or tell me I was being an idiot. You're a philosopher of cognitive science who actually knows the predictive processing / free energy literature (Friston, Hohwy, Andy Clark's Surfing Uncertainty) rather than someone who skimmed one pop-sci summary of it, and I want you to think carefully and take both the film and the theory seriously. No reading lists, no bibliography, no "if you want to go deeper check out X" — I just want the analysis. And don't quote the movie back at me, I've seen it six times, I know the lines.

Where we landed: I claimed the whole film is basically a case study in what happens to a predictive system that can't accumulate prediction error across time. Leonard can't lay down new episodic memories, so he can't revise the model that's driving what he expect to see next; instead he outsources his priors to the physical world — the Polaroids with the little captions, the notes in his coat pockets, the tattoos. And the ending is what makes it philosophically nasty instead of just sad: he knows. Teddy tells him a great deal, some of it verifiable and some of it not (including the hint that the Sammy Jankis story is really Leonard's own, the insulin and his wife), and Leonard hears all of it, and then deliberately sets up his future self. He writes down Teddy's plate as the clue, writes himself an instruction not to trust Teddy, takes Jimmy's clothes and car, burns the last of his wife's things. So the next version of him wakes up with corrupted top-down predictions and, crucially, no machinery left to detect that they're corrupted. The handwriting looks like objective evidence to future-Leonard and only present-Leonard knows where it came from.

My friend's pushback was two prongs. (a) I'm just draping a neuroscience framework over a thriller and the film isn't about prediction error at all. (b) It doesn't matter anyway, because predictive processing and the free energy principle are somewhere near unfalsifiable — they can absorb any story you tell them — so pointing at Leonard and saying "see, priors" isn't evidence for anything, it's just a vocabulary.

Here's what I want from you:

1. Start with brainstorming out loud before you commit to a reading. List the moments where a PP reading genuinely bites, and separately list the ones where it obviously doesn't and I'd be forcing it. Be honest — if large parts of the film are not about this, say which parts and why.

2. Then do the actual reading. What I care about most is precision. Leonard hears a detailed, partly corroborated account from Teddy and doesn't update. In PP terms what has to be going wrong — is the prior just too strong, or has the precision on incoming evidence collapsed, or is it a third thing? Is this the same shape as the aberrant-salience / delusion accounts, where the problem isn't the content of the prediction but how much weight the system gives to the error signal? And does Leonard differ from a delusional patient in a way that matters, given that he can't carry the error signal forward at all?

3. Active inference. I kept saying the ending is active inference gone as wrong as it can go: instead of updating the model to fit the world, he rearranges the world so that future evidence fits the model. And there's the bit where he explicitly chooses to have a purpose over finding out the truth, which I think is the epistemic-value versus pragmatic-value tradeoff in expected free energy, made into a scene. Does that reading hold up, or am I just relabeling a moral choice as a computational one and calling it insight?

4. The extended mind contrast. His notes and tattoos look like the Otto's-notebook move from Clark and Chalmers, but I argued they're not the same thing: Otto's notebook is external memory, whereas Leonard's system is external prior — it doesn't just store what he learned, it dictates what he'll expect. Is that a real distinction or a verbal one? Can PP accommodate artifacts that carry priors, or does it quietly assume priors are skull-bound?

Leonard is enormously confident and almost never surprised, which is arguably the opposite of what you'd want from a well-functioning predictive machine.

6. Take the unfalsifiability prong head on. Is there anything about Memento that would count against a PP reading of it? If nothing could, does that make the reading worthless, or just a different kind of thing than an empirical claim?
