I teach an upper-level seminar on practical rationality and a colleague pointed me at a chapter in a new anthology that opens with a summary of the population ethics debate. I'd like to assign it, but I'm a metaphysician, not a value theorist, and I can't tell how careful it is. Here's the opening paragraph, typed up from the book:

"Parfit's mere addition argument is a paradox about the transitivity of 'better than.' He starts from a world of happy people and notes that adding more people whose lives are worth living makes it better; repeated, that step forces us to accept that a vast world of lives barely worth living is better than a small world of excellent lives. Since nobody accepts that, Parfit concludes we have to give up transitivity. Temkin and Rachels made the same point with a continuum of cases: if a thousand tiny improvements can't take you from a mediocre life to a wonderful one, then tiny improvements don't matter, which is absurd. The upshot, now the consensus view, is that welfare can't be measured on a single scale — so when two outcomes contain different people, there's simply no fact of the matter about which is better."

Second, the last sentence: does that follow from anything true earlier in the paragraph, or is a much bigger thesis being smuggled in as a conclusion?
