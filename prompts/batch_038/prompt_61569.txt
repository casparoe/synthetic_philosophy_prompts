Molinism is standardly presented as a three-stage account of divine knowledge. By natural knowledge God knows all necessary truths together with the entire space of possible creatures and circumstances; by middle knowledge God knows, prior to any decree of the divine will, every true counterfactual of creaturely freedom — every conditional of the form *if this possible creature were instantiated and placed in these complete circumstances, it would freely perform this action*; and by free knowledge God knows, subsequent to his decree, which of those possibilities he has chosen to actualize. The providential payoff is supposed to be decisive: since God knows what every possible free creature would do in any situation, he can select a set of circumstances in which creatures freely cooperate with his purposes, weakly actualizing the world he wants without causing a single free act. The principal difficulty is the grounding objection: if such a counterfactual is true before God wills anything and before the relevant creature exists or acts, what could make it true? Its truth appears to float free of any truthmaker, and if the creature is genuinely able to do otherwise, there seems to be no fact of the matter for God's knowledge to latch onto.

I am evaluating an unorthodox response that concedes the grounding objection while claiming to preserve Molinism's providential advantage. Call it the capacity account of middle knowledge. Its commitments are these:

1. There are no truths of the counterfactual-of-creaturely-freedom form. Such conditionals are systematically neither true nor false.
2. Nevertheless, prior to any decree, God possesses a non-propositional practical capacity — divine know-how rather than a body of know-that — whose exercise yields, for each creative option he contemplates, an ordering of the outcomes he should expect. The relation of this capacity to God's planning is meant to be like the relation of a master player's read on an unfamiliar opponent to that player's choice of move: it issues in expectations and dispositions to act, not in a database of conditionals.
3. Omniscience is knowing all that there is to know. Since the conditionals in (1) are not true, there is nothing there for God to know, so his not knowing them is no defect. God never believes a falsehood about the future, because his expectations are held with calibrated confidence rather than as certainties.
4. God can nonetheless be disappointed: creatures sometimes do what he did not expect. Providence is therefore consummate risk-management rather than guarantee.

Please compare this capacity account with classical middle knowledge, addressing the following:

(a) Is the difference between the two substantive, or is the capacity account simply open theism redescribed in the vocabulary of skill? Specify what would have to differ between them for the two to count as genuine rivals rather than differently worded versions of the same view.

(b) On reliability: a capacity that reliably yields expectations appears to be correct or incorrect about something. If its exercises can be right or wrong, is there not a standard they answer to — and is that standard not precisely the counterfactuals the account denies? Can the capacity account treat its exercises as merely more or less successful without thereby being answerable to any fact, or does it smuggle back in the truths it rejects?

(c) On providence: trace both accounts through a concrete case in which God wishes to bring it about that one particular person freely repents. What does each account say God can and cannot do, what becomes of weak actualization, and which, if either, can sustain the claim that God's purposes cannot ultimately be frustrated?

(d) Situate the capacity account relative to the Thomist alternative, on which God's knowledge of the free act rests on his causing that act by a motion which infallibly elicits it in a manner congruent with the creature's own mode of acting. Is the capacity account a genuine third position between Molinism and physical premotion, or does it collapse into one of them?

(e) Conclude with a reasoned verdict: which account of middle knowledge is more defensible overall, and does the capacity account retain any right to the name Molinism?

Argue from the concepts and commitments themselves rather than by appeal to authority; no citations or references are required.
