I'm putting together a handout for an internal workshop — audience is mostly people who work on LLM evals, engineers with one or two philosophy-adjacent folks mixed in — and what I want is a decision tree that sorts them on what the lottery paradox and the preface paradox actually show. Not a little explainer on the paradoxes; an instrument. They answer the questions, and at the bottom they find out which position they've been holding, including positions nobody in the room has heard of.

The hard constraint is that no question can be answerable from jargon. Not "do you accept the Lockean thesis?" but something more like "a claim is 99.7% likely on the best evidence you have and nothing you already believe counts against it — do you believe it, flat out, yes or no?" Same for the rest: every node should be a case or a plain claim a person can react to without knowing which way it points.

What I need the tree to do:

- Start from the shape of the problem (lots of individually well-supported claims that can't all be true together) and only then start branching. The branches should be the moves that genuinely divide people: whether flat belief is closed under conjunction, whether there's a probability threshold for belief at all or whether that whole picture is wrong, whether the norm on belief is truth or something looser like justification or usefulness, whether the preface author is making a mistake or is perfectly fine and just shouldn't print the contradiction, whether the honest fix is to stop doing belief-talk and work in degrees, and whether a belief set can contain a real inconsistency without everything falling apart.
- Include at least one node that isn't a lottery ticket or a book. I've got two I like, swap or add as you see fit: (1) a monitoring dashboard that throws off something like 800 alerts a day, and the on-call engineer believes each one when she sees it and acts on it, while also knowing from six months of history that some chunk of them were wrong; and (2) a distributed version — a 30-person team where each person confidently believes their one item on a shared checklist is fine, and everybody knows from past audits that at least one item on the list was not fine. That second one is the sneaky one. I want a node that straight-up asks whether the inconsistent set has to live inside a single head, and I want the tree to separate the leaves that care about that from the ones that don't. Someone who thinks the lone author is blameless but the team case is a genuine problem ought to land somewhere different from someone who treats the two cases the same.
- Every leaf gets a name if one exists (non-closed rational acceptance, the degrees-only / no-threshold view, contextualism, relevant alternatives, whatever else you think belongs in there) and "unnamed" if it doesn't, plus one sentence on the most awkward thing the view has to swallow. If two leaves can't really be told apart by your questions, say so rather than papering over it.
- After the tree, give me the logical space behind it: list the combinations of answers your questions permit, mark which are occupied, and for the empty ones say whether they're empty because they're incoherent, because they're just unattractive, or because nobody has bothered to look there. My hunch is there's dead space on the closure-plus-no-threshold side and I want to know whether that's a real constraint or an accident of how I'm thinking about it.

Aim for somewhere around seven to ten questions, and keep the whole thing usable on a single page. American English please. And no bibliography or suggested reading at the end — the engineers won't look any of it up and I wouldn't be able to check it anyway.
