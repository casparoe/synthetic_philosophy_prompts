I keep circling back to the Brontosaurus story as a test case for the Copycat / fluid-concepts picture, and I honestly can't tell whether it's a big win for that view or whether applying the view to it is just a category mistake. Facts as I've got them — flag anything I've botched, but please don't let the answer hinge on the details:

- Marsh named Apatosaurus ajax in 1877 from Colorado material and Brontosaurus excelsus in 1879 from a much more complete skeleton out of Wyoming. This was peak Bone Wars, him and Cope rushing descriptions and names out the door, so lots of taxa got erected on thin evidence.
- In 1903 Elmer Riggs argued there weren't enough differences between the two animals to keep two genera, and since Apatosaurus had been named first, priority decided it: Brontosaurus excelsus became Apatosaurus excelsus and the genus name was officially dead.
- For something like the next 110 years paleontologists didn't use the name at all, while the public never stopped using it — films, museum mounts, corporate logos, a US postage stamp. Two different concepts with the same word, basically, running in parallel for a century.
- Then in 2015 a group working on the diplodocid family tree ran a specimen-level analysis — 81 skeletons, 477 anatomical characters — and concluded Brontosaurus is distinct after all, mostly on build and neck robustness. It's still contested: critics say the choice of which characters matter is subjective, there's no standard for it, and the Apatosaurus type material was never properly described.

The Copykit pitch I want to apply is the one from Hofstadter and Melanie Mitchell: concepts aren't containers with sharp edges, they're nodes in a Slipnet where activation spreads to neighbours weighted by conceptual distance; there's a Workspace where objects get built into descriptions out of whatever's at hand; little codelets propose structures and propose slips; and a cognitive temperature tracks how disorganized things are, which controls how far a slip you'll tolerate — cold and conservative, hot and radical. And the strong claim underneath it: categorization just is analogy-making, so every time you call something a member of a category you've done a little analogical leap, and the boundaries are gradient and context-dependent all the way down.

So, three things.

First, build the mapping properly rather than waving at it. What plays the Workspace here and what plays the Slipnet? Who or what are the codelets — is it individual paleontologists, journal reviewers, the naming commission, the phylogenetic software? What is the object being categorized: the physical skeleton, the species, the name, or the mental picture of a long-necked dinosaur that a kid has? Sketch the local Slipnet and the conceptual distances in it — Brontosaurus to Apatosaurus vs. Brontosaurus to Diplodocus vs. Brontosaurus to the vague popular node thunder-lizard — and say where the temperature is high and where it's low. My hunch is the the Bone Wars were a hot regime (cheap slippage, everyone minting genera) and 1903 was a cooling, with 2015 a small reheating, but push on that rather than just agreeing with me.

Second, and this is the part I actually care about: where does the model break? Three candidates I can't sort out myself. (a) Nothing about the animal ever changed, so in what sense did a concept slip rather than a rule just get applied? Both the 1903 and the 2015 moves were driven by nomenclatural priority and by thresholds over character counts, not by perceived similarity — and Copycat has no slot for an external rulebook that legislates extension regardless of how similar things look. (b) The specialist concept and the public concept came apart for a century. Does the architecture have any way to represent two Slipnets that disagree about the same node, and what would it predict about which one wins, given that the public one demonstrably survived the official extinction? (c) Copycat is one mind over a few seconds; this is a community over 140 years. Is stretching the machinery that far a legitimate extension or a straight equivocation on the word concept?

Third, run it backwards. Does the episode confirm the fluid-concepts thesis — the extension of Brontosaurus went empty, stayed empty for a century, and then got refilled, which feels like about the strongest available evidence that concepts aren't fixed — or does it expose a case the thesis never anticipated, namely a concept whose boundaries get set by authority, procedure and convention instead of by similarity, which is not what Copycat was built to model? Tell me which of (a), (b), (c) you think is actually fatal versus which are just gaps, and what you'd have to bolt onto the architecture to handle it. I'd rather you say the theory is silent somewhere than force it to speak.
