I sit on the eleven-member board of a private foundation devoted to coral-reef resilience, and our program officers score every grant proposal in a single currency: expected hectares of living coral cover surviving to the year 2100, discounted to the present. In three weeks the board must rule on a pilot proposal in "assisted gene flow" — moving heat-tolerant coral genotypes from a warm-water basin into a cooler recipient system so that the recipient population acquires thermal tolerance it does not currently have. Staff estimate a 99% chance of +300 hectares and a 1% chance of −20,000 hectares, the latter representing the introduction of a pathogen that collapses the recipient system and, through larval connectivity, two adjacent systems; the loss is treated as irreversible on any relevant timescale. The conventional alternative on the table for the same money — local nursery outplanting plus shading of nursery stock — is estimated at a certain +80 hectares. Straight expected-value maximization therefore favours the pilot by 17 hectares. One trustee, who works on decision theory, has proposed that we instead evaluate proposals by a rank-dependent, risk-weighted rule of the kind defended in Lara Buchak's *Risk and Rationality* (Oxford, 2013), using a quadratic risk function — the cumulative probability is squared — while keeping utility linear in hectares. Our analyst reports that under that rule the certain option wins by roughly 180 hectares, and that the mechanism is that the quadratic function approximately doubles the effective weight on the 1% catastrophic branch rather than reducing the weight on the good branch. Two trustees have said they will challenge the arithmetic at the meeting, so the memo must show its work.

Several complications are in play, and I would like all of them addressed rather than waved away. First, our CFO argues that the foundation's investment committee already practises mean-variance risk aversion with the endowment, so the grants side should do the same; a second trustee replies that if we want risk aversion we should simply adopt a concave utility function over hectares, which preserves expected-value reasoning; a third insists that concavity over hectares is a claim about the diminishing value of hectares — a distributive view about how much the three-hundredth hectare matters relative to the first — and not an attitude toward risk at all, so adopting it would smuggle in substantive ethics under a technical label. Second, the CFO also invokes the classic public-investment result associated with Arrow and Lind: when a project's risks are spread thinly across a large population of bearers, the aggregate risk premium vanishes and the public investor should behave as if risk-neutral. But here the loss does not spread — it lands on one reef system and the species that depend on it, while the benefits accrue largely to fishing communities elsewhere — and we make perhaps a dozen decisions of this kind per decade rather than thousands per year, so there is little across which to pool. Third, some trustees suspect that our real problem is not risk but ambiguity: the 1% is not a frequency drawn from any repeatable process but a considered judgment, and if that is right, then an explicitly robust treatment — say, evaluating a proposal under a range of plausible probability assignments and requiring it to look acceptable across that range — may be the honest instrument, with rank-dependence merely dressing up a different worry in decision-theoretic clothing. Fourth, there is the question of whose attitudes should count: of the five island communities consulted, three support the release and two do not, and the nonhuman residents of the reef cannot be consulted at all; it is not obvious that deferring to the consulted communities settles what we ought to do, since we and not they are the ones choosing. Fifth, if we adopt a rank-dependent rule we give up the sure-thing principle, and someone will ask whether we are prepared to accept a sequence of offers across successive funding rounds that leaves us worse off than we started.

Please draft the decision memo I will circulate to the board beforehand. It should recommend a concrete decision rule for the grants program — not merely a ruling on this one proposal — together with a ruling on the pilot, and it should give program officers criteria they can actually apply when the next high-stakes pilot arrives. I want the strongest consideration on the losing side treated honestly, not summarily dismissed, and I want the recommendation to be one a trustee who thinks in expected values could accept as a fair statement of the case against her view. Organize the memo in exactly these sections, in this order: (1) "Bottom line" — the recommended rule and the ruling on the pilot, at most 120 words; (2) "The arithmetic, checked" — recompute both options under both rules and confirm or correct the claim that the quadratic risk function roughly doubles the effective weight on the 1% branch, about 200 words; (3) "What the rule is actually doing" — how rank-dependence differs from a concave utility function and from mean-variance practice, about 200 words; (4) "The case for a risk-weighted rule," about 250 words; (5) "The case against it," about 250 words, covering the pooling point, the ambiguity point, and the sequential-consistency worry; (6) "Recommended policy and a screening test" — the rule plus the criteria program officers should apply, including how to handle reversibility, whether the risk pools, whose risk attitudes count, and how wide the disagreement over probabilities is, about 300 words; and (7) "What would change our minds," about 150 words. Aim for roughly 1,500 words in total. No bibliography or citations are needed — I care about the reasoning, not the references — and please write for trustees who have no decision theory beyond the arithmetic shown, without sanding off the technical distinctions that do the work.
