I'm on the decomposition review panel at an AI lab — we sign off (or don't) on any run where a team plans to build a big "amplified" pipeline out of many model calls, have humans or checkers supervise the pieces, and then distill the whole thing down into a single trained model. Our charter says we're suppose to verify that the process preserves both alignment and competence, but it's silent on which of the two gets veto power, and honestly the six rulings we've issued so far don't settle it either.

Our record:

1. **Approved** — content-policy classification, decomposed into seven fixed stages (pull out the claims, locate the relevant policy clause, compare, draft the rationale, and so on). Every stage emitted a short piece of text a non-expert contractor could score against a written rubric without seeing any of the other stages. Then distilled into one model.
2. **Rejected** — strategic forecasting, decomposed into roughly forty steps, but the human overseers only ever rated the *final* forecast for plausibility. The intermediates were numeric, and when we pressed, two overseers admitted they'd basically defer to whatever the model produced.
3. **Approved with conditions** — proof search. Intermediates were checked by an automated proof checker rather than a person. Conditions: the checker's soundness audited by someone outside the team, and the distillation gap measured and reported back to us.
4. **Rejected** — a pipeline in which one stage could rewrite the decomposition mid-run, adding or deleting stages as it saw fit. Our write-up said we couldn't say what system we were approving.
5. **Approved** — summarization of legal filings. The distilled model came in about eight points *below* the amplified pipeline on the team's own benchmark. We approved because the shortfall was measured, disclosed in the model card, and the distilled model was indistinguishable from the amplified one on a battery of honesty/manipulation probes.
6. **Rejected** — a team labeled it "amplification," but it was just five identical copies voting, with no decomposition at all. We said replicating one judgment five times creates no new oversight surface, and a majority vote can produce an answer that none of the copies could defend step by step.

Thursday's proposal is automated red-teaming: find ways to get a target model to emit disallowed content. Structure is nine fixed stages, each emitting natural-language intermediates (candidate attack strings, per-stage critiques, and finally a ranked list of vulnerabilities with severity scores). Trained contractors score each intermediate against a written rubric and never need to see the other stages. Two wrinkles. First, our own staff flagged that the *final* ranked list can't be checked by the contractors — they can tell you whether each stage looks rubric-conformant, but not whether the list is correct or complete, since verifying that would mean actually running the attacks, which is the whole point of the pipeline. Second, stage nine is allowed to propose exactly one extra stage to insert ahead of itself, drawn from a pre-approved menu of six stage types the team has already documented for us. Not free-form rewriting: bounded, single insertion, menu-constrained.

Pilot data: the distilled single model finds about 30% fewer real vulnerabilities than the amplified pipeline. But the amplified pipeline issued a false all-clear twice during the pilot (reported "no issues found" against a target where working attacks existed), and the distilled model has issued zero. The team's argument is that a false all-clear is a safety failure rather than a mere competence failure, so their distillation is a net safety win even though it's worse at the task.

So — what should we decide if we want to be consistent with ourselves? I'm particularly unsure whether "false all-clear" belongs in the alignment column or the competence column, and whether that classification is even something our precedents determine or whether the team is just picking the frame that flatters them.

Write it up as something I can paste straight into the agenda packet, with these sections in this order:

- **Bottom line** — 3–4 sentences: approve, reject, or approve with conditions, plus the single consideration that decides it.
- **The rules our record could be running** — about 250 words. Give me at least three candidate rules, run each against all six precedents, and flag where a rule only works if it's paired with another one.
- **What each rule says about the red-team proposal** — about 200 words.
- **Closest analog** — about 100 words: which of the six, and what work the disanalogy is doing.
- **What we'd be committing to** — about 150 words: whichever way we go, what principle we're locking in for the next proposal that looks like this one, and the strongest honest case for the side you're voting against.
- **The case that would settle it** — about 75 words: sketch a hypothetical future proposal that our precedents handle identically but the candidate rules split on, so we'd know we're choosing a rule and not just ruling on this run.
