Second-year ethics PhD student here, co-advised by someone in conservation biology, and I've been asked to write a comment on a white paper that proposes gene-drive suppression of a couple of high-fecundity, heavily predated vertebrate populations on wild animal suffering grounds. A colleague of mine — smart, not a crank — handed me the following in defense of the radical reading of the drive, and I cant find where it breaks. It's supposed to start from the procreation asymmetry, which I basically accept, and end somewhere I definitely don't want to be.

(1) The presence of pain in a life is bad for the one whose life it is.
(2) The presence of pleasure in a life is good for the one whose life it is.
(3) The absence of pain is good even when there is nobody around to enjoy that absence.
(4) The absence of pleasure is not bad unless there is someone for whom that absence is a deprivation.
(5) So for any life containing some mix of pain and pleasure, things are better if that life is never lived than if it is.
(6) Death is not a deprivation of the one who dies: once she's gone there's no subject left for the missing pleasures to be missing for.
(7) So by (4) and (6) the absence of a dead person's future pleasures isn't bad, while by (3) the absence of her future pains is good — a painless death is a net improvement in how things are, not a loss.
(8) Hence we have no reason to prevent a painless death we could prevent, and a standing reason to prefer fewer sentient beings to more, whether we get there by not procreating, by letting populations crash, or by painless means.
(9) Hence the drive is not merely permissible but required, at any cost to biodiversity, ecosystem function, or the preferences of people who like the animals in question, since none of those count under (3).
(10) If the asymmetry really is (1)–(4), I can't see which of these to drop, and I don't want to drop (1)–(4) because that's literally my dissertation.

Which step do you cut — or, if you think the chain is valid, which premise is cheapest to abandon? Answer in exactly five numbered points, a sentence or two each, with no preamble and no closing summary: the step you reject, why that step looked innocent when I wrote it out, the minimal repair to the argument, what the repair costs, and whether the repaired version still gets me the asymmetry I actually want (procreation is a different matter from rescue and killing). Assume I've seen the usual objections to antinatalism, so don't spend one of your five points telling me the asymmetry is controversial — I want the mechanics of this particular chain.
