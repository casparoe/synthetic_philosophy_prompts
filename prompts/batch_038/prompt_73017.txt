The association I belong to has a standards panel — six elected members, on which I have now served two years — that decides complaints alleging a member's public statement was one they should not have made: testimony, a newspaper interview, a remark at a community meeting, a claim in a trade publication. We rule first on breach, then separately on sanction, and we have several times found a breach while sanctioning no one. We have issued six rulings, and they are now the record I am expected to reason from.

1. A member wrote in a county newsletter that a lake's algal blooms were being driven by fertilizer runoff from the surrounding farms. She had three seasons of her own sampling and a published loading model, the claim was true, and she believed it. Complaint dismissed.

2. A member told a reporter that a landfill was contaminating a school's well, on the strength of a smell at the fence line and one unverified home test kit. She believed it, and it was true: the state confirmed the contamination fourteen months later. Complaint upheld, formal censure. The minute records that she "spoke as one who had checked when she had not."

3. A member testified to a state committee that a pesticide was safe at labeled application rates, relying on two peer-reviewed studies she had read closely and the regulator's own published review. She believed it. A larger independent study the following year found harm at those rates. Complaint dismissed, with one member dissenting on the ground that a statement which turns out false breaches the standard however carefully it was made.

4. A member told a televised town meeting that a plant's emissions posed "no health risk to families in the district." Her basis was the operator's compliance reports, which she had not audited and which a residents' group had publicly disputed; the reports were accurate as far as they went, but did not cover a fugitive-emissions pathway identified six weeks later. Roughly two hundred families acted on what she said. Complaint upheld, no sanction. The minute records that "the evidence she had was the evidence the operator had chosen to give her."

5. A member testified that a municipal water supply met every applicable standard and was safe to drink, on the strength of an analysis she had commissioned and could document line by line. It was true and fully disclosable; privately, for reasons connected to her own child's illness, she did not believe it, and said it flatly anyway. Complaint dismissed, with one member dissenting on the ground that she had asserted what she did not believe.

6. Asked during a contamination scare whether the town's water might be affected, a member holding a single anomalous sample said: "I can't rule it out, we don't have the data yet, and if you are on a private well you should boil it until the county reports." The water was in fact affected. A resident complained that she had alarmed a town on almost no evidence. Complaint dismissed.

I have tried to write down what we are doing as one of the obvious rules — assert only what is true; assert only what you know; assert only what you believe; assert only what you reasonably believe — and each of the first three is flatly contradicted by at least one ruling above. The fourth I cannot make fit either, though I cannot say precisely where it breaks.

The complaint we hear next month is this. A certified drinking-water microbiologist of twenty years' experience, asked on camera at a public meeting during an active scare whether the water in a town of four thousand was safe to drink, said: "The water is safe to drink." She had collected samples herself that morning at four points in the distribution system and run the standard culture method on all of them, with clean results, within her lab's accreditation. She knew, and did not say, that this method has a documented false-negative rate of about one in two hundred at low contamination levels and detects only the organisms on the standard panel. She posted the raw results to the town's website that evening and offered to walk anyone through them; nothing was confidential. Most of the people in the room would decide that night whether to drink the water. Three days later a different laboratory, using a molecular method, found the organism; eleven people were hospitalized. She believed what she said when she said it, and the complaint alleges that she asserted more than her evidence supported.

Please give me the following. First, the rules that are jointly consistent with all six precedents — not the ones I have been assuming, but whatever actually fits — each stated precisely enough to yield a determinate verdict on an unseen case, together with the precedent each rule is doing the most work to explain and the precedent that is most awkward for it. Second, what each surviving rule implies about the new complaint. Third, your recommendation on how I should vote, on breach and on sanction separately, identifying the closest analog among the six: I keep landing on precedent 3 and precedent 4 as the rival candidates and cannot decide between them, so tell me which is closer and why, and what turns on that choice. Fourth, if the record genuinely underdetermines the case, say so plainly rather than manufacturing a deduction, and tell me what principle we would be adopting by deciding either way — what it would commit us to in complaints none of us has yet seen, and what it would put permanently beyond complaint. Fifth, one further complaint, described concretely, that would most sharply reveal which rule we have in fact been applying, with each rule's predicted verdict on it. And throughout, flag the places where you are supplying a judgment rather than reading the record, including whether our habit of finding breaches without sanctions is innocent bookkeeping or a substantive commitment.

If there are standard names in the literature for the positions you identify, use them, but I do not want a bibliography or attributions. Begin your answer with your recommended verdict — breach or no breach, sanction or no sanction — in the first sentence, and then give the reasoning; no preamble and no restatement of the above.
