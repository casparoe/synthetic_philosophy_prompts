I read a long piece on a train last week, from one of those magazine special issues on whether the Enlightenment was a mistake, and it had a couple of paragraphs on Horkheimer and Adorno's Dialectic of Enlightenment that I can't shake — partly because I found them convincing and partly becuase a friend who does history of philosophy said they were garbage and then wouldn't say why. I can't get hold of the book itself right now, and honestly I'd rather not read 250 pages of it just to check a magazine. Here's roughly what the piece said, as best I can reconstruct the wording:

'Written in wartime California and published in Amsterdam in 1947, the book argues that the Enlightenment — the eighteenth-century movement of Kant, Voltaire, and the encyclopedists, with its faith in reason, science, and tolerance — carried inside it the seeds of the totalitarianism that drove its authors out of Europe. Their point is not that reason was misused by bad people, but that reason itself, once it insists on measuring and classifying everything, can only see particular things as instances of a type; and a population reduced to instances of a type is a population that can be counted, sorted, and shipped. Auschwitz on this account is not a relapse into barbarism but the logical outcome of a rationality that had already learned to treat nature, including human nature, as raw material. They describe it as a two-way street — myth is already enlightenment and enlightenment reverts to mythology — meaning there is no difference in kind between the two, only of degree, which is why they can read Homer and Bacon in the same breath. Horkheimer spelled out the mechanism the same year: reason had shrunk into a tool, a calculator of means, and a calculator of means has nothing whatever to say about ends. They offer no way out except art, and their own gesture toward the Marquis de Sade as the Enlightenment's most honest spokesman shows how far the diagnosis went. The argument went on to become the common sense of the humanities — Foucault made it concrete by tracing the asylum, the clinic, and the prison back to the same rationality — and it explains why every promise to use data to make the world better is the last stage of the same disease.'

My instinct is that it oversells, and I've heard Habermas accuse the book of a performative contradiction, that a total critique of reason has to borrow reason to make its case. But I don't want to just take that side, it smells like a cheap way out of a hard book. So could you go through the passage sentence by sentence and tell me what someone who actually works on this text would accept, what they'd call a fair compression, and what's wrong or badly misleading? Specifically: is the Enlightenment in their title really the eighteenth-century movement, do they actually say Auschwitz follows from reason, does the myth/enlightenment line really mean science and myth differ only in degree, is art offered as the exit, is the Sade move an endorsement or the opposite, and is the Foucault linkage a genuine lineage or a category mistake — I keep having the feeling that one of those two books thinks reason is the problem and the other doesn't, but I can't say which. Then tell me whether the closing claim about data and progress survives once the rest is fixed up, or whether it's doing nothing at all, and what the piece leaves out that would most change the picture for someone reading the book for the first time. On Habermas, say whether the performative contradiction charge lands or misses, and what it assumes about what kind of book this is.
