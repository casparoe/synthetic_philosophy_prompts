Okay so I'm reading a popular book about rationality -- one of those forecasting/decision-making ones, I'm not gonna name it -- and there's a chapter on the Dutch book argument. Here's roughly what it says, typed out from the book:

"The cleanest way to see what a degree of belief is: it's a price. If you say you're 70% confident it'll rain, you're committing yourself to paying up to 70 cents for a ticket that pays a dollar if it rains. Ramsey and de Finetti noticed that this has an immediate consequence. If your prices don't fit the axioms of probability -- if you price a disjunction of two incompatible claims at less than the sum of the two, say -- then a bookie can hand you a small package of bets, each of which you would happily accept at your own quoted prices, that loses you money no matter what happens. Not probably loses: loses in every possible world. So an incoherent set of beliefs isn't just messy bookkeeping, its a standing invitation to be fleeced, and the fleecing is guaranteed by arithmetic rather than by luck. That's why this is the best argument for probabilism ever devised: it never asks you to care about truth, or to have any evidence, or to be a good reasoner. It only asks whether you'd take the bets you've already said you'd take. And it runs backwards too -- if your prices do satisfy the axioms, no such package exists -- so coherence is precisely the line between rational and irrational belief. This isn't armchair stuff either. In the famous Linda experiment, most subjects judged 'bank teller and active in the feminist movement' more likely than plain 'bank teller,' a flat-out contradiction, which means the people in that study were each sitting on a Dutch book that any bookie who knew their prices could have collected. The upshot for the reader: rationality is coherence, and self-improvement is mostly the work of auditing your credences until no book can be made against you."
