I rebuilt the MU-puzzle argument the other night and it seems to have gotten away from me. Quick setup in case it matters: MIU system, axiom MI, four rules — tack a U onto anything ending in I; double whatever follows the M; replace III with U; delete UU. Target MU. And the familiar way to see that MU is unreachable is the invariant: only the second and third rules touch the count of Is, one doubles it and the other knocks off three, so starting from a single I you never land on a count divisible by three, and MU has zero Is.

That move — jumping out of the system — I tried to generalize, and the generalization proves way too much.

(1) The question of whether MU is derivable has a definite answer, and the answer is no.
(2) Nothing done inside the MIU system can reveal that answer. The system cant even express the question: no rule, and no run of rules finite or endless, ever mentions counts of letters or divisibility. An exhaustive search inside just keeps going forever.
(3) We settle it anyway, and we settle it by finding a feature of the strings the rules are blind to. So the answer comes from outside.
(4) None of this is special to MIU. Any formal system leaves questions about itself that it can't settle from inside but that can be settled from outside; MIU is just the toy case small enough to check by hand.
(5) If my own reasoning is a formal system — some fixed rules plus inputs, whether or not anyone has ever written them down — then (4) applies to me, so there are questions about my own outputs I can't settle from inside.
(6) But there's no principled barrier to my treating myself the way I just treated MIU: look at the rules, find the invariant, settle the question from outside.
(7) So if I were a formal system, I could settle from outside something that, being a formal system, I couldn't settle from inside — and having that ability is exactly what it means not to be one.
(8) Therefore I'm not one, and no set of rules, however long or however well trained, captures my reasoning.

I can hear that this is the Lucas-Penrose line and I don't want a history lesson on it. What I want is the step. Which one do you reject, why did it look completely innocent on the way past, and what does giving it up cost — including an honest price for the near misses, not just the one you pick. For what it's worth I'm most attached to (4): it feels like the entire point of the excercise, and losing it seems to leave me holding a card trick about counting letters. I don't want to give up (1) or (2), those are just arithmetic. (3) I'm oddly unsure about — doing it didn't feel like insight, it felt like bookkeeping — but I can't turn that into an objection to anything.
