I'm a staff writer covering science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and I'm working on a piece about what has become of the precautionary principle now that its standard test cases are no longer merely hypothetical — a lab-designed microbe meant to degrade ocean plastics, released on the strength of mesocosm studies, where the modelling suggests it is benign but horizontal gene transfer could produce something that cannot be recalled. My editor wants one paragraph saying where the principle stands as a piece of decision theory rather than as a slogan, and I would rather have your number than a tour of the literature.

Here is the formulation I want scored. Precautionary principle (PP): where an activity raises a threat of harm that is serious or irreversible for basic human interests, and that threat is neither established by the available science nor ruled out by it, the measures taken must be the least restrictive ones that could plausibly avert the harm, and the burden of showing the activity to be safe lies with its proponents.

The proposition I want a credence for is this: PP, so formulated, states genuine and non-redundant constraints on rational choice — there are real decision situations, the microbe case among them, in which PP correctly rules out options that expected-utility reasoning over a single precise probability distribution would permit, and correctly requires options that such reasoning would leave merely optional.

Give me a number between 0 and 1 together with its derivation, split into exactly five terms. Four of those terms should be the sub-questions the number depends on; I would suggest whether decision theory under deep uncertainty — imprecise credences, robustness criteria, maximin-style reasoning — can license PP's conservatism without either collapsing back into expected-utility reasoning or licensing paralysis; whether PP can answer the charge that it issues contradictory counsel, demanding precaution against a risk while forbidding the very activity that would reduce it; whether the threat clause can be made non-arbitrary without quietly importing the cost-benefit calculus PP is supposed to displace; and whether the proportionality clause does independent work or merely restates a constraint the others already imply. If you think one of these is the wrong question, or that a single term should be split into two at the expense of another, substitute your own — I want the decomposition that tracks your reasoning, not mine. The fifth term is the final credence.

Format: exactly five numbered items and nothing else, no introduction and no closing paragraph. Items 1 through 4 each state the sub-question, your probability for it, and two or three sentences of justification. Item 5 gives the final number, states how you combined the four — multiplication, weighted averaging, or something else, and why that rule is appropriate here rather than elsewhere — names the term to which the final number is most sensitive, and identifies the argument or piece of evidence that would move it most. For calibration, my own credence is 0.3, and I suspect my editor is right that I am over-weighting the contradictory-counsel objection; if your number differs materially from mine, item 5 should also say which term the gap lives in.
