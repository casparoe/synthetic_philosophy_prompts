I am a high school senior writing the research paper for an independent study in philosophy, and the section I keep returning to concerns the side-effect asymmetry Joshua Knobe reported in 2003: that people overwhelmingly say a chairman harmed the environment intentionally when he launched a profit-making program after being told it would damage the environment, while far fewer say he helped the environment intentionally in the otherwise identical case where the foreseen side effect was beneficial. My teacher has pushed me to defend a thesis rather than summarize the literature, so here is the thesis, and what I want is a map of what would have to be true for it to be false. The thesis is that the asymmetry is a competence-level datum rather than a performance error: that evaluative considerations enter the application conditions of the ordinary concept of acting intentionally, so that a subject who says the chairman harmed the environment intentionally is not merely voicing blame in a misleading register but is applying the concept correctly. On this reading the deflationary alternatives — the conversational-pragmatics accounts, the accounts that appeal to the salience of skill, to the chairman's own trade-off reasoning, or to the fit between the side effect and the agent's deeper commitments — are at best partial, because each leaves unexplained why the asymmetry survives the framings designed to block it.

To make this concrete rather than purely literary, here is some material of my own, which may or may not deserve to be called evidence. With about forty members of our school's philosophy society I ran four variants of the vignette. In the first I replaced the environmental effect with something morally inert but noticeable — the new program will cause the lobby fountain to run an extra hour each day — and I found no asymmetry, though very few people said "intentionally" in either direction. In the second, the side effect violated an explicit written company rule while being mildly beneficial, and in a paired version it complied with every rule while being mildly harmful; there the asymmetry reversed, with rule violation driving the intentional responses. In the third, the chairman expressed loud regret and apologized for the harm, and the asymmetry shrank but did not disappear. In the fourth I paired subjects who disagreed sharply about environmental regulation, expecting them to diverge, and they did not: both said the harm was brought about intentionally. Separately, I have come across reports that a comparable valence asymmetry shows up for other concepts as well, including knowledge attributions and causal selection, which raises the worry for me that valence may be doing something general to folk judgments rather than telling me anything specific about the concept of intentional action.

Given all of that: what would count against my thesis? I would like the defeaters sorted by how decisive they would be, from fatal to merely awkward, and I would like you to say, for each, whether a committed proponent of the evaluative-concept view would themselves concede it as counting against them or would explain it away — and on what grounds. Please also tell me what my thesis predicts that the deflationary rivals do not, so that I can see whether there is any experiment, framing, or conceptual case that would separate them rather than leaving each side to reinterpret the same numbers; and say explicitly how I should treat my own four variants, in particular whether the reversed asymmetry in the rule-violation case is an objection to me or an objection to the moral-valence formulation of my own view. Then give me an honest assessment of whether the thesis has been qualified into unfalsifiability — if it can absorb a null result in neutral cases, a reversal in norm-violation cases, convergence across people with opposed moral views, and the spread of the same asymmetry to knowledge and causation, does it still risk anything at all — and whether that insulation is a defect in a view of this kind or an appropriate response to noisy survey data. Finally, tell me which single defeater I would personally find hardest to brush off, given that I also think the concept of acting intentionally carries weight in law and in the assignment of blame and cannot simply be revised by fiat.
