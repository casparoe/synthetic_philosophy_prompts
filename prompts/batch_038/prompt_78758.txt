Been going back and forth on something in Hofstadter's superrationality and I want an actual side taken, not a survey of the options.

Setup: the whole thing seems to run on a closed-class assumption. Superrational thinkers reason that all superrational thinkers facing the same dilemma land on the same answer, so in a symmetric one-shot prisoner's dilemna each cooperates because they're effectively choosing the joint outcome. Fine. But now open the class. Say you're in a symmetric one-shot PD with payoffs T=5, R=3, P=1, S=0, against a stranger who you think is a Hofstadter-style superrational reasoner with credence p and an ordinary payoff-maximizing defector otherwise. Note what each type actually does: the superrational one cooperates regardless of your move (their reasoning is about what reasoners converge on, not about your behavior), and the CDT one defects regardless. So cooperating gets you 3p and defecting gets you 5p + (1−p) = 1 + 4p — defecting wins for every p, no threshold, and the gap *grows* with p. Check that arithmetic, but I think it's right: the more confident you are that you're facing a superrationalist, the more expensive cooperating becomes, since the thing you're giving up is T−R = 2 rather than P−S = 1.

Argue the strong version of what I take this to show: that superrationality isn't a decision theory at all but a membership rule for a self-sealing club — a norm of consistency among people who've already agreed to think alike, misdescribed as a way of deciding. It gives no verdict exactly where verdicts are needed, and it can't help bring the closed class into existence, so it has no bootstrapping story either.

Two things I want you to hit directly. First, the obvious unfairness charge: I've given you mixed credences about your opponent's type while giving the superrationalist certainty that you're one of them, and maybe genuine superrational reasoning doesn't admit of mixed credences at all without dissolving. Either show the charge misses or explain what survives once you grant it. Second, say whether team reasoning or evidential decision theory does any better in the same mixed setting, or whether they inherit the identical problem — I want to know if the trouble is about cooperation or specifically about closure.

End with the single weakest link in your own case. Don't retreat from the thesis, just tell me where it's most exposed. No reading lists or citations, I'm offline.
