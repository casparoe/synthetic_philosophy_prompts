Doing a second-year module on the philosophy of childhood at the moment and this week we're on Locke's Some Thoughts Concerning Education, alongside the bits of the Essay about the discipline of children and how ideas get glued together. I've got a pile of his actual verdicts down in my notes and I want to work out what rule he's really running, because I don't think it's the one our tutor keeps reaching for.

Here's the list as I have it. He won't let a child be taught to read by task or chiding, and instead suggests making the letters into a sort of game with dice or counters so the boy learns without noticing he's being taught. He'll never have a child beaten for a task — the rod is only for wilful rebellion, and then it should come from the father's own hand, not a servant's, and not in front of an audience. He splits crying in two: grief and tenderness get sympathy and distraction, but crying that's aimed at getting his own way gets shut down hard, immediately, because tears must never be allowed to work. He's adamant that servants mustn't scare children with stories about goblins and spirits, and that a fear should be worked off by degrees rather than avoided. He reckons a gentleman's son who won't need Latin shouldn't be flogged through it, wants French before Latin, Latin by conversation rather than grammar rules, and thinks composing Greek verses is a waste of years. He wants the feet washed in cold water, shoes thin and leaky, plain food, no strong drink, hard beds, lots of hours outside. He says a child's questions should be answered properly and never sneered at. He says don't cross a child's innocent desires just to assert authority, but never let him have something because he demanded it. He leans enormously on the child's sense of credit and reputation, and warns against talking to him as though he's already disgraced. And he wants plenty of play and slack, with the father severe early and friendly later.

New case for the essay. A boy of about ten, well-disposed, no temper. His tutor reports he can't hold attention on his writing and arithmatic for more than a few minutes at a stretch — he starts off cheerfully, then drifts, chews the pen, looks out of the window, invents games with the counters. He isn't disobedient: told off, he doesn't sulk or cry, he apologises and drifts again. The father wants to know what to do. Options on the table are a shorter task with a small forfeit for drift, letting the whole thing be turned into a game with stakes, treating the inattention as disobedience and using the rod, or concluding the boy isn't cut out for books and putting him to a trade.

So: which principle is each of my listed verdicts an instance of? I can see at least three or four candidate rules sitting behind them that all fit equally well, and I want them spelled out — including whether one of them is really just the association-of-ideas worry from the Essay, that pain must never be paired with the thing you want him to love. Then give me your best single guess at what Locke would tell the father, with the reason for preferring that rule over the others, and tell me what further case — something about this boy or a variant of him — would actually discriminate between the rules rather than being compatible with all of them.
