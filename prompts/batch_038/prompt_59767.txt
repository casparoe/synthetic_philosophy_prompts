Tomorrow our geology department switches off the last of its mechanical seismographs: a smoked-drum recorder that has been tracing ground motion in the basement of the building continuously since 1953. There is to be a short ceremony — the dean, the mayor, two classes of schoolchildren, the retired technician who oiled and re-inked the drum for thirty-one years, and a room full of seismologists. I have been asked to give the five-minute address.

I want the address built on one real idea rather than on nostalgia: roughly the point Dennett made with his two-bitser — that whether a physical state is about something is not a property the state has in itself, but a matter of how reliably the whole arrangement is coupled to the world, and of who knows the coupling. The same squiggle on a drum, in a different building, or after the coupling has decayed, is about something else, or about nothing at all. There is no intrinsic aboutness, and equally no second-class status attaching to the derived kind.

Three constraints. First, the address must not sentimentalize the instrument: nothing implying that it feels, notices, or minds anything. Second, it must not become a debunking lecture — the technician above all should come out honored rather than corrected, and the room's affection for the machine should survive intact. Third, it should end by turning the idea back on the audience, who are themselves, on this view, systems whose states are about things only in virtue of their couplings; I want that to land as sobering and dignifying at once, and not to collapse into either pure comfort or pure deflation.

One further complication: among the visitors is a philosopher who holds that a machine of this sort could never genuinely be about anything, and who is right that a great deal of loose talk about instruments "reading" the world is sloppy. The address should concede him that much, openly, and then leave him with something to sit with rather than a quarrel — without ever naming his position or staging a debate. Schoolchildren must be able to follow the whole thing; the seismologists must not wince at any of it.
