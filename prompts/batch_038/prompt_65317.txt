I teach an upper-level seminar on objective chance, and this term I want to open the unit by having the class work through the first scene of Tom Stoppard's *Rosencrantz and Guildenstern Are Dead*: the coin game, in which Rosencrantz calls heads and heads keeps coming, the two of them end up holding bags at opposite ends of the same distribution, and Guildenstern — unbothered about the money but badly bothered about the implications — cycles through explanations for the run. As I remember them, his candidates are roughly that he is somehow willing the outcome himself as penance for a past he cannot recall, that time has stopped and it is really a single toss being repeated, that something supernatural is interfering, and finally that it is simply chance. He never quite rests on the last one. (The precise tally differs between editions and productions, so please treat it as a run that does not break rather than a specific number.)

What I want is a reading of that scene against the Principal Principle: the norm, in Lewis's original formulation, that when your evidence is admissible your credence in an outcome ought to match the known chance of it, together with the revision — Hall's, which conditionalizes on the whole actual history rather than on a chance hypothesis taken in isolation — that is meant to defuse the problem of chance hypotheses being undermined by the very sequences they render improbable. In particular:

1. Is Guildenstern's refusal to settle on mere chance a failure of deference, or is it precisely the undermining intuition that forced the revision? Please lay out the scene's evidential structure explicitly: which of his hypotheses is the chance hypothesis, what the streak is evidence *for*, and whether the streak counts as admissible evidence at all, given that it is a fact about the past.

2. Do any of the other three hypotheses correspond to positions a philosopher of probability would recognize, or are they purely theatrical? The one about time stopping and a single toss being repeated strikes me as a point about single-case chance and reference classes delivered as a joke, and I would like to know whether that is defensible.

3. Rosencrantz is entirely unruffled. Is he the model deferential agent, or a demonstration of what happens when the principle is satisfied by an agent whose starting credences were never worth having? Does the play distinguish the two, or does it simply need one straight man?

4. Tell me where this reading breaks down. My own worry is that the play is not about chance at all but about fate and about being characters inside someone else's script, that the coin is stage machinery for existential abandonment, and that pressing a probabilistic reading onto it distorts both the drama and the philosophy. How much of the scene survives that objection? And is the Player's way of answering questions about how they all got there — offering chance and fate as interchangeable alternatives — drawing a distinction or erasing one? I would also like your view on the moment, if I am remembering it correctly, where the run is broken not by a change of coin but by a change of hands, once the Player is the one handling it.

5. Finally: does the scene expose anything about deference to chance that the technical literature neglects? It seems to me that applying the principle requires knowing which setup is yours and which toss you are asking about, and that two men who introduce themselves to a stranger with their names mixed up, and whom nobody else in the play can reliably tell apart, are in no position to supply that. Say whether that is a genuine pressure point in the account or a coincidence I am imposing on the text.

The students will have read the technical material but will not have seen the play, and none of them will have the script in the room, so paraphrase rather than quote and describe the stage business rather than assuming it. I want your own reading, not a list of further sources.
