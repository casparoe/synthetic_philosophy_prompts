I have come to accept that the identity of indiscernibles is false, and I would like the exposure of that commitment mapped out as carefully as you can manage. My route to the view was not Leibniz scholarship but a seminar on the metaphysics of physics: after working through the long-running claim that quantum statistics leaves identical particles with no principium individuationis at all, I went back to Black's classic 1952 example in *Mind* — a universe containing nothing but two iron spheres, matching one another in diameter, colour and temperature and every other respect, separated by some fixed distance — and concluded that if such a world can be described without contradiction, no principle requiring qualitative difference in order to have numerical difference can be a necessary truth. Stated precisely, I now hold three things: that Black's world is genuinely possible and not merely coherently describable; that what individuates the two spheres there is bare thisness rather than any qualitative difference, weak or strong; and that consequently any account of particularity on which numerical diversity depends on qualitative diversity is mistaken, and should be rejected rather than reformulated.

What would have to be true for me to be wrong? I do not want a general catalogue of objections but a ranking, from fatal to merely awkward, of the specific pressures on this position. At minimum, please deal with: (a) the suggestion that in the Black world there would really be only one sphere, so that my stipulation of two already presupposes the thisness I claim to have derived from it — how damaging is that charge, and what would my view have to add in order to answer it rather than restate itself; (b) the move that replaces qualitative difference with difference grounded in irreflexive relations — the spheres being a fixed distance from one another, or being a different sphere from one another, or two fermions standing in a relation neither bears to itself — and whether this rescues a version of the principle I ought to have accepted all along, or whether it concedes my point by thinning "discernible" until it does no explanatory work; (c) the worry that my description smuggles in a background space, an external standpoint, or a way of counting that a genuinely homogeneous world cannot supply; (d) the arithmetic and epistemic consequences — whether one can count, quantify over, or refer to items that are in no way discernible, and if one cannot, whether my view therefore costs me something about ordinary plural thought and not merely about Leibniz's system; and (e) the tie to the principle of sufficient reason — if two wholly indiscernible spheres can coexist, am I committed to there being no reason why there are two rather than one, and is that a bullet I can swallow or a slide into a much larger loss?

For each of these, say whether a committed haecceitist about this case would themselves concede it as counting against the view, or dismiss it as question-begging, since I care about the difference between pressure I can absorb and pressure I have merely been trained not to notice. I would also like your judgement on whether an actual physical instance matters here: would a demonstrated case of genuinely indiscernible particles, as opposed to a merely possible one, strengthen my claim, weaken it, or leave it untouched on the grounds that the modal premise is doing all the real work? Finally, be blunt about insulation. For any purported pair of indiscernibles a critic produces, I seem able to reply either that they are really one thing, or that they are two by thisness alone; if that flexibility means the thesis risks nothing and has been qualified into compatibility with every description, tell me so plainly, and tell me whether that is a genuine defect for a metaphysical claim of this kind or simply what such claims look like when they are true. End with the single consideration you would bet on as most likely to make me abandon the view.
