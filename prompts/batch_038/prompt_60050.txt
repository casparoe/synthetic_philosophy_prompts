I am a novelist at work on my third book, and its final act now turns on a question in practical philosophy that I am not equipped to settle myself. The protagonist is a dealer in antiquarian books and mechanical curiosities whose preferences over three of his own possessions cycle: a signed first edition, an eighteenth-century orrery, and a small oil painting. At any given moment he would rather hold one of them plus twenty pounds than the object he currently has, in a strict circle. Across six chapters he makes exactly those trades, each of them freely, each with complete information about what is being offered and what it leads to, and none of them preceded by any device that could bind him later. He closes the novel holding the book he began with and one hundred and twenty pounds poorer, having foreseen this ending precisely, and without having made a single choice that looked, at the moment of making it, unreasonable. A philosopher I correspond with insists the character is impossible rather than merely improbable: that anyone sophisticated enough to see the sequence coming can plan his way out of it at the first node, so that a man who nonetheless ends up poorer has been written irrationally instead of written as irrational. Since the ending is the reason the book exists, I should like a number rather than an overview.

Take the proposition to be this: for an agent who is fully informed, unable to precommit, and who at each node chooses in accordance with a plan he could actually carry through from that node onward — sophisticated rather than myopic choice — cyclical preferences are irrational, and the ground of that verdict is that such an agent remains exploitable by a money pump. Give me your credence in it, then decompose it. I would want at least these terms, each with a figure and one line of justification: the probability that no defensible principle of diachronic choice both blocks the pump and is independently motivated, which is to say that sophisticated choice fails, that resolute choice is not something one can hold consistently, and that diachronic separability cannot simply be denied by fiat; the probability that susceptibility to a foreseeable, avoidable sure loss is a rational defect attributable to the agent himself rather than a symptom of a badly described preference set; the probability that the relevant notion of rationality here is a single diachronically extended standard rather than a family of time-slice-relative ones, so that the question has a determinate answer at all; and the probability that the availability of ordinary self-binding arrangements — an assistant, a standing rule, a contract — is beside the theoretical point rather than a way of dissolving it, given that my dealer has every practical opportunity to delegate and simply declines to. Add any further term you think the decomposition needs, and show me how the pieces combine; if you believe they are not independent, say so and combine them accordingly instead of multiplying.

Tell me which term you are least confident in, and what sort of argument — not what further detail about the case, but what consideration — would move your overall figure furthest. My own credence sits near 0.7 and hers near 0.3; I would like your view on which of your terms we are most likely diverging over, since we have argued past each other for two years without locating the disagreement. Finally, a practical question that I would ask only of someone who has done the theoretical work: if your number is low, what has to change in the character for the sequence to be genuinely inescapable for him — some restriction on his information, some structure in the offers, some feature of how his choices are individuated? I would far rather revise the fiction than write a man ruined by an argument that does not go through.
