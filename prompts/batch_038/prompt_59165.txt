I am auditing a reading group on decision theory that has spent the past month on the claim that agents who cannot causally influence one another may still have reason to cooperate, on the grounds that a universe as large as ours must contain vast numbers of agents reasoning in much the way I reason, so that my choice is evidence about theirs — and evidence, on evidential decision theory, is all there is to choose by. I tried to set the view out as a compact argument for members who had not encountered it, and I now think the argument proves a great deal more than anyone in the group wants. Here is what I have.

(1) The value of an act is the expected value of the outcomes computed on the supposition that I perform it.
(2) The universe is large enough to contain, and may well contain without any upper bound, a very great number of agents who are near-copies of me with respect to the decision procedure they run: the same dispositions, roughly the same information, and structurally similar one-shot choices. Almost none of them could I ever signal, help, or harm.
(3) Conditional on my cooperating in the one-shot dilemma in front of me, it is overwhelmingly likely that they cooperate in theirs; conditional on my defecting, overwhelmingly likely that they defect.
(4) Suppose each of their cooperations yields ten units of good for beings I have reason to care about, and that there are N of them, with N enormous.
(5) My cooperating is then worth on the order of 10N units, and my defecting forfeits the same; the two units I pocket by defecting are noise against it.
(6) Nothing in (2) through (5) depended on the choice being a dilemma. For any act A there are near-copy agents deliberating whether to do A, and my doing A is evidence that they do A.
(7) So for any act whatever, its value to me is dominated by the aggregate over copies, and my own causal payoffs fall out as a rounding error.
(8) Hence, if enough near-copies of me stand to gain more from my skipping a dentist appointment than I stand to lose from a week of toothache, I ought to skip it; and in general my reasons are not mine at all but the pooled reasons of strangers I cannot reach.
(9) That is intolerable: it turns my own life into a pure instrument for uncontactable others and hands out absurd verdicts about trivia.
(10) Therefore (1), or the whole picture of acausal cooperation that (1) supports, must be abandoned.

A causal decision theorist in the group says the chain is simply confused and that (3) is doing something illicit with the word "likely," but he cannot say what, and I do not find that satisfying either. I would prefer to keep (1) and (2) if I possibly can, and I do not want to reject (6) by declaration, since that looks like special pleading dressed up as a diagnosis. So: tell me which step you reject, and explain why that step looked entirely innocent to me when I wrote it down. In particular, I want an account of what has to be true of another agent's situation for my act to be evidence about theirs — one that can be stated without collapsing into "agents identical to me in every relevant respect," which makes the reference class a class of one and quietly deletes the conclusion, and without being so loose that I can gerrymander a reference class to support whatever verdict I happened to want. Finally, say whether your repair leaves any room for cooperation with agents I cannot causally affect, or whether it turns out to refute that idea as well, and if it does, say plainly that (9) was the cheap premise and (8) the one I should have kept. Please write your answer in American English.
