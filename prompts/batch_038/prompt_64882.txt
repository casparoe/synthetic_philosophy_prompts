My wife died nine days ago, and I have been asked by the family to speak at her funeral on Saturday. I should explain the situation carefully, because nearly everything that is customarily said on such an occasion has been closed off to me in advance, and what remains is narrow enough that I do not think I can find it alone.

Neither of us ever took a degree in anything. We ran a hardware store together for thirty-eight years. She was, however, a serious amateur astronomer, and in the last year of her life, from her bed, she became absorbed by an argument about time that she had found in a library book and which she explained to me repeatedly until I understood it, or believed I did. As she put it: there is no fact about which distant events are happening *now*, because that depends on how you are moving; two people who pass each other on a street, one walking toward some faraway galaxy and one walking away, divide the universe into "now" and "not yet" differently, and where they disagree can be a matter of whether some enormous distant event has already happened or is still to come. Therefore, she said, if you refuse to let what is real differ between two people merely because one of them happens to be walking east, you cannot also maintain that only the present is real. Everything is equally real. The past is not destroyed; it is merely elsewhere. I believe the argument has a hyphenated name, which I will not attempt to reproduce, and I am aware that she was not the first person to think of it.

Her instructions were specific. No one is to say that she has gone, gone away, passed on, left us, or is at peace now. She did not permit the word "soul," and she did not permit any suggestion that she is watching over anyone, or that she lives on in us in any sense that would involve her still having experiences. She used to say that putting a person in the past tense is like saying that Paris was. Her sister teaches physics at a community college and has told me, with some heat, that the argument is not licensed by the physics at all: that relativity says simultaneity across a distance is simply not a meaningful notion, that the argument quietly stretches a local present into a slice across the whole universe, and that the real work is done by a philosophical premise about what must count as real, not by any measurement. Her sister also objects to the argument being used as a comfort, on the grounds that even if it is right, it does not put my wife within anyone's reach, and that she would be furious if the room went home believing that science had proved she is still around. Her sister will be sitting in the front row. The room will hold perhaps ninety people: her churchgoing relatives, who will be listening for the customary formulas and will notice their absence; my side of the family, who sell things for a living; and three or four members of her astronomy club, who know the argument as well as she did.

What I am asking for is a speech of about six minutes that can actually be read aloud, that a person who has never thought about any of this can follow without feeling addressed as a child, and that uses at most one image and no technical vocabulary. It must keep every one of her prohibitions. It must not claim for physics what her sister denies physics supports — I want the argument presented as the picture she chose and defended, not as a verdict handed down by Einstein. And it must still give the room something true to hold, including something I can honestly say about the summer of 1974, at a lake in Michigan, which she returned to for fifty years. If you judge that some part of this cannot be done without either lying or betraying her, tell me so in two or three lines before the speech and offer the nearest honest alternative.
