I have spent the better part of two decades reading rationalist blogs and forums, so my default way of thinking about belief runs through incentives, payoff structures, and the gap between what an agent says and what actually moves them. I am drafting a long-form post for a small reading group I help run, and I want a genuine piece of literary-philosophical criticism at its center rather than a book report. Please adopt the role of a professional philosopher who works on the theory of reasons and also publishes literary criticism — someone equally at home with the wrong kind of reasons debate and with close reading. Write in American English.

The pairing I want is Kazuo Ishiguro's The Remains of the Day read through the wrong kind of reasons problem. Since you cannot consult either text, here is the scaffolding I will hold you to. The novel is narrated by Stevens, a butler at Darlington Hall, who in 1956 takes a motoring trip through the West Country, ostensibly to recruit the former housekeeper Miss Kenton — now Mrs. Benn — back into service while his new employer, an American named Mr. Farraday, is abroad. The journey is interleaved with recollections of the interwar years, when Lord Darlington worked to soften the settlement imposed on Germany and hosted conferences and discreet meetings at the Hall, including one period in which Darlington instructed Stevens to dismiss two housemaids because they were Jewish, an order Stevens carried out without protest while Miss Kenton was appalled and threatened to resign but did not. After the war Darlington was disgraced in the press and died; on the trip Stevens twice lets strangers believe he never served Darlington, then assures himself this is only to avoid hearing nonsense about a good man. The novel ends with the meeting with Mrs. Benn, who admits she sometimes wonders about the life she might have had, and then a conversation with a stranger on the seafront who suggests that the evening is the best part of the day. There, Stevens concedes that Darlington, however misguided, at least made his own mistakes, whereas Stevens trusted him so completely that he cannot claim even that for himself; he resolves instead to practice bantering for Mr. Farraday. Work from the novel, not the film adaptation.

On the philosophy side, assume the standard apparatus: cases where an agent has powerful practical reasons to adopt an attitude but where those reasons are intuitively not reasons of the right kind — the threatening demon who will punish you unless you admire him, the payment offered for admiration — and the resulting debate about whether such considerations are genuine reasons for the attitude, mere reasons to want to have it, or not reasons at all. Parfit's contrast between object-given and state-given reasons, Hieronymi's claim that only reasons bearing on the question the attitude answers are of the right kind, Shah's argument from deliberative transparency, and the trouble the wrong kind of reasons makes for buck-passing accounts of value should all be available to you. Name positions and people where it helps, but do not produce references.

Please address the following, and feel free to reorder or merge them where the argument runs better:

1. Which attitude is actually at issue? Admiration for Darlington, belief that he was a good man, trust, or something more like institutional deference — the handing over of judgment? Does the literature's focus on belief and admiration get a grip on Stevens's central attitude, or does deference fall outside the taxonomy in a way that matters?
2. Is Stevens ever moved by object-given reasons at all, or does the novel depict a mind in which state-given considerations do all the work? Locate the moments where the narration betrays which is which — the treatment of the two housemaids, the denials of his employer, the passage in which he catches himself misremembering who made a certain remark about his father.
3. Unreliable narration as the literary form of the philosophical point: the reader is handed the object-given reasons while Stevens supplies state-given ones. Does that vindicate the claim that wrong-kind reasons do not bear on the question, since we watch a question go unanswered for three hundred pages, or does it show that the claim is too clean, given that Stevens deliberates at length and on the page in precisely those terms?
4. If wrong-kind reasons cannot be followed in deliberation, what is Stevens doing? Deliberating, rationalizing, or something the current frameworks lack a word for? Say what would have to be true for each diagnosis.
5. The pier concession — that Darlington at least made his own mistakes — registers a loss. Is it a loss of justification or a loss of authorship? Could the novel be motivating a third category, neither object-given nor state-given, and would that be a real addition to the debate or a confusion dressed up as insight?
6. Miss Kenton as a contrast case: she responds to the same object-given reasons and still does not act on them. Does the novel imply the same diagnosis for her, a different one, or an asymmetry between them that the philosophy cannot express?
7. Bantering at the end: adopting a manner for considerations that have nothing to do with its object. Is this a benign wrong-kind case, and does the novel suggest that late in a life such reasons may be the only ones available — and that this can be wisdom rather than failure?
8. Where does this reading break? Tell me plainly where I would be importing the framework rather than finding it, and whether Ishiguro would resist the pairing at any specific point.

Think carefully and flag any uncertainty about a scene rather than inventing one; do not put words in the novel's mouth, and do not quote or paraphrase-into-quotes any line of it. Be faithful in both directions: do not reduce the novel to an illustration of a taxonomy, and do not stretch the taxonomy until it fits. Close with a paragraph on what the novel adds to the wrong kind of reasons debate that argument alone would not deliver. Finally, if your analysis turns out to generalize to agents whose expressed attitudes are shaped by the payoff of expressing them — a situation my crowd thinks about constantly, in a rather different technical setting — add two or three sentences on that at the very end; if it does not generalize cleanly, say so and move on.
