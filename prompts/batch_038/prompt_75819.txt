I have one academic year of leave, no collaborators who run surveys, and I have committed to producing two things from it: a journal article and a general-audience essay on whether Boomers, Millennials, and Gen Z are real kinds. Help me settle which questions the year should go to, and in what order.

The reframing I want to build on is this: I am inclined to treat a generation as what I will call a trajectory kind — a kind whose members are individuated not by sharing a property at a time but by sharing a developmental sequence, the same exposures arriving in the same order at comparable life stages. On that reading a generation is a group with a shared tempo rather than a shared trait, and the standing objection that members of a cohort are wildly heterogeneous among themselves may simply miss the point.

Given that, give me four or five candidate questions, ranked. For each I want to know what makes it upstream of the others, whether it can be advanced by conceptual argument alone or requires demographic data I cannot realistically gather, and whether settling it would change anything outside philosophy — polling practice, education policy, the marketing use of cohort labels. Then tell me which of these three you would cut, and why: the boundary problem of where a generation starts and stops; the identification problem in age-period-cohort analysis, where age, period, and cohort are linearly dependent so that no model can estimate all three effects without extra assumptions; and whether cohort labels are interactive, in the sense that naming and measuring a generation changes how it turns out. Finally, say plainly whether my trajectory-kind reframing is worth defending at length or is cosmetic — and if it is cosmetic, which question I should put in its place. I also want to know what, over the course of the year, would make you re-order the list.
