so i got into a three hour arguement with my friend last night about tipping and queues and jaywalking and about whether you have to follow local rules when you're visiting somewhere, and I want to write down what I actually believe before it evaporates and find out whether it hangs together. He says it doesn't, but mostly he just kept saying "that's inconsistent" without saying which part, so I'm not getting anywhere with him. Here's the package, with the little names I've been using for the pieces.

Background, because the names matter: I sort conventions into three kinds. **Coordination conventions** — which side of the platform to stand on, which way to walk down a crowded hallway, when to step off the curb at a signal. Nobody gains from them, they just prevent collision. **Allocation conventions** — queues, first come first served, pulling a number at the deli, the airport gate line. These actually distribute a scarce good, so breaking one isn't just friction, somebody specifically loses a place they'd already earned. And what I've been calling **tribute conventions** — tipping, the automatic service charge, buying the round because it's your turn, the envelope at a wedding. The practice mostly just moves resources from one party to another and the coordination story doesn't really apply.

My commitments:

1. No convention creates an obligation just by existing. What makes breaking one wrong, when it is wrong, is that other people have calibrated their plans around it and you upset the calibration. I call this the calibration principle: moral weight tracks how many people have settled their expectations on the practice and how expensive the upset is for them.

2. So convention-based obligations are real, but they're grounded in reliance, not in agreement. I don't think you need to have consented, tacitly or otherwise, and I don't buy that merely being in a place is consent to its rules.

3. I'm a philosophical anarchist about law. The fact that something is legally required doesn't add any moral weight by itself. Law matters to me only as evidence about what people around me expect.

4. Ignorance excuses blame but doesn't delete the obligation. If I tip 15 where 20 is the settled expectation, or I cross against a signal I didn't know was there, I did something wrong and I'm just partly off the hook for the blaming.

5. Obligations have to be the kind of thing you could reasonably be blamed for. If there's no version of the situation in which blame makes sense, I don't want to say there was an obligation there. This is supposed to be a constraint on everything above, not a seperate theory competing with them.

6. Visitors should default to complying with local conventions, including ones they think are bad, and the burden of justifying defection goes up the less they understand the practice. Call it the visitor's presumption. My reason: a visitor is missing most of the local information about who's depending on what.

7. But the presumption is rebuttable, and I think tribute conventions are the easiest to rebut, since there's no coordination function getting damaged. I personally think American restaurant tipping is a bad practice and I'd like it gone. I still tip 20 when I eat out here, because the person who loses from my defection is this server tonight, not the norm.

8. I'm allowed to defect from a convention I judge unjust if I compensate the people who lose from my defection — pay and defect. I wouldn't do this with a queue. I would do it with a tribute convention.

9. Jaywalking at 3am at an empty intersection with nobody in sight isn't wrong at all. No residue, no small wrongness. The same act at 5pm in front of a school, or with a kid watching me do it, is wrong — and part of what's wrong in the second case is what the act does to the practice, not just the collision risk.

His three objections, for what they're worth: (a) that 6 and 2 can't both be true, since a visitor is the paradigm case of someone who never agreed to anything; (b) that the second half of 9 smuggles in norm-erosion reasoning that, if I actually applied it consistently, would force me to keep propping up a practice I say I want abolished, which undercuts 7; (c) that 1 turns morality into whatever the local majority happens to have settled on, so I've built a bootstrapping machine and then in 3 denied that the state is allowed to have one. I couldn't answer (b) at all last night. On (a) and (c) I have a strong feeling they miss but I can't articulate why, which is annoying.
