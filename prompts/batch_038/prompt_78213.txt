I'm co-teaching a seminar in the spring, working title something like "Honesty, Tact, and Translation" — half philosophy students, half people from an intercultural-communication program — and separately I've been helping a company whose HQ is in Amsterdam and whose second-biggest office is in Osaka write a single global policy on giving employees feedback. Both projects have convinced me of the same thing: nobody in either of them has ever specified what they actually want from a theory. The Dutch side says directness is a form of respect, the Japanese side says that same directness is a deliberate injury, HR says everyone should just "be honest," and the readings on my syllabus argue about whether white lies are wrong without ever saying what a correct account of the whole territory would have to be able to do. So before I read another one of them: write the desiderata. What does an adequate normative theory of honest and polite communication have to deliver — which cases it must get right, which distinctions it must not collapse, which empirical or conceptual facts it must be consistant with? Give me the consideration behind each requirement, not just the label. Adequacy is relative to a purpose, so take the purpose to be: usable to adjudicate the Amsterdam/Osaka fight, and teachable to people who are going to go write real workplace and clinical guidelines.

Here's what I have so far, mostly as raw material for you to sort, extend, and tell me I've got wrong. I keep finding four distinct things lumped together under "dishonesty": flat-out asserting what you believe to be false; asserting something literally true while implicating something false; saying nothing with real propositional content but performing it in a register that carries a false attitude — the effusive thank-you note for a gift you hated, which I've been calling register deceit, and I want the theory to say whether that's a moral category at all or just a mannerism; and simply not mentioning what the other person had a claim to be told, where the silence does all the work. A theory that handles only the first and treats the rest as noise should fail. Then there's the cross-cultural wrinkle I think the literature mostly misses: what an utterance communicates depends on local convention, so the same remark can be honest by the speaker's community's rules and systematically misleading by the hearer's — which forces the theory to say whose conventions fix the content, and whether a speaker can be simultaneously blameless and deceptive, or culpably ignorant about the conventions they're operating inside. It also has to cover the other direction, candour and not just deception: when you're required to volunteer unwelcome truths nobody asked for, and when strict truthfulness is itself a way of doing harm — bluntness as a weapon, the colleague who prides himself on telling it like it is and is mostly just being cruel with an alibi. And it can't make ordinary social life pervasively immoral; if the theory implies that every pleasantry, every "lovely to see you," every softened performance review is a wrong, that's a cost it has to explain rather than a feature to celebrate.

So give me the list, and mark which requirements are non-negotiable versus which are really one camp's prejudices dressed up as criteria — I suspect the demand that the norms be culture-independent is one of the latter, and so is the demand that a lie must involve an intention to deceive, but you tell me. Then say which single requirement does the most to exclude otherwise attractive views, because that's where I'd want to aim the seminar. And do the part I want most: flag any pair of desiderata you think can't be jointly satisfied. My guess is that "whose conventions fix the content" collides with "the norms must not be hostage to which community you happen to stand in," and seperately that a robust duty of candour toward intimates collides with a right to keep parts of yourself unexposed — but argue it, don't just take my word. Finish with a tight scorecard, a sentence or two each, for the four families people actually defend this stuff with: the respect-for-rational-agency prohibition on lying; the practice-of-trust view where the wrong is erosion of a shared institution; the entitlement view where it turns on what this particular hearer had a right to be told; and a tact-or-virtue view on which the question is what a perceptive and kind person would do here. Keep the weight on the criteria and their motivating cases, the scorecard short. And please no reading list or bibliography — I've got a stack, what I'm missing is the spec.
