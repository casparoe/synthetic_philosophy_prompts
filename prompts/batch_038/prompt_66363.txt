I cover science and ideas for a monthly magazine, which mostly means I write about attention, institutions, and what technology does to small places. This isn't work though — it's a favor I've talked myself into and I need help.

Here's the situation. Kettle River, pop. about 11,000, is where I grew up. Every August for like 60 years the town has done the Pageant: two nights in the fairgrounds parking lot, on a flatbed trailer with a painted canvas backdrop, where residents play themselves and their own ancestors in a loose scripted thing about the founding of the county — bad acting, blown lines, a guy named Dale who has played the same three roles since 1979 and refuses to give them up. The script gets rewritten every year by whoever's chairing. Nobody would call it art and everybody in town can tell you exactly where they sat. This spring, a streaming executive who grew up two blocks from the fairgrounds paid for a proper 400-seat proscenium house downtown — real fly space, real lighting grid, the works — and it opens in six weeks. He's signed a national arts management firm to program it: touring productions, an eight-show season, subscription model. The Pageant chair has said publicly, twice, that the new building ends the Pageant, because the Pageant is going to be moved indoors, ticketed, lit correctly, and "fixed." The drama kids at the high school, meanwhile, have never in their lives stood on a stage with a curtain and are absolutely thrilled and think the chair is being ridiculous.

I've been asked to give the remarks at the ribbon-cutting. Six or seven minutes. The room: the donor and his family, the arts-management rep, the town council, the state arts people, about forty high school drama students, a couple hundred locals, and my college roommate, who now teaches 18th-century French philosophy two hours away and will catch every single move I make including the ones where I'm cheating.

I want you to write the remarks so that Rousseau's argument against the theater — the long letter he wrote to d'Alembert in 1758, arguing against putting a theater in Geneva — does actual structural work in the speech. Not as a name-check, not as decoration. Specifically, I want these four things carrying the thing:

1. His point that a theater is not good or bad in the abstract but right or wrong for a particular place, depending on its customs and its scale — he thought a theater suited Paris fine and Geneva not at all, and his reason was about what each place already was, not about plays.
2. His point that theater's business is to please, not to correct: it flatters what an audience already finds funny and admirable rather than changing it, and his example was comedy making the one honest, blunt person in the room the joke while the smooth operator gets the laugh.
3. His unease about the spectators themselves — a couple hundred people sitting still, in the dark, not speaking to each other, for three hours; and about the actor as someone whose trade is being someone else for money.
4. His counter-proposal instead of a ban: the open-air festival where the crowd is itself the show. He ends by imagining basically a stake stuck in the ground in a public square, people gathered round it, and each other as the spectacle.
