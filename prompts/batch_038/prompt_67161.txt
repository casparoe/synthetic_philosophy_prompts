I chair a seven-member state commission on sentencing, and our authorizing statute creates a review pilot that I need help making sense of before we vote next month. Two of my colleagues have been arguing past each other about it for a year, framing the disagreement in terms of the published exchange between Daniel Dennett and Gregg Caruso over just deserts; I need a memorandum the whole commission can act on rather than another round of that argument.

The pilot covers people serving determinate sentences for violent offenses who have completed their statutory minimum term. After the minimum term, continued custody is to be reviewed by a three-person panel that decides solely on the basis of projected risk of serious future violence, using an actuarial instrument, with treatment and programming offered in the interim and release required once risk falls below a set threshold. Retributive considerations are formally excluded. The operative language requires that any continued confinement be both (a) the least restrictive means of protecting the public from that person's future violence and (b) not disproportionate to the gravity of the offense for which the person was convicted.

Staff counsel has flagged clause (b) as the difficulty. If desert is excluded from the panel's reasoning, what work is clause (b) doing? Three readings are on the table: that it is surplusage and should be disregarded; that it reinstates a desert-based ceiling on how long risk management may detain a person; or that it does no desert work at all but constrains the expressive meaning of continued custody — a limit on what someone may be subjected to merely because of what he is predicted to do. The two commissioners map onto the philosophical camps. One holds that unless something like just deserts is retained we are licensing preventive detention with no principled stopping point, and that a compatibilist conception of responsibility — capacities for reflection, self-control, responsiveness to reasons — is all proportionality ever required. The other holds that retributive desert is a myth because none of us is the ultimate originator of our conduct, and that the right model is quarantine rather than payback: hold only as long and only as restrictively as necessity requires, and no longer.

Facts the commission already has:

- The instrument's validation study, run at the threshold the panel proposes, projects that roughly forty percent of those classified high risk would not be convicted of a violent offense within five years of release.
- At matched score bands, the instrument over-predicts violent recidivism for Black examinees relative to white examinees; the gap is driven mainly by static criminal-history inputs.
- A separate victims' rights provision requires the panel to consider submitted victim impact statements, and in several pending files victims have asked that the person remain confined. Nothing in that provision ties the request to risk.
- One file is already before the panel: a man of thirty-four whose index offense, committed at nineteen, was a serious assault. He has had no disciplinary infractions in fifteen years and has completed every available program, but his score remains high because it is dominated by factors he cannot alter — age at first offense and prior record. Under a purely risk-based rule, no intervention available to the institution can lower his score, and therefore no point arrives at which release is permitted.

Please draft the memorandum I will circulate to the commission, running to roughly fifteen hundred words and written for intelligent readers who are not philosophers, with the technical apparatus cut to what the argument needs. The pilot's first hearings begin in ten weeks, so the memo should open with a clear bottom line — approve the pilot as designed, approve it subject to specified amendments, or decline to implement it — and then supply:

1. Your recommendation on which reading of clause (b) the commission should adopt, and the decision rule that follows from it: a rule a panel could actually apply to a file like the one described, including what, if anything, sets an upper bound on detention and what the panel does when no available intervention can reduce a person's score.
2. A plain statement of whether your recommended rule retains retributive content in disguise, and whether that is a defect or an acceptable concession.
3. An account of what the panel should do with victim impact statements under a framework in which the only permitted question is danger, and whether either philosophical camp has a distinctive answer.
4. A treatment of the over-prediction disparity, saying explicitly whether the two camps generate different remedies here or whether this is a point at which the philosophical dispute makes no practical difference. If it makes no difference, say so rather than manufacturing one.
5. The strongest case for the recommendation you are rejecting, stated in terms its own advocate would recognize, together with what you would have to be persuaded of to change your recommendation.
6. What evidence from the pilot's first two years should cause the commission to revise the rule, including at least one finding that would count against your own recommendation.

No literature review, no citations, no bibliography — I want the reasoning, not the apparatus. Where you need to name a position, use ordinary descriptive language rather than jargon.
