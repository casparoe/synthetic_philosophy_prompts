I write a science-and-ideas column for a mid-size website — mostly psychology and neuroscience, some physics fad pieces, the occasional thing about games and AI. Last week I was interviewing a woman who's sold a couple million copies of books about mindfulness and letting go of regret, and I pushed her on it: doesn't telling people to stop ruminating amount to telling them their losses aren't worth anything? Her answer was that the past doesn't exist anymore and the future doesn't exist yet, so the only real thing is this moment, and regret is therefore literally about nothing. Said it in the tone that means the interview is over.

I couldnt tell then and still can't tell whether that sentence is a deep claim, a trivial one, or a switch being flipped mid-sentence. I went and read up on presentism versus the block universe afterward and it got worse, because the physicists I normally quote for that beat apparently think something closer to the opposite (something Einstein wrote to a friend's family about the past-present-future distinction being an illusion — I'd want to check the exact wording before printing it). And the same line keeps turning up doing completely different jobs in my inbox: meditation teachers, physics popularisers, grief forums, and one argument I covered about whether deleting an AI that has memories of yesterday harms it.

So can you lay out the menu of precise claims that 'only the present is real' could be standing for? I want the readings pulled apart and written as propositions I could actually argue with: the indexical one, the one about what exists, the one about whether time passes or things move through it, the ones about what we can experience or act on, and the ones about what matters. For each, say whether it's trivially true, genuinely contested, or just false, and flag which ones an eternalist would sign off on as cheerfully as a presentist — I suspect that's where the slogan gets its false air of profundity.

Then tell me which reading, if any, actually gets her from that premise to 'regret is about nothing', and whether she needs any metaphysics at all to run the argument or whether she's borrowing the authority of physics for something that's really a claim about value or attention. And if you think one specific equivocation is doing the work in the room — one word meaning two different things across her sentence — name it, because that's the follow-up question I want to walk into the next interview with.
