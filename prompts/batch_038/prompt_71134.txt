I'm a novelist, not a philosopher, so bear with me — I've fallen down a research rabbit hole for a book and come out holding a view I'd like stress-tested rather than flattered.

The book: three years in, the narrator is a teenager who gets handed a diagnosis about halfway through, and the whole second half turns on what that label lets her become. Somewhere in the reading I picked up Hacking's stuff on looping effects — the idea that classifying human kinds doesn't just describe people but recruits them, and that the people so classified change the classification in turn — and I've sharpened it into something I've started thinking of as the three-legs condition. On my version, for a category to actually loop you need (1) a public name that travels, (2) a gatekeeping institution that hands the name out with real consequences attached, and (3) a community around it that the labelled person can join and take the label up as an identity. My claim is that all three are necessary: a category missing any one leg produces at most some labelling effects but no loop, and that's why the loop is unstoppable where it gets going and never gets going where it doesnt.

What would count against this? I want the map of its exposure, not a defence of it.

Specifically, I'd like you to deal with: cases where the loop seems to run on fewer than three legs — Hacking's own transient mental illnesses are my biggest worry here, since as far as I can tell nobody ever joined a fugue community or wore the diagnosis as an identity, and yet the category plainly shaped who turned up in the clinic; whether leg (2) does any work that isn't already done by leg (1), or whether I've just double-counted; whether the same three-legged machinery runs for kinds outside psychiatry entirely, and if so whether that shows my conditions are too permissive or badly underdescribed for what's actually distinctive about psychiatric kinds; what someone working in psychiatric epidemiology, or in the autism literature, would point to as data that would embarrass me; and whether there's a slide from my view into plain social constructionism on one side, or into the trivially true claim that words affect people on the other — in which case I've said nothing at all.
