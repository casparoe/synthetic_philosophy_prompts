I host a fortnightly podcast on the philosophy of mind. I should say at the outset that I have no academic training in the subject. I spent my working life as a structural engineer and came to this material as a reader in my fifties, working slowly through the general surveys and the free encyclopaedia entries on my own, so my grasp of the terrain is real but self-taught and uneven. My listeners are much like me: intelligent, curious, untrained. Questions that would play well in a departmental seminar frequently land badly on the show, because they assume a shared vocabulary my audience does not have.

Next week my guest is a philosopher who defends an actualist higher-order thought theory of phenomenal consciousness. As I understand her position: a perceptual or bodily state is conscious only in virtue of some further mental state that represents it; that further state is not itself conscious, at least not in the same direct way; and, unlike the earliest versions of the theory, hers requires that the higher-order representation attribute its target to the subject herself, as her own state. She has also argued that the brain-imaging evidence pointing to prefrontal rather than posterior regions as the seat of awareness favours her account over first-order accounts.

She is a fluent and charming interviewee, which is precisely the difficulty. When pressed with a case in which the higher-order representation misfires or seems to be absent altogether, her habitual reply is that the objector has confused a mental state with the representation of that state. When someone objects that nobody ever notices any such higher-order state going on in themselves, she replies that her theory never required anyone to notice it, since the higher-order state is supposed to be unconscious. Both deflections are available to her no matter what question is put, so I need questions that make them expensive to deploy.

Please draft my running order for the interview: fifteen questions in total, organised in the sections below and in this order. Aim for roughly 1,800 to 2,200 words for the whole document.

For every question, give me four things: the question itself, phrased as I would speak it aloud on air and no longer than about sixty words, with any technical term glossed in the same breath; one sentence headed Exposure saying what the question is meant to expose; one or two sentences headed Likely reply giving the answer she is most likely to give; and one sentence headed Follow-up containing a second question keyed specifically to that reply, which I can use if she gives it. Where her best reply genuinely wins the exchange, say so plainly under Likely reply and tell me to move on. I would rather know in advance which fights I lose. No generic questions of any kind: nothing about how she came to the field, nothing about what consciousness has taught her about being human.

Orientation. About 150 words, for my private preparation rather than for air. State her commitments as precisely as you can, and identify the two pressure points on which the rest of the interview should turn. Include one warning about a mistake I am likely to make when introducing her.

Empty higher-order thoughts. Three questions. Cases in which the higher-order representation has no target at all: confabulation, the subject who is distressed by a pain-like state she does not actually have, and the blindsight subject who insists she saw nothing while guessing correctly about the stimulus. At least one question must ask whether such a subject is thereby in a conscious state that resembles pain, and at least one must ask what the felt character of that state is supposed to be made of, given that the first-order state it represents is missing.

The idle first-order state. Two questions. If the higher-order representation is doing all the phenomenal work, what explanatory work is left for its target, and why is it not simpler to say that only the higher-order representations exist? I want her to name something the target state contributes that a subject could in principle detect the loss of.

Ownership. Three questions, on her self-attribution clause. Work in two cases of my own invention, which you may sharpen as you see fit. In the first, a shared cortical implant causes a subject's higher-order representation to be driven by another person's nociceptive activity, and it represents that activity as her own; on her theory she has a conscious pain, though she has no pain. In the second, a subject's higher-order representation tracks her own visual state accurately, but a disorder of bodily self-representation means it carries no ownership tag at all. Ask whether the clause is doing independent explanatory work or merely plugging a hole, and whether a subject who must represent a state as her own before she can be conscious of it has simply been handed the first-person character the theory was supposed to explain.

Animals, infants, and the conceptual bill. Three questions. Whether a dog needs concepts in order to have experiences; the mismatch between how finely we discriminate and how coarsely we can describe what we discriminate; and, the one I care about most, if she answers that the higher-order representation can be rich, non-conceptual and non-linguistic, what then makes it higher-order rather than just another perceptual state directed at the world.

The missing witness. Two questions, on the regress and its evidential consequences. Since she is never aware of her higher-order states except by having yet further states about them, and since everything anyone could ever learn about her consciousness reaches us through her reports, is there any single case in which her theory and a first-order rival would come apart?

Evidence and the closer. Two questions, the second of which is the last thing I say on air. The first should pin down what finding she would accept as telling against her view, and in particular whether a well-documented subject with a targetless higher-order representation who is nonetheless in no conscious state would refute her. The second is the closing question, and it must be phrased so that neither of her two standard deflections can be used to answer it.
