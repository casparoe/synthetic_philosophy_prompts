Right, so in about three weeks I'm giving a 30-minute talk at a workshop on meaning and representation in ML, and I could really use a prep sheet for the Q&A.

The audience: maybe 35 people, mostly ML/NLP researchers who use "the model represents X" as harmless engineering shorthand and will get twitchy if I sound like I'm telling them their vocabulary is illegitimate; two philosophers of mind — one a fairly orthodox Dennettian who'll think I'm on their side but overplaying it, one who defends something like Searle's line that nothing whose states are merely interpretable can have content in its own right; and a semanticist who mostly cares about compositionality and will want to know what my view says about embedded contexts.

The thesis, roughly. The two-bitser is normally read as a point about history or wiring: the very same physical state is a quarter-detector in one setup and a quarter-or-balboa-detector in another, so nothing intrinsic to the device fixes what it's about. I want to push it somewhere Dennett didn't go — content isn't fixed by the device's past, it's fixed by the space of alternatives it's actually coupled to right now, in the environment its deployed in. Train a coin discriminator for the US, ship it to Panama, and the correct description changes not because of anything that happened to the machine but because of what's in front of it. Applied to language models, my claim (I've been calling it the Panama thesis) is that a deployed model's states get their content at deployment time rather than training time: the same weights are about different things depending on which counterfactuals the interface actually lets it discriminate and which responses the surrounding pipeline rewards.

Then the bit that will get me in trouble. I argue this makes derived intentionality not a second-class kind at all, because there's no residual original intentionality anywhere for the derivation to bottom out in — on the same logic the humans doing the interpreting are themselves quarter-detectors who got relocated to a wider environment. So "the model means such-and-such" is a plain fact about coupling, not a useful fiction, but its also not a fact you can discover by looking inside the model.

Two corollaries I want to defend: (a) the disjunction problem is a non-problem, since disjunctive content is just what you get when you describe a device from outside its home environment, which means so-called misrepresentation is environment-relative correctness; (b) any probe or interpretability result that reads content off internal activations without saying which deployment it's relative to is underdetermined. I want to use (b) as a friendly amendment to representation-level analysis, not an attack on it, and I need to sound like I mean that.

Give me the objections this room will actually raise — six to eight, ordered by how much damage they'd do to the talk rather than by how interesting they are. Phrase each one the way the person most likely to raise it would phrase it: the ML person's version of "isn't this unfalsifiable" sounds nothing like the Searlean's version, and the Dennettian's objection will be that I've thrown away something they wanted to keep. For each, give me a reply I can deliver in about ninety seconds, what I should concede up front before I'm forced into it, and where my reply is softest. Also tell me which objection is really a version of the "you've just relabelled instrumentalism" charge and whether there's an honest answer to it, and flag the single question I'm dreading and hoping nobody asks — with my best shot at it anyway.
