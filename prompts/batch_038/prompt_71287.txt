Been going down a rabbit hole on Libya 2011 and I think I've hit a wall I can't get past on my own. The story I keep running into: R2P's language from the 2005 summit is what licensed the operation, NATO stretched "protect civilians" into helping the rebels take Tripoli, and that betrayal is why Russia and China have vetoed basically everything on Syria since. Ok. But here's the bit that doesn't fit for me — the Security Council resolution that actually authorized the force already had a fence built into it: it allows "all necessary measures" to protect civilians "while excluding a foreign occupation force of any form on any part of Libyan territory." So whoever writes these texts plainly knows how to rule out an outcome they dont want, in explicit words, in the operative clause.

So why was there never an equivalent fence against regime change? Two options as I see it and I cant tell which is true. Either it was a drafting failure — they just couldn't agree what regime change even means mid-civil-war (does arming the rebels count? does recognizing the transitional council count? does refusing to negotiate with Gaddafi count?) — or the line is unwritable in principle, because when the thing threatening the civilians *is* the government's army and air force, then "protect them" and "destroy the capacity that's threatening them" collapse into the same order and no clause can separate them. Which is it?

And a follow-on: would a sunset trigger actually do better — say the authorization lapses automatically the moment the threat to the named cities is broken, so the mission can't drift into the endgame — or does that just relocate the discretion to whoever gets to declare that the threat is broken?
