I teach one fifty-minute discussion section a week for a first-year world-history survey: twenty-five non-majors, whiteboard only, no slides. Next term the course enters a three-case unit on societal collapse — the palace-centred states of the eastern Mediterranean around 1200 BCE, the Classic Maya city-states of the southern lowlands, and Rapa Nui — and my section falls in the week before any of them is taught. My brief is therefore not to teach a case but to install an instrument. I want the students to arrive at the three cases already able to ask, of any candidate society, what precisely is being claimed to have ended. Please do not explain the concept to me. Plan the fifty minutes.

**1. Order of ideas.** Give me the sequence with the dependency reasons attached: what must be in place before the next piece can be heard at all. By the end I want students able to say that "did X collapse?" is unanswerable until you specify which of several things you are tracking — population, language, territory, the political system, elite institutions, everyday subsistence practices, people's own sense of who they are — and that these come apart. Tell me what has to precede that result and in what order. One piece I expect to include is the point that the archaeological record is systematically biased toward what elites built in stone and what administrations wrote down, so the loss of a palace or a script registers loudly while the persistence of a way of life registers as silence. Say where that belongs and why it cannot come earlier or later.

**2. The carrying case.** I am considering the dissolution of the Soviet Union, on the grounds that every student will grant that it collapsed and no student will say that Russia ceased to exist, so the gap between those two judgements does the work for me. It also lets me show a single event registering as liberation for some and catastrophe for others, and it gives me a way in to the argument that shedding an administration whose costs exceed its returns can be a rational choice for the people inside it rather than a disaster visited on them. Assess the choice instead of accepting it: what must a case have, and what must it lack, to carry this idea? Does mine qualify? What would you use instead, if anything — bearing in mind the case may not be one of the three the unit covers later, since I do not want to spend them in advance.

**3. Misconceptions, and where each is headed off.** I predict four: that collapse means the people died; that collapse is always self-inflicted environmental stupidity; that collapse is one event with one cause; and that if some institutions survive, nothing collapsed. Add the ones I have missed, including especially any misconception my own sequence generates. I am most worried about the deflationary overcorrection — students concluding that collapse is merely a word, that nothing ends and everything just changes — so tell me what that error costs and how the section keeps the concept's teeth.

**4. A counterweight.** Recommend one case where the honest answer really is that something ended, and explain why it resists the deflation rather than simply being grim.

**5. Two judgement calls.** First: I have heard it claimed that in some post-collapse settings skeletal indicators of physiological stress suggest ordinary people were better off after the political breakdown than before it, and I am not confident how far that generalises. Advise whether to raise it at this level, and if so how to frame it without overstating it. Second: students will want to spend the closing ten minutes asking whether our own society is collapsing. Tell me whether to admit that question, when, and how to keep it from becoming an unfalsifiable argument.

**6. The exit check.** Give me one question to ask at the end, and tell me what a merely agreeable answer sounds like as distinct from an answer showing the student can now operate the distinction. The question has to separate students who understood the point from students who simply enjoyed the debunking.

Finally, list what I should deliberately leave out and be willing to say I am leaving out. No reading list, please — I am not assigning anything.
