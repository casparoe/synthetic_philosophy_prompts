I run a philosophy-of-science reading group and we've been stuck on Hempel's ravens for two months now, which is embarassing because we all accept the Bayesian answer. So here's what I want you to chew on carefully, as someone who has actually argued about this: the calculation settles the arithmetic — a white shoe does confirm "all ravens are black," just by a negligibly small amount, since non-black things hugely outnumber ravens — and yet nobody in the group thinks the puzzle is thereby dissolved. We've drifted into three camps and I genuinely can't tell what we're disagreeing about. Camp one says the calculation vindicates Hempel's own verdict, so Nicod's criterion was just wrong and we're done. Camp two says the question was malformed from the start, because "confirms" was never a context-free relation between a sentence and an observation — what a white shoe does depends on your background knowledge and on the sizes of the populations, so in a small, nearly-exhausted domain the shoe is *not* negligible, which means the standard "dissolution" is really a claim about which situations we were tacitly imagining. Camp three thinks both of those duck the real issue, which is whether looking at non-ravens can ever tell you anything about ravens given how you'd actually go about sampling them.

Start by spelling out what each camp is committed to, then diagnose: is this a substantive disagreement, or is "confirmation" doing three different jobs — a logical relation between propositions, a shift in degree of belief, a property of an evidence-gathering procedure — with us talking past each other under one word? I lean hard toward the second, so please argue with me rather than agree. And whatever you land on, tell me what would actually count as progress here: a distinction, a formal result, a change of question, or an honest "nothing, and here's why." Skip the survey of proposed solutions — we've all read it twice.
