You are to write as a philosopher of mathematics who has taught Gödel's incompleteness theorems to audiences without mathematical training many times, and who is equally comfortable saying that a proposed analogy is actively harmful. Think carefully before answering, and treat the specifics of my situation as constraints rather than as decoration.

Some background, so that you know what you are planning for. I spent thirty-six years as an analytical chemist, most of it in method validation: calibration curves, reference standards, establishing that an instrument's readings can be trusted by procedures that do not themselves depend on the instrument. Since retiring I have worked my way through a fair amount of philosophy, and the trustees of our county library have asked me to lead a ninety-minute session on the incompleteness theorems for their Thursday evening discussion circle. Fourteen people, mostly retirees, no slides, one whiteboard with a working tray of markers, and a good deal of tea. Roughly half of them have met incompleteness only through popular books and magazine articles; two are quite committed to particular readings of it. One is certain that the theorems refute the possibility of machine understanding, on the grounds that a mind can see the truth of a sentence its own formal machinery cannot prove. The other holds that incompleteness is the mathematical counterpart of the measurement problem in quantum mechanics, and that both express the same limit on observation from within. I expect both of them to speak early and often.

My draft plan is this, and I would like you to assess it before you replace or repair it. I intend to open by handing round a card bearing a sentence that says of itself that it is not true, and to let the group feel the vertigo. I would then introduce the notion of a formal system and of effective axiomatization; then Gödel numbering, as the trick that lets a system talk about its own sentences; then the first theorem; then the second; and finally a closing list of things the theorems do not show, which is where I meant to deal with my two enthusiasts. My worry is that the closing list arrives too late — that by the twentieth minute the card trick has already licensed exactly the conclusions I want to deny, and that a list of negations at the end cannot undo an image planted at the start. Tell me whether that worry is well founded.

The central thing I want your judgement on is an analogy of my own devising, which I have not yet tried on anyone. I want to say that a formal system is like an instrument whose calibration cannot be certified by the instrument itself: you always need a standard external to it, or a higher-order procedure, and no amount of cleverness lets the device validate its own validation. My suspicion is that this is worse than useless, because it invites the audience to hear "external standard" as "something beyond the system altogether," which is precisely the slide my first enthusiast wants to make. I would rather you told me plainly where the analogy holds, where it fails, and whether a repair exists or whether I should abandon it and carry the session on a different example. If you propose a replacement, specify what the example must have and what it must lack.

Two further points I want addressed explicitly. First, I am tempted to make the second theorem bite by observing that an inconsistent theory proves everything, including a statement of its own consistency, so that the theorem is not a lament about limits but a real constraint with teeth. Is that honest at this level, or a trap that will be heard as a claim that consistency is unfounded? Second, how should I present the fact that the undecidable sentence becomes decidable once one adds a suitable axiom — the stepping-outside move — without handing it to the machine-understanding argument as a premise?

Deliver, in this order: a short brainstorming section in which you consider at least three candidate carrying examples and say which you reject and why; then the teaching plan itself — the order in which the pieces must go in, with what depends on what; a map of the misconceptions this audience will bring and the ones my own presentation will manufacture, each tagged with the point in the sequence where it must be headed off; one question I can put to the room near the end that would show me whether the idea has landed rather than merely been enjoyed; and a list of what I should openly refuse to explain at this level, with the sentence I should use to refuse it. Do not offer me a bibliography or any reading list, and do not cite sources; I want the plan to stand on reasoning alone. Do not soften your assessment of my analogy.
