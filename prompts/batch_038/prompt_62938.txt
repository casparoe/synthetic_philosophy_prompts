ok this has been eating my brain for a week and I want to see if anyone can make it concrete instead of just telling me it's all verbal.

I work at a small tissue-engineering startup. We grow cartilage and skin constructs — we seed a patient's own cells onto a porous polymer scaffold, drop it in a bioreactor, and over about six weeks the cells chew through most of the scaffold and lay down their own extracellular matrix in its place. Operationally each construct is one thing: one lot number, one inventory row, one line item on the invoice, one device in the regulatory submission.

I've been reading about the special composition question, and van Inwagen's answer in particular — roughly, that some things compose a further thing only when their activity adds up to a life, so on that view there are no tables or statues or piles of sand, just organisms (plus simples arranged tablewise and so on). Our constructs land in a genuinely weird spot. They're made entirely of living cells, but the whole construct doesn't look like an organism: nothing about the construct as a whole is self-maintaining, it doesn't metabolize as a unit, if you take it out of the media feed it just sits there and slowly dies back. It's more like an extremely well-organized heap of cells that happens to be held in shape by a plastic lattice we bought from a supplier.

Two people here already disagree about what follows. One says the construct is just cells arranged constructwise, so on that view there's no construct at all and our whole ERP system is cataloguing things that don't exist. The other says the cells are each alive, so a construct is really a crowd of tiny organisms and it only becomes a genuine single thing once it's implanted and integrated into a patient — i.e. our product literally doesn't exist until it leaves the building.

Please describe the most important implications of taking this seriously for how I actually run things. Specifically:

- What's the construct, if not an object? Is there a respectable way to keep a single lot number while admitting nothing unified corresponds to it, or does that just collapse into pretending?
- Where's the boundary case during those six weeks? Scaffold plus cells, then scaffold being replaced — is there a moment composition starts, and does gradual replacement of the scaffold matter differently than gradual replacement of the cells?
- Would mereological nihilism or universalism actually be *easier* for inventory purposes? I keep half-joking about what I've started calling inventory nihilism, where the only rows in the system are living patients and everything else is a location plus a quantity. Is that coherent, and does it cost me anything I care about (traceability, liability, recall)?
- Does any of this bite on the regulatory side — like, is there a difference between declaring a device and declaring an arrangement of cells?
- And where have I mis-stated the view? I'm especially unsure whether single cells count as lives in the relevant sense, since that seems to decide which of my two coworkers is right.

I'm not looking for a defense of any position, I want to know what would change at my desk on Monday if I actually believed this stuff.
