I would like to defend a fairly strong claim about the impossibility results in algorithmic fairness, and I want you to tell me what would refute it. Please write the answer in British English.

The claim is this. The familiar incompatibility results — the one showing that calibration within groups, balance for the positive class and balance for the negative class cannot all hold outside degenerate cases, and the companion result showing that predictive parity, equal false positive rates and equal false negative rates cannot all hold when outcome rates differ across groups — are not artefacts of a clumsy formalisation that better philosophy could dissolve. On my reading they are the surface form of something structural. Given unequal base rates, any group-blind mapping from a score to a decision operates under a fixed budget of error: satisfying one criterion is achieved only by transferring the violation to another, and no improvement in the statistical quality of the score enlarges that budget. I want to keep two senses distinct. Robustness in criterion-space: the impossibility survives widening the admissible criteria to anything that depends only on the joint distribution of score, outcome and group membership, together with mild monotonicity. Robustness in architecture-space: the impossibility survives changes to the decision problem itself — richer or continuous decisions, randomised and group-dependent post-processing, better-measured outcomes, repeated deployment. I hold the first confidently and the second only provisionally, and part of what I want from you is a verdict on whether the second can be made to hold at all.

Please do the following.

First, give me the defeaters, ranked from fatal to merely awkward, and for each say precisely what the mathematics or the world would have to look like for it to bite. Address at least: the objection that the trilemma is a counting fact about a two-parameter family of thresholds rather than a theorem; the possibility that randomised group-dependent thresholds evade the impossibility rather than relocate it, and what the evasion costs in calibration and in ex post individual unfairness; the move from binary decisions to graded ones (sentence length, credit limit, interest rate) under a welfare-weighted objective, and whether that genuinely creates slack or merely redraws a Pareto frontier; noisy or biased outcome measurement, including the uncomfortable case where correcting the proxy widens measured disparity; decision-relative rather than person-relative scores; intersectional and many-group settings, where small cells may behave differently from the two-group case; and dynamic settings in which the decision changes the base rate over time, so that some policy converges on a world where the criteria are jointly satisfiable. For each, tell me whether it is live or merely logically possible.

Second, say which of these a committed defender of my claim would concede as counting against it and which they would dismiss, and on what grounds. Then state what the claim predicts that its rivals do not — patterns that should show up in the relationship between base-rate ratios, model accuracy and error-rate gaps — and assess whether those predictions are risky or compatible with anything that could turn up.

Third, be blunt about whether the phrase mild monotonicity conditions has hedged the claim into near-vacuity: is there any conceivable criterion the claim excludes? If the position is well insulated, say whether that is a defect for a thesis whose content is supposed to be that the trade-offs are structural rather than conventional.

I am most easily persuaded by constructions rather than arguments. If you think the claim is false, the most useful thing you can offer is an explicit family of decision problems with unequal base rates in which some group-blind rule satisfies all the standard criteria simultaneously, or an argument that the error budget strictly increases with information. No references or reading lists, please; I want the reasoning.
