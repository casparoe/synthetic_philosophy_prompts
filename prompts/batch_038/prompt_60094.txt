We volunteer on the review panel for a small institute — think biosecurity plus tech forecasting, ~20 people, mostly grad students and postdocs, money from a donor who keeps telling us to be nimble. One of the things the panel does is hear requests from members who want to go off and do something on their own initiative: publish a thing, release a tool, warn somebody, pass along information they aren't supposed to have. Two years of that has produced a track record, and neither of us can agree on what rule we've actually been applying. The record as best I remember it:

- A member found what he was confident was an exploitable bug in a widely used open-source crypto library and wanted to publish a working demonstration. We said no, not until the maintainers had been given time to patch. Nobody on the panel disputed that his demo was accurate.
- A member was certain a collaborator had fabricated a dataset and deleted the files before anyone else could inspect them. The data did turn out to be fabricated. We censured her anyway; the minutes say something about her having destroyed a thing other people had a right to examine.
- A member posted a preprint the same day he finished it, claiming his method beat the standard baseline, no internal review. We waved it through, on the grounds that anybody could check it inside a week and the worst case was a few people wasting a few days on a failed replication.
- A member had done informal testing suggesting a consumer device was more dangerous than the regulator's approval implied. Huge fight. We landed on: he could post his raw measurements, but not his characterization of how risky the device was.
- A member wanted to quietly tip off a friend that the company about to hire her was doing something genuinely bad, based on information he got from a source he wouldn't name. We blocked it. Minutes again gesture at the people being affected not being in a position to weigh in.

Now the new case, which is what we've been screaming about. One of our members has spent a year building a cheap field assay for a crop fungus — the kind that takes out a whole harvest if you notice it late. Her test catches it maybe six weeks earlier than anything commercially available. She's run it on roughly 200 of her own samples with results she'd bet the house on. Nobody outside the lab has validated any of it, and the standard agronomy folks woud say her sample is too small and her controls are homemade. The protocol isn't dangerous in itself — you can't weaponize a color-change assay read by a phone camera. She doesn't want to publish yet. She wants to hand the written protocol plus a batch of kits to four farms she personally trusts, in a valley she thinks is about to get hit, so they can start culling this season. Those four farms have said yes enthusiastically, though they've heard her pitch and not seen the data. If the test throws false positives, they cull healthy plants and eat maybe 15% of a season's revenue. If it throws false negatives and they trust it, they don't cull and the fungus gets the valley, including farms that never heard about any of this. Also: a company is circling her, and if she goes the normal publication-plus-validation route she's fairly sure a slower, pricier commercial test gets there first and her version dies.

So — what should our panel decide if it wants to be consistent with itself, and which of the five is doing the most work as the closest analog? My roommate thinks the whole record collapses into one principle, roughly "go ahead when the harm is contained and other people can check you quickly, don't when third parties carry the risk," and I think that's at least two principles in a trench coat and we've just been reaching for whichever one gives the answer we already liked. Tell me who's right. Lay out the candidate rules that fit all five precedents — I suspect there's an epistemic-deference one ("don't act alone when your confidence outruns the relevant experts' by more than they can close cheaply") and maybe a "it isn't yours to give away" one, and I want to know whether either actually fits everything or whether I'm pattern-matching — then say what each rule says about the assay case, whether they genuinely disagree there or just sound like they do, and which single sixth case we could have put to the panel that would have separated them. One extra wrinkle I keep losing the argument on: my roommate says this is a textbook unilateralist's curse situation, and I say the curse is about someone being alone in thinking an action is a good idea, whereas she's alone in thinking an empirical claim is true, and those are different failures with different fixes. Is that a real distinction or am I just defending her because I like her?
