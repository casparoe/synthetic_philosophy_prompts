ok so I'm a junior and I run the philosophy club at my school, and I've gotten way too into the extended mind thing (Clark and Chalmers, the Otto-with-the-notebook case). The problem is our meetings just dissolve into people yelling intuitions at each other. One guy is like obviously your phone is literally part of your mind now. Another guy is like fine then the whole school library is part of your brain, which is absurd, so the entire view is dead. And I've realized nobody in the room actually has a shared idea of what a *good* theory of this would even look like, so there's nothing to argue about yet.

So before I try to referee anything: write me the spec. What does a theory of cognitive extension have to actually deliver to count as adequate? I want the full requirements list, and for each one the case or consideration that motivates it — not a summary of the debate, the criteria.

Stuff I suspect belongs on the list but I want you to tell me if I'm wrong:
- it has to say why Otto's notebook counts and a random novel sitting on his shelf doesn't
- it can't just make everything cognitive (the library problem)
- it has to explain why the skin-and-skull boundary ever felt like a meaningful boundary, or admit it never was one
- it has to cover more than belief/memory, because my club only ever talks about remembering facts

Cases the list has to handle, including three that are mine and not from any paper:
1. My grandma can drive all over her town perfectly, but only when my grandpa's in the passenger seat. Alone she gets lost two blocks from home. He never tells her directions, he just asks her things.
2. Autocorrect on my phone finishes my texts sometimes in a way I would 100% have written. When that happens, whose sentence is it?
3. I can only recite the periodic table if I trace the rows on my desk with my finger. Hold my hand still and it's just gone.
4. Plus whatever you think of the standard notebook case, and something involving an AI tutor since that's basically how I do homework now and I refuse to believe that one's simpler than grandma.

Then the part I care about most: sort the requirements into ones that are genuinely non-negotiable versus ones that are just a camp's pet prejudice wearing a requirement costume. I'm pretty sure half of what people demand of these theories smuggles in their conclusion, and I want you to call those out by name. Also tell me if any two requirements can't be satisfied together — if the list itself proves something's gotta give, spell that out, that would be a real result. And finish with which single requirement knocks out the most candidate theories.

Don't bother scoring the existing views against the list, I'm gonna make my club do that, that's the fun part.

Format: one JSON object, exactly these fields and nothing else —
- "desiderata": array of objects, each with "name" (short label), "requires" (what the theory has to do, one or two sentences), "motivating_case" (the concrete case or consideration behind it, plain english), "status" (one of "non-negotiable", "negotiable", "probably-a-prejudice"), "status_reason" (why it goes in that bucket)
