I am in the final unit of a cognitive science seminar on the varieties of minds, and for our last session I have volunteered to produce a handout that lets the class sort real systems into Dennett's four kinds of creatures — Darwinian, Skinnerian, Popperian, and Gregorian. I would like you to write that handout as a decision tree, to the following specifications, because I think the usual presentation of the taxonomy is quietly doing something I want to avoid.

The usual presentation is a ladder: natural selection fixes the response repertoire; operant conditioning adds a second layer; an internal selection process over candidate actions adds a third, so that a bad guess costs the guess rather than the guesser; and external, culturally manufactured symbols and tools add a fourth. Each level is supposed to include the ones below it. I want the tree built instead on the hypothesis that these are four independent questions about *where the selecting happens* — in the gene pool, in the individual's own history of rewards and punishments, inside the individual's head, or in a pool of artifacts and other minds the individual draws on — and that the ladder ordering is a contingent fact about terrestrial animals rather than part of the definitions. The tree should therefore terminate not in a single label but in a four-slot profile recording which layers the system has and which one is doing most of the work; you should then map profiles back onto the four canonical names and either name or explicitly decline to name the non-canonical combinations.

Requirements:

1. At most six binary questions. No question may use the words Darwinian, Skinnerian, Popperian, or Gregorian, or any obvious cognate, and each must be answerable by someone who has never encountered the taxonomy — ideally by observation or by a counterfactual intervention, such as: if the system were prevented from being shaped by the consequences of its own behavior over its lifetime, would its repertoire collapse to a fixed set?

2. One question, placed early, must fix the boundary of the thing being classified: the organism or artifact on its own, or the organism together with its scaffolding. I suspect this prior choice changes the answer for several of my cases, and I want the handout to make that visible rather than bury it.

3. Please handle engineered systems honestly. A chess engine, a controller produced by a genetic algorithm in simulation, and a trained language model each have something occupying the first slot, but the selection that produced it was performed by people rather than by natural selection. The tree should say whether that counts, counts differently, or must be bracketed.

4. Run the tree over this list and give the resulting profile for each, marking which placements are confident and which are shaky: a foraging honeybee performing the waggle dance; an octopus that learns by trial and error to open a jar, having been neither taught nor able to teach; a ten-month-old infant; a person with late-stage dementia who can no longer read a recipe or follow an instruction but still cooks a familiar meal; a chess engine with a hand-written evaluation function and brute-force search; a language model frozen after training, with no memory between sessions, but supplied at run time with a scratchpad, tools, and a documentation library; a controller evolved in simulation and then deployed with no further learning; a bump-sensing robot vacuum; a slime mold that finds the shortest route through a maze; and Otto, whose beliefs about bus schedules live in the notebook he carries.

5. After the tree, give a table of the profiles that are logically possible but empty, and for each say whether it is empty because the combination is incoherent, because nothing happens to fill it, or because nobody has looked. I expect at least one of my cases to land in a cell that has no name at all.

6. Flag rather than paper over any pair of profiles your questions cannot in practice separate. I suspect the third layer and a sufficiently rich behavioral history will resist separation, since an internal model may be nothing more than trained associations, and I would rather the handout admit this.

For each nonempty leaf, add one sentence on the most awkward commitment it carries. Keep the whole thing to roughly two pages, with no citations or reference list; I will add those from our syllabus myself. Finally, tell me whether the order of your questions matters. If asking the boundary question second rather than first changes any verdict in item 4, that seems to me to be evidence about whether this taxonomy is really a hierarchy, and I would like the handout to say so in one or two sentences at the end.
