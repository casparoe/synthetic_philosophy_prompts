I'm sitting in on a philosophy of science seminar this term and the prof keeps circling Wigner's 1960 lecture on the unreasonable effectiveness of mathematics without ever letting anyone land it, so two of us stayed after and tried to force a resolution with our own argument on the whiteboard. Now I think its a disaster, because it seems to conclude that the applicability of math is literally a miracle, which nobody in that room would say out loud but also nobody managed to block. Here's what we had, more or less in the order we wrote it:

(1) Either mathematics is a priori — known independently of experience — or it is empirical in something like Mill's sense, i.e. hardened generalizations from observed regularities that we've dressed up as necessary truths. (2) If it's a priori, then there's no reason at all to expect structures mathematicians develop for their own private reasons to line up with the structure of the physical world, so the fact that they do is a coincidence of enormous size. (3) Coincidences of enormous size don't just happen; the correct response to one is to reject whichever assumption generated it. (4) So we reject the a priori horn: mathematics is empirical. (5) But if mathematical claims are empirical, they're answerable to observation, in which case the empirical content of a mathematical claim is just a repackaging of the observations it summarizes — math can't tell us anything the data didn't already contain. (6) Yet it demonstrably does: Dirac's equation came with negative-energy solutions nobody wanted, and the way out was to read them as predicting a positively charged electron, which then turned up in cosmic ray data a few years later; the prediction came out of the formalism, not out of the observations. (7) So (5) is false, hence (4) is false, hence the a priori horn stands, hence by (2) and (3) the fit between mathematics and physics is an unexplained coincidence of enormous size — a miracle, in other words.

Every one of those steps looked fine to us as we wrote it and the conclusion makes me queasy. Two things I can half see and can't develop: (3) needs some way of measuring the size of a coincidence that we never supplied — enormous relative to what background, and who's keeping score? — and (5) seems to slide between "no single experiment could ever overturn 2+2=4" and "mathematics adds nothing to a physical theory," which I'm fairly sure aren't the same claim at all. But I don't know whether patching those two spots stops the slide, or whether the argument is basically valid and I just have to choose the premise I hate least. So: which step do you reject, and what does rejecting it cost? If it's valid, tell me which premise gives the least, keeping in mind that I'd rather hold onto (6) than anything else, since the novel-prediction cases are what make this whole topic feel substantive rather than just aesthetic, and I'd rather give up (3) than (2) because I don't want to be in the business of grading coincidence sizes. And if you think the usual escape routes actually work — a Quinean holist who says math is empirical but only ever tested as part of the whole web, or a Hamming-style deflationist who says we see what we go looking for and only remember the hits — say explicitly what each of them has to pay, and whether either one blocks (7) or just redescribes the problem in friendlier vocabulary.
