I am preparing a seminar case on Dennett's three stances and his real-patterns criterion, and the episode I want pressed into service is the investigation of von Osten's arithmetic horse, together with a contrasting episode from computer chess. I am not after an exegesis of Dennett and not after a retelling of either story; I know both. What I want is the mapping done carefully, in both directions.

So that we are working from the same base, here are the facts I take to be established. A horse exhibited across Germany in the early 1900s appeared to answer arithmetic, calendar, and spelling questions by tapping a hoof. A commission convened by Carl Stumpf reported in 1904 that no trickery was involved. Stumpf's assistant Oskar Pfungst then varied four factors independently: whether horse and questioner were isolated from spectators, whether the questioner was the owner or someone else, whether the horse could see the questioner, and whether the questioner knew the answer in advance. Hans answered correctly roughly nine times in ten when a knowledgeable questioner was in view, and roughly six percent of the time when the questioner was out of view; correct answers tracked the questioner's knowledge, not the questioner's identity. Pfungst's explanation: tension accumulates in the questioner as the taps approach the target and is released, involuntarily and without the questioner's awareness, at the correct tap, and the horse reads the release and stops. Two further details matter to me. Pfungst reproduced the effect in the other direction, taking the horse's part himself with human subjects and detecting their small unconscious head movements at the final tap. And he never achieved the reverse manipulation: he could not deliberately produce the cueing movements in order to make the horse tap out a wrong answer, so the hypothesis was supported by correlation and role reversal rather than by intervention. The owner remained unconvinced and continued exhibiting the animal.

Here is the refinement I want you to work with, and to argue against if you think it mistaken. Treat the real-patterns test — a description earns its keep in proportion to the predictive leverage it buys relative to the descriptive complexity it costs — as answering only whether an ascription pays. It appears to say nothing about a second question, which I will call locus: whether the state the ascription posits is a state of the system to which it is ascribed. Hans looks like a case in which the intentional description pays and mislocates. The pattern is real, compressive, and exploitable; it simply is not in the horse alone, since removing the knowledgeable, visible questioner removes it. The obvious repair is to relocate the ascription to the dyad and to credit the horse with a genuine and finely tuned perceptual competence instead of arithmetic.

Against that background, I want five things.

1. Make the mapping explicit. For each of the three stances, state what description of Hans's performance it yields, what that description predicts, and what it costs. Then answer the harder question: is the intentional description genuinely more efficient than a design-stance description of an animal trained to watch for postural release, or does the design stance already contain everything the intentional stance earns, so that the intentional description pays only for being shorter rather than for being truer?

2. Run the locus question. Does relocating the belief to the dyad rescue intentional realism about Hans, or concede that the stance is bookkeeping? If the dyad move is generally available it seems to license ascriptions to any coupled system; say what constrains it, or admit that nothing does. Also address whether Pfungst's failure to perform the reverse manipulation leaves the owner's original claim merely unproven rather than refuted, and whether Dennett's criterion has the resources to register that distinction at all.

3. Identify where the theory goes silent. In particular: the debunking displaced the intentional ascription rather than eliminating it, and the competence it uncovered in the horse — involuntary-cue detection — is real and remarkable; and the same finding hardened into a standing methodological control in later animal-cognition work. Does the framework have anything to say about the epistemic status of a control that exists because an intentional ascription once misfired, or is that outside its scope?

4. Contrast with the 1997 rematch between Kasparov and Deep Blue. In the first game a bug sent the machine into an unintended loop from which it exited by selecting a random legal move; Kasparov, denied access to the internals, read the apparently pointless move as profound strategic intelligence, and his play deteriorated thereafter. In the second game he detected enough creativity to allege that human players had intervened during play; IBM maintained that human input occurred only between games and refused his request for the machine's log printouts, releasing them publicly only later. Apply the same two questions: did the ascription pay, and where was its locus — in the machine, in the opening book and grandmaster tuning and between-game modifications, or nowhere? State the symmetry sharply. Hans gives a real pattern in the wrong place; Deep Blue gives a pattern in a defensible place that was not real. Then say whether the second case is harder for the framework than the first, given that the design stance existed and was complete but was withheld from the ascriber.

5. Deliver a verdict. Does the Hans case confirm the real-patterns criterion, constitute an anomaly it can absorb, or fall into a category the framework never anticipated? Say precisely what would have to be added to the criterion to handle both episodes, and judge whether the addition is a patch or an insight.

Where Dennett's framework has nothing to say, say so rather than speaking on its behalf. I want analysis, not a reading list.
