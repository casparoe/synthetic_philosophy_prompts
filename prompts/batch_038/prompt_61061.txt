**Student answer:**

Smith's Theory of Moral Sentiments (1759, six editions in his lifetime) opens from an epistemic problem rather than a moral one: I cannot feel your pain. Smith puts it bluntly — "As we have no immediate experience of what other men feel, we can form no idea of the manner in which they are affected, but by conceiving what we ourselves should feel in the like situation." What seperates him from the self-love theories he attacks is that he thinks this failure of access is a limitation of the senses, not of our concern: even the most selfish man has principles in his nature that interest him in the fortune of others.

Mechanically, I think there are two channels. The first and fastest is transmission: the spectator picks the passion up off the agent's face, voice and gesture. Smith himself describes the passions as being "transfused from one man to another, instantaneously, and antecedent to any knowledge of what excited them." The second is the imaginative act quoted above, which is best read as the fallback used when the expression is ambiguous or the cause is hidden: the spectator reconstructs what he would feel in the agent's place and uses that reconstruction to test whether the passion he has caught is an appropriate one. Either way, judging is a matter of feeling: sympathy is by definition an agreeable fellow-feeling, so we approve wherever we sympathise, and the painful cases — grief, terror — are precisely where the impartial spectator has to be brought in to explain why we still call the response proper. This also gives Smith his distinction between propriety, which asks whether the affection suits its cause, and merit, which asks whether the action deserves reward or punishment; approval attaches to the motive and the affection, never to the outcome, which is why he can say we go along with resentment only when we can sympathise with what provoked it, and get justice out of that.

The impartial spectator is where the mechanism turns into a standard. From infancy I recieve praise and blame from the people around me and internalise those reactions until I carry a judge inside me and start viewing my own conduct as an onlooker would. Smith's point is that this internalisation is what makes judgement impartial rather than self-serving: from inside my own situation everything looks larger than it is, and the spectator-standpoint shrinks my grievances back to their real size. So my answer to the third part is yes, largely: the impartial spectator is the sediment of actual spectators' reactions, laid down by repetition until it feels innate. Smith confirms as much in the section on custom and fashion, where he concedes that local habits distort our sentiments of approbation — which only makes sense if those sentiments were built out of social feedback to begin with. What he adds to Hume's general point of view, then, is a developmental history for it.
