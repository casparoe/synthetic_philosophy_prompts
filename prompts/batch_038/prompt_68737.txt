I mark the second-year real analysis problem sets at a mid-sized university, and this year one of them has turned into a teaching problem I don't know how to solve from the front of a lecture hall.

Of thirty-two scripts, eleven proved that a countable union of countable sets is countable by writing, for each member of the family, "fix an enumeration," and then diagonalising down the resulting grid. In the margin I could only write that the step requires Choice, which I now think taught them nothing. Four of them came to my office hours with what seemed to me a perfectly reasonable objection: they had been told repeatedly that finitely many choices are unproblematic, they were making only countably many, and so where exactly was the difficulty? One asked me what would have gone wrong if she had written down an enumeration rather than chosen one, and I did not have an answer that was both honest and comprehensible. Another pointed out, correctly, that we had used exactly the same move earlier in the term without comment, and I could not tell him why that instance was different.

The department has given me an optional ninety-minute workshop slot in week six, ahead of the exam, and twenty-four students have signed up. I would like you to design the session. I did one undergraduate logic course and can follow an independence argument when it is written out slowly, but I have never taught this material and I do not want to improvise it.

What I want them to leave able to distinguish are four things they currently run together: making finitely many choices; making countably many choices simultaneously; making countably many choices each of which is constrained by the ones before it; and making one choice from each member of a family they could never so much as list. And I want them to feel that the pressure point is not the number of choices but whether there is a rule.

Specifically:

- The need for choice must emerge from something the students do, not from anything I say. They should attempt a task and either get stuck, or get away with something in one round and then discover it does not carry over to the next. I would very much like the countable-union proof to be one of the things they attempt and fail to complete, so that my own marking feedback becomes intelligible to them retrospectively.

- Physical or paper materials only — envelopes, index cards, boxes, dice, string, whatever you need. No laptops. Give me the materials list with quantities for twenty-four students, and say how they should be grouped.

- A scoring or incentive scheme, with your reasoning for it. If there are points, they should reward the thing I want rewarded — a written specification that another group can execute and check — and not something accidental like speed or apparent confidence. Please make it explicit that "just pick one arbitrarily" scores nothing, and tell me how to enforce that without being pedantic.

- A minute-by-minute timeline for the ninety minutes, marking the places where I should let a group sit in a confusion that I could resolve in one sentence.

- A debrief that maps each round onto the corresponding distinction, and that closes with the students themselves sorting a set of proofs from my own syllabus by how much choice each genuinely requires. I intend to supply the names Countable Choice, Dependent Choice and full Choice in the debrief and not a moment earlier. The proofs I want classified are: a countable union of countable sets is countable; every infinite set has a countably infinite subset; a nested sequence of nonempty closed subsets of the reals with diameters tending to zero has nonempty intersection; a function on the reals is continuous in the epsilon-delta sense if and only if it is sequentially continuous; and every vector space has a basis. Please be careful here. Not all of these need what they appear to need, and at least one of them can be done with nothing at all, by an argument a second-year should be able to follow. I want your classification to be right and your justification for each entry to be short enough to say aloud.

- An honest forecast of what will go wrong the first time I run it: which round a group will clear by a route I did not intend, which one will dissolve into a debate about whether mathematics is invented, and the student who has read about Banach–Tarski over the weekend and wants to announce that we have refuted Choice. Give me a sentence for each.

- Above all, a candid statement of the limits of the exercise. Everything I have described above teaches students that a choice function cannot be written down, and I am aware that this is not the same thing as its failing to exist. Tell me exactly where the activity risks installing a misconception — confusing definability with existence, or leaving them thinking the issue is about our knowledge rather than about what the axioms license — and what I must say in the final ten minutes to close that gap without converting the workshop into a set theory lecture. If some part of the point simply cannot be conveyed by an activity and has to be asserted by me, say which part and give me the assertion.

I am not after a generic lesson plan that would serve equally well in a discrete mathematics course or a philosophy seminar. I want something built tightly around the specific error my students made and the specific objection they raised when I marked it.
