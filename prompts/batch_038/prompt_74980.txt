I am building a session for a philosophy-of-biology seminar on the problem of organismal individuality, and I intend to use John Carpenter's *The Thing* (1982), the adaptation of Campbell's 1938 novella, as the central case. I would like a reading that treats the film as a serious piece of philosophical argument about what counts as one living thing, rather than as a paranoia thriller that happens to feature a monster.

The premise is that an extraterrestrial organism assimilates other life forms and then imitates them so completely — behavior, speech, memory — that its imitations are indistinguishable from the originals to any ordinary means. Two moments in the film interest me most. The first is the blood test: after the men watch a severed portion of one imitation detach from the corpse it came from and scuttle away under its own power, MacReady reasons that each of the Thing's cells is an independent organism with its own survival instinct, and he therefore touches a heated copper wire to a dish of each man's blood, on the expectation that a sample drawn from an imitation will recoil. One does, and its owner is exposed. The second is the ending: the station is burning, and two survivors sit in the snow sharing a bottle, neither able to establish that the other is not the Thing.

Please work through the following.

1. What criterion of individuality does MacReady's inference actually presuppose? Is he reasoning about parts and wholes, about levels of selection, about the absence of any division of reproductive labor, or about something immunological? Set out the implicit theory and say whether it is the theory the scene's success requires — that is, whether the test would work if the Thing were, for instance, a genuinely unified higher-level individual.

2. Run the Thing against the definitional proposals that structure the "what is life?" debate: the self-sustaining chemical system capable of Darwinian evolution that came out of origins-of-life research; historical or lineage-based definitions on which being alive is a matter of standing in the right genealogical relation to other living things; metabolism- and homeostasis-centered definitions. Which does it satisfy, which does it fail, and does it matter that it has no species-typical morphology, no germ line — every cell being totipotent and reproductively competent — and apparently no fixed boundaries? Blair's computer projection that the creature will assimilate all life on Earth within a few years is a piece of epidemiological modeling; is it coherent given the reproductive strategy the film attributes to it?

3. Decide, if you can, whether the Thing is one organism, a serial colony, a lineage, or a process, and say whether that question is well posed at all. Draw on whatever real biology you think bears on it — composite and symbiotic individuals, dissociated sponge cells that reaggregate, clonally transmissible cancers that behave like independent parasitic organisms, teratomas, rampant horizontal transfer and what it does to the idea of a single tree of life, and the major-transitions account of how new levels of individuality get built and what has to be suppressed for them to hold. The Thing looks like a transition run in reverse; develop or reject that thought.

4. Treat assimilation as a vagueness problem. If the substitution happens cell by cell, when does the original organism cease to exist? Consider Van Inwagen's answer to the special composition question — that some things compose something exactly when their activities constitute a life, so that organisms are the only composite material objects — and say whether the film dramatizes a sorites for that view or whether the view dissolves the film's problem. Then the Lockean question the blood test seems to bypass: if the imitation has the memories, dispositions, and psychological continuity of the person it replaced, in what sense is it not that person? Does the film endorse a biological criterion of identity, or does it merely inherit one from the genre?

5. Say what kind of problem the ending poses. Is MacReady and Childs' stalemate an instance of skepticism about other minds, a problem about the operationalizability of individuality, or something else? Could any test settle it in principle, and would the film survive as a film if it could? You might also consider the opening, in which MacReady loses a chess game to a computer and pours his drink into it, and whether that frames the real question as imitation-without-understanding.

6. Finally, tell me where this reading overreaches. The film is usually read as a Cold War and epidemic-era allegory of contagion and mistrust. Judge honestly whether the individuality reading does explanatory work in the scenes or whether I am projecting, and identify the moments that resist it.

Close by saying what the film contributes to the debate that a journal article on individuality cannot — what it can show that argument alone cannot. I do not want a bibliography, a reading list, or page references; name the theories, thinkers, and experiments you are drawing on in the body of the answer, but I am after the reasoning rather than the apparatus. If any of my descriptions of the film's scenes are inaccurate, correct me rather than working around the error.
