I have been reading about whether things inside games and virtual worlds are real — the view that a virtual sword is not a picture of a sword but a genuine digital object — and I keep encountering a pattern I cannot name. The same person will insist that nothing really happens in a game when the question is whether a virtual wrongdoing (destroying something another player spent months building, say) can be morally wrong, and will then insist that their in-game friendships, standings, and hard-won items are genuinely valuable and genuinely theirs when the question is whether they have been cheated. The pattern is not confined to games: someone who says an avatar was not "really" them when accused of harassment will often say that avatar's reputation really is theirs when it has been damaged. In each case the ontological status of the same kind of thing seems switched on or off according to which conclusion needs it.

Is there an established term for this? It resembles special pleading, but there nobody states a general rule and then exempts a case from it; each half of the pair looks perfectly reasonable in isolation. It is not quite equivocation on "real", since I do not think the speaker is shifting between two senses of the word so much as applying two different thresholds for what counts as real. Nor is it motte-and-bailey, since nothing is retreated to and later reclaimed. I would like the nearest established concepts — in ethics, metaphysics, or argumentation theory, wherever they live — how each differs from what I have described, and whether the literature on virtual reality already treats this as a named failure mode. If nothing fits, say so plainly and coin a term, with a definition and two boundary cases showing where it stops applying.

I would also ask that you separate what is settled here from what is genuinely disputed among people who work on the topic; I would rather be told that philosophers disagree than be handed a confident-sounding label that is really your own invention presented as standard.
