You are to answer as a philosopher of science who also has genuine technical command of behavioral genetics — someone who can move between the formal definition of a variance ratio and the ethics of a funding decision without losing the thread. Please think the matter through carefully before you begin writing, and resist the pull toward generalities: I am not asking whether childhood programs "work," but whether one particular inference from one particular kind of statistic is sound.

Some background on my own position. I spent thirty-six years as an analytical chemist, mostly developing separation methods, and I retired three years ago. Since then I have been reading philosophy in a serious way, and more recently the social sciences, where I find the mathematics familiar but the habits of inference strange. I mention this so that you will know what I need explained and what I can be assumed to follow: I am entirely comfortable with variance, with sampling error, and with the difference between a calibration curve and a measurement, but population genetics was never my field and I do not want to assume I have the vocabulary right.

The situation is this. I sit on the board of a private foundation with a modest endowment that funds programs for children in a post-industrial region of the country. Two applications are before us for the spring cycle: a two-year preschool enrichment program, small-group instruction combined with home visits, open to every child in three of the poorest districts; and a public-health proposal to replace service lines in a water system where testing came back badly and to run a screening and treatment protocol for the children served by it.

A fellow trustee, a man who made his career in quantitative analysis and whom I respect, opposes both. His reasoning, as I reconstruct it, runs as follows. The twin studies, he says, put the figure for performance on cognitive tests at sixty or seventy percent, and he says further that this figure rises as children grow into adults. From this he concludes that the preschool program's gains will wash out by adolescence, because whatever the early environment contributed will be displaced by inherited differences as the children age. He would rather the foundation fund a screening scheme: test all six-year-olds with a short nonverbal instrument and give the top decile an intensive multi-year scholarship, on the grounds that this targets the durable component of the variation rather than the transient one. Of the water proposal he says only that environment accounts for thirty percent at most, and that most of that thirty is parenting rather than plumbing. A fourth trustee goes the opposite direction and maintains that the entire statistic is worthless because the twins in those studies were overwhelmingly raised in the same household, so nothing derived from them can prove anything.

I find all of these positions unsatisfying and I am not equipped to adjudicate among them. What I want from you is a judgment, defended. If my reconstruction of the quantitative trustee's argument is uncharitable in any respect, repair it first and evaluate the repaired version; I would rather be told he made a better case than he did than be handed an easy target. And where you think the statistical question and the ethical question come apart — where a correct reading of the arithmetic would still leave his proposal objectionable, or where an incorrect reading would leave it defensible — please say so explicitly rather than letting one carry the other.

Organize your reply under the following headings, in this order, and at roughly these lengths:

1. **Bottom line** — about 100 words. Which of the three positions is closest to correct, and what the foundation should do with the two applications.

2. **What that statistic is, and what it is not** — about 350 words. Define the quantity precisely enough that I could defend the definition to a numerate layman, and state the conditions under which its value would change even if nothing about the children changed.

3. **His three inferences, and hers** — about 450 words. Treat separately: that a high figure measured early predicts fade-out; that a figure rising with age favors screening over universal enrichment; that the residual share bounds what an environmental intervention can achieve; and, finally, that shared household rearing invalidates the whole enterprise. For each, say whether it holds, fails, or holds only under assumptions he has not established.

4. **A suspicion I cannot state properly** — about 200 words. It occurs to me that if our enrichment program succeeded, the figure he keeps citing would rise rather than fall, and that he would then read this as evidence of failure. Tell me whether that is right, why, and how to say it in a board meeting without sounding as though I am making a joke of the arithmetic.

5. **What evidence would settle it** — about 250 words. Describe study designs and the patterns of results they would produce, not the literature; I am not asking for a reading list and I cannot check one. Say which findings would strengthen his screening proposal and which would kill it.

6. **What I say on Thursday** — about 250 words. Plain language, no technical terms I would have to stop and gloss, three or four sentences I can actually deliver to people who are trying to spend money well, followed by the motion you think I should put.
