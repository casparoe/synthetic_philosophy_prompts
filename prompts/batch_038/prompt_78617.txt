I am finishing a master's in mathematics with a growing side interest in philosophy, and this term I have been sitting in on two rooms that talk about the same theorem in incompatible registers. In my algebra seminar, Zorn's lemma is plumbing: it turns up in the proof that every proper ideal is contained in a maximal one and in the proof that every vector space has a basis, nobody remarks on it, and when a first-year asked whether it was controversial the professor said something to the effect that the matter had been settled ninety years ago by the plain fact that everybody uses it and it works. In the philosophy of mathematics seminar the same principle is an open wound. There we spent two weeks on the reception of Zermelo's well-ordering proof, on the objections pressed at the time by Borel, Baire, Hadamard and Lebesgue — that an object singled out by an uncanvassed infinity of arbitrary choices is somehow not genuinely given, even if it is not refuted — on the Banach–Tarski decomposition as a reductio that never quite landed, and on the fact that the consistency and independence results had, by the 1960s, shown the axiom to be neither provable nor refutable from the rest of ZF, which appears to have ended the argument by removing anything that could end it. Each room thinks the other is being obtuse, and I cannot tell which one is.

What I want is a diagnosis of the structure of this disagreement, not a survey of choice principles and their equivalents and not a reading list. Concretely, what kind of dispute is it now? The candidates I can see: it is a verbal one, since 'exists' and 'justified' plainly do unmarked double duty, with one room meaning provable-in-ZFC-and-epistemically-permissible and the other meaning given to intuition; or it is two different questions trading under a single name — the truth of a sentence about the universe of sets versus the methodological licence to invoke it inside a proof — where the independence results pushed the first question out of reach and left practitioners arguing about the second while believing they are arguing about the first; or it is a genuine clash of standards over what could count as evidence for an axiom at all, intrinsic plausibility against fruitfulness, in which case the impasse registers competing norms rather than competing beliefs; or it is substantive and simply hard. My own working hypothesis, offered to be tested rather than defended, is that a good part of the dispute is presentational: identical content reads as a technical tidying device when phrased as the lemma Kuratowski and Zorn proved about chains and upper bounds, and as an ontological postulate when phrased as the existence of a choice function on an arbitrary family of nonempty sets, and the intuition mathematicians report seems to track the phrasing even though the equivalence over ZF is a theorem. If that is right, the quarrel is substantially about the psychology of mathematical intuition rather than about set theory — but I doubt it is the whole story, because the hierarchy of fragments (countable choice, dependent choice, the ultrafilter lemma, which is known to be strictly weaker than full choice, and full choice itself) generates disagreements that no rephrasing dissolves, and because in constructive and type-theoretic settings the Diaconescu–Goodman–Myhill result that full choice entails excluded middle converts the question from a matter of taste into a choice of logic. Please tell me where my hypothesis holds, where it fails, and which diagnosis best fits what each side actually claims.

Finally, given your diagnosis, what would count as progress here? I want specifics: a distinction that would separate the sub-disputes and let each be settled or abandoned on its own; a formal result, for instance one that pins down exactly which fragment of choice a contested theorem requires, and whether results of that kind can ever adjudicate the justificatory question rather than merely refining it; a change in the question being asked; a new foundational setting in which the issue is recast rather than resolved; or a shift in what working mathematicians treat as licensing an axiom. If your answer is that no progress should be expected, argue for that and say whether the impasse is a defect in the debate or an accurate register of something about set-theoretic justification. And please be willing to conclude that one of the two seminar rooms has stopped asking a question that remains meaningful — and to say which one, and why.
