I serve as parliamentarian for a professional association of about 2,400 members. Our bylaws contain exactly one amendment clause: any provision may be amended by a two-thirds vote of the members present and voting at a general meeting, provided that written notice of the proposed amendment was given at the preceding meeting. The board is entirely volunteer, no one on it is a lawyer, and we get proposals that touch the amendment clause itself perhaps three or four times a year — almost always from someone who has just lost a vote and wants to raise the threshold, or from a regional chapter that wants its seat on the board protected forever. When one of these surfaces I have roughly twenty minutes before the chair puts the question, and afterwards I have to reduce my reasoning to writing for the minutes within a week, where it may be read by counsel for the losing side. We have no budget for outside advice unless a dispute actually turns litigious, and the only real check on a bad ruling from me is a membership vote two years later. I would like a decision procedure I can run on any proposed amendment to the amendment machinery: the questions to ask in a fixed order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, the points at which the procedure outputs a definite ruling (in order, out of order, amendable, not amendable), and the points at which it stops and hands the question to the membership or to a court because it is not mine to decide as presiding officer.

The procedure needs to be grounded in what the theory of legal self-reference actually has to say, but translated for a lay chair. I want it to take a position — or at least an explicit flag — on the recurring puzzles we hit: whether a rule-making power includes the power to change the rule that confers it; whether a body can bind its successors at all, and if not, what is left of the phrase "shall never be amended"; the difference between making a provision harder to change by imposing a more demanding manner and form of amendment and making it substantively unchangeable; whether an amendment adopted under the old clause remains valid after the clause is amended; and what to do when the bylaws are simply silent on a self-referential move, or when two clauses of the instrument conflict about which one governs. Please tell me which of these distinctions must survive into the checklist intact because the ruling changes if they are blurred, and which can be dropped as too fine for a room of non-specialists at nine in the evening — and be candid about the places where my procedure will be a judgment call wearing the clothes of a test, and about the situations in which it will confidently produce the wrong ruling.

Then run it on three proposals we have actually had, or something close enough to be useful. First: an amendment raising the threshold for all future bylaw changes to a two-thirds vote of the entire membership rather than of those present, with a clause stating that this higher threshold applies to itself and may be repealed only by the same standard — the proposer has told the board this will lock the change in permanently. Second: an amendment declaring that a certain regional chapter's guaranteed seat on the board "shall never be amended or abolished." Third: a challenge, raised the morning after a meeting, to an amendment passed the previous year, on the ground that notice was defective under the amendment clause as it then stood and that the defect makes the whole thing void rather than merely irregular. I want the written reasoning I would put in the minutes for each, the questions in order with my answers, and the ruling — including for at least one of the three a ruling that I and most of the board will not want to hear, with an explanation of why the unwelcome answer follows rather than a softer one. Finally, state plainly what this procedure cannot do for me: which of these questions it can only defer, and what would have to be true — a state statute, a charter, an incorporation document, a court — for my ruling to stop being the last word in the room.
