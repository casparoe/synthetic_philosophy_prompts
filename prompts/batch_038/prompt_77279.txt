**Everything is option luck: a granularity problem for the brute/option distinction**

**1. The distinction and the work it does.** Luck egalitarianism says an inequality is unjust when it tracks brute luck rather than choice. Dworkin's version splits luck in two: brute luck is how things turn out independently of what you did (being born with a condition, being struck by lightning), option luck is how a deliberate gamble you could have declined turned out. This distinction is load-bearing. It's what lets the luck egalitarian say we owe something to the person who got sick through no doing of their own and not to the person who lost their savings at the racetrack.

**2. The problem.** Lena cycles to work and is badly hurt when the lorry in front of her loses its brakes. Now watch what happens as we change how finely we name her choice:

- *Coarse:* Lena chose "cycle to work." Under that description she took a small, ordinary risk, and the brake failure does nearly all the explanatory work. The injury looks overwhelmingly like brute luck.
- *Intermediate:* Lena chose "cycle on this route, at this hour, in traffic." Now some real share of the risk was hers.
- *Fine:* Lena chose "cycle on this route at 8:14 immediately behind this particular lorry, whose brakes were already failing." Now there's no brute luck left at all — the injury is simply what the chosen option consisted in. Pure option luck.

Nothing about Lena changed across the three descriptions. The only thing that changed was how much of the world we packed into the name of the option she took. And we can always pack more: at the limit the option is "do the thing that in fact ended in being injured," and every outcome whatsoever is option luck. Go the other way and the option is "get up and go to work," and practically nothing is. The distinction doesn't carve the world at a joint; it slides with our vocabulary.

**3. A reply I expect, and why I don't think it rescues anything.** Someone will say: options are acts, not sentences — Lena performed one act, and how risky it was is a fact about the world, not about how we talk. But that relocates the problem rather than solving it. The riskiness of an act is a matter of the probability distribution over its outcomes, and a probability distribution is only defined once you've settled how outcomes are individuated. Whether you lump "injured in traffic" and "injured by this lorry's specific defect" into one cell or two is exactly the granularity question, and the act itself doesn't settle it.

**4. The insurance market doesn't escape either.** Dworkin's device for handling risk — asking what people would insure against behind a veil of ignorance — looks like a way to fix granularity, but it isn't. A premium is the price of a risk under a canonical description of the covered event, so the device has to assume there's one right level of description at which to write the policy. That's what was in question. The hypothetical market just hides the choice inside the contract drafting.

**5. Fix attempt one: the agent's own description.** Take whatever description Lena actually had in mind. Then the classification tracks her epistemic situation rather than our vocabulary. But this makes justice hostage to mental bookkeeping — two identically situated cyclists get different treatment because one thought about lorries and one didn't. And it seems to run backwards: someone who forms no expectations about risk at all has, under this fix, essentially no option luck to answer for, so gets compensated most generously. Culpable inattention becomes exculpatory. That needs a story.

**6. Fix attempt two: the reasonably informed description.** Use the coarsest description that captures every risk foreseeable on the evidence available to someone in Lena's position. This dodges the bookkeeping problem but stops being a theory about luck, because now whether an outcome counts as option luck depends on a social norm of prudent attention, and the norm is doing the distributive work. I also worry it's circular: I suspect "reasonable attention" will itself get calibrated by our intuitions about who should be compensated, which is what the distinction was supposed to explain.

**7. What I'd rather propose.** Drop the binary. Responsibility isn't a switch thrown by "she chose it"; it comes in degrees, set by two things — how much of the bad outcome was foreseeable on the evidence the agent had or could cheaply have gotten, and how costly it would have been for her to avoid it. Brute luck is then the limiting case of zero foreseeability and option luck the limiting case of near-certainty, and the granularity problem never gets started, because we're no longer sorting acts into two boxes; we're reading off a number.

**8. The uncomfortable bit.** On this account responsibility tracks epistemic diligence, so the inattentive are *less* responsible for their own bad outcomes. Either that's a reductio of section 7, or it's the correct result and the binary was flattering us. I genuinely dont know which, and this is the main thing I want comments on.
