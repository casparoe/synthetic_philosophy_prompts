A friend of mine and I spent most of last night arguing about cliodynamics, and we ended it the way such arguments end, trading insults about periodization. She is a genuine convert. She holds that the structural-demographic account of secular cycles — popular immiseration through labor oversupply and falling real wages, elite overproduction and the intra-elite fragmentation that follows from it, the fiscal squeeze on the state, with age structure operating behind all three — licenses real forecasts about political instability on the scale of decades, and she cited the much-publicized 2010 call for a decade of growing instability as evidence that this is not armchair history. I made the usual case against her: that the models are calibrated on the very cycles they are then said to predict; that a forecast whose resolution is generational cannot be scored on any human timescale; that the celebrated hit was a call for turbulence general enough that no one could have specified it in advance in a way that would let it fail; and that the historical sample is small enough that one inconvenient century becomes a methodological crisis. She thought I was just a defeatist about the social sciences generally, which is not the same as winning.

I would like to settle the matter obliquely, by commissioning a piece of satire.

Please write the "Important Disclosures, Methodology Notes, and Risk Factors" section of a subscription research product issued by an invented boutique forecasting firm — a name in the house style of macro-consultancies will do — which applies cliodynamic secular-cycle modeling to geopolitical and market outlooks and sells the resulting forecasts to institutional investors. Roughly 700 to 850 words. The register should be that of a fund prospectus: flat, defensive, hyper-literal compliance prose, with the firm's own numbering conventions, a short definitions list for its in-house ratings vocabulary, and a closing all-purpose disclaimer.

The critical instruction is that the firm is a sincere believer. Nothing in the document may sneer at cliodynamics. The theory as described must be technically accurate and internally coherent: the wage and population mechanism, the excess supply of elite aspirants relative to the positions of power available to absorb them, the state's revenue problem, the composite stress index the firm computes from these components, and the Ibn Khaldunian solidarity variable that the firm must concede it can only proxy. A reader who works in the field should be able to tell that the writer understands the theory, and should find the piece fair rather than a hit job; the exaggeration should be of real features of the practice, not of invented ones.

The comedy comes from the fact that the firm, in the ordinary course of covering itself legally, ends up stating my side of last night's argument in the driest possible language. At minimum, please include: a "Track Record and Non-Reliance" subsection handling the firm's much-publicized decade-ahead call with maximum legal care; a definitions list of three or four in-house ratings terms that apply credit-rating vocabulary to civilizations; one passage in which the firm's proxy for collective solidarity is disclosed with total candor and total inadequacy; a conflict-of-interest paragraph that is funnier for being utterly ordinary; the admission that the firm's own org chart is a textbook instance of the elite overproduction it warns clients about; and the disclosure that the calibration sample consists almost entirely of preindustrial polities plus one industrial society, which is the client's. Beyond those, invent at least two further disclosures of your own that draw on genuine methodological features of this style of modeling rather than on my complaints.

Stay deadpan throughout: no winks, no authorial nudges, no parentheticals informing the reader that something funny has occurred, and no sentence that steps outside the document to explain itself. It should nevertheless be legible to someone who has never heard of cliodynamics, which means the jargon ought to be self-explanatory in context or glossed in-house in the way such documents gloss things. Do not name any real scholar, firm, publication, or country; do not quote from any real text; and do not append footnotes, references, a bibliography, or a reading list of any kind. Please write the piece in American English.
