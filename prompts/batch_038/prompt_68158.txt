I am in the middle of drafting the framing chapter of a monograph on group agency, and a seminar I taught last term convinced me that the chapter's real difficulty is not which theory of shared intention to endorse but what one is entitled to demand of a theory of it in the first place. So I would like the specification written out before I commit to any candidate. Please give me the desiderata that an adequate theory of joint or shared intentional action must satisfy, each paired with the case or consideration that motivates it, and each marked as either non-negotiable or a preference belonging to one camp, with your reason for the marking. The purpose is fixed, because I suspect the list is sensitive to it: the theory must be good enough to do two downstream jobs. It must underwrite the attribution of intentions to organized bodies — a faculty committee, a corporate board, a treaty negotiator speaking for a delegation — in contexts where the attribution carries legal or quasi-legal weight. And it must state what a human-machine team would have to add to well-coordinated behaviour before we could say the two of them act together at all. Any putative requirement that bears on neither job should be flagged as optional, and any item that is really two requirements wearing one name should be split.

I should tell you where my own suspicions lie, so that you can test them rather than flatter them. Several things routinely presented as requirements strike me as the signature commitments of particular programmes rather than neutral constraints: the insistence that the account be modest, in the sense of being confined to small-scale cooperation among parties who stand in no asymmetric authority relations; the insistence that shared intention reduce without remainder to interlocking individual planning states; the demand that the theory capture whatever participants report as the distinctively joint character of the experience of acting together; and the demand that it be continuous with what developmental and comparative work shows about shared goals in infants and other primates. Against these, the requirements I take to be genuinely load-bearing are: that the theory explain how a body's action can fail to be readable off any aggregation of the members' own attitudes, as when a committee reaches a verdict that each member, asked individually, would disavow; that it handle cases in which participants describe the very same joint activity under incompatible descriptions, so that they are and are not intending the same thing; that it neither cheapen shared intention to the point where queues, crowds and mobs qualify nor tighten it to the point where two neighbours who silently and simultaneously clear a shared driveway after a snowstorm fail to qualify; that it say what happens to the joint commitment as members leave and are replaced; and that it be conservative, in the sense that it may not quietly revise its account of what an individual intention is in order to make the shared case come out right. Please also address two cases of my own construction. First, two researchers on different continents produce a joint paper coordinated entirely through asynchronous edits to one manuscript, never communicating in real time: does any theory on your list license the claim that they wrote it together intentionally, and if so on what grounds? Second, three people plan an undertaking that one of them knows a fourth is joining under a false impression of what is being planned: what has to be true for the first three, and what for the fourth?

The part I most want is the analysis of joint satisfiability, and I would rather have an honest negative result than a harmonious list. My hunch is that three demands cannot all be met at once: full reducibility to individual attitudes; an account of the standing participants have to rebuke a partner who defects and of the entitlement each acquires to rely on the others' continuing participation; and an account of how an organized body can arrive at an intentional state that no member holds and that each would individually reject. If some such subset really is unsatisfiable, say which requirement should go and what explanatory work we forfeit by dropping it; if it is satisfiable after all, show me the model that satisfies it rather than asserting that one exists. Tell me, in addition, which single desideratum does the most to eliminate candidate theories. Then, briefly and without ceremony, how the principal contenders fare against the finished list — the planning-theoretic account, the plural-subject account, the account that locates collective intentional states wholly in the heads of the individual participants, the account that treats the group perspective as primitive, and the group-agent account. A table is welcome for that scoring section, and it may be short. No bibliography, no page references, and please do not invent them; name positions and arguments freely and I will supply the citations myself.
