For most of my career I drafted referendum and initiative legislation for a national legislature, and I now advise an organisation working on electoral reform abroad, so I have watched the following pattern play out in several countries under quite different institutional rules. I still do not have a precise term for it, and I should like to know whether one exists.

The pattern begins with a contested issue that a government is under pressure to settle. A referendum is offered on a narrow formulation of the question, and the side wanting the change wins, sometimes by a hair. The result is then written into the constitution, or into a statute shielded by a special amendment procedure, on the explicit ground that the people have spoken and the matter is now beyond ordinary politics. The argument offered for locking it in is that a bare parliamentary majority is too fragile a foundation for something of that importance, and that later legislatures must not be free to undo what the electorate decided.

What I keep noticing is a symmetry that participants on both sides fail to register. The same actors hold that the decision carries authority because a majority of the electorate made it, and that majority rule must now be constrained so that a future majority cannot reverse it. The constraint is installed by the very mechanism just declared untrustworthy: another vote, or another parliament, operating under a higher threshold. The position therefore amounts to saying that popular decision is authoritative when it produces the outcome one wishes to preserve and unreliable when it might produce a different outcome later, yet both halves are advanced as expressions of the same reverence for the electorate. The dispute is then conducted as though the two camps were appealing to rival values, when in fact each is appealing to the identical value and reaching opposite conclusions.

Two cases will show the shape of it.

In the first, an electorate voted by a narrow margin to withdraw from a supranational union. The vote was advisory as a matter of law, and leading advocates had said before the vote that the country retained full freedom to change course later. Within about a year the result was being described as having settled the question for good, and reopening it was characterised as a betrayal of the voters rather than as an exercise of the same popular authority that had produced the result.

In the second, of a kind that appears in several subnational systems, voters approve a measure that both prohibits some practice and provides that no future legislature may permit it, the only route to reversal being another vote of the whole electorate. Critics say the measure is undemocratic because it disables citizens who were not party to it. Supporters say it is democracy at its purest because the citizens who were party to it decided. Both are right about the facts, and both appeal to the same standard.

My question concerns the structure itself, and whether it is named anywhere in democratic or constitutional theory. I am aware of several neighbouring ideas, and for each I want to be told what part of my description it covers and exactly where my description goes past it: the objection that the living should not be ruled by the choices of the dead; the objection that unelected judges overriding elected legislatures is undemocratic; the idea that a polity can rationally bind itself now against a foreseeable weakness of will later; the logical puzzle of an amendment rule being amended by means of its own procedure; and the puzzle that a people cannot democratically decide who counts as the people. None of these seems to be what I have described, since here the binding is performed by a contemporary popular vote rather than by an earlier generation, a court, a moment of weakness, or a rule about membership, and the difficulty is not one of legal validity but of how one and the same appeal to popular authority can be made to support both fixing a decision and forbidding its release.

Is there an established term for that symmetric structure, as distinct from a term for either side of it taken alone? If the nearest established concept covers part of the phenomenon, name it and delineate both the fit and the mismatch. If nothing established fits, propose a term and define it with its edges visible: specify what would and would not count as an instance, whether an ordinary law that is merely difficult to repeal qualifies, whether a referendum that settles nothing but registers an attitude qualifies, whether a lock imposed by a constitutional court on the same subject matter qualifies, and whether the phenomenon survives at all if the initial vote is unanimous rather than narrow. I am after the carving, not the label; a bare name, or a general account of why constitutions entrench things, would not answer the question I am asking.
