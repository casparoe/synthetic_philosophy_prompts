Four months ago I joined a six-person prop shop, and roughly 70% of my comp sits in two long-dated positions I can't sell, can't hedge, and that pay off like a St. Petersburg ticket — almost certainly small, absurdly unlikely to be enormous. The firm sold me the package with a straight expected-value calculation, and ever since there's been an argument running in our Slack that hasn't moved an inch. I'm the one whose actual livelihood is the stake, so I care more then I probably should.

Three camps:

1. The Bernoulli camp. The EV is only infinite (or in our case only insane) because utility is unbounded; give yourself any concave utility function and the ticket has a perfectly sane price, the paradox has been handled since 1738, and the rest of us are wasting our evenings. They think camp 2 is reinventing risk aversion with physics vocabulary.

2. The ergodicity camp — the time-average versus ensemble-average people. They say an expected value is an average across parallel copies of you, and you only get to live one path; under multiplicative wealth dynamics the typical path can shrink while the ensemble mean grows, so maximizing EV is a category error and what you should be maximizing is the time-average growth rate of your wealth. They insist this is not risk aversion in disguise — it's a claim about the dynamics, not about tastes — and that expected utility theory quietly assumes the process is ergodic when it never earns that assumption.

3. The counterparty camp. Says neither of the above matters, because no real ticket has an infinite EV: the payoff is capped by whatever the writer can actually pay, so the entire literature is about a casino with an infinite balance sheet, and the moment you model the solvency of whoever's paying you, the paradox is just arithmetic.

Here's what made me suspect nobody in this argument is arguing about the same thing. We ran a toy version in a notebook: each round your wealth either goes up 50% or down 40%, coin flip, whole book at risk. Mean multiplier 1.05, so the average path grows; geometric mean is sqrt(0.9), so the median path bleeds out, and across 10k simulated paths the mean wealth climbs steadily while the median heads for the floor. Camp 1 says: exactly, that's a concave utility function doing its job, Bernoulli wins, nothing new under the sun. Camp 2 says: no, that's the demonstration that EV is the wrong statistic — the point isn't that you happen to like log, it's that for a single path the mean is describing something that isn't happening to you. Same numbers, both claiming victory.

Then someone re-ran it risking only a quarter of the book per round instead of all of it, and both the mean and the median grow. Camp 1 says that proves it was never about the game, only about your stake and your preferences. Camp 2 says it proves the growth rate is the right object, because whether the game is good for you depends on how it's embedded in your dynamics. Camp 3 says you're all arguing about a fiction since nobody can actually write that ticket at scale.

My hypothesis, offered as a hypothesis and probably too easy: camps 1 and 2 aren't disagreeing about a decision at all. Camp 1 is answering "given a utility function, what should a one-shot choice look like," camp 2 is answering "what happens to my wealth if I keep doing this," and words like rational and the value of this bet are doing double duty across both. Camp 3 isn't a camp, it's a factual objection that nobody is actually contesting. And nothing moves because the simulation can't discriminate between the readings — it was constructed to be consistent with both.

Is that right, or too glib? What kind of disagreement is this really — verbal, two questions under one name, a hidden formal assumption doing all the work (additive vs multiplicative dynamics, one-shot vs repeatable, whether stake size is part of the decision or handed to you from outside, whether there's an ensemble to average over at all), or is one side just wrong in a way that could be shown wrong? And given whatever diagnosis you land on, what would count as progress here: a distinction, a theorem, a lab experiment with real subjects, changing the question — or should we expect none, and if none, why not? I do have to decide this quarter whether to stay at the firm, which is functionally a decision about whether to keep betting my whole bankroll on this thing, so please tell me how confident you are in the diagnosis and single out the one claim in your answer you're least sure about.
