In two weeks I am presenting a paper at an interdisciplinary animal-welfare seminar. The room will be mostly applied ethologists, two veterinary anesthesiologists, a welfare economist, and a philosopher of biology who works on animal consciousness; almost none of them read early modern philosophy, and several have come precisely because they regard Cartesian mechanism as the historical root of our habit of discounting behavioral evidence of pain. One of them has co-authored a widely used framework for inferring affective states in farmed animals and is openly skeptical of any inference from behavior to experience.

My thesis is revisionist and, I expect, unwelcome: that Descartes' beast-machine doctrine is not a denial of animal sentience but a denial of reflective awareness. My argument runs in three steps. First, the operative Cartesian distinction is between thought and bodily sensation, and Descartes consistently grants animals the latter — states such as fear, hunger, and anger, located in the body and brain. Second, on his account the human body is an automaton in exactly the sense in which the animal body is; the mind–body union is what converts such states into felt ones, and the criteria he actually deploys to separate humans from animals are language and general adaptability, not the presence or absence of nociception. Third — and here I anticipate trouble — the resulting view makes Descartes an unwitting ancestor of higher-order theories of consciousness, since on his picture a state can be real, behaviorally efficacious, and yet not felt; that is precisely the gap welfare science tries to close with behavioral and physiological indicators. I intend to close by arguing that our field has been answering a Cartesian question rather than escaping it.

Prepare me for the question period. Give me the objections this audience will actually raise — not the ones a history-of-philosophy panel would — ordered by how much damage each would do, and phrased in their vocabulary: depth of anesthesia, affective state, indicator validity, sentience as a graded property, precaution in policy. For each, supply a reply I can deliver in about two minutes and an honest note on where that reply is soft. Then tell me what I should concede during my opening remarks so that the anesthesiologists do not write me off, which single reply has to land for the talk to succeed, and the question I should be hoping nobody asks.
