For a reading group I attend on measurement and governance, we were assigned a chapter from a recent trade book about performance data — a well-regarded book, by a former management consultant who now writes about data-driven government. Its account of Goodhart's law runs as follows:

"The law comes from Charles Goodhart, the Bank of England economist, who noticed in the 1970s that the statistical relationships the UK authorities were relying on to steer the money supply stopped holding as soon as they were used to steer it: banks and other financial institutions rearranged their business to hit the announced targets, and the correlation between the target and the underlying goal evaporated. Goodhart stated the point as: 'Any observed statistical regularity will tend to collapse once pressure is placed upon it for control purposes.'

The mechanism is always the same, and it is always strategic. The people being measured know what the measure is, so they adjust their behaviour to score well on it rather than to do the thing the measure was tracking, and that is why target and goal come apart. Economists subsequently proved the result in general form; in macroeconomics it is known as the Lucas critique, which is essentially Goodhart's law with a formal model attached. The general lesson was put by Goodhart himself as: 'When a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure.'

Once you see it, you see it everywhere — hospitals shaving waiting times by deferring referrals, schools teaching to the test, researchers managing citation counts, call centres meeting handle-time targets by transferring calls. It also names the central difficulty in machine learning, where the phenomenon is called specification gaming: give a system a proxy for what you want and it will find a way to score on the proxy without doing what you wanted.

Two consequences follow, and they are uncomfortable. First, the failure cannot be repaired by measuring more carefully. A better-instrumented proxy is still a proxy, and any proxy optimized hard enough will come apart, while adding further metrics only multiplies the number of things that can be gamed. Second, for the same reason, the only durable alternative to a fixed target is to abandon fixed objectives altogether and let the system learn continuously from human judgement, which is not a metric and so cannot be gamed in this way. Goodhart's law is therefore not advice about measurement hygiene; it is a proof that optimization against any proxy eventually makes things worse."

I came to this sympathetic, having watched metrics go wrong in my own organisation, but a colleague who works on monetary statistics says the chapter is careless, and I cannot now tell which parts to keep. Could you audit it for me?

1. Take the passage claim by claim and sort it into what a specialist in this material would accept, what is a defensible simplification for a general audience, and what is simply wrong. Please say which category each belongs to rather than giving an overall verdict.

2. Does the sentence quoted from Goodhart say what the chapter says it says? I am especially unsure about the assertion that the mechanism is "always strategic." My colleague gestured at Goodhart-type failures in which nobody is gaming anything, but I did not follow the point. Is there a recognised distinction here, and if so, which of the chapter's own examples fall on which side of it?

3. Is the identification of Goodhart's law with the Lucas critique fair? My impression is that the Lucas critique concerns econometric models and expectations rather than targets, but I may have that wrong. Tell me what the two claims share and precisely where they come apart, and whether either requires that someone be trying to score well on a measure.

4. Who actually wrote the line about measures becoming targets, and does the chapter's attribution to Goodhart matter for anything substantive, or is it harmless folklore?

5. Do the two "uncomfortable consequences" follow from anything true in the chapter? In particular: is it correct that better measurement and additional metrics cannot help; is it correct that continuously learning from human judgement escapes the problem instead of reproducing it, given that human ratings are themselves something a system can be optimized against; and does the collapse of a proxy's relationship to a goal establish that outcomes get worse than they would have been without the target, as the final sentence claims?

6. Suppose everything you judge to be wrong is stripped out. Is a non-trivial core left — something that would actually change how a public body chooses and uses indicators, including how hard it optimizes them and what it monitors alongside them — or does the correction leave the chapter saying little more than that measurement is hard? I would like the accurate version stated positively, not only as a list of the chapter's errors.

I am not looking for citations, references, or a reading list; I want the reasoning and the corrections.
