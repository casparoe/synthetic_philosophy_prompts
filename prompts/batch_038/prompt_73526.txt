I TA a 75-minute section for an upper-level aesthetics course — 13 students, one long weekly session — and next week is the testimony unit. I want to build an activity where they run into the problem themselves instead of me explaining it for twenty minutes and then having one of those polite discussions where everyone agrees with the reading.

Here's the specific thing I want them to feel. The stock framing is that testimony works fine for ordinary factual claims and somehow goes wrong for claims about whether a work is beautiful, moving, or great. But I have a hunch I want the activity to actually put under pressure rather than just illustrate, and I don't want it designed to confirm my hunch by construction. The hunch: the breakdown isn't mainly about justification. A belief you pick up from someone else's word about a work is non-projectible — you can't say what else it commits you to, you can't use it to predict anything about a neighboring work, and you can't articulate what would count against it. It just sits there, inert, doing no work. If that's right, the interesting failure should surface not when they're *forming* the belief but when they're forced to *use* it.

So design me a game around that. Constraints I care about:

- Fabricated works only. Please don't build it on real films or albums or novels, because half the class will have prior familiarity and then I'd be measuring the wrong thing. Invented works, described in writing, are fine and actually preferable. Supply the descriptions yourself — I have some of my own time to prep but not much.
- No lecture up front. Nobody hears the phrases aesthetic testimony or acquaintance principle until the debrief.
- A scoring scheme in points or tokens, no real money. I want abstaining to be a live, scored option, because I suspect the most revealing data is who declines to judge and what they say when you ask them why.
- Some information asymmetry and roles. I'm picturing testifiers, testifiees, and maybe a set of pressers whose entire job is to ask "what else does that commit you to?" and "what would change your mind?" — but restructure that if you have something better.

Give me the materials list, a minute-by-minute timeline that fits 75 minutes, the rules, the scoring, and the debrief questions. In the debrief I want an explicit map from things that happened in the room onto the transmitted-belief versus acquaintance distinction, AND onto the non-projectibility idea — and I want you to tell me bluntly whether the activity can actually discriminate between those two explanations or whether it confounds them, because if it confounds them I'd rather find out from you now than from my students in week nine.

Then tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it. I can already see one: if the works exist only as written descriptions, students may form genuine aesthetic reactions to the *descriptions*, which would contaminate everything downstream. Say how to block that, or how to turn it into a feature.

And be straight with me about what the activity structurally can't reproduce and that I'll simply have to supply by hand in the debrief. I'm assuming at least this much: in real life aesthetic testimony mostly *works*. A friend tells me a record is great, I listen, she was right. An activity engineered so that testimony fails will feel rigged unless I handle that asymmetry somehow — so tell me how.

Please write the whole thing in American English.
