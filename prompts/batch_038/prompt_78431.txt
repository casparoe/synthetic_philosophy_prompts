Here's the pattern. My grandmother spent the last six weeks of her life in a hospice place near us, and the whole family knew the scan had been catastrophic — her doctor had told my mum in the corridor, my mum told all of us, nobody hid it from each other. But around my grandmother, and around my aunt who is a bit more fragile, the conversation went like this: my aunt would say something like "they're still working out what's next, aren't they," and my mum would say "yes, they're taking their time, he's holding steady." Both of them knew. Neither one was trying to plant anything in the other. There was no wink, no change of tone, no raised eyebrow — it was said warm and plain and serious, and if a stranger had walked into the room they would have taken it completely at face value, the falseness was only visible to people already inside it. And it wasn't a one-off. It was a whole standing arrangement, the kind where if my brother had walked in and said the flat true thing out loud, everyone would have felt he'd done something cruel — not that he'd been honest. That's the bit that got me. Breaking it feels worse than the false statement does.

Smaller versions of the same shape are everywhere once you start looking. My mate's kid forgot every single line of her part in the school play and froze on stage, and afterwards both of us told him "she was really brave up there," and both of us knew exactly what that meant, and neither of us was fooling the other, and it wasn't a joke either — nobody was laughing, it was doing real work. Or at my job, my manager asks "how are you holding up with the restructure," and I say "getting there," and she knows I'm not, and I know she knows, and we both walk away feeling better and nobody has been deceived about anything. Afterwards, if you asked any of us "so did you lie?", we'd all say no and mean it. Which I'm not sure is right. The nearest things I can come up with myself all miss: it isn't a white lie, because a white lie needs somebody to actually be taken in; it isn't sarcasm or irony or a joke, because none of it is marked as not-for-real and an outsider would be fooled dead easy; it isn't quite an open secret either, because the secret isn't what's being talked around, the false sentence itself is the shared performance. I suspect people who write about dying and nursing have their own word for this and that the lying philosophers have a different one, and that neither of them covers the whole shape, but I've not read either.

Where it gets weird is that I fell into some Augustine on lying a while back (found a chunk of it online and skimmed it, no idea which book, so correct me if I've mangled him). My takeaway was that for him what makes a lie is your mouth going against what's in your heart, and whether anyone actually ends up believing the false thing doesn't really matter, and the main person harmed is the liar himself. If that's roughly right, then the hospice conversation is a lie on his account — a mild one, but a lie — even though nobody in the room believed it, which cuts against my instinct and against what the family would have said.
