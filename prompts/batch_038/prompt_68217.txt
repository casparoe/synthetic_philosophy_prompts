You're the person an editor calls when a "your language changes how you think" story is about to run — a philosopher of language and mind who has actually kept up with the experimental literature, not the pop version, the version where you know which findings held up and which ones quietly died. I cover science and ideas for a magazine and I've been sitting on this for about a year now. Every few months a new Whorf-flavored press release lands on my desk and I have to decide whether it survives my skepticism, and I've noticed I'm doing it by gut. I want to know if the gut adds up to a principle, becuase I'm fairly sure it doesn't and I can't see where it breaks.

Please think carefully about this before answering, and start with some brainstorming rather than going straight to a verdict: sketch three or four candidate principles that might sit behind my list, note for each one which of my verdicts it costs me, and only then commit to one. I'd much rather see the branching than a tidy answer that silently drops two of my cases on the floor.

Here are the verdicts. For each I'm saying whether I think the strong claim — that the language is doing something to the thinking, not just to the talking — is true:

1. Russian speakers are faster to discriminate two shades that straddle the siniy/goluboy boundary than two shades the same objective distance apart inside one category. BELIEVE, and I believe this one more than any other, because in that experiment the advantage went away when people were made to silently repeat something while doing the task. That's as close to a mechanism as this field ever gets: block inner speech, effect vanishes, language was load-bearing.

2. German and Spanish speakers pick gendered-stereotype adjectives for a bridge — elegant and pretty from one group, sturdy and big from the other. DON'T BELIEVE. It's a rating task about a noun, of course the noun's gender leaks through, that's word association wearing a lab coat. The detail that ought to bother me is that they were apparently tested in English, so the gender wasn't even in front of them. I keep not letting that bother me and I don't know why.

3. Speakers of the Australian and Mesoamerican languages that use absolute compass directions instead of left/right: they track north across days of blindfolded disorientation, and when you ask them to lay out cards showing a temporal sequence they run it east-to-west, and the sequence flips when you rotate them in their chair. BELIEVE, strongly. And I'm aware this is the one my colleagues find least persuasive, because it rests on a handful of fieldworkers' observations about a small number of speakers — which is exactly the evidential shape of Whorf's Hopi material, and I don't believe that.

4. Whorf on Hopi, the claim that the language doesn't treat time as a substance or carve it into tenses, and Malotki coming along and showing it does. DON'T BELIEVE Whorf's version. But I also don't accept the bigger conclusion people draw from it, that the whole enterprise is a graveyard.

5. Pirahã and exact number: the language has no exact number words, and in the matching experiments speakers can't reproduce an exact set size once you get past about three items. HALF BELIEVE. I believe the performance data. I don't believe the gloss — that this shows they can't think about exact quantity — and I think most of the coverage collapses "hasn't got the tool" into "can't do the thing."

6. The futureless-language economist's finding that speakers of languages without a distinct future tense save more, smoke less, and so on. DON'T BELIEVE. A language family travels with culture, geography, institutions, wealth history. I can't specify the confound to my own satisfaction, but I'm confident it's there and I say so in print anyway, which is maybe its own problem.

7. Bilinguals giving more utilitarian answers to trolley-type dilemmas in their second language. HALF BELIEVE. I think the effect is real and I think it's emotional distance rather than conceptual structure — and I suspect that distinction is going to matter enormously to whatever principle you land on.

8. The accident-memory finding: people who hear a non-agentive description of something breaking are worse at remembering who did it than people who hear an agentive one. BELIEVE.

9. Fifty words for snow. DON'T BELIEVE, obviously — it's on the list so you know I'm not a true believer.

My tentative rule is roughly this: a claim counts as language-shapes-thought only if (i) the effect shows up in a task with no words in it, (ii) it shrinks or disappears when you block inner speech, and (iii) the two languages genuinely differ in the relevant structure rather than just in vocabulary size. I already know (i) and (ii) are pulling in opposite directions and I don't know what to do about it — the case I'm most confident about is the one where the effect dies under verbal interference, and the case I find most moving is the one where the effect obviously can't be subvocalization.

What I want back:

- The principle, or the small set of them, stated precisely enough that I could hand it to a fact-checker and they could apply it to an unfamiliar press release without coming back to me. Tell me which of the cases above motivates each clause — I want to know why each clause is there and what it costs me.
- Where I have to choose. If the principle that saves all nine is baroque, say so, and then give me the simpler version and name exactly which verdicts it overturns. I would genuinely rather lose a verdict than carry a six-clause rule around.
- Its verdicts on cases I haven't listed. Give me at least three, and make them the kind that would actually cross my desk — a real domain, a real sort of manipulation, not an invented language with invented properties.
- A straight answer on which of my verdicts your principle cannot accommodate, and whether you think I should change my mind or that the principle is the thing that's wrong.

One practical constraint: don't give me a reading list. I can't check citations, and a plausible-looking author-and-year that turns out to be invented is much worse for me than no citation at all. Argue from the structure of the cases and the kinds of evidence they rest on. If you think one of the studies I described above doesn't exist, or isn't what I think it is, tell me plainly and say what you'd put in its place rather than working around it quietly. Same with names — if you're not sure who ran what, describe the finding without attaching a person to it.
