I have come to accept what I will call the strategy-stealing assumption about races over transformative technology, and I want a careful account of how exposed that commitment actually is. Let me state it as precisely as I can before asking you to attack it, since I suspect much of the disagreement in this area is about which proposition is being held.

The assumption: in a race between parties developing the same kind of transformative system, a head start is worth far less than it appears, because whatever the leader does can be adopted by a follower once the follower can see it working. If the advantage consisted of a method, the method is learnable; if it consisted of accumulated inputs, the inputs remain purchasable; if it consisted of a market or institutional position, a follower with comparable capability can contest it. Therefore racing — accepting elevated risk of a bad outcome in exchange for being first — buys little, and the rational posture is to slow down, disclose enough to prevent surprises, and seek agreement, because the cost of waiting is small and the value of winning is systematically overestimated.

Here is the toy model I take to be the cleanest expression of the view. Two players, discrete periods, a capability that grows with cumulative investment along a publicly known production function. In any period a player may continue investing or deploy. Deploying early yields a lead proportional to the capability gap at that moment, and carries a globally shared accident risk that the deploying party does not bear alone. My inference runs: because the production function is public and the inputs purchasable, the follower can reproduce the deployer's investment trajectory at a later date, so the lead decays toward nothing; and because the accident risk does not decay, the expected value of deploying first is negative.

Now tell me what would count against this. Specifically:

1. **Premise autopsy.** Identify which premises are load-bearing — publicness of the capability function, purchasability and substitutability of inputs, low discounting, absence of capacity constraints, the reversibility or boundedness of accident costs, symmetry between the players, and the claim that a lead consists of a strategy at all. For each, describe what the world would have to look like for it to fail, and by what mechanism rather than by label.

2. **Constructive counterexamples.** Take at least two of those premises and build the smallest modification of the toy game in which early deployment genuinely dominates. I want the structure of a model that defeats the view, not a gesture toward one.

3. **The positional-advantage clause.** Defenders typically concede that a lead is un-stealable when it converts into something not replicable by adoption — a standard others must build on, a scarce complement, an installed base, a locked-in set of values or governance arrangements. Assess how much work that clause does: is it a principled restriction on the assumption, or is it elastic enough to absorb every counterexample?

4. **Inheritance from the formal argument.** In combinatorial game theory, strategy-stealing establishes that in a symmetric game where an extra move can never hurt, the second player cannot have a guaranteed win — that is, it establishes a first-mover advantage, not its absence. Tell me whether my usage inherits any force from that argument, whether the shared name is doing covert rhetorical work, and precisely where the analogy breaks.

5. **Empirical exposure.** What does this view predict that its rivals do not? Which classes of observed race would be informative, and what pattern within them would be genuinely damaging rather than merely inconvenient? Where you reach for historical examples, give me the structural features that matter rather than leaning on the name alone, and flag any case whose details you are not confident of; three clean archetypes would be worth more to me than ten shaky anecdotes.

6. **What a defender would concede.** Which of these objections would a committed proponent accept as counting against the assumption, and which would they dismiss as begging the question? I want to know in particular which objections secretly depend on rejecting the view's account of what counts as a strategy.

7. **Ranking and insulation.** Sort the defeaters from fatal, to serious, to awkward, to harmless. Then say honestly whether the view has been qualified into unfalsifiability. Note that it functions as a practical, decision-guiding assumption rather than an empirical theory, so tell me whether insulation is a defect for a view of this kind or a legitimate feature of one meant to be conservative under uncertainty, and how I should weigh it either way.

For calibration: I am moved most by a demonstration that some plausible asymmetry makes a lead non-replicable, and least by reminders of how badly things could go if the assumption is wrong — the latter seems to me to change the stakes without touching the truth of the assumption. If you think that ordering is itself mistaken, say so and explain why.

Format your answer in numbered sections corresponding to the above, ending with the ranked defeater list. Argue rather than survey, and if you think I have mis-stated the assumption in a way that makes it easier or harder to defend than intended, correct that first.
