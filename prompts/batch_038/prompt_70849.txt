I am in the middle of a dissertation chapter on epistemic utility theory, and for the better part of a year I have been sitting inside a dispute that seems incapable of making contact with itself. I want a diagnosis of the disagreement rather than a ruling on which side is correct.

The dispute concerns the status of the scoring rule. One side — I will call it the invariance camp — holds that the central accuracy-theoretic results (probabilism established as a dominance theorem, conditionalization established by a diachronic accuracy or Dutch-strategy argument) go through for any scoring rule that is strictly proper, and that strict propriety is not an optional add-on but the formal expression of the idea that a credence function ought to expect itself to be the most accurate option available to it. On this view, which strictly proper rule one adopts is a free parameter, more like a choice of units than a choice of principle, and to treat the results as hostage to it is to misunderstand what the theorems say.

The other side — the sensitivity camp — replies that this deflationary description fails exactly where it matters. It claims that how sharply a credence function must be peaked in order to escape domination, whether an imprecise credal state is accuracy-dominated at all (which turns on both the rule and how one aggregates accuracy across a set of representors), whether a diachronic argument yields full conditionalization or only a weaker updating policy, and which comparative accuracy judgments hold between two incoherent credence functions, all depend on the rule and not merely on its strict propriety. It adds a methodological charge: strict propriety cannot be motivated without already presupposing much of the accuracy-first picture the theorems are offered as establishing, so the supposedly free parameter is smuggled in rather than chosen.

My own contribution to the impasse has not helped. I constructed a rule as a positive mixture, s = (1−λ)Q + λL, where Q is the quadratic score over a finite partition, L is the logarithmic score, and λ is a weight in the unit interval. Since a positive mixture of strictly proper rules is strictly proper, the invariance camp ought to count this rule as fully admissible by its own lights. Working through a three-hypothesis partition, I found that a given incoherent credence function is dominated at both λ = 0.05 and λ = 0.90, but that the margin of domination differs by orders of magnitude between the two settings, and I could not settle whether the same alternative credence function does the dominating in both cases. Each camp read this as confirming its own position. The invariance camp said the verdict was invariant and my example therefore proved nothing. The sensitivity camp said the verdict was never the interesting output — the theory is supposed to tell an agent how badly a doxastic state fails and what it should be replaced with, and on my own numbers both of those are at the mercy of λ.

Here is my hypothesis, offered as a hypothesis and not as a conclusion. I suspect the camps are answering different questions and that one word is carrying two meanings. When the sensitivity camp calls the choice of rule arbitrary, it means that nothing outside the accuracy-first framework licenses that choice; when the invariance camp hears arbitrary, it hears unconstrained by the mathematics, and it answers — correctly — that strict propriety is an extremely strong mathematical constraint. If that is right, one side is asking whether the theorems are true and stable under admissible variation, while the other is asking whether they are informative: whether a theorem of the form "every rule with property P condemns this credence function" tells an agent anything at all unless she already accepts that P is the relevant property.

What I want from you is an assessment of that hypothesis. Is the impasse a verbal one driven by an equivocal term, a case of two questions sharing a single heading, a substantive disagreement about method that neither side can argue for from neutral ground, or — the possibility I most want tested — an unposed technical question that both sides gesture at without stating? If it is the last, state the question precisely: for instance, whether the class of normative verdicts invariant across all strictly proper rules can be characterized, and whether the non-invariant remainder is empty, negligible, or contains the very results the theory was built to deliver. Please also tell me whether my mixture example, assuming the numerics are right, genuinely discriminates between the camps, or is simply a predictable consequence of mixing a bounded with an unbounded score that neither side should care about.

Ground the diagnosis in what each camp actually claims, as I have described those claims; I do not want a survey of positions or a map of the field. Then tell me what would count as progress — a theorem, a distinction, a reformulation of what a vindication is supposed to accomplish, or an argument that no progress should be expected because the two questions can only be settled in tandem. If, having diagnosed the dispute, you think one camp is right, say so, but only after the diagnosis and only with reasons that do not presuppose it.

Two administrative requests. Please write in American English. And please do not include references, footnotes, or a reading list: I am working offline, I have no way to check citations, and I would rather have your reasoning than your bibliography.
