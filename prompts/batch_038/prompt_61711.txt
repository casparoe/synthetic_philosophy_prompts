I'm in a monthly philosophy-of-computing reading group and last week we got into a fight about P vs NP that never really resolved. One guy — call him D — kept reaching for the paint analogy: mixing two cans of paint is easy, un-mixing them is impossible, so there are operations that are cheap in one direction and hopeless in the other, and that's basically all P≠NP says anyway. He swapped in the scrambled-egg version halfway through. His punchline was that P≠NP is as secure as the second law of thermodynamics, meaning it doesn't need a proof to be worth betting on, and that people who worry about someone proving P=NP, or about a model stumbling onto some universal shortcut, are making a category error — like worrying that someone will find a way to un-fry an egg.

The analogy obviously lands, everyone in the room nodded at first, but on the walk home I felt like something was sliding. Paint feels irreversable because of statistics over huge numbers of molecules, and I cant tell whether that's the same kind of thing as a hash function being hard to invert or a totally different kind of thing wearing the same clothes. Also D wouldn't say what happens to his analogy if P turned out to equal NP, whereas the thermodynamics half would presumably keep going regardless, and that asymmetry bugged me.

So can you audit it properly? I want the correspondence table first — what plays the paint, what plays the mixer, what plays un-mixing, what plays "easy" and "impossible" in the complexity-theoretic sense — then the disanalogies, but sorted into the ones that actually undercut D's use of it and the ones that are just aesthetic complaints. Specifically: what does the analogy import from physics (entropy, the arrow of time, irreversibility as an observed regularity) that computational hardness doesn't have, and is there a real gap between "we have candidate one-way functions" and "we have observed irreversible processes"? Does something like Shor's algorithm demolishing factoring-based cryptography have any analogue in the paint case, and if not, what does that tell us about the strength of the comparison? And I'd like you to be explicit about what "hard" even means here, since worst-case hardness and average-case hardness seem to come apart in ways the paint story can't express. Then the verdict: does his conclusion survive — is P≠NP really as safe as the second law, or was the analogy load-bearing the whole time? And if it fails, is there a better one — not a slogan, something that keeps the intuitive pull without misrepresenting what the conjecture claims. If you think the paint story is fine for teaching but dangerous for the epistemic point he used it for, say exactly that and mark where the line is.

Hard cap of 550 words, table included. And tell me how confident you are in the main claims, plus flag the single point you're least sure of — I'd rather know where you're guessing than get a smooth answer.
