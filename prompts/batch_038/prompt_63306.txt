I'm writing a paper for my eighteenth-century ethics seminar, and I have constructed an argument against Smith's impartial spectator that I now believe is unsound, though I cannot locate the unsound step. The argument is meant to show that Smith is a conventionalist whether or not he intends to be.

(1) On Smith's account, the propriety of a sentiment or action consists in the degree to which an impartial and well-informed spectator, imaginatively entering the situation of the person concerned, can go along with it.

(2) That spectator is not some entity we consult; he is an idea we assemble by abstracting from the actual sympathetic responses of the people around us, stripping away their ignorance and their partiality toward themselves and their intimates.

(3) So the content of the impartial spectator's verdicts is fixed by the content of a given community's actual verdicts.

(4) Hence where two communities' actual spectators differ — in their sympathies, their customs, the general rules they have formed — their impartial spectators differ accordingly, and approbation is indexed to a form of life.

(5) It follows that the reformer who condemns her own society is not appealing to any standard beyond it: she is only setting one historically conditioned spectator against another, and there is no fact of the matter about which of the two is properly impartial.

(6) Yet Smith plainly thinks the impartial spectator can convict the actual consensus, since he treats custom as having perverted judgment in cases like duelling, which the world excuses, and the ancient exposure of infants.

(7) So Smith must abandon either (2) or (6): either the impartial spectator is not assembled from real spectators, in which case his naturalistic account of conscience collapses into something intuitive and unexplained, or his reforming verdicts are unprincipled.

I would rather keep (2), since the sentimentalism is the reason to read Smith at all. Tell me which step fails, why it looked acceptable when I wrote it, and what I would have to surrender to block the inference. If some strengthened version of the argument does survive, say what has to be added to make it work.
