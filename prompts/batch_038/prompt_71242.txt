I sit as one of nine members of the Standing Commission on the Attribution of Responsibility for Armed Conflict, a treaty body that issues non-binding findings on who brought a given war about. Each finding does two things: it apportions shares of responsibility among the parties, which a separate reparations mechanism then uses, and it states to whom the finding is addressed — a particular ministry, a state's continuing institutions, or something else. Our case law is internal and unpublished. Apart from the general historical background to the first finding, everything the Commission has ever said about attribution is contained below; please do not import doctrine from outside it.

The record so far.

**Finding 1 (the founding case: the outbreak of the First World War).** The Commission assigned the major share to the Imperial German government, lesser shares to Austria-Hungary and to Imperial Russia for ordering general mobilization, and a small share to France for the firmness of its guarantee to Russia. It grounded the major share in two things jointly: the unconditional assurance given to Austria-Hungary in early July 1914, coupled with pressure for a rapid strike while the occasion was still usable; and a settled expansionist policy evidenced by the memorandum of war aims drawn up in September 1914 and by the leadership's conviction over the preceding two years that a preventive war was preferable to a later one. The finding was addressed to the imperial government and its advisers. It refused expressly to extend to the German or Austrian peoples, and refused expressly to treat militarism or national character as a bearer of responsibility. Two members dissented, on the ground that Russia's general mobilization — not the assurance to Vienna — was the last step that could have been otherwise.

**Finding 1A.** A petition later asked the Commission to reopen Finding 1, arguing that subsequent historiography treats the September memorandum as a consultation document solicited from economic and military elites rather than an adopted programme of policy. The Commission declined to reopen and gave no reasons beyond saying that its findings concern what parties did and what they could foresee. It has never said whether the war-aims strand of Finding 1 survives, or whether the assurance-to-Vienna strand now carries the finding alone.

**Finding 2 (the delegation case).** A short war began when State Q's armed forces, operating under a retaliation protocol adopted eleven years earlier and triggered by an ambiguous border incident, struck without any civilian decision. Q's ministers were out of communication for the first nine hours and ratified the strike afterwards. Q had no expansionist aims and was at that moment negotiating a border settlement. The Commission held Q responsible for a major share, on three stated grounds: the adoption of the protocol was itself the operative act; the later window in which no one could have revoked it did not exculpate, because Q had manufactured that window; and ratification was no cure. The finding was addressed to Q's government.

**Finding 3 (the mutual-escalation case).** A war was triggered by a non-state armed group's attack on a state that had neither directed nor been able to prevent it. Four parties then escalated in sequence, each acting on what it took to be the others' preparations, and by the end no party could have halted the sequence alone. The Commission divided responsibility evenly among the four, expressly found that no party bore a major share, and stated that where escalation is driven by mutually reinforcing expectations, apportionment by asking who took the last step that could have been otherwise is arbitrary rather than merely difficult.

**Finding 4 (the clean-hands case).** State W had spent a decade building a war-capable economy and articulating territorial claims, but during the crisis worked sincerely and verifiably to avoid war, offered concessions, and was pushed in by another state's ultimatum. The Commission gave W a minor share and the ultimatum-issuer a major share, reasoning that long-standing ambition does not by itself make a party responsible for a war it demonstrably tried to prevent. The finding did not say whether it narrowed Finding 1 or was confined to its facts.

**Finding 5 (the legislative case).** The operative act was a statute, enacted by a parliament with overwhelming public support and reaffirmed by three successive governments of different parties. The Commission addressed the finding to the state's continuing institutions rather than to any ministry, holding that where the responsible act is legislative and repeatedly reaffirmed, attribution to a single government misdescribes what happened — while again declining to extend the finding to the population. In the same finding, the Commission refused to adjust shares on the basis of a private memorandum, discovered decades later, showing that a leading minister had wanted the war throughout, holding that evidence of undisclosed state of mind is not material to its findings in either direction.

**The new case, now before us.** Eleven years before the outbreak, State R's parliament enacted the Guarantee and Response Act: an automatic military guarantee to its ally S, executed by a hardened, pre-sequenced mobilization system that could not be halted once a small standing body of officers and jurists certified that the trigger conditions were met. The parliamentary debates show R's sponsors arguing that the Act would make war less likely by removing doubt; R had no territorial claims against T, its principal rival, and R reduced its own forces over the following decade. R and T then concluded two confidence-building agreements. An armed group operating from territory under T's administration attacked an R outpost. T had tolerated the group and refused three requests to suppress it; our own inquiry found that T's officials had indications of an impending attack and did not act on them, but no evidence of direction. The standing body certified the trigger conditions within twenty-six hours. R's foreign minister, abroad and out of contact with the certification process, sent cables urging S to seek arbitration and asking the body to delay; these cables were unknown to S and T at the time and surfaced only in a later archival release. Certification produced R's mobilization, which T detected within four hours. Unable to reach R's leadership and reading the mobilization as preparation for attack, T mobilized; S mobilized; the war began nine days later. When R's government regained contact it made no attempt to reverse the mobilization, endorsed the war publicly, and its parliament voted war credits four days later with near-unanimous support.

What I need from you:

1. Which attribution doctrines are actually available to us — that is, which rules are jointly consistent with all five findings, stated precisely enough to decide cases? I expect more than one to survive, and I expect at least one candidate that the record quietly rules out; say which and why.
2. For each surviving doctrine, what finding does it require here: shares among R, S, T, and the armed group if you think shares can attach to a non-state actor at all, and the correct addressee for R given that the operative act was a statute reaffirmed by successive governments.
3. Your recommended finding, on the assumption that we should decide as the body that wrote these five findings and not as ourselves. Identify the closest analog in the record and explain why each of the others is either distinguishable or controlling.
4. Where the record genuinely underdetermines the result, say so plainly and state what principle we would be adopting with each available choice — and what we would thereby be committing ourselves to in the next case of this shape.
5. Whether R's foreign minister's cables should affect the shares at all, given that nobody knew of them and nobody acted on them, and what answer each surviving doctrine gives. Finding 5 seems to bear on this, but tell me if you read it differently.
6. One further hypothetical, stated in enough detail to be decidable, on which we could seek an advisory opinion and which would most cleanly separate the surviving doctrines — together with what each doctrine says about it.
