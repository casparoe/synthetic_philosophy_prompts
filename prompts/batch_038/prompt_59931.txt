So I'm auditing a grad seminar this semester on medieval and ancient theories of time and foreknowledge, and a couple weeks back we did De Interpretatione 9 -- the sea battle tomorrow, whether there's already a fact of the matter, the bivalence question -- plus Ockham's way out of theological fatalism, the hard fact versus soft fact business about the past. Half the seminar seemed to think it was a curiosity you dissolve by tinkering with the semantics of future-tensed sentences and then moving on with your life. I kept thinking about 1914 instead, and I want you to take that seriously with me rather than telling me its a stretch. The case I want run through the machinery is the July Crisis. Give me the sequence in enough detail that the analysis has something to bite on -- the Austrian ultimatum in late July, the Russian preparatory measures and then general mobilization on July 30, the German ultimatum and the declarations over the next couple days, the deployment plan that went west through Belgium on a fixed railway schedule, and the episode at the very start of August when a garbled report suggested Britain might stand aside and the Kaiser apparently wanted to send the whole army east instead. Flag the contested stuff as contested (both that exchange and, really, the whole claim that the plan was unalterable -- historians fight about this), but treat it as evidence about how the actors understood their own options, which is what I care about. Then do the mapping: pick candidate moments and say, for each, whether it was already determinate that there'd be a general European war, and what makes it determinate or not.

The part I actually want you to chew on is a distinction I've been trying to sharpen with friends and haven't managed. In the ancient and medieval cases, the future gets settled by something outside the agents -- fate, or divine foreknowledge, or just logic plus the necessity of the past. In 1914 the settling looks like it was done by artifacts the agents built themselves: mobilization tables, timetables, plans with no usable eastern-only variant, and a staff corps whose professional judgment was that countermanding an order already in motion would produce chaos rather than peace. So: does the hard/soft fact distinction survive the transfer? Something like "general mobilization was ordered on July 30" is a hard fact about the past, but it only entails anything about 1916 in conjunction with standing plans and the dispositions of the people who'll execute them -- and those dispositions look like exactly the soft stuff Ockham wanted to keep flexible, since they include what future agents decide. Is the mobilization machinery converting soft facts into hard ones, or just hiding the softness inside the artifact where nobody has to look at it? And here's the wrinkle I can't resolve on my own: the actors believed the settling had already happened, that it couldn't be stopped now, and that belief seems to have been part of what made it come true. The sea battle has no analogue -- nobody's conviction about tomorrow's battle helps decide it -- so tell me whether that reflexivity is a genuine extension of the framework, or a different phenomenon wearing fatalism's clothes.

Then I want the honest reverse direction. Where does it misdescribe things -- in particular, is calling this fatalism a way of laundering a claim about what people knew they could have done into a claim about what was metaphysically settled? And if your conclusion is that the lens mostly just relabels "the situation became irreversible" with some Aristotelian vocabulary, say so flat out.
