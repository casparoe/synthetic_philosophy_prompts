I cover science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and for the better part of two years I have been reporting on migration administration: the hearings, the appeals tribunals, the contractors who manage the queues. What stayed with me was not any single decision but a structural feature of the whole apparatus — that almost nobody ever decides, in the active sense, to let a person in or keep a person out. The decision is smeared across forms, quotas, standing rules, and procedural defaults until there is no one left to hold responsible for the outcome. I have concluded that reportage cannot quite capture this, because the moment you describe it accurately it sounds like a complaint about paperwork, and I want it to land as a moral situation. So I am commissioning a piece of speculative fiction, and I would like you to write it.

The conceit is this. Somewhere — deliberately unspecified, and it should not be a real country or a recognizable real border — a small polity has settled a long legal fight about the basis of its authority to turn people away by unbundling that authority and issuing it as a fixed number of transferable shares. The shares were distributed once, at founding, to whoever was then resident; they can now be sold, inherited, lent, and pledged like anything else, and there is no requirement that a holder live in, have visited, or even be able to locate the place. Each admission case opens a tender window of some days. During that window, holders may tender shares against admission. If the tendered shares reach a stated threshold, the applicant is excluded; if they fall short, admission is automatic. Note the inversion I want you to preserve carefully, because it is the philosophical engine of the story: exclusion is the thing that costs effort and organization, and inclusion is what happens when nobody bothers. The framers sold this as a moral improvement, and for a while they were plausibly right — before the exchange, admission had been decided case by case by individual officers at something close to whim, and the new system was at least transparent, priceable, and appealable. Somebody in the story should make that case and should be right about it; I do not want a parable in which the market is simply the villain.

For the spine, I want a woman who works as a registrar at the exchange: her job is procedural, nightly, and dull — verifying tenders, entering them in the ledger, publishing the count. She holds two inherited shares, worth almost nothing, which for years she tendered on the admitting side out of something like habit and then, as her own life narrowed, stopped tendering at all. The story should turn on a night when an exclusion clears the threshold by a margin of exactly two shares, and on the applicant behind that case, whom she knows slightly and cannot help. From there I want the plot to follow her attempt to find out who actually willed this — which should lead her to the fact that the decisive holdings are largely absentee, accumulated cheaply from people who never thought about them, including one block that passed to an heir abroad who has never heard of the town and cannot be reached in time. She should discover, and the reader should feel, that the capacity to exclude has become a kind of asset that people hold without ever exercising, and that this changes what the right is. Do not resolve the question. The last image should be a small physical act rather than a summation.

A few requirements, since I will be editing this rather than merely reading it. Between 1,800 and 2,600 words, close third person or first person on the registrar, and a title of your choosing. The mechanics must become legible through concrete procedural detail — a fee schedule, a window that closes at a certain hour, a column in a ledger — and never through an explanatory speech; nobody in the story should be a professor of anything, and no philosopher should be named or alluded to by name. No epigraph, no chapter headings, no closing address to the reader. I want the argument about whether a community's authority to turn people away is the kind of thing that can be owned, and what is lost when it can, to be carried entirely by what happens to her over the course of the week — such that a reader who finishes it could state the two strongest opposing positions better than before, without the story having stated either.
