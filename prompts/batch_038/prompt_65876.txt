Our philosophy of mind reading group has now spent something like four months on multiple realizability and I've become convinced we're not actually disagreeing about the same thing — but I can't say what we're disagreeing about instead. Help me diagnose it.

Here's the shape of the split. One camp (call them the autonomy people) says: psychological kinds are multiply realized across species and substrates — octopuses, birds, silicon, whatever — so no psychological kind is identical to any neural kind, so psychology isn't reducible to neuroscience, and the whole type-identity program is dead. They also hold this is a metaphysical point that survives however the empirical details come out. The other camp (the deflationists, roughly the Shapiro/Polger line) says: for a difference in realizers to count as multiple realization of the same kind, the difference has to be irrelevant to the causal contribution that makes the realized kind the kind it is — run the actual comparative neurobiology and most of the alleged cases fail, because what an octopus arm does is not what pain does. They also press the grain point people like Bechtel and Mundale make: individuate neural kinds coarsely enough to be the ones psychology's generalizations quantify over and the diversity largely evaporates; individuate them finely and you get plenty of diversity but lose the match with psychology.

Nobody has moved an inch, and the same four examples get recycled every week. So here's my working hypothesis, which I want you to attack rather than polish. The stall is that 'multiple realization' is doing triple duty and the two camps keep winning different versions of it. Version one is metaphysical: are the realizer kinds distinct from the realized kind? Version two is empirical: are there in fact diverse realizers of one and the same psychological kind across species or substrates? Version three is explanatory: could neuroscience's generalizations actually derive or replace psychology's? The autonomy camp slides between two and three; the deflationists attack one and two and never really engage three. And underneath that, I think the sides are answering different questions — one is asking whether psychology has autonomous explanatory authority, which is a claim about scientific practice, and the other is asking whether an identity claim about kinds is true — and those don't have to come apart or come together in any fixed way.

Second half of the hypothesis: there's a fourth thing tangled in, which is that realization isnt one relation. When someone says a mental property is realized by neural properties they're usually describing a whole having a property in virtue of its parts having properties, not two properties of the same individual at the same time. If you mix those up, some of the arguements in the room stop making sense — the exclusion worry, the plasticity cases, and the whole "MR proves too much" complaint that if variation inside one brain over time counts, then psychology's own kinds are multiply realized by other psychological kinds and the argument can't carry the anti-reductionist weight it was built for.

So: is my diagnosis right, partly right, or wrong about the interesting part? Take a definite stand — tell me what kind of disagreement this actually is (verbal, substantive but underdetermined, two questions sharing a name, an unchecked empirical premise, ill-posed), and say what you'd bet on instead of laying out the options and leaving me to pick. Where you think one of my hunches is just mistaken, say so and say why, because I'd rather lose a hunch than keep a bad one. Then, given whatever diagnosis you land on: what would actually count as progress here — a distinction, a new argument, more comparative data, a change of question — and is there any reason to expect progress at all? Be specific about whether more neuroscience could settle any part of it, or whether the issue sits upstream of the data.
