After forty years in experimental physics I have taken up philosophy in retirement, and the argument that has seized me is the Dutch book defence of probabilism: an agent whose betting quotients violate the axioms of probability can be led to accept a set of wagers that loses money whatever happens, so incoherent degrees of belief are objectively defective rather than merely unfashionable. I should like to know what would count against this, ranked from fatal to merely awkward, with the ranking argued rather than asserted. Please distinguish the candidates a defender of the argument would herself concede — the bridge from credence to betting behaviour, say, or the assumption that the agent must take the whole package at once, or the possibility that an agent whose beliefs are not pinned to a single probability function is immune to books in the first place — from those she would rightly dismiss. I also want to know what the thesis predicts that its rivals do not, and hence where it is genuinely exposed.

One worry of my own. A colleague proposes testing coherence under a harsher protocol: the bookmaker offers wagers one at a time, observes each answer before choosing the next, and the agent may refuse any offer but pays a small fee for refusing. Does guaranteed loss still track incoherence under such a protocol, or has the conclusion come to depend on the size of the fee and on whether the agent can foresee the sequence? If the theorem survives only under protocols no actual bettor ever faces, I would find that more troubling than any counterexample. Tell me whether that instinct is sound, and whether the view has been qualified into a safety that leaves it no content.
