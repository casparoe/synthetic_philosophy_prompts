I am preparing for a prospectus meeting with one of my doctoral students, and I would like a rigorous second opinion before she defends the proposal. My graduate seminar this term is on explanation in the historical sciences, and her project sits exactly on the seam between the covering-law account of historical explanation and its narrative-explanation critics. Her point of departure is Book VI of John Stuart Mill's A System of Logic (1843), specifically the chapters there on the empirical laws of society, on the derivative laws of society, and on the inverse deductive or historical method.

Because I am working from an old edition and do not wish to risk misquoting it, I have set out below my own condensed paraphrase of the relevant stretch of argument rather than Mill's sentences. Please treat what follows as my rendering, not as his wording. Do not try to restore the original phrasing, supply page or chapter numbers, or list primary or secondary sources; work only from what I give you and from whatever you know of the surrounding twentieth-century debate.

My paraphrase, in seven steps:

1. Uniformities of succession among social phenomena that have so far been established by observation alone, without being derived from the laws of human nature, Mill calls empirical laws. They are known merely as matters of fact; what causes produce them, and whether several causes are jointly at work, remains unsettled.

2. Because an empirical law has not been connected to its causes, it gives no ground for confident expectation outside the times, places, and circumstances in which it has been observed. A uniformity that is the joint product of several causes may fail whenever those causes come apart, so the generalization can break down at any point where circumstances change, and we cannot say in advance where that point lies.

3. Empirical laws of society therefore serve only as provisional material for a science. They must be brought into connection with the laws of the causes that generate them — the laws of human nature operating under specified circumstances — before they can support prediction or practical guidance. A uniformity so derived Mill calls a derivative law; unlike the empirical law, it is inferable from those causes independently of the particular observations that first disclosed it.

4. There are two routes between the empirical and the derivative. The direct deductive route starts from known laws of human nature and a given state of circumstances and deduces what must follow. The inverse deductive, or historical, route starts from the observed regularities of history — the observed succession of states of society — and works backwards, asking which candidate laws of human nature, and which competing way of treating social phenomena, would actually generate that succession; the observed sequence then functions to verify the laws supposed to produce it.

5. Both routes rest on a general claim about the causal structure of history: that every state of society is the effect of the state immediately preceding it, so that history as a whole is a series of states each produced by the one before. Without this assumption the historical route would not lead to laws at all, since there would be no reason to expect any order in the succession.

6. Mill also holds that the multitude and variety of causes at work in society, and the impossibility of isolating them experimentally, rule out a chemical or experimental method in social inquiry; and that reasoning from a single cause alone — for instance from self-interest, in the manner of the geometrical method — yields at best approximate generalizations, holding on the whole or for a given people at a given stage, which must be corrected by taking the remaining causes into account.

7. The empirical laws that survive this treatment are, on Mill's reckoning, largely local and temporary: uniformities true of European society across the observed period, which a science of society must eventually show to be consequences of more general laws, or else show to be mere coincidences.

Three things are troubling me, and they are what I need your help with.

First, the status of an empirical law. My student claims that Mill's empirical laws are covering laws by another name, and that his derivative laws are simply those same covering laws once their causal credentials have been made explicit. She proposes the coinage narrative-covering law for the derivative kind, on the ground that a derivative law is only ever deployed within an account of a particular historical sequence. I suspect this collapses a distinction Mill is at pains to keep: an empirical law, on his telling, does not license subsumption at all, precisely because its range of application is unknown, whereas a covering law in the later debate is supposed to license expectation as a matter of nomic necessity. Please say carefully what, on the paraphrase above, actually goes wrong when a uniformity has not been connected to its causes, and whether that failure is one of justification, of scope, or of explanatory force — and then tell me whether the coinage earns its keep or merely dresses up a category mistake.

Second, her larger thesis, which she has drafted as follows: "Mill dissolves the dispute between covering-law and narrative accounts of historical explanation, because on his view narrative and law-governed explanation are not rivals but phases of a single inquiry — the historical method identifies the sequences, deduction supplies the laws, and the sequences then verify the laws." My objection is that the inverse deductive method looks like an epistemology of historical generalization — a way of discovering and testing candidate laws — rather than an account of what it is for a narrative to explain an event. If so, she has transposed a methodological doctrine into a theory of explanation, which is much the same transposition that Mill's empiricist critics and the later anti-covering-law camp both complained about. Assess the thesis on its merits: identify its strongest and weakest points, say whether the transposition charge holds, and state which reading of Mill you would defend if you had to argue the case in the meeting.

Third, two smaller cruxes that will come up. Are Mill's empirical laws and his approximate generalizations the same kind of item, or do they fail for different reasons? And does the claim in step 5 — that each state of society is the effect of the immediately preceding one — supply anything with the content of a covering law for historical narrative, or is it a merely formal presupposition of inquiry that licenses no expectation about any particular succession?
