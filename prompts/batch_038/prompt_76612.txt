I want a thought experiment built to order, for a seminar on Adam Smith's moral theory. What I'm chasing is whether the impartial spectator is ever anything more than idealized consensus -- whether it can come apart from what actual spectators would say when they're properly informed and unprejudiced. So:

Build me a case where every actual spectator knows all the relevant facts, none of them has any stake or partiality toward anyone involved, they see the agent's motives perfectly clearly (no deception, no hypocrisy), they unanimously approve of what he does -- and Smith's impartial spectator still disapproves. Constraints: an ordinary realistic setting (a workplace, a neighborhood, a hospital ward), nothing science-fictional or exotic; the scenario itself should run one paragraph, no more. Stipulating that the spectators are biased or ignorant is cheating, since that's just the easy version of the exercise.

Then do the check. Point to where in Smith's machinery the disapproval actually gets generated -- the change of situation in imagination, the supposed impartial spectator the agent carries in his own breast, whatever it is -- and name the single structural feature of the spectator setup that's doing the work. Tell me what happens to the case if I pull that feature out: does it collapse back into an ordinary story about prejudiced or ill-informed crowds being corrected? Finish by giving the best attack from someone who reads the impartial spectator as nothing but a well-informed, disinterested idealization of real spectator sentiment, and say whether you think that attack lands.
