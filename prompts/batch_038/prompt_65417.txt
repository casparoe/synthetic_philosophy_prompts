Been going back and forth with a friend who does evals work about this over the weekend and I still can't articulate the problem cleanly, so I figure I'll just dump the whole thing on you. I'm writing a section of a workshop paper on iterated amplification and distillation. The setup as I understand it: you take a bunch of copies of some weak model, let them divide up a hard task, and the amplified system produces something better than any one of those weak models could alone; then you distill — train a fresh model to reproduce the amplified system's behavior across lots of inputs — so you get the performance without paying the amplification overhead on every single call. Then you iterate on the distilled model. My snag is about what actually survives the distillation step, and I dont think it's the snag people usually point at.

Here's what I keep circling. When the amplified ensemble answers a hard question, the epistemically interesting output isn't just the verdict, it's the fact that the verdict came out of subagents that disagreed, plus which ones disagreed and about what. Now suppose you distill on (question, final verdict) pairs. The student learns the majority answer in the cases where the ensemble was badly split, and it never learns there was a split — the disagreement is precisely the thing that got compressed away. That seems bad in a way I can't name properly: it looks like a lot of the ensemble's trustworthiness came from the visibility of unresolved disagreement, and distillation destroys that while making the output look *more confident* than the process that generated it actually was. Is that a real problem or am I tangling two things together? I want you to explain what's right and what's wrong with the worry, and be specific about the mechanism — is this an information-loss point, a calibration point, a point about what the distillation loss function is even optimizing, or something else? And say whether it gets worse or better as the task gets more decomposable, because I genuinely can't tell which direction it goes.

The obvious reply is "don't distill the verdict, distill the whole deliberation transcript," which sounds like a fix but I think just relocates the problem, in two directions I'd like you to sort out for me. First, cost: emitting a full deliberation is roughly as expensive as doing one, so you've given up the thing distillation was for — or else you owe an account of what the student is actually saving. Second, and this is the part that confuses me most: even if you train on transcripts, nothing guarantees the student is *performing* the deliberation internally rather than producing transcript-shaped decoration on top of a verdict it reached immediately. So the disagreement-visibility comes back, but as cosmetics. How serious is that, is it any different from the general worry about whether a model's stated reasoning tracks its computation, and where does that leave the amplified-and-distilled pipeline?

Last thing, which is what my advisor actually pushed on. Does any of this bite differently on alignment properties than on competence? The folk claim is that distillation carries capability over more reliably than it carries over alignment, and I want to know whether there's a real conceptual reason for that asymmetry or whether its just a hunch people like. My guess at the reason: competence is mostly a property of the input-output map, so imitating the map transfers it, whereas honesty or corrigibility is a property of what the system does in the cases where the transcript and the verdict come apart — and by construction the distillation data contains very few of those cases, because they're exactly the ones where the amplified system's output was already the aggregate. If that's roughly the right story, spell it out and tell me where it breaks down. If it's wrong, tell me what the better story is, and whether there's any asymmetry at all worth defending.
