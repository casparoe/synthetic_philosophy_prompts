I cover research funding and health policy for a science magazine, and I've been sitting on a story for a couple of months about a mid-sized foundation that has to pick exactly one of three programs with a fixed pot of money. I can't name them, so here's the setup with the identifying stuff scrubbed out.

The pot: roughly $400m over ten years.

- Program A — a curative therapy for a rare neuromuscular disease. On the foundation's own estimates it frees about 2,000 people from severe disability for the rest of their lives (call it 40 years each) and does essentially nothing else.
- Program B — a cheap management regime for a very common chronic condition. Cures nobody, takes a moderately limiting condition down to mildly limiting for about 400,000 people over the same 40 years.
- Program C — an environmental fix (think: substituting out a solvent in a widely used consumer product) that stops about 60 million people from each getting roughly three days of genuinely horrible but completely resolving illness, spread over the next four decades.

The board ran a round robin. A beat B, B beat C, C beat A. Same twelve people, no abstentions. And here's the bit I keep going back to: in each of the three pairings, at least two of the members who voted for the winner had voted the other way in one of the other pairings. There's no faction you can point to, no cure-people versus numbers-people split.

Meanwhile the foundation's own analyst has a spreadsheet that converts all three into healthy-years-equivalents and comes out B, then C, then A — so it contradicts two of the three board verdicts, and nobody on the board seems especially bothered by that either.

The governance document says the ranking has to be complete and acyclic, on the explicit grounds that otherwise the fund gets churned: reopen the decision every year, swap A for C, then C for B, then B for A, each swap looking like a strict improvement while the foundation looses money on transitions and burned runway. Their strategy lead made that argument to me almost word for word and I didn't have an answer, which is definately the thing driving this piece.

So: write me an essay on the controversy underneath all of it, call it 1,500–2,500 words. Is 'better than' transitive when you're comparing outcomes of this kind, and if it isn't, is that a defect the foundation has to engineer around or a feature of the choice they should just accept? Weigh the arguments on both sides — including how much weight you'd actually put on the churning worry, since that's the one that gets deployed at me — but don't land in the middle. I want a verdict and a defense of it.

Two ground rules, because I've read a lifetime's worth of hedged answers:

1. "They're simply incomparable" is off the table. The board has to fund one, and their rules force a comparison. Argue from inside that constraint.
2. Tell me which of the three pairwise verdicts you'd overturn, and why that one rather than either of the others. If you think none of them should go, tell me what you'd change about the decision procedure instead — and show that the fix doesn't just relocate the cycle somewhere else.

Use the numbers I gave you rather than a generic stand-in for the problem. And if your position implies something ugly about Program C — 60 million people is not a small number of people — own that in the text instead of waving at it. No references or reading list, I just want the argument.
