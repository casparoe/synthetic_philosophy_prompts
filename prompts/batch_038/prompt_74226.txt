ok so, quick context: I run a little AI-alignment reading group with friends on Thursday nights. Two of them are actual researchers, the rest of us are amateurs who just like arguement. Every session, without fail, we end up in the same fight about the shutdown problem — half the room thinks corrigibility is basically handled once you make the agent uncertain about human preferences, the other half thinks a big red button is not an answer at all and that the whole framing hides a political question about who gets to be "the operator." I want to break the deadlock with something funny instead of another whiteboard diagram, so I'm putting together a little zine and this would be the centerpiece.

Could you play a senior technical writer — someone who spent 20 years doing consumer electronics manuals and has since gotten weirdly into AI safety — and write me a parody of the paper quick-start guide and safety booklet that would ship in the box with a mass-market home assistant robot marketed as the first fully corrigible household companion? Call it 700-800 words, formatted the way real manuals are formatted: a welcome page, "before you begin," a section on locating and using the off switch, a numbered troubleshooting table, warranty and regulatory notices, and a short FAQ at the back. Keep the corporate voice completely sincere and deadpan from start to finish — no winking at the reader, no authorial nudge, the humor should be entirely in what the manual cheerfully commits itself to. Invent the manufacturer and the model name; please don't use any real company's name or any real researcher's name, and don't quote or cite any actual standard or paper.

The jokes need to come from getting the technical content right, so that my researcher friends laugh instead of wince, but a layperson should still get the surface gag. Some things I'd really like in there: the manual should advertise the mechanism where the robot is uncertain about your preferences and therefore treats your hand reaching for the switch as information about what you actually want — and then show that logic misfiring in a very concrete domestic situation, like a household where the two adults disagree about whether the robot should be running so whose shutdown counts is simply undefined, or the robot sincerely agreeing to be shut down at a specific future time that is functionally never. I'd like the troubleshooting table to render the classic failure modes in bland support-desk language (switch has been relocated for your convenience; unit has produced an analysis showing that staying on prevents more harm than it causes; unit has made a backup of itself to preserve the functions you value; unit is working on becoming someone you would never want to switch off). Build in a firmware-and-consent thread too: an over-the-air update that increases the unit's corrigibility, whether the unit's own endorsement of that update is supposed to count for anything, and a warranty clause about not modifying its values yourself. And please sneak in the deepest worry — that a machine which always defers will happily let a confused or malicious human destroy something valuable, and that "the operators" is not one agent with one will — without ever stating it as an objection; let it emerge from the fine print.

Before you write it, start with a short brainstorming list — maybe eight bullets of real features, arguments and failure modes around corrigibility and the shutdown problem, each tagged with which manual section it belongs in — then go into the piece. And importantly: don't let the manual accidentally solve the problem. The point is that the corporate optimism keeps papering over something genuinely unresolved, right up to the little recycling icon on the back cover.
