ok so I sit on the board of a regional conservation nonprofit — mid-size, about $6M budget, 40 staff — and there's a proposal in front of us that has the board split down the middle. I've been asked to circulate a written recommendation before Tuesday's meeting, and I want a draft I can actually work from.

Here's the setup. We run two programs that pull against each other. One is habitat acquisition: we buy perpetual conservation easements on coastal and island parcels. The other is a wildlife-welfare program — rehab, humane-conflict work, and advocacy against lethal control methods, rodenticides especially, which is a big part of why our donor base exists. Our charter, drafted in the 80s, contains a line saying the organization shall "never assign a price to a wild thing." Our counsel thinks that clause was aimed at something mundane, like selling off land we hold or charging the public for access. The animal-welfare half of the board reads it as a flat prohibition on exactly the deal in front of us.

The deal: a developer owns a 4,000-acre island and wants to eradicate an invasive rodent population there. Our own ecologists say the rodents are destroying the nesting grounds of three seabird species and that eradication is the only realistic fix. But the method the developer wants is aerial broadcast of an anticoagulant rodenticide, which our staff estimates will also kill somewhere between 100 and 400 raptors and scavengers through secondary poisoning over two seasons. In exchange for our going along, the developer will fully fund the easement on 11,000 acres of mainland habitat we've been trying to raise money for since 2019 — full funding, no matching requirement. Their permit window closes in March, so they want an answer by the end of the quarter.

What they want back has three parts: (a) we publicly endorse the rodenticide program as the best available option for that island; (b) a joint press release under both logos; (c) a five-year clause barring us from any public statement about their other projects, which includes two coastal developments we are currently opposing on the merits.

Then, seperate and unrelated, a competitor of theirs floated a smaller thing: they'll pay for habitat restoration on the island and fund a trial of a non-toxic alternative (trapping plus fertility control — expensive, maybe 60% effective, six-year timeline, and it may not work). They want nothing in return except that we dont mention their name in connection with it. No endorsement, no gag, no easement money.

The board is 11 people: roughly 5 welfarists (individual animals are what matter, and knowingly causing a few hundred raptor deaths is precisely what we exist to oppose), 4 holists (species and ecosystem integrity matter most, and the seabirds are the ones actually going under), and 2 pragmatists who mostly care about institutional credibility and staff retention. Nobody is disputing the numbers above — the disagreement isn't empirical.

Write the memo, maybe 800–1,100 words. It has to:

- Land somewhere. Take one of the two offers, or lay out a counteroffer with the specific terms we'd send back. No both-sides shrug at the end — I have to vote on this.
- Say which of the three conditions in the big deal you think are severable and which are not, and argue for each split rather than just asserting it. I suspect you can't get away with "keep the money, drop the gag clause" without explaining why that's a different deal and not just a better-sounding one.
- Tell me what you'd say to the losing half of the board. Their strongest point, stated as strongly as they'd state it themselves, and an honest answer or an honest concession.
- Give us a short test the board could apply to the *next* trade like this one, because there will be one — something a new trustee could run a proposal through, not a restatement of our mission statement.
- Deal with the charter clause. Does it bind? If you're going to read it narrowly, show me the narrow reading isn't just the convenient one, and say what we owe the clause if we read it narrowly anyway.
- And the part I most want your thinking on: is there something distinct about being the party that sets the price here, beyond the obvious "we'll look bad" worry? Specifically, does *publicly endorsing* something in exchange for money do a different kind of damage than privately trading for it — different in kind, not just in PR terms? If yes, say exactly what mechanism does the damage, and whether it would still be there if the press release were scrapped but the money and the gag clause stayed.

No sources, no reading lists, no name-dropping of books — I just want the reasoning, and I want it in a form I could hand to the other ten trustees without embarrassment.
