I convene a second-year module here called something like "Rules, Authority and Nonsense" — 22 students, and I've got one 75-minute seminar slot in week 6 where I want to do the session on self-amending legal systems. They'll have had the lecture on the basics already (rule of recognition, Hart, the usual), but they won't have met Hofstadter's Nomic or Suber's book on the paradox of self-amendment, and I'd rather they didn't hear either name until the very end. Design me an in-class game that makes them feel what it's like to live under a rulebook that can rewrite itself.

Here's what I want to happen. They get an initial rulebook — short, printed, maybe eight rules — containing an amendment procedure with a supermajority requirement, one entrenched rule stating that it cannot be amended, one rule stating that every rule may be repealed, and a rule setting up a single adjudicator (rotating, so everyone gets a turn) whose rulings bind until overturned. I want the incentives and structure arranged so that somewhere around the middle of the session the group passes an amendment that retroactively changes who was entitled to vote in an earlier round: the amendment, if valid, makes its own enabling vote invalid, and if invalid, entrenches itself. They should run into that on their own. If its not going to happen naturally, the nudge has to come from the rulebook or the scoring, not from me. Payoffs shouldn't need real money — tokens, points on the board, whatever — but there has to be a genuine reason for a table to want a given amendment passed that isn't just "because the lecturer says so". Two or three of my students are aspiring corporate lawyers and will rules-lawyer absolutely everything; I actually want that, but tell me how to stop one table from monopolising the whole hour.

Room constraints: no printer on the day, no laptops, just a whiteboard, a pack of index cards and a timer, so the materials need to be things I can hand out or they can write themselves. Give me a minute-by-minute timeline, the exact text of the initial rulebook (short rules, plain English), a debrief that maps each moment of play onto a piece of the conceptual machinery — validity, entrenchment, retroactivity, and the difference between a rule being logically unamendable and merely politically hard to amend — plus a candid note on which part of the paradox the game simply cannot generate live and that I'll have to supply by hand, with a worked example of that bit ready to go on the board. And tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and what to do about it in the moment. British English please, and keep it practical — I won't have time to build anything elaborate the night before.
