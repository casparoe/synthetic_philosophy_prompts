I run an informal foundations reading group — mostly mathematics graduate students, two philosophers, and one economist — and we have just spent three weeks arguing about the axiom of choice without ever pinning down what we actually disagree about. The flashpoints were these: whether the Banach–Tarski decomposition is a reductio of choice or merely a surprising theorem; whether one member is right that admitting any choice principle whatsoever forces you to admit excluded middle; and whether the economist's arguments, which quietly lean on nonprincipal ultrafilters and Hahn–Banach, survive in a universe with less choice than ZFC.

Please build us a self-diagnosis instrument. Eight to ten questions, each putting a concrete mathematical situation to the respondent — countably many pairs of indistinguishable socks, a family of nonempty sets indexed by the reals with no selection rule in sight, a set of reals that admits no measure, a proof that turns on an ultrafilter, a well-ordering of the continuum — each with two or three possible responses, plus a key that reads a position off the pattern of responses. No question may name a choice principle or ask anyone to endorse one; we should be sorted by what we say about cases, not by which label we claim. Assume respondents are fluent in ordinary mathematical language but know nothing of forcing or constructive set theory.

The leaves must include, at minimum: unqualified ZFC; dependent choice but no more; countable choice but no more; the Boolean-prime-ideal-and-Hahn–Banach package without full choice; the constructive position on which choice collapses into excluded middle; determinacy; a multiversist position; and a deflationary position on which the whole quarrel is verbal. But derive the tree from the logical space rather than from that list, identify which combinations of answers no position occupies and whether each empty cell is incoherent, unstable, or merely unexplored, and flag any two leaves your questions cannot in fact distinguish.

Throughout, mark which claims about implication, independence, and consistency are settled mathematics and which remain contested among people who know the subject, and do not let a contested philosophical verdict pass for the consensus of set theorists.
