I'm a second-year PhD student on my department's research integrity panel — seven of us, three faculty, two grad students, two from the research office. We're deciding whether a postdoc doctored a westen blot figure in a published paper. Our policy says misconduct requires both that the image was manipulated in a way that misrepresents the data and that it was done knowingly, but it never says how we're supposed to vote. Last week's straw poll: 5–2 that the image was manipulated (two of us had already said we'd just go with whatever the one member who actually understands blot imaging said, and she said yes), 4–3 that it was knowing, but 3–4 against finding misconduct overall. So a majority says each ingredient is present and a majority says the charge fails, and it's not just one confused person being inconsistent with themselves.

Now one faculty member wants to formalize the expertise thing — split into subpanels, one per question, then combine their answers — and another says whatever we write down won't be anybody's actual judgment, just an artifact of the order we happened to vote in.

Draft the memo I'll circulate before Monday's meeting: what we should decide about this postdoc, and what voting procedure we should adopt for future cases. Give reasons the other six could actually argue with, and take the "that's not a real collective judgment" objection seriously instead of waving it away. No citations, I'll add any myself.
