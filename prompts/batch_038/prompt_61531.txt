So I run terminology for a small certification body — we write the standards that paint and coatings manufacturers get their products tested against, and our definitions end up quoted verbatim in contracts and in regulatory submissions, so a sloppy one its not just a nitpick, it's a lawsuit waiting to happen. The committee meets four times a year, there's maybe a dozen definition disputes per meeting, and I get about ten minutes per term to bring something to the table. The people in the room are chemists and lawyers, not philosophers, and they will not read anything that looks like a paper.

Here's the problem. We keep having the same fight in different costumes: whether a term should be pinned to what the stuff *is* or to what the stuff *does*. "Water-based" — is that about the carrier actually being water, or about the coating performing like the water-carried coatings we've always called that? "Flame retardant" — mechanism or performance? Every time some new chemistry shows up, or a test method gets more sensitive, one camp says the term obviously still applies and the other says it obviously doesn't, and nobody can tell whether we're having a factual disagreement or just waving intuitions at each other and calling it arguement.

My sense is there's usable machinery for this in philosophy of language. The Kripke/Putnam line about how a term like water got fixed to a sample rather than to a description, so that finding out it's H2O was a discovery about the world and not a change of meaning. And then the two-dimensional stuff built out of that, where you can ask two genuinely separate questions of a candidate definition: one where you hold the world fixed and vary what we know about it — suppose we learn that the clear drinkable liquid in the lakes was XYZ all along, does water then mean XYZ? — and one where you hold what we know fixed and vary the world — in a world where H2O is a gas at room temperature, is that gas still water? Half my committee answers no to both and thinks they've answered one question.

Can you turn that into something I can actually run on a single term in ten minutes? I want a sequence of questions to ask, in order, that a chemist or a lawyer can follow cold without having read any of the background: what each step is testing, what counts as a settled answer at that step, and which of four outcomes it produces — define by role, define by constitution, define by a measurement procedure, or refuse to define at all and retire the term. Mark clearly the places where the two questions come apart and there's no fact of the matter left, so I know when to stop philosophizing and put it to a vote as an explicit policy choice. And be honest about where the procedure will misfire, or where it will just relocate the fight into new vocabulary. No citations or reading list, I couldn't chase them anyway — just the machinery and how to apply it.

Then run it on four of ours: lead-free, currently defined as below a numeric threshold, where the question on the table is whether a future toxicology finding that the threshold is ten times too high changes what the term meant or only what it ought to mean going forward; flame retardant, which half the committee wants tied to a halogen mechanism and the other half wants purely performance-based; water-based, with a real formulation in front of us that's 55% water and 45% co-solvent; and durable, which is my own baby and which I've been trying to get a proper definition of for two years — be blunt on that one, and if the procedure says it can't be defined and should be swapped out for a test method plus a number, say so plainly rather than softening it, because I'd much rather hear it from you than in the meeting.

One format thing and it's not negotiable: no headings, no bullet points, no bold, no tables, none of that. Write it as continuous prose, the way you'd write a long email to a colleague — the procedure should still come through as a clear ordered sequence, just in sentences. Anything that looks like software documentation does not get read in my building.
