For three years my research group has run everything through a shared internal wiki: instrument calibration procedures, the troubleshooting lore that never made it into any published methods section, running notes on which reagent batches misbehave, and the decision history behind every protocol revision. Nobody has memorized it and nobody is supposed to — a first-year student can run a difficult assay in an hour because the wiki tells them what to do next. Last month the server was down for ten days. We did not merely lose access to information; we lost the capacity to do work we had been doing routinely, and the postdocs who had been here longest were hit hardest, since their own recall had been calibrated against the wiki rather than stocked with its contents.

One of my postdocs draws a strong conclusion from this. He says the wiki is not a place we store knowledge but part of our cognitive system, so that the group's thinking was literally spread out onto the server, and the outage was something more like a temporary lesion than a loss of access to records. I suspect he is dressing up an ordinary point — we were cut off from information we depend on — in ontologically loaded language, and that treating the server as part of our minds buys him nothing he could not say more plainly.

Which of us has the better of it? I would like an answer that says what actually turns on the disagreement rather than declaring it merely verbal, and that specifies what would have to be true of the wiki — its availability, how we relate to what is in it, how it gets revised — for his claim to amount to more than a redescription.
