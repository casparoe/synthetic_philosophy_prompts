I work on observability for distributed systems: structured logging, distributed tracing, metrics, and services that report on their own internals. I have done that for about a decade and I am completely fluent in it. My philosophy background is nil — no coursework, and my knowledge of the introspection debate comes from pop-science pieces about confabulation and from arguments with colleagues about whether a language model's descriptions of its own internal state should be treated as data.

What I want is not an explanation pitched at a layperson; it is a translation. Take the philosophical debate about the reliability of introspection — self-knowledge of one's own current conscious states — restate it position by position in my vocabulary, and then tell me what did not survive.

Please handle these positions, in this order:

1. the traditional claim that a person enjoys privileged, or unusually secure, access to their own present experience;
2. the view that introspection is an inner monitoring mechanism with its own reliability profile, structurally like a sensor pointed at the system it belongs to;
3. the view that we do not read our mental states at all but infer them, using the same rough-and-ready theory of mind we apply to other people, which is why confident confabulation is so common;
4. the stronger claim that reports about one's own ongoing experience are pervasively inaccurate rather than merely noisy at the margins, and that we cannot tell from inside the channel when they are trustworthy;
5. the transparency idea, that I settle a question about my own belief by attending to the world and the reasons bearing on it rather than by inspecting anything internal;
6. the view that for some mental states a sincere self-ascription partly constitutes the state, so the report is not the kind of thing that could be a misreading.

For each, give me three things: what maps cleanly onto notions I already have — instrumentation, sampling, lossy buffers, calibration, ground truth, the oracle problem, probes that change the behavior they measure, a monitor that fails whenever the thing it monitors fails; what you had to distort or stretch to make fit; and what has no expression in my vocabulary at all.

Then two further sections.

An honesty audit. Go back through your own translations and flag every place where a word from my side — state, read-out, channel, ground truth, signal, noise, instrument — silently carried a commitment the philosophers would dispute. I want to see where the translation was doing argumentative work rather than reporting a correspondence.

The residue I actually care about. In my framework, a state that no probe can read and that leaves no trace in behavior is simply not part of the system's contract; there is nothing further to it. The debate seems to presuppose that there can be facts about what it is like for someone that outrun every possible read-out, including their own. Can my vocabulary express that claim? If it cannot, tell me which of these you think is true: that the operationalist instinct behind my vocabulary is correct and the philosophers are pointing at nothing, or that my vocabulary is missing a distinction I have never needed because my systems always have an outside.

One short closing paragraph: of the items you listed as untranslatable, which are untranslatable only because they depend on features humans have that our systems lack, and which would remain untranslatable for any self-reporting system, however built?

No citations, no reading list, no invented references — I want your reconstruction, and I accept that it is a reconstruction. Where you are relying on a contested reading of a position, or on a version of it a specialist might reject, say so in the relevant section rather than presenting it as settled. Finish with a calibration note in my terms: how confident are you in these translations overall, and which single claim in your answer is the one you are least sure of, and why?
