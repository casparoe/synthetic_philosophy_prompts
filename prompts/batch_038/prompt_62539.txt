I'm three years into a materials-science PhD and auditing a metaphysics seminar on dispositions this term, which turns out to be an awkward combination: the seminar's examples are all drinking glasses and live wires, while my bench is full of objects whose behaviour doesn't fit the template. I've been keeping a private list of cases where I feel confident about the verdict, and I'd like to know whether those verdicts add up to a principle. To be clear about what I'm after: not a survey of who proposed what, and no references — I've encountered the standard machinery already (finkish dispositions, masks and antidotes, and the revised conditional analyses that appeal to a shared intrinsic character plus a majority-of-cases clause). What I want is the analysis itself, in condition-by-condition form, tested against my list.

Here are the cases, with my verdicts.

1. Steel bearings shipped wrapped in vapour-phase corrosion inhibitor paper. The inhibitor saturates the air in the package and prevents oxidation. My verdict: the steel is still disposed to corrode; the packaging masks the disposition. When the inhibitor reservoir is exhausted after a couple of years and the bearings bloom with rust, I take that to be the original disposition manifesting late, not a new disposition acquired at the moment of exhaustion.

2. A cornstarch-and-water suspension. Strike it hard and it resists like a solid; leave it alone and it pours. My verdict: it is *not* disposed to be rigid. It is disposed to *become* rigid when struck, and I want those treated as different dispositions with different manifestations, even though the second looks like a route to the first.

3. An epoxy with microcapsules of monomer and a dispersed catalyst. It cracks under load, the capsules rupture, monomer wicks into the crack plane, polymerises, and the crack closes. My verdict: it is disposed to crack, and separately disposed to heal. The fact that no crack survives the day does not make the ascription of cracking false — the crack really happened.

4. An ordinary brittle mug into whose base I've potted a shaped charge, wired to an accelerometer so that it fires the instant an impact is registered. My verdict: the mug is not fragile, even though the conditional "if it were struck sharply, it would shatter" is straightforwardly true. My lab-mates think I'm being precious about this one.

5. The same mug with the charge replaced by a "custodian" — a device that stiffens the lattice, but only for as long as stress is applied, so that the mug bounces off the floor intact. My verdict: the mug is fragile, even though the corresponding conditional is false. Cases 4 and 5 are the pair that bothers me most: same mug, same stimulus, opposite outcomes, and my verdicts track neither the truth of the conditional nor the outcome.

6. A museum glass in a sealed vault on which nothing ever acts, and where no stressor of the relevant kind ever occurs. My verdict: fragile. The same glass after being properly annealed. My verdict: not fragile. So whatever separates these two cannot be a matter of whether the manifestation ever occurs.

7. Now a case I've invented, because I can't find a real one: a glass whose own lattice, with no added machinery whatever, spontaneously reorganises into a tougher configuration whenever stress is applied — case 2's physics applied to fracture, or case 5's custodian with the machine deleted. My verdict is genuinely torn. My instinct says not fragile, because nothing about it ever cracks; but it is structurally identical to the custodian mug minus the device, and I cannot see why the presence of a machine in the neighbourhood should change what the glass is like right now, all by itself.

8. A foam cradle that saves a fragile component from breaking in something like two drops out of three. My verdict: still fragile, but less so — and I have no idea what "less so" is supposed to mean if a disposition is a matter of what would happen. I note with some dread that any clause of the form "in most cases the stimulus brings about the manifestation" is going to misfire here.

9. My brother, who has flushed scarlet since childhood, has been on a low-dose beta-blocker for a year; the flushing response no longer fires. My verdict: he is still disposed to blush. My cousin, an amateur actor, has simply stopped flushing after a decade of stage work — the response is gone, so far as anyone can tell. My verdict: she is not disposed to blush. From the outside these two are indistinguishable: neither blushes when embarrassed, and neither has any observable difference in what they'd do.

10. Finally, the case I keep reversing on. Suppose the custodian in case 5 runs on a timer and is energised only during shipping hours, six in the morning to six at night. At three in the afternoon the mug is in the crate under a live field; at seven it is on my desk. Is the mug fragile at three? I want to say the mug does not change its properties when the timer switches off, since nothing has happened to the mug; but I also want to say that a mug under a live field genuinely would not break if dropped, and that fragility is supposed to be about exactly that.

The best principle I've managed on my own is this: x is disposed at t to manifest M when S'd just in case x has at t some property P that would bring about M in S-circumstances unless something interferes with P's doing its work — where "interferes" is meant to exclude the shaped charge, which doesn't so much obstruct P as bypass it entirely. You can see the difficulty: that last clause is doing all the work, and I can't say what it excludes without already knowing the answer.

So: give me one principle, or at most two or three competing ones, stated precisely clause by clause, and for each clause tell me which of my cases forces it in. Then run each candidate over the whole list and report where it matches my verdicts and where it conflicts. Where it conflicts, say plainly which verdict I should abandon and argue for it — and if a candidate saves all ten only by way of a clause I could not independently motivate, tell me so, and tell me whether the simpler principle that overturns two or three of my verdicts is the better bargain, and which two or three those ought to be. I'd also like to know which single case on my list is doing the most work: which one, if I withdrew it, would let the analysis collapse into something much less baroque.

After that, give me your principle's verdicts on three situations I have deliberately not adjudicated. First, a pane of glass bonded between two sheets of polycarbonate so that the composite carries the load and the glass ply is never permitted to reach its fracture strain: is the ply fragile? Second, a nucleus whose disposition to decay seems to have no further microstructural story behind it — does my analysis need a basis clause at all, and can anything mask or fink a chance? Third, a mug that is not fragile, but such that if it were struck a device would first make it genuinely fragile and then let the blow break it — the conditional true, the basis acquired at the very moment of triggering, and the shattering authentically caused by that acquired basis rather than by the device. Fragile, not fragile, or no determinate answer, and why should I accept whichever you say?

Finish by telling me what kind of case would refute each candidate principle you offer — what I should be looking for, either on my bench or in my intuitions, to find out that the analysis is wrong.
