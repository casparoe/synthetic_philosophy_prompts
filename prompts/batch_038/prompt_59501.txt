I am writing a novel built around a quarrel between two characters, a Peircean and a Jamesian, and before I can write it honestly I need to know what kind of quarrel it actually is. As I understand the positions: on Peirce's side, the truth of a claim is the verdict the community of inquirers would converge on were investigation pursued far enough, and pragmatism is a maxim for clarifying concepts by their conceivable practical effects rather than an account of what makes a belief true; on James's side, in the words of the second of his pragmatism lectures, "the true is the name of whatever proves itself to be good in the way of belief, and good, too, for definite, assignable reasons," with the doctrine earning its keep precisely where the evidence has not yet come in — moral and religious hypotheses above all. My hypothesis, which I want tested rather than confirmed, is that the two are not disagreeing about truth at all: Peirce is answering a question about the logic of inquiry, James a question about who may believe what in advance of the evidence, and James has lodged the second question under the first one's name — which would explain why Peirce's protests, down to his coinage of the name pragmaticism, consistently talk past him. Is that right, or is there a substantive disagreement about the nature of truth that survives the separation? Please diagnose the structure of the impasse from what each man actually says, tell me whether it is verbal, substantive, or two questions wearing one label, and say what would count as progress: a distinction, a case that forces the issue, or the recognition that none is forthcoming. Reasons, not a survey of pragmatism. And since this bears directly on how I characterize both men, state how confident you are in your diagnosis and single out the point on which you are least sure. Please answer in British English.
