Working title: Who sets the terms? Religious belief as a regulative commitment

1. Set-up. Two readings of what Wittgenstein is up to. (a) Expressivist: religious utterances look like descriptions but are really expressions of an attitude or a way of living, so they were never in the truth-aptness business. (b) Fideist/hinge: they are beliefs, but framework-level ones, insulated from evidence — the stuff that stands fast for us and against which particular claims get tested. My claim is that both get the same thing wrong. They both make the mark of a religious belief a matter of *insulation*, of evidence failing to reach it. That's the wrong axis.

2. Proposal. The distinguishing feature isn't that evidence can't get in, it's who gets to say what would count as evidence against. For an ordinary empirical belief the standards of defeat are set independently of the belief — you didn't get to decide in advance what would refute your claim about when the bus runs. For a religious commitment, on my account, the commitment supplies its own defeater-conditions. So: call a commitment *regulative* when its own content determines what counts as a reason for and against it, and *descriptive* when that determination comes from outside. Take the exchange where the believer says he believes in a Last Judgement and his interlocutor hedges, saying only that it's possible. On the insulation reading the difference is one of confidence, and Wittgenstein's insistence that it isn't comes out as a pious refusal to admit it. On my reading the hedge is issued from inside a game where the picture functions as a hypothesis, so the two aren't differing in degree of strength on one question — they disagree about who adjudicates. That, I think, is why he talks about a difference in kind rather than in distance, and it's also why he can say the believer isn't going to be argued out of it by evidence while still refusing to call the belief irrational.

3. Two places I already don't trust.
(i) Over-application. The test doesn't pick out religion. Trust in a partner, loyalty to a friend, the axioms I work with in arithmetic, a political conviction, arguably the principle of induction — all look regulative by that definition. If religion lands in a class that big, have I explained anything or just relocated it? I keep flip-flopping on whether the breadth is a bug that makes the definition useless, or the honest result, since Wittgenstein's point may never have been that religion is sui generis but that a whole family of commitments gets misfiled as hypotheses.
(ii) The mode/content split. I want to say the content stays descriptive — the believer insists it is about what will happen, and that insistence is part of what the commitment is — while the mode of holding it is regulative. If that works I escape (a). If it doesn't, section 1's first reading was right all along and I've just handed it a new label. I genuinely can't tell which.

4. A bolt-on I'm unsure about. If regulative commitments aren't tracking some proposition's truth, then the accuracy-first machinery in formal epistemology — proper scoring rules, coherence cashed out as immunity to accuracy domination, that sort of thing — can't represent them at all, because there's nothing there to score. That would explain why that framework produces such poor accounts of faith: not because it's hostile to religion, but because it was built for descriptive commitments only. The obvious objection is that being right about the world is exactly what the believer takes to be at stake, and then I need a two-tier picture I don't have.
