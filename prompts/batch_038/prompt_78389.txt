I'm a software engineer, no philosophy background at all — I've spent the last decade on build systems and artifact signing, and I've been reading history on the side, and I keep tripping over the same problem shape from both directions. So bear with me if this is a weird way to ask.

The episode is Glastonbury Abbey, 1191. Here's what I've assembled from pop-history stuff, so please correct me where I'm off — I don't read Latin and I'm sure some of this is folklore about folklore:

- the abbey burned down a few years earlier (1184?) and the monks were rebuilding and badly short of money
- they dug in the cemetery and announced they'd found Arthur's body, and Guinevere's
- they produced a lead cross, buried under the slab, that supposedly named Arthur and placed him in Avalon — and as far as I can tell this is also the first time anyone claimed Glastonbury *was* Avalon
- Gerald of Wales wrote it up within a couple of years, said he'd been there in person, and said Henry II had tipped the monks off after hearing the burial site from a Welsh bard
- the bones got reburied with ceremony, later moved again with a king present, the tomb became a pilgrimage draw, and the whole thing was wrecked at the dissolution; the cross went missing somewhere along the way

So today there is zero physical evidence and only the write-ups. What I can't stop thinking about is that this is structurally a provenance failure, and my day job has a whole vocabulary for that:

1. Every claim about an artifact is a chain — object, who found it, who handled it, who vouched for it, who copied the vouching. The claim is only as good as the weakest link, and crucially the links have to be *independent*.
2. Mirrored sources. Ten registries all carrying the same package is not ten attestations, it's one, replicated. If every later chronicle about Arthur's bones traces back to Gerald, or to a single monastic report, then "many witnesses" is one interested witness rendered ten times.
3. Self-signed trust anchor. The party benefiting from the claim is also the party authenticating it. No external authority in the loop.
4. No revocation. Once the bones are in a marble tomb and the abbey's identity depends on them, retraction is essentially impossible. The forgery becomes load-bearing infrastructure.

What I want is a real analysis, not the standard "monks faked it for cash" take — I've read that one already. Specifically:

1. Correct my facts, then tell me which of them the whole analysis actually hangs on. If I've got something wrong that flips the verdict, say so up front.
2. Do the mapping properly. Who is the trust anchor here, which links are genuinely independent, and does the lead cross add any evidential weight at all or is it just a self-signed certificate with extra steps? Was there anything in the 1191 package that a sharp skeptic standing in the cemetery should have found convincing?
3. The question I care most about: what evidence, in principle obtainable in 1191, could establish that a particular skeleton is Arthur's? Is that claim unfalsifiable by construction, or merely very hard? Because the answer seems to decide whether this was a fraud or just an ordinary unfounded identification made by people who wanted it to be true.
4. Pull in whatever the actual philosophy of testimony has to say — I gather there's an old argument about how much evidence a report of something very surprising requires, and something about a hearer's background knowledge overriding a report. And is "look at their motives" a legitimate defeater or just an ad hominem dressed up?
5. The recursive part. The exhumation is now a solidly documented historical event, and what it established isn't true. So did the legend become history, or did the *belief* become history? Also the dig invented the Avalon identification on the spot, which then fed the legend for centuries — the pipe runs both directions. Does the best account of the legend/history boundary have room for that feedback loop, or does it quietly assume one-way flow?
6. The bones and the cross are gone; the entire basis is now text about text. Does that put us in a worse epistemic position than for any other medieval event, or the same one?
