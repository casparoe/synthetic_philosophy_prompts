So I run a little reading group on the ethics of doing more than you have to, and last week I tried to build an argument showing that supererogation is a category that cant be stabilized — either it collapses into obligation or it takes obligation down with it. Half the group thinks it's devastating, the other half thinks I made a dumb mistake somewhere in the middle, and I honestly can't tell which. Here's the thing:

(1) If an agent can do A or can do B, and A is morally better than B, then doing B is wrong.
(2) Grenade case. Dana can throw herself on the grenade or not. Jumping on it is morally better. So not jumping on it is wrong, and there's no room left over for "permissible but less good." That's supererogation collapsing into duty right at the start.
(3) But keep going. Beneficence has no ceiling: for anything you actually do, there was some morally better thing you could have done instead — give more, stay longer, absorb more of the cost yourself. So for every act X, there's a morally better available Y.
(4) From (1) and (3): every act you perform is wrong.
(5) Here's the principle I really don't want to give up. Wrongness has to be avoidable. If literally everything you could have done was wrong, then "you ought to have done otherwise" doesn't pick anything out, and it isn't fair to call what you did wrong. Blame needs at least one permissible item on the menu.
(6) From (4) and (5): nothing is wrong.
(7) (2) and (6) can't both hold. So (1) is false — betterness doesn't by itself make the worse option wrong.
(8) Except (1) is my only bridge from "this would be better" to "that was wrong." Drop it and I have no account of what's wrong with strolling past the shallow pond when wading in and grabbing the toddler is available and better. So the argument either eats supererogation or it eats wrongdoing, and I can't find the third option.

If I had to pick I'd keep (5) and (3) and sacrifice (1), but then I'm explaining the pond case with what, a threshold that I'd have to defend from scratch? And I'm not sure (5) survives contact with the fact that people who believe in supererogation are perfectly happy to wedge wrongness apart from blameworthiness in the other direction.

So — which step do you reject, and why did it look innocent when I wrote it down? I want the price tagged on each candidate: what I loose if I drop (1), if I drop (3), if I drop (5), and whether (2) and (4) can really be run off the same principle at all. Also tell me if there's a gap that makes the chain invalid before we even get to choosing a premise, and if you think some term is doing double duty across the steps, name it and say exactly where the switch happens — I have a vague suspicion about this but I can't pin it down.
