So I'm a novelist, mostly historical, and I've been sitting on this thing for three years — it might end up being a book in five parts, each one a society in the middle of what people now politely call "collapse." The problem is I have like twelve candidate settings and I have to cut to three, and my own judgement is completely shot from rereading the same material over and over.

Can you play the role of a comparative archaeologist who also happens to have story instincts — someone who's read both the collapse literature and the pushback against it (the resilience/transformation camp, the people who think Diamond got the Pacific islands badly wrong, the folks who argue most of these were relocations and reorganisations rather than deaths). Think carefully about this one before you start scoring, and don't just pattern-match to the standard textbook version of each case.

The candidates:

1. Eastern Mediterranean around 1200 BCE — the palace systems, Hattusa, Ugarit, Mycenaean citadels, the whole systems-collapse / Sea Peoples story
2. Classic Maya southern lowlands, 9th century
3. Rapa Nui / Easter Island
4. Cahokia and the broader Mississippian world
5. Angkor and the Khmer hydraulic city
6. Norse Greenland
7. Chaco Canyon
8. The Indus cities — Harappa, Mohenjo-daro, the de-urbanisation
9. Post-Roman Britain, 5th century
10. Great Zimbabwe and whatever replaced it

Score each one on three axes. These are my own terms, invented for this book, so please use my definitions and not whatever you'd normally mean by these words:

- **Continuity (0–10)**: within about two or three generations of the political/economic breakdown, how much of the population was still living in the same landscape, speaking the same language, making recognisably the same stuff. 10 = pure transformation, nobody actually went anywhere. 0 = genuinely gone, empty land, replacement population, or dead.
- **Legibility (0–10)**: could a person alive at the time have *experienced* it as an ending? 10 = it compressed into a single lifetime, you could watch it happen. 0 = too slow, too patchy, or too regionally uneven for anyone standing inside it to know they were in a collapse at all.
- **Myth load (0–10)**: how much of the popular telling is flatly contradicted or heavily qualified by specialists. 10 = the version your average reader has in their head is mostly wrong. 0 = the popular version is roughly what the specialists say.

Weights: continuity 0.45, legibility 0.35, myth load 0.20. Give me a weighted total out of 10, one decimal place.

Then I want, in this order:

1. A brainstorm pass first — a couple of lines per candidate, just the single strongest piece of evidence for each of the three scores. Sloppy is fine here, this is thinking out loud.
2. A tier list, S/A/B/C, with one sentence on why anything in S is in S.
3. Your three picks for the book, and for each, one sentence on what a novel set there would actually be *about* — not the plot, the thing it would be arguing with itself over.
4. Which of the ten you're least confident about, and why — I care more about honest uncertainty than a clean table.
5. One candidate that scores well on my axes but novelists basically never touch, and one that's badly overexposed and should probably be left alone.

Two house rules. No bibliography, no citations, no paper titles or dates — I can't check them and I don't want a reference list I'd have to go chase down. Reasoning only. And if you think my weighting is dumb, say so plainly and give me your alternative three under whatever weights you'd prefer instead.
