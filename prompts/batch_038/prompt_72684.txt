Our department runs a work-in-progress group in which each participant circulates a one-page statement of their position in advance, and the session is devoted entirely to attacking it. Mine is on scientific realism, and before I send it round I would like the statement audited for internal coherence rather than explained back to me. I know this literature well enough: I am not looking for a survey, a bibliography, or a primer on the positions I list, and I would rather you engage with the set as a set than lecture me on structural realism generally. Here is the statement, grouped as it will appear.

On the history of science:

1. The pessimistic meta-induction is sound. Caloric, phlogiston, and the luminiferous ether were all embedded in theories that were empirically successful by the lights of their own day, and all were ontologically false. Any realism worth holding should be built so that its commitments would have survived those transitions.
2. My realist commitment therefore extends no further than structure: what is carried across theory change is the mathematical content — the equations and the relations they encode — and that is all we are entitled to claim as known. This is roughly the epistemic structural realism associated with Worrall. My flagship example is the recovery of Fresnel's optics within Maxwell's theory. I also expect future change to modify even this structural knowledge, and I am comfortable describing what we currently have as approximate.

On what structure is:

3. At the same time I have drifted toward the ontic reading associated with Ladyman and French: structure is not merely the boundary of what we can know but what the world consists in, and objects, if there are any, are derivative. I want to keep both this and the more cautious motivation behind (2), since they seem to me to point the same way.
4. My main evidence for (3) comes from quantum theory. For a system of identical particles, exchanging two of them changes the state at most by a sign, so nothing in the physics singles out this electron rather than that one. I read this as showing that particles are not individuals in any robust sense, and as independent support for the ontic thesis.
5. On the Ramsey-sentence triviality objection — that existentially quantifying over relations lets any domain of the right cardinality be fitted with some structure satisfying the theory, so structural knowledge would collapse into arithmetic — my preferred reply is to restrict the quantification to natural, or better, causal relations. I do not want to buy the objection off with a cardinality constraint, and I do not want to concede that any part of the Ramsey sentence is knowable a priori.

On argument and metaphysics:

6. The no-miracles argument remains the central positive case for realism, and it is an inference to the best explanation: the success of our theories would be inexplicable unless they were structurally true. I do not accept deflating it into a story about predictive reliability or about the pragmatics of theory choice.
7. Methodologically, questions of this kind should be settled case by case from the history of theory change, not by a priori argument. I distrust any position in the realism debate that no actual episode could confirm or disconfirm.
8. I am a nominalist about mathematics. I do not believe in abstract objects and I do not think structural realism needs them: to say that the world has such-and-such structure is to say something about concrete physical systems, not to add structures to one's ontology.

What I want is an audit of this set, not a defense of it. Go through the commitments and identify which pairs genuinely pull against each other and which only look as though they do — I would rather have the apparent conflicts dismissed explicitly and with reasons than have everything flagged as a problem. Order the genuine tensions by severity rather than presenting them as equally bad, and for each one say what the minimal revision is and what that revision costs, including which of the remaining commitments it leaves weakened or unsupported. I suspect several of these are in tension but I do not know where the trouble actually is, and I have deliberately not decided in advance which of them I would sacrifice, so please do not assume that any particular item is the expendable one. Finish by telling me which single change to the set would clear the most trouble at once, and whether that change is available to me given (7): a fix that requires me to give up the case-study methodology should count as a much heavier cost than one that does not, and I want that difference reflected in your ranking.
