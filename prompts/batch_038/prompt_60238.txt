Right — me and my mate Dan were at it until about one this morning and I've barely slept since, so I need someone neutral to tell me whether I'm talking rubbish.

The row was about A Christmas Carol. Not whether it's any good, obviously — the third spirit. Dan's line is that Dickens is philosophically incoherent: the Ghost of Christmas Yet to Come shows Scrooge a definite future, his own unwept death and the Cratchit household with Tiny Tim buried, and that only makes sense if the future is already settled. But if it's settled then the redemption plot is a cheat, because Scrooge can't do anything about it; and if he can do something about it, then the ghost never showed him the future at all, only one possibility among others, which means the spirit gave him no knowledge whatsoever, just a scare. His phrase, which he was extremely pleased with, was that the book either commits fatalism as a metaphysics or fraud as an epistemology.

My line is that Dan's missed the fact that Scrooge does the philosophy himself, at the graveside. He asks the spirit whether what it's showing him is what will happen or only what might happen, the spirit doesn't answer — just goes on pointing — and Scrooge then supplies his own account: that courses of conduct foreshadow ends which follow if you persevere in them but change if you depart from them. So the visions are conditional predictions rather than flat future-tense truths, and the ghost's silence isn't a hole in the argument, its the whole point: the answer is the one the character has to produce himself and stake everything on.

Which puts the book bang in the middle of the sea battle problem. Aristotle's worry about whether the claim that there will be a sea battle tomorrow is already true or already false; Diodorus and the Master Argument, which seems to shove you towards whatever will happen happening of necessity; the Ockhamist reply that turns on separating hard facts about the past from soft ones; Łukasiewicz wanting a third truth value for the future; branching time and the thin red line, where one branch is privileged without the others being mere possibilities. And there's a bit of the debate I want pressed hard, because I think it's the genuinely interesting bit and Dan never engaged with it: self-fulfilling prophecy versus self-defeating prophecy. The third spirit looks like the second kind — a warning whose entire value lies in the fact that believing it makes it false. A warning that works is a prediction that turned out wrong. So can Scrooge rationally believe what he's shown? Doesn't the story need the vision to be true in order for him to have any reason to act, and false in order for acting to be possible? Note also that the thing doing the showing isn't God but a spirit, so you can't just dissolve the whole business into providence and foreknowledge, though I'd want you to say whether that helps or makes it worse.

So what I'd like is a proper reading of the book through this lens, arguing the case rather than just surveying options. Specifically: whether Dan's dilemma actually bites or whether it's a false choice; where each of the three spirits sits, given that the past is presented as flatly unalterable and the future as anything but, and whether the book is implicitly running a different semantics for each tense; whether the ending — waking on Christmas morning, Tim alive — settles the question or just papers over it; and, most importantly, where my reading strains. I want the honest version, not a flattering one, so tell me plainly where I'm bolting a logic-chopping apparatus onto a sentimental ghost story, and where the book resists the reading and a fair critic has to admit it. Finish with what the novel actually adds to the future-contingents debate that the arguments alone don't give you — something about what it's like to be shown your own death, maybe, or about how a conditional prediction has to be taken up by the very person it's about in order to mean anything.
