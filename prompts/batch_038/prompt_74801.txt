I am a novelist, and my fourth book follows a woman in her late forties who undergoes a corpus callosotomy for epilepsy that has stopped responding to anything else. I spent two years preparing for it — the neuropsychology, a long correspondence with a consultant neurologist who read the manuscript twice and marked it up without mercy — and the novel is done. What is not done is an essay a literary quarterly has commissioned for a special issue on the divided mind: three thousand words on what the clinical material did to my conception of the sentence. My editor expects the usual furniture, and I have deliberately left it out: there is no appeal to Parfit's treatment of division in *Reasons and Persons*, no Nagel and his bat, no meditation on whether the patient is one person or two. The draft runs long and I have cut the connective tissue below, leaving the spine in five sections. I am not asking for encouragement. I am asking you to find where it breaks.

THE CONJUNCTION PROSTHESIS: NOTES ON WRITING A DIVIDED SUBJECT (condensed draft)

1. The clinical picture as I actually use it. Sectioning the corpus callosum is a containment strategy, not a cure: it stops seizures propagating, and it leaves the patient, in ordinary life, largely indistinguishable from anyone else. The strangeness surfaces under laboratory control. Present a word briefly to one visual field only and it reaches the hemisphere on the opposite side; the hand controlled by that hemisphere can retrieve the object, while the hemisphere that owns speech insists it saw nothing at all. Ask the speaking hemisphere to explain an action initiated on evidence it never received, and it does not say that it does not know. It supplies a reason, fluently and without embarrassment. Separately, and rarely, the two hands are observed working at cross purposes in the world — one fastening, one undoing. And the callosum is not the only bridge; other commissures and subcortical routes remain.

2. Two readings, one of which I want to refuse. The tempting reading is arithmetic: two streams, therefore two subjects, therefore a moral and legal puzzle about who is being treated. The reading I want is less dramatic and I think more strange. The discovery is not that a self can be halved but that a self was never simply present to be halved. A single first-person singular is a *binding convention*, an ongoing production, maintained in part by an organ whose remit includes the manufacture of explanations for conduct it has not witnessed. I have been calling this commissural narration: the continuous generation of one grammatical subject out of material that was never jointly available.

3. The literary claim, which is the reason the essay exists. Free indirect style has always been a way of letting a single sentence belong to two vantage points — the character's and an unnamed narrator's — without announcing the second. It is an integrative technology: it presumes that the two vantage points can share a subject, because in the ordinary case they do. My patient's chapters run the device in reverse, under a formal rule I imposed on myself and never broke: in those chapters no sentence may use a coordinating conjunction to join two first-person clauses. When two intentions must appear, they appear as two sentences, with no syntactic bond between them. The conjunction was doing the work the callosum used to do. I have removed it, which means the reader has to supply the join, and supplies it silently, on every page, without being told that anything is missing.

4. The objection I expect and my defence. The objection: this is the familiar narrativist thesis about selfhood wearing a lab coat; the interpreter is a hypothesis about how explanations get generated, not a theory of personhood; and I am sliding from a claim about a cognitive module to a claim about what a subject is, which is precisely the slide the clinical material is supposed to warn against. My defence is narrower than it looks. I do not assert that the patient has two selves, or none, or that personhood has been distributed. I assert that the grammatical singular is doing unearned work in the case report and in the novel alike, and that the sectioned brain is the place where that work becomes audible rather than assumed.

5. Two things in the full draft that I do not trust. First, I claim in passing that later experiments have disclosed rather more comparison across the hemispheres than the classic studies implied; I cannot support this from anything I could cite to a specialist, and it is doing rhetorical work for me by softening section 1. Second, I write that the two hands working at cross purposes is evidence of two wills rather than two motor plans, and I suspect this sentence commits exactly the offence section 4 defends me against.

Three things, in this order. First, accuracy: go through section 1 and section 2 and mark every claim about the neuropsychology or about the philosophy of personal identity that is wrong, out of date, or compressed to the point of being false — tell me what the corrected formulation would be in your own words, and do not send me a bibliography or a reading list, since I have one already and the substance is what I lack. Second, the load-bearing test: for each of my inventions — the phrase commissural narration, the prohibition on conjoining first-person clauses, the claim that the reader becomes the commissure — say whether it genuinely depends on the clinical facts or whether it would survive unchanged if my character had a brain that was never sectioned at all; I need to know which of these are structure and which are jewellery. Third, the objection: state the strongest objection a philosophically literate neurologist would put to section 2, and then say whether section 4's defence actually meets it or merely restates the thesis in a quieter voice. After that, name the single sentence in the whole draft most likely to cost me the confidence of a specialist reader, and give me one concrete rewritten version of it. Close with a final line beginning Verdict: followed by exactly one of SOUND, SALVAGEABLE, or DECORATIVE, where DECORATIVE means you judge that the essay's central analogy would survive the deletion of the patient.
