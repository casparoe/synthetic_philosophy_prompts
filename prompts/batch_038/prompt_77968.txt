I'm teaching a twelve-person upper-level seminar on intention and practical rationality — philosophy majors, mostly third-years, eighty minutes a week in a small room with movable chairs. We just finished the unit where they read Bratman on plans and agglomerativity, McClennen on resolute choice, and Gauthier's constrained maximizer, and we spent one session on Kavka's toxin puzzle. The discussion was competent and completely flat. Everyone agreed that you can't intend to do what you're sure you won't do, nobody felt the slightest pull toward intending, and my TA came away thinking its a verbal trick rather than a puzzle. I want to give them a session where they get caught in it instead of admiring it from the outside, and I want a real runnable thing, not a lecture with a thought experiment projected on the wall.

Here's what I've been kicking around — develop it, improve it, or throw it out if you have something better. I want the class to face a toxin-structured offer live in the room. The "toxin" should be something genuinely unpleasant but harmless and legal: a shot of bitter vegetable juice, a spoon of something vile from the international aisle, that kind of thing, with an opt-out that doesn't socially mark anyone. The reward can't be cash (the department won't fund it and I don't want to be buying participation), so we're talking a chocolate bar, a coffee voucher, maybe two bonus points. Three problems I can see but can't solve, and I want your judgement on each rather than a menu. First, the reward is trivial next to the act, so the offer may simply not tempt anyone — tell me how to tune the ratio, or whether the currency needs to be something else entirely, like a class-collective good. Second, there's no brain scanner and they know it: self-report on a sealed card makes lying free, and letting peers predict each other turns it into a bluffing game about classmates rather than about the offer. Pick a mechanism and defend it, and tell me honestly whether any classroom device can reproduce the predictor reliability that does all the work in Kavka's setup, or whether that feature has to be supplied by fiat from me — and if so, the exact words, said at the exact moment, that keep the spell from breaking. (For what it's worth, I ran a Newcomb-style box game in this seminar two years ago and it collapsed because two students spent the whole time meta-gaming me as the predictor.) Third, they've already read the puzzle, which cuts both ways: half of them will try to game whatever mechanism I set up, and the sincere ones will be self-consciously performing an intention, which may make intending impossible for a brand new reason. Relatedly, I'm worried the live version imports reasons Kavka's setup deliberately strips out — they're in a room with people whose opinion matters, and I'm the one handing out the reward, so keeping your word and not looking like a liar become genuine reasons to drink, which arguably converts the toxin case into ordinary promise-keeping and dissolves the puzzle rather than staging it. Say whether that's fatal or fixable. I'd also like a precommitment round where anything in the room counts as a self-binding device — public announcement, escrow with a classmate, a written contract, handing over a phone — so they find out whether the puzzle got solved or just replaced; and I need you to make the call on timing, whether the drinking happens ten minutes later in the same session or at the start of next week's, since those seem to test different things and I can't do both.

So: a minute-by-minute run sheet, materials, roles, the verbatim wording of the offer as I should read it aloud (this matters more than anything else — a sloppy offer and half the room reasons about a different puzzle), a debrief that maps specific moments in the game onto the gap between the rationality of intending and the rationality of the act, and a blunt list of what predictably goes wrong the first time I run it plus what I do in the moment. Then be frank about which feature of the case the activity simply cannot reproduce and what I have to supply verbally afterward. And please push back where it's warranted: if you think my framing is off, that the puzzle resists being staged, or that the honest version of this session teaches them something closer to a lesson in self-deception and reputation than in the rationality of intention, tell me that at the top instead of building me a polished game that quietly misses the point. I'd much rather be told it can't be done well than handed something that only looks like it works.
