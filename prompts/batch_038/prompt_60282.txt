ok so i'm teaching a seminar on social choice and i want a handout that stages Sen's liberal paradox as a live argument rather than a puzzle that gets solved on slide four. write me a dialogue between two characters who are both sharp and neither of whom gets to win cheaply:

- one thinks the impossibility is a real, deep result: once you build liberty into the social choice function as protected pairs — a personal sphere over which an individual's own ranking between any two alternatives in that sphere determines the social ranking — and you also want the social ordering to be Pareto-responsive and complete/transitive over all logically possible preference profiles, minimal liberalism genuinely cannot survive. on this view the lesson is that the liberal ideal has to give something up, and the interesting question is what.
- the other thinks the paradox is an artifact of the wrong machinery: rights aren't constraints on how a social ordering is computed from preference profiles, they're rules about what people are permitted to do, and the moment you formulate liberty as a game form (who may choose what, given everyone else's choices) instead of as a restriction on a preference-aggregation procedure, the contradiction evaporates.

they should actually engage each other's moves, not give parallel lectures. specifically i want the dialogue to hit these beats:

- set up the prude and the lewd reader explicitly, with the full preference profile and the two protected pairs, and show where transitivity or Pareto bites. no hand-waving.
- the Pareto-respecting side should ask the pointed question: isn't the prude's preference about what the lewd reader reads a *meddling* preference, and doesn't the paradox secretly depend on allowing interpersonal nosiness into the domain? the other should answer whether restricting the domain that way is a principled fix or just rigging the profile set so the theorem can't fire.
- somebody should raise the waiving-the-right response (the lewd reader could choose to let the prude decide) and whether that's a real escape or a re-description of the problem — and note that in the classic profile both of them waive.
- the game-form advocate should press on whether protected pairs even *are* what liberals mean by a right, and get a substantive answer rather than a shrug — e.g. that a game form with no evaluation of outcomes can't tell you whether an exercise of liberty is good, only that it was allowed.
- end unresolved, with each of them naming the assumption they'd most want to attack next.
