I'm the TA for a grad seminar on authenticity in art, and I've gotten sick of how every treatment of forgery I've read collapses into "Goodman says one thing, Dutton says another, next topic." My advisor thinks the whole debate is stale; I think it just hasn't been laid out properly. So I want you to build me a classification instrument — a decision tree or a short quiz with a scoring key, whichever ends up sharper — that sorts people by what they'd actually say about cases, not by which name they'd drop. Starting point: you've been looking at and loving a painting for years, and it's just been conclusively established as a forgery, one you can't tell from the original even hanging next to it. Endpoint: a named position on whether your admiration was misplaced the whole time. Each branch node should be a single claim that really does split the space — whether a work's identity includes its history of production, whether aesthetic value supervenes on what you can experience of the object, whether appreciating art is partly recognizing an achievement rather than just registering properties, whether "indistinguishable now" licenses "indistinguishable forever," whether the damage lands on the viewer, the imitated artist, the market, or the historical record, and whether your verdict is supposed to carry over unchanged to music, fiction, and (weirdly) to software. Phrase every node as a question someone could answer without already knowing which leaf it leads to.

The leaves I need present, at minimum: the look-is-all-there-is view; the Goodman-flavored one where the forgery is a training device and present indistinguishability is a contingent, self-correcting state of affairs; Dutton's split between artistic and aesthetic value, on which the forgery is a fine picture and a failed performance; the achievement view that credits effort and risk-taking; a position that keeps your aesthetic response intact but calls the thing a fraud on someone other than you; the historicist view where a work is something like an indicated structure and forging is a category confusion; and the deflationary option that "the painting" was never the sort of thing that could be forged at all. Then mark any cell of the space that's coherent but empty, label it unnamed, and give me one sentence on whether nobody's there because it's incoherent, unattractive, or just unexplored. And flag honestly which pairs of leaves your instrument can't actually separate — I suspect the achievement view and the historicist view are going to come out the same on paper, and I want to know if that's a defect in the tree or a real convergence in the debate. No bibliography, please, I know where to look, I just want the instrument.

Seed it with three cases I want threaded through the questions. First: a forger — call her Vess, purely invented — who serves her time, comes out, and paints under her own signature; the new work is honestly worse, but the collectors who bought the forgeries keep them on the wall and re-hang them as "anonymous, after [whoever]," and the prices don't drop. Does your tree route that to the same leaf as a straight con, and if not, what node did I miss? Second: a system that produces canvases in the manner of a living painter, with her knowledge and blessing, and she signs the back of the stretcher herself — forgery, collaboration, or a third kind of thing, and does the provenance node fire differently when the original author consents? Third, the reverse: an authenticated old master that everyone agrees is a dud, sitting next to a forgery of the same subject that everyone agrees is superb — can your tree preserve the intuition that the first is a bad something and the second a good non-something, or does it have to give that intuition up? Aim for roughly ten questions or nine leaves, one page-ish, and keep the questions concrete: no question should be anything like "do you think history matters to art?"
