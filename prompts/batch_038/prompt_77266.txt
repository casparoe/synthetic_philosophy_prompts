I build a root-cause tool for a distributed system — when something falls over, it's supposed to tell you which deploy or config change actually did it — and at 2am I argued myself into a wall. No philosophy background here, so treat me like a smart person who's never seen the literature. The whole tool rests on:

(1) X caused E just in case E wouldn't have happened if X hadn't. Difference-maker test, nothing more.

(2) Nothing in (1) says the difference-maker has to be something that happened. We cite omissions constantly in postmortems: the on-call didn't ack the page, there was no health check on that endpoint, nobody rolled back. If omissions are allowed there, they're allowed generally.

(3) But once they're allowed generally the test explodes. Take our last outage. The on-call didn't restart the service — fine, that one feels right. But also: nobody else in the company restarted it, nobody else on the planet restarted it, the vendor never shipped a client with retries, and our founders picked a language ten years ago that made the eventual rewrite impossible. Every one of those supports a true if-X-had-happened, E-wouldn't-have claim about that outage.

(4) So under (1) all of them are causes, and it generalizes: for any event E and any X that failed to prevent E, the absence of X caused E.

(5) A theory on which everything caused the outage can't tell me who to page or what to fix. It's not a theory of causation, it's a way of saying nothing. So (1) is false.

Here's the part that makes me suspicious of my own escape hatch. My patch was: only admit counterfactuals whose antecedent is a change you could actually make locally to the system, holding everything upstream and independent fixed — flip this one node's value, watch E. That kills the founders case, since you can't hold the rest of the world fixed while changing a decade-old decision. But now whether the vendor's client caused the outage depends on which variables I put in my model. Add a variable for client retry behavior and it's a cause; leave that variable out and it isn't. That seems to prove too much in the other direction: the process crashed the way it crashed long before I drew any graph, and I don't want to end up saying my modeling choices made it true or false that a buffer overflow caused it.

(6) So the naive test collapses and my patch collapses, and maybe causation isn't a real relation at all, just a story we tell afterwards.

Which step do I actually reject, and what does rejecting it cost? I'd rather keep (1) than (5) — the difference-maker idea is the only thing that makes the tool mean anything, and I don't want to throw it out. But I'm not attached to (2) either. If omissions are the problem, I need the line, becuase every version I come up with either lets the founders back in or kicks the on-call out, and the on-call one I'd bet my job on. And if you think the tool can still be built on whatever survives, tell me what the acceptance test for a candidate cause should be.
