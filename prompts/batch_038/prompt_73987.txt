I sit on the awards panel of an interdisciplinary institute's programme on brain-inspired computing, and we have a single seed award of roughly £1.1 million to allocate this round. I was added to the panel late, after a recusal, so I have less history with the applicants than my colleagues, and the programme officer has asked me to circulate a written opinion before we meet on Thursday. The proposal in question is a three-year engineering programme to build a low-energy inference accelerator whose arithmetic units operate at deliberately coarse bit-precision — the group proposes a datapath no wider than four bits — with the resulting loss of reliability made up by running many parallel redundant channels and combining their outputs statistically. Its case rests partly on measured energy-per-inference figures for a named workload family and partly, unusually in our experience, on an explicit intellectual warrant drawn from von Neumann's unfinished late book The Computer and the Brain, which was published posthumously in 1958 having been intended as the basis for a lecture series he did not live to deliver: the claim that the nervous system gets by with far less precision per component than the machines of his day, yet achieves greater reliability per unit of energy through parallelism and redundancy, so that trading precision for redundancy is a genuine engineering principle rather than a biological accident.

The panel is split four ways. Two of our engineers hold that the warrant is load-bearing: if the precision-for-parallelism trade is a real principle, the architecture is worth funding even if the first prototype misses its targets, because the design space has been systematically under-explored; if it is not a real principle, then the proposal amounts to a bet on four-bit arithmetic with extra steps, and the redundancy and error-correction overhead will swallow whatever the coarse datapath saves. A third thinks the historical framing is decorative — proposal writing as intellectual packaging — and that we should simply judge the benchmarks, the milestone structure and the cost. A fourth, a neuroscientist, thinks the framing is not decorative but mistaken in a specific way: von Neumann himself flags that the nervous system's operation is mixed digital and analogue rather than cleanly digital, that much of its signalling may be chemical rather than impulse-based, that we have no settled account of where or in what form its memory sits, and above all that its operating code, if it has one, may have a logical structure unlike any arithmetic or logic we already possess — which on her reading is a caution against transferring design principles across the analogy at all, not a licence for it. She also objects to a sub-project in which the group would validate their chip's error-tolerance curve against a simplified spiking-network model, arguing that a toy network is not the nervous system and that the comparison can be tuned to return whatever answer the applicants want.

Please draft the memo I will circulate: a recommendation — fund, reject, or fund with conditions — with a timetable attached to it, and reasons the applicants could actually respond to rather than a summary of the panel's mood. I want it to state plainly whether the von Neumann material is load-bearing or decorative in this particular case, and, if load-bearing, to identify which specific claims the architecture depends on and what would have to be true of them for the design rationale to hold; to adjudicate as far as it can between the engineer's and the neuroscientist's readings of the same text, rather than treating the citation either as neutral ornament or as a fatal flaw; to say what conditions, if any, would make the proposal fundable, since I am inclined towards something like a requirement that the applicants list the design choices they would have made differently had the historical warrant turned out to be false; to give a fair and non-dismissive treatment of the strongest argument against whatever you recommend; and to set out a criterion the panel could apply next round to proposals that lean on warrant from the history of science, as we expect several will. Write it in British English, and work from the case as I have described it — I do not want a bibliography or a reading list, and I would much rather you reasoned about the substance than gestured at sources.
