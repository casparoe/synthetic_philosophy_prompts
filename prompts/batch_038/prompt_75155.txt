Compose a short piece of humour — scripted dialogue or set-up and punchline — about the 1979 critique of adaptationism that Stephen Jay Gould and Richard Lewontin built around the mosaicked spaces beneath the dome of the Basilica di San Marco in Venice. I want something that is genuinely funny to an audience of evolutionary biologists rather than merely recognisable to them, so the constraints below matter.

The joke must turn on the substance of the argument rather than on its vocabulary. The punchline should exploit at least one of the following asymmetries, and it should be evident which:

- The paper's target is a style of reasoning, not the fact of adaptation: the practice of proposing a plausible selective story for a trait, testing it only for internal consistency, and never asking what the trait would look like if it were instead a byproduct of the organism's overall geometry, a developmental or allometric constraint, a correlated effect of selection on some other feature, a piece of phylogenetic inheritance, or drift.
- The architectural analogy itself. A dome carried on four arches leaves four tapering triangular spaces as a matter of geometric necessity; their number and their shape follow from decisions taken about the arches and the drum, and the evangelists painted into them were fitted to the leftover surface afterwards. The comedy available here is that the ornament looks designed for the space while the space was never designed for the ornament — and that reasoning backwards from the picture to a purpose is exactly the error Voltaire put in the mouth of Dr Pangloss in Candide.
- The subsequent history of the example. Critics pointed out that the San Marco spaces are more properly pendentives, that a builder might have chosen squinches or corbelling instead, and that the choice of device was therefore itself a design decision — with the consequence that the flagship illustration of a non-adaptive byproduct may have been selected for its effects. Gould's rejoinder was that the origin of a feature and its later co-opted usefulness are separate questions, a distinction he subsequently sharpened by introducing, with Elisabeth Vrba, the term exaptation.

I am most interested in a joke built on the third item, since the reversal there — Gould and Lewontin held to the methodological standard they set for everyone else, and required to exclude alternative architectural hypotheses before asserting necessity — is internal irony rather than a cheap shot.

Form and register:

- Roughly 120 to 180 words. A brief exchange between two or three speakers is welcome, as is a monologue in the voice of a conference talk, a referee report, or a guided tour of the basilica.
- The joke must survive the substitution test: if the evolutionary-biological content were swapped for the corresponding terms from economics or linguistics, it should collapse.
- Do not rest the whole thing on a pun involving the word spandrel, and do not write a knock-knock joke.
- No character may be a strawman who is simply too obtuse to follow the argument; the strongest version lets the adaptationist land a real point.
- Assume the reader already knows what a spandrel, a pendentive, and a just-so story are, and do not stop to define them.

After the joke, add one or two sentences identifying which strand of the argument the punchline depends upon, so that I can confirm the humour is doing the work I intend. No bibliography or list of further reading is required, and please do not attribute any wording to Gould, Lewontin, Voltaire, or their critics unless you are confident of the exact phrasing.
