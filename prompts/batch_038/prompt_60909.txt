I teach a graduate seminar in the philosophy of history, and this term we are working through Braudel's account of the three time scales in The Mediterranean and the Mediterranean World in the Age of Philip II: the nearly immobile history of a region's relationship to its sea, mountains, and climate; the slower collective rhythms of economies, societies, and states that he associates with conjunctures; and the history of events and of great individuals, which he treats as the least consequential of the three, comparing it to foam carried along on the surface of a moving tide. My own route into philosophy was through statistics rather than through the humanities: I completed a master's degree in econometrics and spent two years at a central bank doing nowcasting and business-cycle dating before I ever took a philosophy course, so the vocabulary I actually think in is unobserved components models, Kalman filtering, local linear trends, band-pass filters, spectral density, unit-root and stationarity tests, structural-break estimation, and variance decomposition. I have been telling my students that this background is a handicap rather than an asset when reading Braudel, and I would like to test whether that is true.

So here is what I am asking for. Take Braudel's strong claim — not the trivial one that slow processes deserve attention, but the strong one, that the slow carries the reality, that the event is the least real and the least explanatory of the three levels, and that the historian's choice of a time scale is therefore a philosophical commitment rather than a stylistic preference — and restate it as faithfully as you can entirely in my vocabulary: as a claim about the frequency content of a long historical series, about the persistence of shocks, about signal-to-noise ratios, about how much of the variance of the series is attributable to which component. Please resist translating by loose paraphrase, and resist importing Brauldian assumptions under econometric names. I want to see what the doctrine is committed to once it is forced to say something a statistician could check.

To keep this concrete, since I suspect the difficulty lies in the details rather than in the generalities, let me describe a small exercise I ran last summer with a research assistant. We assembled annual series of wheat prices and of nominal wages for Castile and for England over roughly 1500 to 1650, taken from published compilations, together with a mortality series and a crude proxy for shipping volume. We then fit an unobserved components model whose three components were chosen deliberately to mirror Braudel's three scales: a local linear trend with a very small slope variance standing in for the longue durée, a cyclical component restricted to a band of roughly eight to twenty-five years standing in for the conjuncture, and an autoregressive irregular term standing in for the event. Three results unsettled me. First, on the baseline specification the variance shares come out close to equal — the irregular component accounts for about as much of the movement as the trend does — which, read naively, looks like the data contradicting Braudel's hierarchy, the dust carrying as much weight as the tide. Second, when we allow the trend to take a handful of large discrete slope shifts, estimated rather than imposed, with breaks clustering in the 1580s and again in the epidemic years, the trend absorbs nearly everything and the cyclical component becomes statistically indistinguishable from zero; the structure looks all-explaining only once we let it be punctuated by events, which seems to invert Braudel rather than vindicate him. Third, and most worryingly, the eight-to-twenty-five-year band was chosen by us and not identified by the data: the spectral density across that range is close to flat, and the variance shares move substantially when we change the bandwidth or substitute a Hodrick–Prescott trend for the local linear one. There is also an artifact I should mention, since it may matter: one of the wage series has a persistent level shift at exactly the point where the source changes its accounting unit, and that shift loads onto the trend component as though it were a structural transformation.

Then I want the residue report, which is the part I am really after. Which of Braudel's claims come through intact — is the anti-event thesis perhaps a claim about where the power of a series is concentrated, or about the low signal-to-noise ratio of narrative history, or about the irreversibility of shocks rather than their magnitude? Which claims survive only with distortion, in particular the idea that geography constrains rather than merely changes slowly, and the idea that a component is structural in some sense stronger than persistent? And which claims cannot be stated at all in a language of components, filters, and variance shares — for instance, that one time scale is more real than another, or that choosing among them is a matter of commitment rather than of convenience? Give me a verdict on that residue, and give it to me as an argument rather than as a survey: is the residue a genuine defect in my vocabulary, which is descriptive, observer-indexed, and silent on explanatory priority, so that a variance decomposition simply cannot be made to answer Braudel's question; or does the failure show that his hierarchy was never a claim about the world at all, but a rhetorical emphasis wearing metaphysical clothes, which is why it disappears as soon as it is asked to be operational? My colleague in the history department holds the second view and I incline toward the first, and she has objected that my exercise begs the question against Braudel from the start, since specifying three components with different frequency restrictions already assumes that the levels are separable. Please address that objection directly, and tell me either what would settle the disagreement between us or why it cannot be settled. Write the whole thing in American English and in continuous prose only, with no headings, no bullet points, no boldface, and no tables — the way one would write a long letter to a colleague.
