My AP econ teacher has this line he uses to end arguments: "if your preferences can be turned into a money pump, they're irrational." Today he aimed it at me. I asked whether someone could have a cycle — A over B, B over C, C over A — and be totally fine as long as nobody ever actually walks them through the sequence of trades that drains them. He said no, the cycle is the mistake whether or not anyone exploits it, and then he moved on to the next slide.

I don't think that's obviously right but I couldnt say why in the moment. Can you split that one sentence into the different precise things it could be asserting? It feels like it slides around depending on stuff like: who's doing the pumping (an all-knowing bookie who's read my preference ordering vs. just the world happening to hand me trades one at a time), whether what leaks out is money or utility, whether I'm allowed to see the whole sequence coming and refuse the first trade, whether the pump has to actually happen or only be possible, and whether being pumpable is meant as a symptom of a defective preference set or as the defintion of defectiveness. Give me the versions numbered, and for each say whether it's something nobody could deny, whether it's a real substantive claim that might come out true, or whether it's just false — plus which one people who repeat the line probably have in mind, and specifically which one my teacher needed to answer my question the way he did.

Two things my friend who's deep into this stuff said that I can't evaluate on my own. One: if you can foresee the sequence you just refuse the first trade and never get pumped at all, but doing that might require being a planning agent who sticks to a plan rather than someone who's merely better informed, and it's not clear that's free. Two: a fully transitive person can get drained too — when their preferences shift over time, or when they pay for information — so exploitability might not be tracking irrationality at all. Are those real considerations, do they actually rescue intransitive preferences, and do they knock out any of the versions from your list? Don't just give me the standard "cycles are irrational because money pumps" story — I want to know which parts of that argument are holding up the roof and which ones are decoration.
