So I'm writing the final paper for a pragmatics seminar and, purely by bad luck, I'm also rereading The Remains of the Day on a week off, and the two have fused in my head to the point where I can't stop seeing Stevens as a Gricean test case. The guy never violates a maxim. He answers precisely what's asked, he's scrupulous about being informative and truthful, he apologises for going on. And yet the entire catastrophe of his life gets communicated anyway, entirely in the gaps. Think of the evening Miss Kenton comes to tell him about a marriage proposal she's received — he's busy with the important guests and brushes her off, and the conversation stays perfectly on the surface, and somehow the reader is left with the fact that he has just lost her. Or the two Jewish housemaids: he insists his employer was certainly never antisemitic and then, in the same breath, concedes there was a brief and entirely insignificant period when the girls were dismissed for being Jewish, apparently without noticing that he's just handed over the evidence against his own claim. Or his father dying upstairs during the big conference at the Hall while he goes on serving. Or the last pages at Weymouth, the stranger on the seafront, and his resolution to start practising bantering for his new American employer, which reads to me like a man who has understood everything and is about to do nothing with it.

The thing my paper wants to turn on, and the thing I can't get to work, is this. Grice makes speaker meaning depend on a pretty specific reflexive intention — the utterer has to intend the hearer to recognise that intention, and the effect is supposed to come about by means of that recognition. Stevens has nothing of the sort. He isn't lying, he isn't being coy, he isn't even doing the polite British thing where you both know you know; he is self-deceived, and the recognition of what he's implying is precisely what he can't survive. And yet I recover the implicatures reliably and stably — I could tell you what he means by congratulating Miss Kenton and I'd bet you'd agree, and there are readings where two people get different ones and we can argue about which is better. So either the intention clause is doing no real work in this kind of case, or the intention belongs to somebody else (Ishiguro, presumably, or some implied-author figure), or there's a third option I haven't thought of. My seminar partner's answer is that it's a non-problem, because utterances by fictional characters aren't genuine speech acts at all and whatever happens here is a parasitic pretend-version of real implicature — which strikes me as a cop-out that dissolves the interesting question instead of answering it, but I can't quite show that.

I also want you to be honest about where the Gricean lens flattens the novel — the places where reading Stevens as a maxim-follower makes him look like a mere technical puzzle and loses what makes the book devastating, and the places where the reading just doesn't fit and a good critic has to say so.
