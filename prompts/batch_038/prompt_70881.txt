I work on statutory drafting, and I have become uneasy about a rule I rely on every week and about what it seems to assume.

The practice. Across the common-law world a rule of construction tells interpreters that, unless the context so indicates, words importing the masculine gender include the feminine. The mid-nineteenth-century British statute aimed at shortening the language of Acts of Parliament said as much, and United States federal law still says something close to it in 1 U.S.C. § 1. For most of the period in which that rule was in force it ran in one direction only, and drafting manuals treated it as a licence: the drafter was to write *he*, on the ground that a single masculine pronoun already covered everyone and that *he or she* would be redundant or clumsy. The modern British provision, section 6 of the Interpretation Act 1978, was made symmetrical — the masculine includes the feminine and the feminine the masculine — and generic *he* was retired in favour of gender-neutral drafting.

The same tradition, meanwhile, held that the neutral word did not reach women. In *Bebb v The Law Society* (1913–14) the Court of Appeal refused a declaration that a woman was a "person" within the meaning of the Solicitors Act 1843, reasoning in part from long and uninterrupted usage; Parliament had to intervene by statute in 1919. In Canada the Supreme Court held in 1928 that women were not "qualified persons" for appointment to the Senate under section 24 of the British North America Act 1867, and only the Privy Council's reversal in *Edwards v Canada (AG)* (1929) undid it. So the marked term was widened by rule to cover women while the unmarked term was narrowed by usage to exclude them.

Hofstadter's satirical essay "A Person Paper on Purity in Language," in the 1981 anthology *The Mind's I*, is usually read as a comment on all this: it presents an imaginary scholarly paper whose argumentative structure is that of the polite defence of generic *he*, with racial terminology substituted for sexual, so that a reader who found the original inoffensive flinches at the copy.

Please treat the drafting rule and the case law as a single practice and tell me what it presupposes. Specifically:

1. Reconstruct the implicit theory of meaning. Does the rule suppose that a gendered term's contribution is a range a sovereign stipulation can simply widen, with nothing left over, or that the term carries something — a picture, a default case, a sense of who is being described — that survives the stipulation? What has to be true about the relation between authoritative stipulation and readers' uptake for the rule to be more than an assurance on paper?

2. Which rival accounts does it rule out: meaning as fixed by communal use rather than by decree, generic use as a matter of implicature rather than semantics, or any view on which a term's cognitive residue is part of what it says?

3. Can one theory accommodate both limbs — the widening of *he* and the narrowing of *person*? Or do they presuppose incompatible models of what an ordinary word carries? Consider whether "unless the context indicates otherwise" is doing the real work in both cases, and if so, what kind of context: text, the state of the common law, legislators' expectations, or who is imagined when the case is read.

4. Separate what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits well with. Is it compatible with a wholly instrumental explanation — economy of drafting and printing, no semantic thesis at all — and where exactly is it philosophically underdetermined?

5. Say what Hofstadter's substitution test does and does not establish. Does the asymmetry of reaction refute the semantic story, or only a claim about sensibilities? And answer the obvious objection: that the racial analogue differs because of the history of those terms rather than because of how generic language works.

Finally, tell me what would have to change on an uptake-sensitive account — whether symmetry cured the problem or merely re-encoded it, what a court would need to be able to find out about actual readers, and whether gender-neutral drafting settles the question or simply relocates the default.
