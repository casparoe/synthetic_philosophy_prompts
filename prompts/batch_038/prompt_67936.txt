So I'm about four months into a novel whose whole plot hangs on a teleportation-style continuity company — call it Meridian — and two of my beta readers have been fighting about the underlying philosophy for the better part of a year, to the point where they've stopped answering each other and just do parallel monologues at me. One is a straight-up Parfitian: he thinks identity over time is nothing over and above psychological connectedness and continuity with the right kind of cause, that there's no further fact about which of two duplicates is really me, and that what matters in survival is that psychological relation (he calls it Relation R) rather than identity — identity, he says, is the wrong question and its the reason the whole business feels paradoxical. The other is not a soul person, which I think is what he assumes she is: she's a physicalist who holds that the identity facts are brute, that you could fix every physical and psychological fact in the universe and it would still be a further question whether this one is me, and that a view on which nothing answers that question can't explain why the original's terror in the scanning room is rational. Both are smart, neither is a strawman, and I honestly can't write characters who disagree convincingly until I know what they'd actually agree on.

So: draft the joint statement their adversarial collaboration should have produced. I want a numbered list of claims both of them can sign — including about the standard cases themselves (destructive scan and rebuild out on the other planet; the read-only upgrade where the Earth body walks out unharmed and a duplicate gets made anyway; the branching case where two copies wake up simultaneously in different cities) — and it has to be non-vacuous, meaning claims some third position would actually reject, not just "we both find the puzzle hard." Then a disagreements table, one row per live dispute, with columns for: the dispute in neutral terms, how the Parfitian would state his side so he'd sign it, how she'd state hers so she'd sign it, what each thinks the other is missing, and whether that row is substantive or is really about the word survive. Then the cruxes — the sub-questions where an answer would genuinely move at least one of them — and for each crux a concrete case, argument, or piece of evidence both would accept as bearing on it, with each side's prediction written down in advance so it can't be reinterpreted after the fact.

Two extra things I need for the book. First, I've given Meridian a contractual wrinkle neither of them have been asked about: a stored scan that never gets instantiated, sitting in cold archive, which the estate can license or delete — plus a sole-instance clause that charges a premium for a guarantee that only one copy is ever awake. Have each camp say what their own view predicts about behavior in the signing room: does the brute-fact person pay the premium, does the Parfitian have any reason to care about the archive, would either of them sign a contract allowing a dormant pattern to be woken decades after they're dead? Second, be honest about which parts of the dispute are merely verbal. If some of it turns out to be about what survive means rather than about the world, say which part and why, because that changes how I write them — and keep the language close to how people actually talk, no jargon I'd have to footnote, without sanding the disagreement down to nothing.
