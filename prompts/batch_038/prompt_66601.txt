I am preparing a session for a graduate philosophy-of-medicine seminar on naturalism and normativism about health and disease, and I need a pair of constructed clinical cases built to order. Please follow the specifications below exactly.

The two positions in play, stated here so that nothing depends on recalling the literature:

**(A) Biostatistical naturalism.** Disease is an internal state that depresses some part-function below the species-typical level for the bearer's reference class, indexed at minimum to age and sex. Biological functions are fixed by the design of the species — roughly, contributions to survival and reproduction — and are read off physiology, not off what anyone happens to value. Values enter medicine downstream, in deciding which pathological conditions to treat; they never enter into deciding whether a condition is pathological in the first place.

**(B) Goal-relative normativism.** Health is the ability, in standard or reasonable circumstances, to realize one's vital goals — the set of goals whose achievement is necessary and jointly sufficient for the person's minimal happiness. Abilities are counted at second order: someone who could, through ordinary training or ordinary assistance, acquire the capacity to pursue a goal counts as already having the ability to pursue it. Whether a person is ill therefore turns partly on that person's own goal-set and partly on their circumstances, and cannot be read off a population distribution.

**The two cases.**

*Case 1:* (A) must classify the condition as a disease, and (B) must classify the bearer as healthy and not ill. The divergence must come from the bearer's own vital goals — not from social stigma, discrimination, or anyone else's evaluation of the condition.

*Case 2:* (A) must classify the condition as no disease at all, because the relevant part-functioning is statistically typical for the reference class, while (B) must classify the bearer as ill, because the ability to realize their vital goals in standard circumstances is substantially reduced and cannot be recovered at second order through training or ordinary assistance.

*Matching constraint:* the two cases should concern the same organ system or the same family of physiological trait, the same reference class, and the same kind of clinical encounter — ideally a routine outpatient visit — so that what varies between them is essentially one thing: how the trait bears on the bearer's vital goals. If you find that you must vary something else in order to get both verdicts, state plainly what you varied and why it could not be held fixed.

*Further constraints:* contemporary and realistic clinical setting; no genetic engineering, no implants, no future medicine, no exotic pathogens. One paragraph each, roughly 150 words. Do not use the stock examples: left-handedness, deafness and Deaf culture, autism, short stature or dwarfism, homosexuality, menopause, baldness, or ADHD-as-social-construct. Either invent a plausible trait or use a real one well off the beaten track. If you invent the physiology, specify enough of it — the function at issue, the reference class, where the individual falls in the distribution, what the trait does and does not prevent — that a reader can check both verdicts from your paragraph alone, without outside facts. And no stipulated intuitions: do not assert anywhere that a condition is or is not "really" a disease. The verdicts must fall out of (A) and (B) as stated above.

**After the two cases, provide the following, in numbered sections.**

1. *Derivation.* For each case, walk through (A) step by step — function at issue, reference class, the individual's standing relative to it, verdict — and then through (B) — the vital goals, whether the circumstances count as standard, whether the ability survives at second order, verdict.

2. *Diagnosis.* Identify the single feature of each case that is doing the work. Then say what happens if that feature is removed: does the case collapse into agreement between the theories, and if so, which theory's verdict changed?

3. *Defense.* Give the best available reply for the side that comes out badly in each case. For (A) in particular, consider whether the distinction between the value-free notion of disease and the evaluative notion of illness lets the naturalist concede everything the normativist says about Case 1 while resisting Case 2 — and say whether that reply is a genuine defense or merely a narrowing of the target.

4. *Double edge.* Say whether each case also creates trouble for the theory it favors: whether Case 1 exposes (B)'s inability to criticize an idiosyncratic goal-set, and whether Case 2 can be deflected on the ground that (A) is a theory of disease and not a theory of everything medicine ought to care about. State whether that deflection succeeds, and what it costs (A) if it does.

5. *Robustness.* Say briefly what happens to the pair if (B) is replaced by a hybrid account on which a condition is a disorder only if it involves both a failure of internally designed function and a harm or disadvantage judged by social values. Which of the two cases still separates the views, and which no longer does any work?

Aim for 900–1200 words in total, in polished prose. No bibliography, reference list, or citations are needed, since both positions are defined above and everything the cases require should be self-contained in your own paragraphs.
