Our office book club does one big-ideas book a quarter and this quarter somebody picked Consciousness Explained. We're maybe a quarter of the way in, and last week we did the part about the Cartesian theater and the multiple drafts thing. I write backend code for a living and I've never taken a philosophy class, so I mostly sat there and listened. Two people in the group got into a real arguement and the rest of us couldn't referee it, so I figured I'd ask you.

Yuki is a data engineer and she doesn't buy the book at all. Marco does ML and has read a lot of this stuff online, and he's basically on the book's side. Here's roughly how it went — I wrote it down afterwards because it was the most interesting thing that's happened at these in a while:

1. Yuki: He says there's no Cartesian theater, no single spot in the brain where everything gets assembled for somebody to witness. Fine. But then he keeps talking about drafts, and about which draft gets published or written into the record. Publishing implies a publisher and a reader. He didn't delete the theater, he renamed it and hid it.

2. Marco: The publishing is just more drafts. Nothing gets published to anybody. The brain tells stories to other parts of itself, which do more processing on them. There's no bottom level and there doesn't need to be one.

3. Yuki: There does need to be a bottom level, because there's a fact about what I saw five seconds ago. Either I saw two seperate flashes, red then green, or I saw one thing moving and changing color in the middle. Those are two different experiences and exactly one of them happened. If your theory can't say which one, it isn't a theory of experience, it's a theory of reports about experience.

4. Marco: For almost everything it can say which. The claim is way narrower than that. It's about cases like the color phi one, where they flash a red light here and a green light there about a tenth of a second apart and people report a single light traveling across and flipping color halfway. There are two candidate stories about the insides — either the flip got assembled before it ever became an experience, or the person had the first experience and then the memory got edited afterwards — and those two stories make exactly the same predictions about every possible report, every reaction time, everything. So there's no fact of the matter, and insisting there is one isn't deep, it's empty.

5. Yuki: Identical to whom? They're identical only because you already defined experience as whatever gets reported. That's the whole trick. You build the report into the definition and then announce as a discovery that there's nothing over and above the report.

6. Marco: That's not a trick, it's the only evidence anybody has ever had of anything. If two hypotheses could never differ in anything anyone might say, do, remember, or introspect differently, then the difference between them isn't a fact about the world, it's a fact about your vocabulary.

7. Yuki: Then the model is unfalsifiable. Whatever way the experiment comes out, it gets redescribed as more drafts and more judgments about order. Name me one result that would count against it.

8. Marco: Sure. It predicts that in exactly the window where the timing gets short enough, people's confidence about which came first should fall apart; that you should be able to get the same reported experience out of two completely different underlying timings; and that if you make the second flash relevant to a task before the flip is supposed to have happened, you'll catch people already responding to it. If somebody built a case where subjects reliably and correctly report an order the model says can't be pinned down, that would wreck it.

9. Yuki: That's not a prediction, that's a redescription with numbers attached.

10. Marco: And the unified stream you're defending is itself something the machinery produces. The feeling that there's one me watching one continuous show is exactly the kind of content those drafts generate. You're using the output of the machine as evidence about the machine.

11. Yuki: Here's the part I can't get past. If the feeling of being a unified watcher is an illusion, who is it an illusion for? An illusion needs somebody being fooled. You can't say nobody is unified, it only seems that way, without a thing that it seems that way to.

12. Marco: The seeming is the thing. A system can be in a state that misrepresents itself without there being a smaller self inside it that gets fooled. You don't need a witness, you just need the misrepresentation.

They went in circles on 11 and 12 for another ten minutes and then we ordered pizza.
