I cover AI and cognitive science for a magazine, and for about three months now I've been trying to write a piece on interpretability that doesn't just amount to "researchers are finding features inside neural nets, wow." Every draft dies on the same question, which nobody on either side will answer straight: does a trained network actually *have* representations — is there a fact of the matter about what its internal states are about — or are we projecting our own categories onto a blob of matrix arithmatic and then being impressed that the projection predicts things? I've decided to build the piece around a fake-but-deadly-serious document: the joint statement that a committed interpretability realist and a committed skeptic about neural representations could both sign after a genuine adversarial collaboration.

Here's the case that convinced me the two sides aren't merely talking past each other, and that I'd like you to treat as the shared test bed for the document. A researcher shows me a small image classifier. They hunt for a direction in the hidden activations that lines up with "striped," and they find one; add a multiple of that direction at inference time and the model starts calling things tigers — a zebra, a striped sweater, a parking meter that happens to have stripes painted on it. Very cool. But the same model, read out with a different probe architecture, gives a different subspace that also decodes "striped," and there's a second direction that decodes it only for cartoonish images and not photographs. Ask which one is *the* representation and the room splits. My philosophy friend says this is just the 1980s thermostat debate with GPUs; the researcher says the thermostat had no steering experiments. Both think the other is dodging something.

So: write that joint statement, in American English. I want a numbered section of claims both parties can sign — about what steering and activation-patching results establish and what they don't, about what "feature" and "representation" are doing as terms of art in the field, and about which of the open questions are empirical and which are really definitional. Then the residual disagreements as a table, with columns for: the disagreement stated neutrally, the wording the realist would accept for their own side, the wording the skeptic would accept for theirs, the experiment or argument or thought case both agree would bear on it, and each side's prediction about the outcome, written down before the result. No strawmen — don't let the skeptic come out as a nihilist who thinks the work is worthless, and don't let the realist come out as someone who thinks a linear probe settles metaphysics. Where you judge part of the dispute to be verbal, i.e. where the two are using "representation" to mean different things and would agree on every observable fact, say explicitly which part, why, and what is left over afterwards that isn't verbal. And please dont tack a bibliography onto the end; I need the document itself, roughly the length of something two people with reputations on the line could actually put their names to.
