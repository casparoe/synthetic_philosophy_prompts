I help run intake at a small mutual-aid group, and one of our longtime volunteers has a phrase she reaches for constantly: what goes around comes around. She uses it in at least two directions and I honestly cant tell whether she means the same thing both times. When a landlord who'd been pushing families out of their units got hit with a huge back-tax bill, she said it approvingly, like the universe had balanced a ledger. But when someone at a meeting argued we should be doing more for a client who's clearly made a wreck of his own life — gambling debts, burned bridges, twice jailed, no family left who'll take his calls — she said the exact same phrase, and there it landed as "he sowed this, so it isn't ours to fix," and the conversation just ended. I tried pushing back and got nowhere, mostly because halfway through I realized I don't know what claim the sentence is actually making.

I know the doctrine behind it is supposed to be subtler than the English proverb. I've read that in some of the Buddhist versions what accrues is tied to the intention behind an act rather than to the outcome, so hurting someone by accident doesn't weigh the same as doing it on purpose. There's also a question nobody in our group ever addresses: who reaps, and when? If the ledger is settled on the same person in this one lifetime, that's an empirical claim about how the world runs, and it looks bad, given how often cruel people die comfortable in their sleep and how often decent people get flattened. If it's settled on their children, or in a later life, or by some god keeping the books, those are wildly different commitments with wildly different consequences for how you'd argue with her. And then there's the desert question, which is the one I actually care about, plus a couple of other things I keep conflating: whether karma is supposed to work like a natural law that needs no administrator, whether merit and demerit can be transferred or shared at all, and whether "it will come back to him" is even the same kind of statement as "he deserves it."

So can you take that one sentence apart for me? I want the distinct precise theses it could be standing in for, ordered from the most deflationary to the most ambitious, and for each one I want you to say what kind of claim it is — a platitude about social reciprocity, a live empirical hypothesis, a thesis about moral desert, a rule for how to conduct yourself — and then whether it's true, false, or unfalsifiable-but-doing-something-useful. Where a reading quietly slides between "this will happen" and "this should happen," or between "he caused it" and "he deserves it," or between the person who sowed and the person who reaps, point at exactly where the slide occurs. Then finish by telling me which reading, if any, would actually license her second use, the one that shuts down the arguement about our obligations to the client. Please write it as continuous prose, the way you'd write an email — no headings, no bullet points, no bold, no tables — and give me your bottom line about that second use in the first couple of sentences, before you work back through the readings.
