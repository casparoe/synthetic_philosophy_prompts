I chair the Research Integrity Appeals Panel at my university. Next Thursday we hear an appeal against a finding of fabrication made last term by a single investigating officer, and I need to circulate a written opinion to the other four members beforehand so that the meeting starts from something rather than from scratch. I would like you to draft that opinion for me.

The case, stripped of procedure, is this. A doctoral researcher in linguistics spent five weeks in a rural district collecting vocabulary and narrative material from, as the fieldnotebooks state, a single principal informant. The finding officer concluded that no such informant existed and that a substantial part of the material was invented. The evidence before the officer fell into four strands, and I want the opinion to work through them separately before it reaches a verdict, because I think the officer ran them together and the appellant has exploited that.

First, a research assistant who accompanied the trip states that she was the only other member of the party, that she never saw the person described in the notebooks, and that the notebooks were written up in the evenings from conversations held in the vehicle rather than in anyone's house. Her statement was taken in writing eleven months after the trip; she was never interviewed in person, and she had earlier made a formal complaint about being omitted from the authorship of a paper arising from the same fieldwork.

Second, a pattern in the records. Forty-one of the forty-seven dated notebook entries bear a timestamp within two minutes of the hour or the half hour. In eleven comparable datasets from the same department, produced by projects where nothing untoward has ever been alleged, the figure ranges between nine and fourteen per cent. There are no travel or subsistence claims for the second and third weeks of the trip, though there is a single fuel receipt covering the whole period.

Third, a dispute about what the notebooks mean. The appellant maintains that the word rendered in English as informant throughout the notes is a term they learned in an earlier project in the same region, where it denoted a person one consulted occasionally rather than a person one worked with over weeks, and that the finding officer simply assumed the department's own standard sense, in which an informant is a long-term language consultant who is paid and logged. If the appellant is right, the notes never asserted the existence of the person the officer set out to disprove.

Fourth, material the officer did not see. After the finding was issued, a head teacher in the district wrote to the panel confirming that interviews were conducted in his schoolhouse on four of the dates recorded in the notebooks. He adds that he cannot now recall whether the appellant or the assistant was present on those occasions, and his letter arrived after the closing date for new evidence. The appellant's own account, meanwhile, is detailed, internally consistent, corroborated by nothing, and given by someone whose two earlier publications from the same region were checked during the investigation and found sound.

Our charter is unhelpfully terse. It provides that a finding of fabrication must rest on either first-hand observation or the testimony of a reliable witness; that the absence of corroborating records shall not of itself constitute evidence; and that the standard is the balance of probabilities, having regard to the seriousness of the allegation. Three of the five members of the panel, including me, have spent some years reading Indian philosophy, and my own view is that the charter's binary is too crude for a case of this shape. I would therefore like the opinion to assess each of the four strands against the classification of the means of knowledge worked out in the Nyāya tradition — perception, inference, comparison, and testimony — asking in each case whether that strand meets the conditions the tradition itself imposes on the corresponding means, and not merely whether it would satisfy an Anglophone evidentiary intuition.

That framing should do real work, and I want the opinion to press it in at least four places. On the second strand, the opinion should say whether the timestamp pattern is a genuine mark of fabrication at all, or whether the comparison sample already shows the mark occurring where there is no fabrication, and it should distinguish between a mark that fails to be established, a mark that is not confined to the class it is supposed to indicate, and a mark that is defeated by other things we know. On the fourth strand and on the assistant's statement, the opinion should say whether the tradition's requirement that a speaker be reliable is a matter of their knowing how things are, of their not wishing to mislead, or of both, and how the answer bears on a witness who is plainly competent but possibly hostile and on a witness who is self-interested but has no evident motive to deceive. On the third strand, it should say whether fixing the reference of a term by noting its similarity to a known case is a distinct way of coming to know something in that tradition, and if so whether the officer's error was an error of that kind rather than a mere error of fact. On the first strand, it should confront the question of what exactly the assistant is reporting: she reports not seeing someone, and I want to know whether the tradition can accommodate an awareness of absences, by which route, and whether the charter's rule that missing records count for nothing rests on a view of that question that we should be willing to defend. If it is relevant, I should also like the opinion to note whether the tradition treats memory as a source of knowledge in its own right, since the assistant's account is now largely memorial while the notebooks are contemporaneous — but only if it is relevant, and only as an aside.

The opinion must end with a clear recommendation: dismiss the appeal and confirm the finding, allow it and set the finding aside, or remit the matter for fresh investigation on specified terms of reference that you state. You should be candid about what each course costs the appellant. We must decide within twenty working days of the appeal being lodged, which expires on the eleventh of next month; the appellant holds a postdoctoral offer elsewhere that lapses if the finding stands and cannot survive a four-month reinvestigation either, so a remit is not a costless way of avoiding a judgement. Finally, the opinion must contain a short section stating the strongest consideration pointing the other way from your recommendation, and answering it rather than acknowledging it in passing.

Two constraints on the writing. It should be in British English throughout, formal in register, and between roughly 1,200 and 1,500 words. And it should contain no footnotes, no bibliography and no list of sources or further reading — I want the analysis, not the scholarship behind it, and three members of this panel have no philosophical training at all, so any technical apparatus you introduce must be made to earn its place in a sentence or two of plain prose.
