I am reading a draft position paper by a colleague of mine — I'm the philosophically trained reader he asked for comments on — and its central move is an analogy. He writes that language is a game in Wittgenstein's sense: think of the builders calling out for slabs and beams, or the shopper dispatched with a slip calling for five red apples. Games are constituted by their rules and by the community that plays and polices them. From this he draws three claims: (i) there is no fact about what a large language model means by its outputs over and above which moves it is disposed to make and which moves the community accepts as correct; (ii) because family resemblance shows there is no essence of meaning, the skeptic who insists the model isn't really playing the game has nothing left to point at; and (iii) training on a corpus is initiation into a game, structurally the same thing as a child being taught one. He adds that talk of inner states, of grasping a rule, or of reference lurking behind use is precisely the picture Wittgenstein was dissolving.

I want an audit of the analogy, not a paraphrase of it. Please give me:

1. An explicit correspondence table: the player, the game, the rules, the moves, the referees or scorekeepers, winning and losing, initiation and training, opting out, one game among many. Map each onto its counterpart in his picture of language and of model training. Where a cell has no honest entry, leave it empty and say so rather than forcing a fit.

2. The features of the source domain the analogy quietly imports and the target may lack: that a game's rules can in principle be written down and consulted; that a game is entered voluntarily and can be left; that there is a determinate boundary between playing it and not playing it; that correctness is settled by an authority or by the consensus of the other players; that a game is detachable from the rest of life and pursued for its own sake; that the legal moves form a finite, fixed set. For each, say whether ordinary language has the feature, whether a trained model plausibly has it, and what the loss costs his argument.

3. Which disanalogies are load-bearing for (i), (ii), and (iii), and which are cosmetic — games are frivolous while language is serious, and so forth. Be ruthless about the distinction; I would rather have four disanalogies that matter than a dozen that decorate.

4. How the analogy interacts with the rest of the Wittgenstein material he leans on. Does the rule-following discussion support the identification of being disposed to make community-approved moves with following a rule, or does it problematize exactly that identification? Does family resemblance license claim (ii) at all, or is it a thesis about how concepts are related rather than a deflationary thesis about what meaning consists in? Is community ratification a Wittgensteinian notion or an import from conventionalism — and if you think the difference between agreement in the language people speak and agreement in the opinions they hold matters here, show me how.

5. A verdict on each of (i), (ii), and (iii): which survive the audit, which collapse once the analogy is withdrawn, and what would have to be argued independently to rescue them.

6. If the analogy is the wrong instrument, propose a better one for what he is actually trying to say, or explain why the phenomenon resists analogical treatment altogether.

I want commitment rather than balance. Take a definite position on whether his deflationary conclusion survives, and if it survives only in part, say which part and on what grounds. Because his reading of Wittgenstein is itself contested — conventionalist against quietist, and Kripke's skeptical solution against the more standard reading — state which reading your audit presupposes, defend that choice briefly, and flag the specific points where a different reading would flip your verdict. Do not close by laying out the options and leaving me to choose among them.
