I run operations for a county ambulance service of about 140 staff, covering several small towns and a long stretch of interstate. Fourteen months ago we adopted a dispatch-optimization platform from an outside vendor. Its job is not to predict where the next emergency will occur but to balance two things we specified for it: response time on the call currently in hand, and keeping at least one unit held in reserve in the corridor where a second call is statistically most likely. Waiting positions are assigned automatically; the dispatcher on duty sees the recommendation, can override it, and is required by our runbook to record a reason if they do.

On the night of the sixth, a cardiac arrest call came in from a town on the county's western edge. The platform assigned a unit eleven minutes out and held the closer unit, four minutes out, in reserve on the interstate, because its model put the probability of a second call in that corridor at roughly three in ten. The dispatcher accepted the recommendation without comment. The crew of the held unit was told to stay put, and stayed put. The patient did not survive. Forty minutes later a second call did come in on the interstate, a single-vehicle rollover, and it was covered by a mutual-aid unit from a neighboring county; mutual aid is not in the platform's data and no one had thought to include it. The corridor weighting that made holding that closer unit optimal had been raised by one of our own analysts three weeks earlier, after two near-misses on the same stretch of road; a colleague emailed at the time that the change was too steep, which looks prescient now and looked like ordinary professional disagreement then. Nobody broke a rule. The crews followed the runbook, the analyst worked from a validated model, our medical director signed off on the specification in writing and has since taken a position two states away, and the vendor built precisely what we asked for. Under our union contract, discipline requires a willful or negligent act, and our HR people cannot find one. The county's insurer has indicated that a report finding no individual at fault is considerably cheaper than one that names anyone. The family's attorney has indicated the reverse: she says she will not file over a tragedy, only over a decision.

I have eleven days to submit the internal incident report, and it must close with one of four findings: name a crew, name the analyst, name the medical director or the vendor, or record the death as a system event with no individual findings. Each option feels wrong in a way I cannot quite articulate, and I am conscious that I am hardly a disinterested observer, since this platform was my project and "the system did it" is exactly what someone in my position would find it convenient to believe. Two further things complicate the decision. The crew chief of the held unit has asked to see me; he says he wanted to drive anyway, that he had his keys in his hand, and that he intends to tell the review board that he disobeyed nothing but should have. He was trained to defer, and deferring was correct. And I keep circling back to the aggregate numbers: our own audit estimates that average response times have fallen enough this year that the platform has plausibly saved more lives than the one it cost us on the sixth, and I genuinely cannot tell whether mentioning that to the family would be an honest answer or an indecent way of changing the subject.

So I want advice, and I want it argued rather than surveyed. Which of the four findings do these facts actually support, what does committing to it oblige the agency to change, and what will it cost us with crews who have to go on following a runbook that just told them — one way or the other — whether following it can make them the villain? What do I say to the crew chief, and is it better or worse to let him take this on himself? And underneath both: when a family insists that someone must be to blame, and every candidate turns out to have done exactly what they were supposed to do, is that insistence a demand I should try to meet, a confusion I should gently dissolve, or something else entirely? If it is a confusion, does an honest agency say so out loud, or does saying so amount to a second failure to the people who are owed an explanation?
