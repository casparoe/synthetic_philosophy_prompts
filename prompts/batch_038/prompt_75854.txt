I teach the ethics of war alongside a colleague with whom I have been in disagreement for the better part of a decade. She has committed herself in print to verdicts on four cases, which I can state exactly, and a journal has now circulated a fifth case for comment; she has told me she intends to respond to it. I want to know what she will say, and whether her own precedents determine it. I am not looking for references, a survey of the literature, or named positions from the canon — I want the rule-reconstruction, and I would rather you reason from the cases as I have stated them than from what anyone has published about them.

Her four verdicts. In each she answers two questions: whether the agent acts culpably in fighting at all, and whether the opposing side is permitted to kill him.

1. A twenty-year-old conscript in the aggressor state's army, raised from childhood on his government's account of the war, kills three defending soldiers in a set-piece engagement. Not culpable; the defenders were permitted to kill him.

2. The career lieutenant colonel who planned and personally led that engagement. He enlisted twenty years earlier, took the official casus belli at face value, and has since said that he would have refused the order to cross the border had he known what my colleague takes to be the truth. Not culpable; liable to be killed.

3. A conscript in the same army who learns mid-campaign, from a captured staff document, that the casus belli was fabricated. His unit shoots deserters on the spot, and his family lives in a garrison town where the authorities would make an example of them. He remains in the line and kills a defending soldier. Not culpable — though she adds that his being in the line at all was already a wrong, and she marks the difference between that and permissibility explicitly, calling it excused. Liable to be killed.

4. A brigade commander in the same army who, knowing perfectly well that the war is aggressive, orders the bombardment of a town whose only military value is a half-empty fuel depot, foreseeing several hundred civilian deaths. Culpable — and she remarks pointedly that the identical commander ordering the identical strike in the course of a just war would be no less culpable, so that, in her phrase, the aggression adds nothing to the charge.

The fifth case, which the journal has specified in unusual detail and which I expect she will find uncomfortable: a warrant officer in the aggressor state's army — a volunteer, not a conscript — who serves as one of eleven interchangeable technicians maintaining the data link through which targeting packages reach his air force's drones. He has satisfied himself, from material any soldier in his position could have obtained without special access, that the war's stated grounds were invented. His term expires in six weeks; the army is in disorder; desertion is not, as a matter of fact, being punished; his family has already left the country. He has taken no part in any in bello violation — on his own careful reckoning the strikes his link supports have been discriminate and proportionate — and he holds no command authority over anyone. He stays out his six weeks.

What I want:

(a) Your prediction of her verdict on the warrant officer, on both questions, and an account of whether she will treat those two questions as connected or independent.

(b) At least three candidate principles, each of them consistent with all four precedents as stated. Where a principle is itself ambiguous in a way that matters for the fifth case, give both readings separately rather than choosing one silently.

(c) What each of those principles predicts for the warrant officer, then your single best guess about what she will actually write, with your reasons. In particular, weigh her two asides — the one in case 3 and the one in case 4 — which strike me as pulling in opposite directions, and tell me whether they are reconcilable and, if so, at what cost.

(d) Whether her verdicts commit her to the symmetry thesis, that combatants on the two sides stand in the same moral relation to one another once fighting has begun, or whether some asymmetric reading accommodates all four precedents equally well.

(e) One further case, not the one the journal circulated, that would discriminate most sharply among the surviving principles, together with what she would be obliged to say about it under each.

Open with your predicted verdict in a single sentence — culpable or not, liable or not — and only then give the reasoning. No preamble, and no restatement of the cases back to me.
