Utility regulation keeps doing something I can't make philosophically coherent, and I'd like to know what it's actually commited to. When a state opens its electricity market to competition, the incumbent utility is often left holding power plants it built years earlier under an obligation to serve, plants that now can't compete with cheaper generation. Regulators routinely let it recover those costs anyway — a separate line on every customer's bill for a few years, sometimes financed by bonds issued against those future bill payments. The stated reason is that the utility spent the money prudently, at the state's direction, with a reasonable expectation of getting it back in rates.

Meanwhile the same agencies, and the same economists, will tell you flatly that sunk costs are irrelevant to any decision, that only future costs and benefits count, and that the book value of an uneconomic plant shouldnt enter anyone's calculus.

So what view of rationality, or of obligation, licenses both claims? Is the recovery charge a genuine right — something like a legitimate expectation or an implicit bargain between the state and the company — or is it a political payoff wearing principle as a costume? Tell me what the practice strictly presupposes versus what it merely happens to fit, and what a regulator who took "sunk costs never matter" completely seriously would have to do differently — and what would break if they did. Bonus if you can say whether it matters that the people paying the charge weren't the ones who got the cheap electricity.
