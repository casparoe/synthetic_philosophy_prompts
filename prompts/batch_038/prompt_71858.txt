I sit as one of seven members on the Capital Projects Review Panel of a state transit authority. On Thursday we vote on two matters at once: the cost contingency reserve for the Meridian Line light-rail extension, and whether to adopt a standing protocol governing how future projects select their comparison sets. The second vote matters because our reserve policy, adopted three years ago on the advice of an external auditor who pressed what he called the outside view, requires that every capital estimate be "anchored to the observed outcomes of a reference class of similar completed projects." It nowhere says how that class is to be chosen. Whichever party controls the choice of class controls the number, and both sides in Thursday's case understand this perfectly well.

The project. The Meridian Line is an 11-station extension, 14.8 km, mostly at grade with a 2.4 km bored river crossing; delivery is by design-build contract whose tunnel scope is fixed-price; right-of-way has been acquired for 84% of the alignment; and funding comes from a dedicated statutory stream with a 2033 completion deadline.

Staff compiled four candidate classes from completed projects. All figures are real-cost overruns against the original baseline estimate.

- **A** — urban rail (metro, heavy, light) in high-income countries, completed since 1990, capital cost above US$1bn. n = 258. Median overrun 44%; 22% within budget.
- **B** — light rail only, completed since 2000, in jurisdictions using design-build procurement. n = 61. Median overrun 18%; 41% within budget.
- **C** — fixed-guideway projects delivered by this authority since 1995. n = 9. Median overrun 31%; 3 of 9 within budget.
- **D** — the sponsor's proposal: rail projects anywhere since 1980 combining a fixed-price bored contract, pre-acquired right-of-way above 75%, and a ring-fenced funding stream. n = 14. Median overrun 6%; 9 of 14 within budget.

The project sponsor argues that D is the only candidate that holds fixed the factors that actually generate overruns — scope growth at the civil/systems interface, transfer of geotechnical risk, and appropriation risk — and that a class ignoring known cost-relevant features is not a comparison class at all but a smear across dissimilar cases. Our staff analyst argues that D is gerrymandered: the three conditions are conjunctive, adopted after the fact, and each conveniently excludes projects that went badly; a median drawn from n = 14 is not a stable statistic; and if the panel accepts D it will have no principled ground on which to refuse the next sponsor a class of four. She also points out that if similarity is pressed far enough every class narrows toward the singleton, while if it is relaxed the class ceases to compare like with like, and the policy supplies no principle for locating the line.

Please draft the memo I will circulate to my fellow panel members on Wednesday. It should open with the recommendation in two or three sentences and then give the reasons, running to roughly 900–1,200 words, and it must do three things: (i) recommend a specific treatment of the Meridian Line — a class to adopt, or an explicit weighting across classes — together with the resulting reserve expressed as a percentage of the sponsor's bottom-up estimate, and state what would make you revise it; (ii) recommend a concrete standing protocol for class selection that the panel could apply to future projects, addressing small samples, post hoc conjunctive narrowing, and the authority's own thin track record; and (iii) give the sponsor's objection its strongest form and say whether it loses or wins, and on what ground. Be candid about how much discretion survives whatever protocol you propose, since a rule for choosing classes must still be applied by parties with a stake in the answer.

Argue from the case and the figures above. You have no access to the relevant literature and none is needed; I want your reasoning, not a list of sources or authorities.

After the memo, append one table with a row for each candidate class you consider — A, B, C, D, plus any class or weighting scheme you construct yourself — and exactly these columns: `class` (the label); `membership_rule` (the inclusion conditions, one sentence); `n` (sample size); `estimate` (the overrun figure or probability this class yields for the Meridian Line); `held_fixed` (which cost drivers the class actually holds constant); `best_objection` (the strongest reason a panel member could give for rejecting it); `weight` (your recommended weight in the final estimate, as a decimal from 0 to 1, summing to 1 across all rows you adopt); and `disposition` (exactly one of: adopt, adopt_with_adjustment, retain_for_sensitivity, exclude).
