I am preparing the ethical annex to a targeting-doctrine review, and before it circulates internally I want an adversarial consistency audit of the principles I have committed to in writing. My anchor throughout has been Anscombe's objection to Oxford's conferral of an honorary degree on Truman, and the pamphlet she issued about it; I take her position on intentionally killing the innocent to be basically right, and I have tried to build a workable doctrine on top of it. What I have not done is check whether the resulting package coheres. Here it is, stated as my own commitments rather than as quotations from anyone.

(1) Some acts may not be chosen, whatever the consequences. Chief among them: choosing to kill people who are neither fighting nor supplying the means of fighting, using their deaths as a means to one's ends. The wrongness of such a choice is not a function of the balance of outcomes and admits of no weighing against them.

(2) What separates murder from tragic permission is intention. The distinction between killing the innocent and merely causing their deaths as a foreseen side effect of an act aimed at something else is the distinction that does all of the moral work here; I do not think anything else in the analysis carries independent weight of its own.

(3) Intention is not an inner occurrence — not a pang, a feeling, or a private event — but a feature of what an agent does under a description, made visible in the structure of his practical reasoning. Accordingly, I reject any test of permissibility that directs us to inspect the commander's state of mind.

(4) A commander who orders a strike on a munitions plant, knowing with practical certainty that some of the workers in the adjoining housing will be killed, acts permissibly, provided the plant is what he is aiming at and the deaths form no part of his aim.

(5) Foreseen harms are nonetheless subject to a threshold: a strike is impermissible if the civilian deaths it is expected to cause are out of proportion to the military advantage sought. I need this in order to avoid licensing anything whatsoever so long as the bad effect is unintended, and I take a proportionate-reason condition to belong to the traditional doctrine.

(6) A leader who has come to treat civilian deaths as a routine line item — a cost of doing business — has damaged his character, and that damage is a moral fact about him, not a prediction about his future choices. Given (3), however, this cannot be a fact about his inner life; it must be a fact about the sort of thing he is prepared to do.

(7) I am not a pacifist: combatants may be killed and a war may be justly waged. I reject the inference from an absolutism about the innocent to a prohibition on fighting. At the same time I hold, as a matter of fact about modern industrial war, that essentially every means of decisive effect involves the mass killing of people who are not fighting.

(8) I reject consequentialism generally: I deny that there is an aggregate called the good to be maximized, and I deny that an act's permissibility is settled by the expected value of its upshot. Hence I reject the supreme-emergency exception — no catastrophe, including losing the war, licenses a state in choosing to obliterate a city. Elsewhere, though, I am content to let rights be overridden when enough is at stake: I would break a promise to save a life, and I think a physician may breach confidentiality to prevent a killing.

(9) A strike whose declared object is to break the enemy population's will to sustain the war is a legitimate instrument of statecraft, provided the deaths are not themselves the object aimed at — only the terror they produce is.

What I want is an audit, not a lecture: no survey of the literature on double effect, no history of the bombing debate, no recitation of the standard conditions. Specifically:

- Separate the genuine tensions from the apparent ones. I expect several pairs among (1)–(9) to look contradictory and not be so; identify them, and say in a sentence or two what the two different objects of evaluation are that make them compatible.
- For each genuine tension, rank it by severity, meaning how much of the rest of the package depends on the commitment that would have to go. State what must be revised, dropped, or restated, and what the revision costs elsewhere in the list.
- Where the cheapest repair is a restatement rather than a retreat, say so explicitly and supply the restatement.
- Do not assume I am attached to any particular commitment. I have deliberately not said which one I would sacrifice, and my preferences are not to be inferred from the order in which I have listed them.

End your reply with a single line in exactly this format:

Verdict: (X)–(Y) | concede: (Z)

where (X)–(Y) are the labels of the pair of commitments constituting the most serious genuine tension you found, and (Z) is the single label whose revision or removal does the most to stabilize the rest of the package.
