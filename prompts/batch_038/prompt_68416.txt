Been building an argument for my quals prep and it got away from me — the conclusion is something I definitely don't believe. The set-up: I wanted to use Condorcet against the epistocracy people, and it ended up proving way more than that.

(1) Condorcet jury theorem: if n people each answer the same yes/no question, each with probability p > 1/2 of getting it right, and their answers are independent, then the probability that the majority is right goes up with n and tends to 1 as n grows.

(2) So on any question meeting those conditions, a big enough crowd beats any single expert, whatever the expert's own accuracy — expert accuracy is some number below 1, and the majority's probability can be pushed above it just by adding people.

(3) Real voters do meet the conditions on most public questions. They're individually better than chance (they get most ordinary factual questions right, and majority opinion has a decent record of tracking the truth over the long run), and they aren't perfectly correlated with one another.

(4) Adding anyone with p > 1/2 makes the group more reliable, so the reliability-maximising committee is the whole electorate. Excluding anybody — property qualifications, age limits, credential tests — makes the group worse, not better.

(5) What gives a decision procedure authority over a question is its reliability on that question, and nothing else.

(6) So the whole electorate deciding by majority is the most reliable procedure available on every public question, and there's no epistemic case for giving expert bodies any say, let alone a veto.

(7) The same reasoning applies inside the procedure. Independence is doing the work, so we should prefer designs that maximise it: secret ballots, no deliberation, no shared briefings, no common evidence. Deliberation just makes people copy each other and collapses n real voters into a handful of effective ones.

(8) But a crowd that is deliberately kept from talking to each other and from hearing what the people who actually study the question have to say cannot approach certainty about anything like epidemiology or financial regulation.

(9) So either (1) is false, or the theorem is of no use at all in defending democracy.
