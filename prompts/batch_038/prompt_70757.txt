I sit in on a graduate reading group in the philosophy of sleep and dreaming, keep the minutes, and can therefore tell you exactly where the discussion has stopped moving. The group has split into two camps. The first holds a deflationary line in two stages. Stage one is Malcolm's argument from the 1950s: a dream is not an experience, because nothing that happens during the night plays any part in fixing what a dream report says — what licenses a report of having dreamt of a burning house is only the waking impression — so there is no fact of the matter, distinct from that impression, about what the dream was like, how vivid it was, or when in the night it occurred; raising such questions is a misuse of the concept rather than a hard question with an answer we lack. Stage two is Dennett's mid-1970s argument, which reaches a similar destination by an entirely different road: even granting that the concept is in order, the hypothesis that dreams are experiences earns its keep only if some conceivable observation would support it, and since a dream's content might in principle be assembled and written into memory at or near the moment of waking — the pre-recorded-cassette picture, suggested to him by results in which the apparent content and even the apparent timing of a perception seem to be settled after the fact — no such observation exists, so the experience hypothesis is idle rather than refuted. The second camp answers with the empirical record: the early-1980s lucid-dreaming studies associated with LaBerge, in which trained sleepers produced an agreed pattern of eye movements from inside REM sleep and later reported having dreamt of producing exactly that pattern; the thoughtlike mentation reports collected at non-REM awakenings; and Jennifer Windt's recent book-length defense, which argues that such reports taken at face value describe episodes bearing the marks of genuine experience — apparent sensory vividness, immersion, a sense of presence, and a sense of being the one doing things.

What has now repeated itself three weeks running is this. One member says the eye-movement studies settle the matter, since a sleeper who emits, in real time inside REM sleep, the very signal he later says he was dreaming of emitting is producing content then and there rather than retroactively. Another replies that this begs the question: what the study establishes is a real-time correlation between physiology and a subsequently produced narrative, whereas the cassette hypothesis was never a claim about physiology but about when a dream's content is fixed, and by that standard the signaling data are simply compatible with it. Neither concedes anything. A third member, who thinks both camps are building on sand, keeps observing that reports about one's own dreams are precisely the sort of introspective deliverable that general skepticism about introspection should make us distrust, whichever way the argument turns. My own working hypothesis, which I have not been able to defend to anyone's satisfaction, is that Malcolm and Dennett get filed together as the two dream skeptics when they are answering different questions — one about what our concept licenses, one about what could count as evidence for an empirical hypothesis — and that the stalemate persists because the word experience is allowed to slide between at least three things: occurrent phenomenal character, introspective accessibility, and reportable content.

Diagnose the stalemate rather than take a side in it. Please answer as exactly five numbered points, each one or two sentences long, with no introduction and no conclusion; between them they should establish what kind of disagreement this actually is (verbal, substantive, two questions answering to one heading, or ill-posed), whether Malcolm and Dennett are genuine allies or merely converge on a slogan, whether some empirical question is doing covert work here and if so which one, what would count as progress — a distinction, an experiment, a formal result, a change of question — and whether progress should be expected at all, together with whatever the two camps are jointly failing to see.
