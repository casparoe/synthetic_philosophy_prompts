ok, rabbit hole time. I've decided the Kalai-Smorodinsky bargaining solution is right and Nash is wrong, and I want to know what should count against that before I write it up for a thing at school.

My case: Nash takes the frontier point that maximizes the product of the two gains over the threat point, and it leans on that axiom where adding options nobody ends up taking can't change the deal. KS instead looks at each player's best achievable payoff, draws a line from the threat point to that pair, and takes where it hits the frontier — which respects "if my opportunities improve and yours don't, I shouldn't come out worse."

Toy problem I made up: threat point (0,0), feasible set = convex hull of (0,0), (4,0), (4,1), (0,3). Nash gives (3, 1.5), KS gives (2.4, 1.8). Now add the option (6,0), so the set becomes the triangle with corners (0,0), (6,0), (0,3). Nash still gives (3, 1.5) — player 1's shiny new oppurtunity is worth literally zero to him. KS gives (3, 1.5), so he gains. Seems decisive to me.

So what am I missing? What does KS give up or get wrong that this example hides, which of those would a KS defender actually concede vs. wave away, and rank them by how much they should move me.
