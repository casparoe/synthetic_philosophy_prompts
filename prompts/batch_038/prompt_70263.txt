TA-ing an upper-level seminar on truth and paradox and I have to grade essay question 2 from the midterm tonight. Question as it appeared on the exam:

> Kripke's construction starts with an empty extension for the truth predicate and iterates: at each successor stage, a sentence goes into the extension just in case it would come out true given the extension so far, and into the anti-extension just in case it would come out false; at limit stages we take unions. Because the operation is monotone there are fixed points, and the minimal one is the union of the stages.
> (a) Explain what "grounded" means here, and why the Liar sentence ends up without a truth value rather than generating a contradiction.
> (b) Now consider two speakers. One says that most of the other's claims about a certain scandal are untrue; the other says that everything the first speaker says about that scandal is untrue. What can a pair of this shape show about the minimal fixed point?
> (c) Suppose that instead of picking one fixed point we treat a sentence as true just in case it is true in every fixed point, and false just in case it is false in every fixed point. Does this supervaluation over fixed points solve the problem raised by the strengthened Liar, "this sentence is not true"? Explain.

Student's answer:

> (a) The construction is three-valued and uses the strong Kleene tables, so a compound loses its value as soon as one of its parts has none. Stage 0 puts nothing in the extension. At each later stage we look at what would be true given what we've already got, and the operator is monotone, so by the usual fixed point result there's a least fixed point, reached by taking unions at limits. A sentence is grounded when its value gets settled from the bottom up by the non-semantic facts, rather than depending on semantic claims that haven't been evaluated yet. Ordinary sentences like "snow is white" are grounded and pick up their values early. The Liar isn't: you'd need its own value before you could assign one, so it never enters the extension or the anti-extension and stays a gap. This matters, because nothing contradictory happens. The theory doesn't say the Liar is both true and false the way a dialetheist would; gaps aren't gluts. It just says the predicate is partially defined, which is how the language can contain its own truth predicate.
>
> (b) This kind of pair shows the minimal fixed point can be omega-inconsistent. If each of the second speaker's claims is separately untrue, the first speaker's generalization ought to come out true. But you can't evaluate the generalization until the instances are settled, and one of the instances is the claim that everything the first speaker says is untrue, which depends on the generalization. So the construction stalls and the generalization is a gap even though each instance has a value. Omega-inconsistency means the fixed point ends up containing a sentence and its negation, so it's flatly inconsistent, and that's why Kripke has to give up classical logic in the object language rather than just allowing gaps.
>
> (c) Every fixed point contains the minimal one, so anything valueless in the minimal fixed point is valueless in all of them. The truth-teller, then, is neither supertrue nor superfalse, and that's the right result for a sentence with no principled value. The strengthened Liar is a different case. It can't be true at any stage, since if it were true it would be untrue, so it comes out false in every fixed point and is therefore superfalse. That's exactly what we wanted: the theory delivers the verdict that the strengthened Liar is untrue, and it delivers it inside the object language, so the revenge worry doesn't get off the ground.
