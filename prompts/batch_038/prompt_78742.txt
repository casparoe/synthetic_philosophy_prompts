So I'm an infra/SRE person, zero philosophy training, and I got dragged into an argument in our on-call channel that never actually got resolved. Two staff engineers — call them Ana and Ben — fighting about a change to our deploy pipeline. Context: we had a bad incident where someone at 2am disabled a rate limiter to "just get it back up" and turned a degraded service into a full outage. After that, Ana's team rebuilt things so the guardrails can't be switched off from inside an incident at all. No override flag, no break-glass token. You have to open a PR and get a second approver, which realistically takes an hour.

Here's roughly the exchange, in order:

1. Ana: if you can override a guardrail at 3am you will override a guardrail at 3am. It's the mast thing — tie yourself up before you can hear the sirens, because you know future-you will want to do the wrong thing while sincerely believing it's the right thing.
2. Ben: the mast story only works because Ulysses knew exactly what was coming. The sirens were a known failure mode with a known shape and a known duration. Your pipeline change assumes every future incident resembles the last one. That's not prudence, that's a bet that the next outage has the same shape as this one — except you've also cut off your own hands if you lose the bet.
3. Ana: an escape hatch labelled "real emergencies only" isn't an escape hatch, it's a suggestion. Every commitment device that has ever worked worked by being expensive or impossible to reverse. Otherwise it commits to nothing.
4. Ben: then you've built a system where the people who understand it best are forbidden from fixing it, and the actual decision now belongs to whoever wrote that config two years ago and has since left the company. Same reason I think constitutions that lock in the choices of long-dead people are illegitimate — wasn't there a Jefferson letter about every constitution expiring after nineteen years or something? The dead shouldn't get a vote in our incident response.
5. Ana: if the config is wrong we change the config. Calmly, in daylight, with two approvers and a written reason. That's the entire point. The binding isn't aimed at the future, it's aimed at 3am.
6. Ben: but at 3am you also don't know whether the config is wrong — and now you've made that unfindable-out. You've made the expensive case unfixable in order to protect the cheap case.
