I cover science and ideas for a magazine, and last week I sat through a dinner arguement that I still can't resolve — which is a problem, because I have to write it up. Two of the guests were an ML researcher who does post-training work and a philosopher of biology. The topic was Dennett's pairing of Darwin and Turing as pulling the same strange inversion, the competence-without-comprehension move.

They went about seven rounds. My notes:

(1) Researcher: Darwin and Turing made the identical move. Both showed you can get the outputs of a designer or a reasoner without a designer or a reasoner — differential reproduction in one case, gradient descent in the other. Same algorithm-shaped trick, ninety years apart, which is exactly why they belong in one sentence.

(2) Philosopher: They aren't the same kind of claim. Darwin's inversion was ontological: it struck an entity off the world's inventory, we no longer need a designer to exist. Turing's was methodological: he set aside what's going on inside the machine, offered a test of outward performance, and pointedly declined to say whether the machine understands. One sentence containing both launders Darwin's confidence into Turing's question mark.

(3) Researcher: Bracketing is just politeness with a deadline. If passing the test is the standard, then passing it settles the matter, and Turing handed us the criterion and then flinched at his own result.

(4) Philosopher: Grant that. The analogy still breaks at where the competence comes from. With selection there was no comprehension anywhere in the pipeline — nothing upstream understood anything, the design got manufactured from scratch by dumb replication with variation. With a model, the corpus is sedimented comprehension: the objective was chosen by people, the code written by people, the evals designed by people. So the honest word isn't inversion, it's relocation. He had a phrase for it, comprehension laundering: hide the understanders upstream, then point at the artifact and say look, no understanding required.

(5) Researcher: That charge was available against Darwin too, and people made it — the environment is the breeder, the ecosystem knows what works, the gene pool carries the information. Pump the description up far enough and you can always find somewhere to stash a designer. And if relocation kills the claim, it kills it for your own comprehension, which came from evolution plus teachers plus a language you didn't invent. That's a reductio, not a criterion.

(6) Philosopher: Not a reductio, because relocating and generating are different relations, and here's a test that seperates them: delete the upstream comprehenders and see whether the competence persists and keeps improving on its own. Termite mounds and hearts, yes. A frozen model, no — the weights don't get better by themselves, it's a snapshot of comprehension that has to be poured in again at every release. Darwin's inversion is about competence that compounds without comprehension; this is comprehension that got cached.

(7) Researcher: Caching doesn't survive contact with how these systems are actually trained. Under RL the reward is a scalar, roughly one bit of better-or-worse, and out of that come behaviors nobody put in the corpus and nobody can articulate. That's a dumb differential process generating novel competence, which is the Darwinian template, not the librarian template. Also your deletion test proves too much — a human raised in isolation wouldn't improve either.

Nobody budged and then dessert arrived. My editor wants to know whether there's a real disagreement here or two vocabularies missing each other. Can you go through it move by move and rate each of the seven from clearly wrong to clearly right, with a line or two of justification each? Then tell me who's right on the sub-questions: is Turing's inversion methodological or ontological, does the upstream-comprehension objection have any bite at all, and is the deletion test in (6) a principled criterion or a dressed-up intuition? Finish with what both of them are missing.
