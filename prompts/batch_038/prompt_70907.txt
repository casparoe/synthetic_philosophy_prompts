You are a philosopher of economics with a serious working knowledge of the socialist calculation debate and of the institutional practice of utility regulation; I want you to think carefully and to treat what follows as an interpretive problem about an existing practice rather than as a request for a survey. Before you commit to an answer, spend a short paragraph brainstorming candidate readings of the practice and noting which details of it each reading struggles to explain. You have no access to sources and I do not want a bibliography or a reading list: where you are unsure of a historical or technical detail, flag it plainly rather than guessing at it.

For the past eighteen months I have worked as an economic analyst supporting intervenor groups in general rate cases before a state public utilities commission, and the work has left me with a puzzle I cannot resolve. Concretely, here is what happens. The utility files a tariff; the commission opens a docket; over roughly a year, the parties litigate, through expert testimony and cross-examination before an administrative law judge, what the price of a kilowatt-hour ought to be. The commission ultimately determines a revenue requirement by taking the utility's rate base, applying an authorized return on the equity portion of it, adding operating expenses, taxes, and depreciation, and allocating the total across customer classes by formulas that are themselves contested. Every input is disputed: the prudence of a particular gas plant or storm-hardening program and whether an imprudent investment should be disallowed from the rate base; whether a demand-side program is an expense or an asset; whether the allowed return should track a peer group of utilities or a theoretical cost of capital; how much of a legacy coal contract belongs in this vintage of rates. At the end, a public body issues a determination that fixes, administratively, the price of electricity for several million people until the next review, several years hence. In the price-cap variant of the same idea, familiar from the RPI-X family of formulas, the revenue requirement is replaced by an inflation index minus an efficiency offset, and that offset is not observed by anyone: it is set by the regulator's judgment about what the firm could plausibly achieve, with the firm keeping whatever it beats.

My training told me this was supposed to be impossible. Mises argued that economic calculation without market prices for the means of production is not merely hard but unintelligible, since there is no common denominator in which to compare rival uses of capital goods and hence no fact of the matter about whether a given plan is efficient or wasteful. Hayek pressed a related but distinct point: the knowledge that prices condense is dispersed, largely tacit, and known only to particular people in particular circumstances of time and place, so no agency can collect it however sophisticated its statistics. Lange's rejoinder was that a planning board could treat prices parametrically, announce a vector of numbers, observe the resulting surpluses and shortages, and revise by trial and error, and that such a board might even outperform a real market, being free of the myopia and unpriced externalities that afflict actual firms. But my commission looks, from where I sit, exactly like a small Lange procedure: it announces a price, observes what happens, revises at the next review, and, because it cannot observe the efficient cost frontier, explicitly benchmarks the firm against comparable utilities as a stand-in for competitive discipline it knows is absent. Sitting in the middle of all this is the familiar result that a firm allowed a fair return on its rate base has an incentive to over-invest in capital, which means the administratively set price alters the very cost data on which the next determination will be based. There is also a historical case that complicates the standard story: a Soviet mathematician who developed linear programming while working inside the planning system argued that the dual multipliers his methods produced were objectively determined valuations, genuine prices arrived at by computation rather than by exchange.

So: what theory of price, and of economic knowledge, does this practice presuppose? Please work through the following, in whatever order you find most illuminating. First, is a commission-determined revenue requirement a price in the sense Mises denied could exist outside of exchange, or is it a different kind of object altogether — a rule for distributing incurred costs, a legal entitlement with an economic justification attached, a parametric placeholder of the Langean sort, a negotiated settlement? Second, distinguish sharply what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits comfortably with: I suspect that at least one of the famous positions is a good description of the practice's self-understanding but is not actually required by it, and that a fourth, frankly instrumentalist reading — these are revisable rules of thumb, and the practice carries no theory of price at all — is a live rival that needs to be taken seriously and then assessed. Third, reconcile or refuse to reconcile two features that look contradictory: the commission insists it cannot know efficient costs and therefore benchmarks firms against one another, while simultaneously claiming to determine the correct price. Is the reliance on comparative yardstick competition a covert concession to Hayek, an admission that knowledge of the frontier can be had only indirectly, from the revealed performance of others, or is it neutral among the rival accounts? Fourth, consider the scope question that seems to me decisive but that I cannot formulate precisely: Mises's argument concerns an economy in which the means of production are not privately owned and traded, whereas my utility buys its transformers, steel, gas, and labor at market prices. Does the practice therefore parasitically depend on a surrounding market order, making it an island of administration in an ocean of exchange and inverting the usual image of the firm as an island of conscious direction in a sea of market coordination? If so, does the calculation objection survive intact against full socialization while simply not applying here, and does the same move explain the Soviet shadow-pricing case, or does that case do something different? Fifth, tell me whether the two superficially similar practices — fixing a tariff and assigning a shadow price to a non-marketed good such as a statistical life, an hour of travel time, or a tonne of avoided emissions, then comparing that computed number to market prices as though the two were commensurable — presuppose the same view of price or importantly different ones. Finally, state what would have to change in the actual conduct of a rate case under each of the three classic views adopted in turn, and say which of them, if any, the practice as presently run already embodies, and where it is philosophically underdetermined because several of them would license it equally well.
