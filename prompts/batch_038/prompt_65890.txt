So I'm not a philosophy person, I work in logistics, but I've been reading about Tibetan Buddhism off and on for a couple years now since my sister got into it and dragged me to a teaching up in Ladakh. Something from that trip has been bugging me and I can't shake it, so I figured I'd better ask someone who can lay the options out properly instead of me guessing.

Here's the thing. The tradition teaches, very insistently, that there's no self — that what we call a person is just a shifting bundle of physical and mental events with no owner sitting underneath them, and that clinging to a permanent self is close to the root mistake that keeps you suffering. And yet the same tradition runs an institution, the reincarnate lama system, that behaves as if a particular dead man comes back as a particular toddler. As I understand it, it goes roughly like this: after a well-known lama dies, senior monks look for signs he left behind, for dreams and visions and omens, sometimes for a child born around the right time in the right direction; the candidates are then tested by being shown objects that belonged to the dead lama mixed in with lookalike decoys, and the child is supposed to pick out his own; the kid who passes gets enthroned, given the predecessor's monastery, title, students, property and job, and is educated from scratch, sometimes decades later, to carry on the dead man's work. Whole lineages have run this way for centuries, and there are rival claimants and political fights over it. The current Dalai Lama has said publicly that he might not come back at all, or that his successor might turn up somewhere else entirely, which makes the whole arrangement look negotiable from the top down.

So my question: what does that practice actually presuppose about what a person is? Note the bits that seem to pull against each other. The child almost never has memories of the previous life — he has to be found by outside signs and object tests, not by saying "that's my drum" in a sentence anyone could check — and yet he's treated as fully and literally the same lama, inheriting authority and estates. Meanwhile the monastary re-teaches him everything, letters, texts, meditation, as though nothing carried over, which is odd if the attainments are really his. And the recognition is done by a committee whose verdict can be disputed, so there's a whiff of it being a social fact, an appointment, rather than a discovery about metaphysics. I'd like you to reconstruct the theory of personal identity that would make all of this hang together: does it need a soul or a transmigrating substance, or would something like continuity of a causal stream of mental events — no owner required — be enough to license picking out this toddler? Which rival views does it quietly rule out (identity as tied to the body or brain, identity as memory, identity as all-or-nothing rather than a matter of degree)? Where is the practice philosophically underdetermined, so that several incompatible theories would each happily license it? And is there any version of strict no-self that makes the institution coherent rather than a bit of folk metaphysics the tradition never got around to cleaning up? Please don't just say "the two truths doctrine resolves it" and stop — tell me what that resolution would actually have to claim and whether it costs anything. Finally, tell me what would have to change in the practice itself if the underlying view were swapped out, e.g. if you replaced it with the view that identity is all-or-nothing and needs a substance, or with a view where survival is a matter of degree.
