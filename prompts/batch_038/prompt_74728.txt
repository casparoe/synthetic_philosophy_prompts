For the past fourteen months I have been keeping a record of the unwritten rules that govern a federation of three co-housing buildings and the two discussion forums their residents use, and I have reached the point where I can no longer tell which of them I should be calling conventions. My working touchstone has been Lewis's analysis: a behavioral regularity in some recurrent situation, sustained in a population because nearly everyone conforms to it, nearly everyone expects nearly everyone else to conform, nearly everyone's preferences over the possible combinations of actions are roughly alike, nearly everyone prefers general conformity to it on condition that the others conform, and nearly everyone would equally have preferred general conformity to some alternative regularity that could have taken its place — with all of this common knowledge in the population, and with the whole thing sitting on top of a coordination problem rather than a conflict of interest. What I want from you is not a summary of that analysis but a principle, or a small number of candidate principles, that reproduces the pattern of verdicts I have actually arrived at case by case.

Here they are. Which side of the shared staircase people keep to when carrying something bulky: a convention, uncontroversially; it was settled in one afternoon years ago, the opposite side would have served identically, and it now runs itself. Residents signing internal messages with a bare first name and no closing formula: I want to call this a convention too, though I doubt the analysis lets me, since nobody gains or loses anything from anybody else's compliance, and I honestly do not think I would prefer that any one resident switch to an alternative closing practice on condition that everyone else keeps to the bare name. Dishes being left rinsed in the drying rack rather than placed in the dishwasher: everyone does it, everyone expects everyone to, everyone complains about it, everyone would prefer that the building generally used the machine, and nobody wants to be the single person whose cup sits wet for eighteen hours until the rota runs — I refuse to call this a convention, it is a trap, and I am glad it is excluded, but from the outside it looks so much like the staircase case that I want to be told precisely which feature does the excluding. The red thread tied to each building's gate: it marked which households had paid into a roof fund that closed twenty years ago, nobody under fifty knows this, the meaning is gone, and yet every household keeps one up, expects the others to, and would notice and resent a bare gate — I want to say it ceased to be a convention at roughly the moment it ceased to mean anything, even though its present structure of conformity and expectation is indistinguishable from the staircase. Not greeting strangers in the stairwell: universal conformity, universal expectation, and every person I have interviewed detests the practice while assuming they are alone in detesting it — a convention, and the most interesting kind, because it is the one most likely to collapse in a week, yet the analysis files it under exactly the same heading as the staircase and registers nothing about its fragility. The passage in the smallest building where everyone going up waits for everyone coming down: not a convention, because nothing else could have been done, and I am glad the requirement of an alternative does the work here, since without it the word would swallow every regularity in the world. The forum rule that posts go in the thread matching their subject: universal conformity, no moderators, universal expectation, deviants get a polite request to move the post, and when asked why people say misplaced posts waste everyone's time — a convention, plainly. The other forum's rule against posting the same question twice: identical in every observable respect, universal conformity, universal expectation, a rule every member can state and cite, an available alternative (no such rule), and, I think, even the conditional preference, since a forum thick with duplicates would be unusable to me as well — except that the sole thing holding anyone in line is that repeat posters are mocked and quietly stripped of posting rights by a knot of veterans, and every one of them tells me in private that they would double-post tomorrow if nothing would come of it. My verdict is that this is emphatically a norm and emphatically not a convention, a regime of deterrence wearing a convention's clothes; and I cannot see anything in the analysis that separates it from the subject-thread rule. That pair is the one I most need help with, because if the two come out the same, then either my verdict is mistaken or the analysis is missing a clause about why people conform rather than about how they are disposed to act. Finally, the greeting ritual in the larger forum, now several thousand members most of whom never meet the same person twice, sustained not by expectations about partners but by visible account-age markers and by gossip that outruns the interactions themselves: I want to call it a convention, even though no member knows most of the others and nothing remotely like common knowledge could obtain across the population. If the analysis demands common knowledge throughout the population, it seems to me to be describing a village and calling it a society.

My own tentative principle, which I suspect is wrong in at least two places, is this: a regularity is a convention in a population just when it is one of at least two regularities that could have solved some recurrent problem the population actually faced, each member's reason for conforming makes reference to others' conformity, and the regularity could be displaced by a coordinated switch. Take that apart. What I want back is a principle stated clause by clause, with the case from my list that motivates each clause and the work that clause does; its verdicts on cases I have not listed, in particular on a regularity that arose by pure drift with no problem behind it at all, and on a rule that a small subgroup follows perfectly while the rest of the population merely tolerates and expects; an account of whether convention and norm are different kinds of thing or positions on one continuum, and whether the story of how a regularity stabilizes over time can classify cases that the snapshot of equilibrium structure cannot; and a frank statement of which of my verdicts the principle cannot deliver. If the honest answer is that only a baroque, multi-clause principle saves all eleven of them while a spare one saves nine at the cost of the red thread or the deterrence case, tell me which you would take and what the two forfeited verdicts cost.
