I'm on my museum's collections committee and we vote Thursday on a bequest, so were going to need a memo I can circulate tonight. The item is a small canvas that's been catalogued since 1931 as a late work by V, a well-regarded Dutch painter of the 1880s. Pigment and canvas dating came back period-correct, but the conservators report says the underdrawing is in a different hand from the surface paint, and that the surface was almost certainly laid in by V's studio assistant — who then had V sign and date it. V's dealer sold it in the 1890s as a V and V never objected; he seems to have taken credit for studio pieces his entire career. So nobody who was in a position to be deceived actually was.

Two wrinkles. The donor insists it hangs as a V and will pull the gift if we reattribute. And our registrar dug up that unsigned works by the assistant now fetch more at auction than V's studio pieces do, which means "studio of V" isn't the financially conservative call here.

Half the committee says a thing can't be a fake unless somebody lied, and nobody lied. The other half says it can't be a V because V didn't paint it. Draft the memo: a definite recommendation (take it as offered, take it and reattribute, decline, or take it and keep it off view), the reasoning, and a real treatment of the strongest case for whichever position you're recommending against. I want it to say what the right attribution is even a claim *about* — whose hand held the brush, who conceived the picture, or what the work has been received as for a century — and then whether the no-lie-so-no-fake line survives once you've picked. Ten minute read, no bibliography.
