ok so I'm in a philosophy of medicine seminar this term (undergrad, about half of us are premeds) and our prof has saddled us with a semi-real task: we're writing the "what counts as a medical problem?" info sheet for the student-run free clinic, and before that we have to agree on a working definition of disease. Last week everyone threw one into a hat, we ended up with six, and I have to bring a ranking to Thursday's seminar. That's me. Help.

Here they are, cleaned up but not strawmanned:

1. Statistical — a disease is a measurable departure, downward, from the species-typical level of functioning of some part or process, where typical is fixed against a reference class matched for age and sex.
2. Design-plus-harm — a disease is a state that (a) comes from some internal mechanism failing to do the job it's there to do, and (b) causes harm or disadvantage to the person by the standards of the society they actually live in. Both parts needed, neither alone.
3. Goals — a disease is anything that reduces a person's ability to reach their own vital goals in their circumstances, where vital goals means the ones they'd have if they were fully informed about themselves and their situation, not just whatever they happen to want right now.
4. Treatment-worthy — a disease is a disvalued condition that medicine, or something like it, is the right social response to. There's no purely biological half: biology tells you what's going on, the value judgment tells you whether it's a disease.
5. Committee — a disease is whatever a properly constituted classification panel would put on the disease list: full empirical information, fair patient representation, no conflicts of interest.
6. Felt — a disease is what its like to be unwell from the inside. If something is wrong for you in a way you can't shake, that's the disease, and the biomarker is at most evidence about it.

Two of these are basically lifted from the readings (you'll spot them), the rest our seminar cobbled together, so don't assume any of them are canonical or go looking for their official versions.

I ran each one against five cases the clinic actually bumps into, and I need you to do the same because that's exactly what I'll get asked about Thursday:

A. Silent. A 45-year-old has a bulge in an artery in her head. It has never produced one symptom, she has no idea it's there, and if it ruptures it's often fatal within the hour. Nobody experiences anything wrong.
B. Weird-but-fine. A guy's value for some marker sits in the bottom 2% of his reference range. He feels completely fine and nobody has shown the low value does anything at all.
C. History. Fifty years ago nearly the whole profession classified a certain pattern of sexual attraction as a disease. The biology didn't change in the meantime; the classification flipped.
D. New test. A screening panel introduced last year turns up early, pre-symptomatic disease in about 30% of adults who felt fine and would never have been tested before.
E. Ramps. A person who uses a wheelchair after an old injury — the injury healed about as well as such injuries do, and their body is now doing what a body like that does — lives in a city with no ramps and no accessible transit and can't get to work. Where's the problem, and is any part of it a disease?

What I want:

- Score all six definitions on each of A through E: 0 = gets it wrong or can't even address the case, 1 = survives with an embarrassing caveat, 2 = handles it. That's 30 numbers, put them in a table so I can screenshot it for the seminar.
- Then one strict ranking of the six, no ties, on how well it gets those five verdicts right overall. Don't just add up the scores and be done, tell me where the totals mislead.
- Then say which one you'd actually print on the sheet, which may not be your number one. Explaining a definition to a patient in four minutes at a free clinic is a different job than winning the argument in seminar, so tell me whether that changes the pick and why.
- Last thing: call out any of the five cases you think is a dud — one where every definition on the list lands in the same place so it isn't discriminating anything. I have a suspicion about one of them and I'd rather find out from you than from my prof.

No citations or reading lists please, I've got those covered, and don't add a seventh definition, six is already too many for one seminar.
