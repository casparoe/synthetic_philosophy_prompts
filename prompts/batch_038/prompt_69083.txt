Berkeley's master argument challenges his reader to conceive of a sensible object — a tree, a book — existing unconceived, and concludes that the challenge cannot be met, since whatever is conceived is thereby conceived, and so is not conceived as unconceived. A familiar reply is that this runs two things together: conceiving of an object *as* unperceived, and conceiving of an object *while* it is unperceived. The second may indeed be impossible for the reason Berkeley gives, but the first is just what his opponent does, and nothing in the argument shows it to be incoherent.

Please give an example of a conceptual issue, arising in a domain where Berkeley's idealism is not itself at stake — mathematics, epistemology, the philosophy of mind, legal or evidential reasoning, or ordinary practical deliberation — that turns on exactly this slippage between what a representation depicts and the conditions under which that representation is produced. Set out the case in enough detail that the two readings can be distinguished explicitly, and identify the spurious result the confusion delivers. Then assess whether the parallel genuinely vindicates Berkeley's opponent, or merely relocates the difficulty, since a critic may insist that in the idealist case there is no clean separation to be had between the content of the conceiving and the act of conceiving it.

Please avoid the stock illustrations of self-refutation and liar-style paradoxes, and say where your example is doing more or less work than the original. Throughout, distinguish what is settled from what remains genuinely contested among those who work on Berkeley and on the domain of your example; do not present a contested reading as though it were the consensus.
