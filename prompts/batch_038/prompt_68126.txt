So I keep coming back to the de auxiliis mess — Molina's Concordia comes out in 1588, the Dominicans under Báñez go after it almost immediately, it escalates into a papal commission that Clement VIII sets up in the late 1590s, grinds away for the better part of a decade, rolls over to Paul V, and then in 1607 just stops without ruling. Both orders end up allowed to keep teaching what they'd been teaching, with instructions not to call each other heretics. Correct me if I've got the shape of that wrong, but that's the shape I'm working with.

I dont want the church politics, I have the outline. I want the episode analyzed as a case of a theory that was offered as a peace treaty and ended up as a faction flag — Molinism as the theory, the commission as the case.

First the mapping, and I mean it properly. What is middle knowledge actually doing in Molina's architecture: the three logical moments, where counterfactuals of creaturely freedom sit between natural knowledge and free knowledge, why God selecting a world out of the ones he surveyed is supposed to be providence rather than luck. And what job it was recruited for in that specific fight. The Dominicans had physical premotion — grace moving the will so that it infallibly consents; the Jesuits thought that ate freedom; Molina's move was that God doesn't have to move you at all, because he already knows what you'd freely do in any circumstances he might drop you into, so he just arranges the circumstances. Give me that as the live options the two sides had, not as a textbook summary of it.

Then the objection that matters. Báñez's line, as far as I can tell, is basically the grounding objection in a doublet: nothing makes those counterfactuals true, and if they're true prior to and independently of God's will, then God's knowing them is a matter of being informed how things stand rather than determining them — providence demoted to picking off a menu somebody else wrote. Is that a fair reading, or am I flattening something the Thomist side actually had?

Then the bit I care about most, and please engage it rather than gesturing at it. Under pressure Molinists sometimes say God's decision to create is what makes the counterfactuals true. Okay, but then they aren't middle knowledge anymore, they're free knowledge, the three moments collapse into two, and the whole structure loses its middle. Is there any move left that keeps the middle genuinely in the middle? And did the commission have anything like such a move available, or was the question simply undecidable on the resources the two orders shared — in which case the deadlock wasn't a failure of nerve.

Last, be honest about where the theory goes quiet. Molinism predicts nothing about a commission stalling for ten years. But does the irenic framing — the theory's headline selling point being that neither extreme has to swallow the other's pill — explain the non-outcome better than the theology does? A compromise rejected by both poles maybe isn't a failed theory; maybe it's a theory doing the only job it was built for, which is letting people stop fighting without having to agree. Tell me whether that reading is a defense of Molinism or a eulogy for it.
