I keep the minutes for a volunteer-run repair workshop, and we have a recurring argument about when something a member says gets entered in the minutes as a pledge we can hold them to. Our chair, Ines, has made that call every time so far. Her record:

- A member muttered "I'll sort the extractor fan" to nobody in particular as he walked out of a workday. Nothing was recorded, and when the fan was still broken a month later nobody treated it as a failure.
- A member emailed the whole list saying she would pay the roofer's deposit by Friday. Recorded; we booked the roofer on the strength of it, and she paid.
- At a full meeting, after the rest of us kept pressing him, a member said "fine, I promise, I'll do the accounts," visibly reluctantly. Recorded. We asked nobody else for months. When he didn't do it, Ines said we were entitled to be cross.
- At the summer barbecue a member announced he would name his firstborn after the tool shed. Not recorded.
- During a workday, to a member who was worried about public liability cover, another said "don't worry, that's dealt with." She cancelled her own policy. He insists he meant only that he was going to ask about it. Recorded, and the group met the claim out of the till.
- A man who left the group two years ago sent Ines a solemn private note offering to rebuild our inventory system. Not recorded. Ines said there was nothing for us to hold him to, and he wasn't ours to hold.

Next month's case is this. At a workday, a member with a long history of over-promising said loudly, and most people agree at least half as a joke, "I promise I'll rebuild the whole back wall single-handed by spring." Another member took him at his word, turned down a contractor's quote, and the workshop spent money on materials that suit only that wall. The speaker now says everyone present knew he was joking.

I can see several different rules that would produce every one of Ines's verdicts so far — one about whether the speaker meant to put himself on the hook, one about whether a hearer was entitled to take the words up and acted on them, one about whether the occasion was a business one rather than a party, and possibly one about who is inside the group and so able to be held at all — and I cannot tell which she is running, or whether she is running one at all rather than reacting case by case. Give me, first, what each of those candidate rules predicts for the back wall, including any refinements they need to survive her earlier verdicts; second, one committed prediction of what Ines will decide, with your reasons for preferring that rule over the others; third, the single further case that would settle which rule is hers, and why that case rather than another; and fourth, since I am the one writing the minutes, what I should enter if the point of the record is that it stays consistent with itself, and what principle we would be adopting whichever way I write it. Take a definite position on all four and give your reasons; I would much rather have a call I can argue with than a menu of possibilities.
