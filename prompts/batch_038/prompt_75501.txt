I am a high school student, and for a bioethics seminar I have been reading about how fertility clinics handle the embryos they create. One feature of the whole arrangement keeps bothering me, and I cannot tell whether it is a genuine contradiction or just loose talk on everyone's part.

Here is the practice as I understand it. A clinic fertilizes a batch of eggs, transfers one or two of the resulting embryos, and freezes the rest. Before treatment begins, the patients sign a form choosing in advance what is to happen to the leftovers: discarded, donated to research, or donated to another couple to implant. Embryos are graded under a microscope, and the ones judged to look poor are set aside. And yet the very same institutions, and the same patients, treat each stored embryo as one particular child. Clinics label vials by patient and keep obsessive chain-of-custody records so that the right embryo goes into the right uterus. Parents describe a discarded embryo as a child they lost rather than as a chance they no longer have. And in February 2024 the supreme court of the U.S. state of Alabama held that a frozen embryo counts as a minor child under that state's wrongful-death statute, so a clinic that destroys one may be sued as it would be for the death of a born child — a ruling that caused several clinics to pause treatment altogether. Meanwhile most other jurisdictions handle the accidental destruction of stored embryos as a matter of property or contract, and courts asked to settle disputes between separated partners generally decline to order an embryo implanted at all.

What picture of the relation between a person and their biological beginnings does this practice presuppose? In particular, does treating that vial as that child require that a person's identity is fixed by the specific gametes they came from, so that a different sperm or egg would have produced a different child rather than the same child under different circumstances? Please reconstruct the implicit view as precisely as you can, and then separate what the practice strictly needs from what it merely sits comfortably with — the record-keeping and grading may only require that embryos are physically distinct objects, while the grief and the wrongful-death ruling may require much more. Say where the practice is philosophically underdetermined, so that rival accounts of identity would license it equally.

Then tell me whether the parts of the practice presuppose consistent things. Does the wrongful-death ruling cohere with grading out and discarding surplus embryos, with donating embryos to strangers, and with counseling that reminds a couple they can always make more embryos next cycle? Is that a conflict of underlying commitments or only of vocabulary?

Finally, I would like the practice tested against two things I could not reconcile with it. Identical twins develop from a single fertilized egg, so one and the same sperm-and-egg origin apparently yields two people; and any one of the hundreds of millions of sperm in a sample might have fertilized the same egg, which makes me wonder whether the practice needs a sharp answer about which sperm is the one that matters, or could tolerate a vague one in which a gamete differing by a single gene would still have produced you. Does the presupposition survive these cases? And if it did not — if who a person turns out to be were not tied to the particular gametes that made them — what specifically would have to change in clinic procedure, in the consent forms, in the liability rules, and in the way parents are counseled after a loss?
