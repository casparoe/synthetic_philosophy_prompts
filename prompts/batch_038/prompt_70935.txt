i run a reading group that's mostly AI grad students with a couple of philosophy people mixed in, and next week's session is the frame problem. i want to open it with a poem instead of my usual throat-clearing summary, and i'd like it to be a sestina — mostly because the form is already the argument. six end-words, recycled through every stanza in that fixed rotation, no way to admit a seventh. the poem literally has nowhere to put everything-not-mentioned-stays-put, which is the one thing you cant write down without writing down the world.

the material: that fable Dennett tells about the bomb left in a room on a wagon, and the three robots they send in, three different failures. the first hauls the wagon out and never once considers what rode along on it. the second was rebuilt to deduce the consequences of its acts and is still mid-derivation — wall colour, wheels still round, that sort of thing — when it goes up. the third was rebuilt again, this time to filter out the irrelevant consequences, and never gets back through the door because it's still enuemrating what will remain unchanged. if you happen to remember the nicknames from the essay, use them; if you don't, don't guess — the three failure modes are what i actually need, and i'd rather have those right than the labels.

what i want the form to do:

- first robot gets stanzas 1 and 2, second robot 3 and 4, third robot 5 and 6, and the envoi is the bomb: all six words crammed into three lines, landing together instead of one per line, so everything the third robot was still listing becomes true and irrelevant at the same instant.
- let the end-words be the entire vocabulary. something like room, wagon, bomb, door, list, plus one you choose — a word that can carry both change and staying-the-same would be lovely but don't force it. the rotation should do real work: the same word coming back around each stanza meaning something slightly different, so you can feel three minds on the same six facts and none of them able to close.
- never print the phrases frame problem, qualification problem or ramification problem. someone who knows the material should be able to tell which robot is which purely from how their two stanzas treat the end-words — the one that never asks, the one that asks and can't rank, the one that ranks and can't finish. someone who doesn't should just get a good and slightly horrible little poem about a room.
- no internal rhyme required, no meter requirement, lines somewhere around ten to twelve syllables, don't be precious about it. keep it to the sestina and nothing else — no title explanation up front.

then after the poem, three or four lines on your own confidence: which of the three failures you're sure you've rendered correctly, which one you suspect you've blurred or merged with another, and the single line you're least sure about and why. and if you bent the story to make the form work — moved the blast, let a robot further along than the fable does, swapped a detail for a better end-word — say so flatly, because i do not want to find out in front of the group.
