ok so I'm a third year and I'm TAing the discussion section for our intro Indian philosophy class — prof asked me to run a 50 min optional review session the friday before the midterm, probably 18-20 people, and honestly half of them skimmed the readings and nobody has any Sanskrit. We just did the pramāṇa unit: Nyāya's four, plus the whole fight about how many you should accept and why other schools think you can cut the list down (Cārvāka's one, the Buddhists' two, Mīmāṃsā's six with postulation and non-apprehension tacked on).

I want a game where they feel the pressure of that fight instead of memorizing a table of numbers for the exam. The thing I want them to walk away seeing is that the tradeoff bites from both directions: adding a pramāṇa is expensive because you owe an account of why it's irreducible, and cutting one is expensive because you actually have to reconstruct the knowledge from your smaller toolkit rather than just claiming you can.

My half-baked version: each team is dealt an accepted list, every pramāṇa on it costs them points each round as a parsimony tax, then I read a case card where somebody comes to know something and the team has to explain how the knowledge arose using only what's on their list. Other teams can challenge, and if you can handle their case with your shorter list you take the points. Please improve that or throw it out entirely.

What I need: full rules and a scoring scheme (no real money, I've got index cards and a whiteboard, thats it), the case cards written out in language a sophomore can parse, a minute-by-minute timeline that actually fits in 50, and a debrief that maps what happened onto the real historical positions.

For the cards please include the standard wild ox one, a case meant to force postulation, one about knowing an absence, a memory case, and — this is the one my section got weirdly heated about — a case where someone gets a fact from a chatbot and the fact turns out to be right. I want that one to be load-bearing: is the bot an āpta, does trustworthiness attach to a person or to a process, and what happens to the expectancy/fitness/contiguity stuff we did on sentence comprehension when the sentence gets produced with nobody meaning anything by it. Also add one that messes with the Cārvāka team specifically, since "you only get perception" sounds like a free win right up until someone asks whether they can even perceive that as a cow rather than a patch of brown.

Two more asks: tell me what'll go wrong the first time I run it and how to referee it (I'm betting someone games the tax by declaring a huge list and then just never losing coverage points), and include at least one card where the scoring rewards a philosophically bad move, with a note on how to name that in the debrief. And be frank about what the game cant reproduce — my guess is it's the requirement that a pramāṇa has to produce a cognition that's genuinely new to the knower, which is why memory gets excluded, but I might have that wrong.
