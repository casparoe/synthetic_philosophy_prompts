I work as in-house counsel and philosophical adviser to Meridian Indemnity, which writes "continuity policies": if a policyholder ceases to exist in the course of an elective persistence procedure, the death benefit is paid to the estate; if the policyholder persists, the policy rolls over to the continuant. Disputed claims go to a three-member Continuity Arbitration Panel that is bound by its own prior rulings unless it can distinguish them, and I sit as its non-voting adviser. Next month I have to circulate a bench memo, and I would like you to act as a specialist in the metaphysics of personal identity who has done consulting work for adjudicative bodies of this kind — someone comfortable reasoning forward from a track record of decisions rather than backward from first principles. Please think carefully and work in stages rather than answering in one leap: begin by brainstorming, as it were on scratch paper, the full space of candidate decision rules that could generate the Panel's record, including at least one rule you expect to eliminate; only then narrow. Assume the two voting members who will read my memo are intelligent but not philosophers, so define any term of art you use in a clause. I do not want a bibliography, a reading list, or citations — I can look things up myself — and I would rather you flag any place where you think my summaries are too thin to support the inference you are drawing than paper over it.

Here are the seven decided matters, in chronological order. The Panel's written grounds are typically a sentence or two, so much of the operative rule has to be reconstructed. (1) Destructive scan: whole-brain destructive scanning, the original destroyed at the moment of scanning, the printed continuant activated ninety minutes later, no overlap. Ruling: did not persist; benefit paid. (2) Verified duplicate with delayed destruction: the same scan, but the original was kept alive and fully conscious for three weeks while the activated continuant was interviewed, confirmed the original's memories and projects, and was accepted by the family; the original then died of the condition that had prompted the procedure. Ruling: did not persist; benefit paid; the continuant was separately denied standing to take over the policy. (3) Suspended interval: no copying and no replacement of any kind — total cessation of brain function and metabolism under deep suspension for eleven months, then revival with memory and character intact. Ruling: persisted; policy rolled over; the Panel's stated ground was that the interval made no difference because it was the same organism throughout. (4) Gradual prosthetic replacement: neuron-scale synthetic equivalents substituted at roughly four percent of cortical tissue per month over two years, the patient continuously conscious and ambulatory the whole time, and — the Panel noted that this mattered — every excised component incinerated as it came out, under the surgeon's standing protocol. Ruling: persisted. (5) Single-stage hemispheric replacement: the left hemisphere replaced by a synthetic functional equivalent in one operation, the patient conscious before, during, and after, the excised tissue destroyed. Ruling: persisted. (6) Momentary diagnostic twin: a mapping procedure produced a duplicate that existed for eleven seconds and was terminated; the original continued unaffected. Ruling: the original persisted; the Panel observed that the transient copy was never a candidate continuer, having been terminated before it could act, remember, or claim anything. (7) Standing backup, never activated: a non-destructive whole-brain template was archived at the policyholder's request and never activated; the policyholder died of unrelated causes nine years later. Ruling: ordinary death, benefit paid; the Panel remarked that an inert archive is not a rival.

The new matter is a first for the Panel. A policyholder underwent the same gradual prosthetic replacement as in matter 4 — identical rate, identical continuity of consciousness, no scanning, no duplication at any point — except that this surgeon did not incinerate the excised tissue. Every component was placed in viable suspension, and fourteen months after the procedure concluded the stored tissue was successfully reassembled into a functioning body at a second facility. The reassembled person has the policyholder's memories up to the first morning of the procedure and none of the intervening two years, has the policyholder's fingerprints, scars, and dental work, and has filed to be recognized as the continuant. The synthetic person has unbroken memory and character across the entire period, has been running the policyholder's business without interruption, and is supported by the policyholder's spouse, who describes the reassembled claimant as "a relative who missed the last two years." Both are alive and both claim the policy; the estate, consisting of the policyholder's adult children from an earlier marriage, argues that neither is the policyholder and that the original ceased to exist at the first replacement. I need four things from you. First, which candidate rules are consistent with all seven precedents and which does the record already exclude — stated precisely enough to yield predictions rather than as moods, and paying particular attention to whether matter 3's suspension is compatible with a purely psychological rule and to what single notion of candidacy could explain matters 4, 6, and 7 together. Second, predict the Panel's verdict on the new matter under each surviving rule, and then recommend the verdict that best coheres with the Panel's own record, identifying which of the seven is the closest analog and saying explicitly what work the analogy is doing. Third, if the record genuinely underdetermines the outcome, say so plainly and tell me what principle the Panel would be adopting by deciding either way — in particular whether it would be committing itself to the view that a person's persistence can turn on facts about a storage warehouse, and whether matter 3 or matter 4 represents the Panel's deeper commitment, since I suspect they cannot both be treated as bedrock. Fourth, propose one further hypothetical matter, cheap enough to bring before the Panel as a declaratory question rather than a live claim, that would discriminate between the surviving rules, and state what each rule implies about how that matter should come out.
