I would like a pantoum on the underdetermination of theory by evidence: five quatrains, twenty lines exactly, with the interlocking rule observed strictly — lines two and four of each stanza recur as lines one and three of the next, and in the closing quatrain the third line of the opening stanza returns as its second line while the opening line itself returns as the final line, so that the poem ends on the words it began with.

Let the two recycled lines be the evidence: a pair of observations stated so flatly that any account of them can host them. The fresh lines of each quatrain should then supply a rival that fits those observations without strain — a lawlike reading, a reading that saves the appearances by revising a background assumption about the instrument or the auxiliary hypotheses, a reading that preserves every prediction while positing an entirely different furniture for the world. The accumulation is to be felt rather than announced; no line may contain the word underdetermination or the phrase empirically equivalent.

The form's ideal is that a repeated line's sense shifts while its wording stays identical, so exploit that: the same two observations should come to mean something different in each stanza purely by virtue of the theory now surrounding them.

The turn belongs in the fourth quatrain, and it must be this turn rather than the easier one. Do not let the poem's menace rest on the observation that infinitely many curves pass through finitely many points; that is a logical truism and it is not what makes the thesis interesting. The menace should be that every rival the poem has managed to name was generated from inside the framework that also generated the evidence, and that the history of science suggests the serious rival is usually the one nobody has yet conceived. A reader who works in philosophy of science should be able to see the merely formal version of the thesis and the historically grounded version held apart, and should be able to say which of them the poem is betting on.

Twenty lines, followed by a note of no more than seventy-five words identifying which lines carry the evidence, where you placed the turn, and what the closing repetition is meant to show.
