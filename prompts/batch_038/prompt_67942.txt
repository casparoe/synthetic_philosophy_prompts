ok so — I TA a first-year philosophy-of-rationality seminar, 24 students, one 75-minute section, and I need a live version of Newcomb's problem that they actually play instead of hearing about. Context for why I care: I was a smug two-boxer for years and flipped about six months ago, and I still cant really articulate what moved me, so I don't want to lecture them toward either answer. I want them to run into it.

The constraint I keep hitting: the dilemma only bites if the predictor feels trustworthy, and any predictor I can build in a classroom is either (a) me, which is obviously suspect, or (b) some rule they reverse-engineer in ten minutes, which dissolves the whole thing into gaming. So design around that. Two ideas I'd like you to work with: the prediction has to be sealed before anyone chooses, in a way they can physically inspect and still not read — give me the concrete mechanism, envelopes, a locked box, whatever, something I could actually set up this weekend. And I want two rounds: one where the predictor is genuinely opaque to them, and one where it's deliberately a dumb visible rule, so they feel the difference between not being able to beat it and not being able to beat it while knowing exactly why.

Deliverable: exact procedure with timings for the 75 minutes, the materials list, a payoff scheme in points rather than real money that they'll nonetheless care about (I control maybe 10% of their particpation grade, that's the only genuine stake I can offer), and a debrief that maps specific moments from the room onto the two competing lines of reasoning without me just writing both formulas on the board. Also tell me straight what predictably goes wrong the first time it's run — I'm already bracing for someone accusing me of rigging it and someone else announcing their choice out loud to intimidate the predictor — how to handle those in the moment, and which feature of the puzzle a room of 24 undergrads fundamentally can't reproduce, i.e. the part I'm going to have to supply by hand.
