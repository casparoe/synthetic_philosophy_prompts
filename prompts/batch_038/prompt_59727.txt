Still buzzing from a three hour argument with my friend and I cant settle it in my head, so: tell me where this thing breaks, assuming it breaks at all.

It was about luck egalitarianism. Here's the chain she walked me through on the walk home.

(1) Luck egalitarianism says an inequality is unjust iff it reflects brute luck rather than the choices of the people it affects.
(2) But every choice is itself the product of stuff nobody chose — genes, parents, the neighborhood you grew up in, which desires you happen to find compelling.
(3) So there's no layer of "choice" sitting underneath the luck; dig anywhere and you hit brute luck again.
(4) So by the view's own criterion every inequality reflects brute luck, and luck egalitarianism collapses into flat equality.
(5) Except it can't collapse into flat equality, becuase the intuition that got the view started in the first place is that it's unfair to make the saver pay for the gambler when they both began with the same stake — and flat equality does exactly that.
(6) So the view needs a distinction it can't ground and can't do without. Luck egalitarianism is either empty or self-refuting.

I couldn't find the exit last night and I still can't this morning. Worth knowing: I want to keep (2). I really do think choice is shot through with luck and I'm not interested in buying libertarian free will metaphysics to save a theory of justice. So if your answer is "just reject (2)", I want to hear why that isn't refusing to engage with the worry.

So: which step is the bad one, and why did it look innocent going in? Then price the repairs for me — what does Dworkin's hypothetical insurance market have to pay to block (3)→(4), what does the Cohen-style talk about chosen versus unchosen expensive tastes have to pay, and what does somebody like Anderson give up by conceding the conclusion and jumping ship to democratic equality instead? And separately: is (1) even a fair statement of the view, or did she already load the dice by writing it as a biconditional?
