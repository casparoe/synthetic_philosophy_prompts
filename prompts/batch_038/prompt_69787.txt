I'm trying to work out what my evidential standard actually is for the why-are-some-countries-rich debate, case by case, because I suspect it's incoherent. Here are my gut verdicts.

North and South Korea: totally convincing. Same language, same peninsula, same starting point in 1945, then an order-of-magnitude gap. No geography or culture story survives that. East and West Germany: also convincing, though I might just be counting Korea twice.

Nogales, Arizona vs. Nogales, Sonora: same valley, same climate, same ancestry, split by a fence — and I find this one much weaker. I keep suspecting the fence itself is the variable (one side sits inside a rich country's legal system and gets its transfers), which isn't really an institutions claim at all.

Botswana vs. Zimbabwe: I refuse to give the institutions camp credit here. Diamonds, plus Seretse Khama happening to be the guy who turned up at independence and Mugabe happening not to. Luck and individuals.

Hispaniola: I lean the other way and accept a physical story — rainfall shadow, mountains, deforestation — even though the colonial histories differ hugely too.

China since 1978: massive growth without anything I'd call inclusive institutions. I treat this as a puzzle the institutions people owe me, not as a refutation.

Argentina, among the richest countries in the world in 1900 and then not: "the institutions decayed" feels satisfying and also feels like it cant be wrong, since any later disaster can be relabelled as decay after the fact.

Cross-country regressions instrumenting institutions with colonial settler mortality: oddly persuasive to me, even though I don't believe the exclusion restriction — settler mortality is a proxy for disease environment, which is still there.

Culture explanations: I wave them off. Except I do think norms about petty bribery get transmitted and stick, which sounds like culture.

Tentative principle of my own: a comparison counts only if the two sides differ in impersonal rules that were already in place before the divergence, and where the difference can't be traced to one person's decisions or to a windfall.

Does that capture the pattern? Give me the principle, or a small set of them, each with the case that motivates its clause; tell me what it says about a comparison I didn't list, and which of my verdicts it forces me to give up. And say what would change your mind about which verdict to drop, and where your reasoning is most likely to fail.
