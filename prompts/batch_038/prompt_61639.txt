You are a political philosopher who has spent fifteen years designing and running experiential teaching sessions on distributive justice and social policy, and who has also spent time doing welfare-rights advice work. You are unusually good at the specific craft of building a game whose rules, rather than whose facilitator, deliver the insight. Think carefully and work through the design problem before you write the final activity.

**The setting I am supplying.** A third-year undergraduate seminar in political philosophy, 24 students, one 110-minute session in a room with six tables of four, a whiteboard, a projector, and a modest printing budget. No participant laptops or phones are required. I have a further session the following week for the theoretical reading, but the activity must be self-contained and must land inside these 110 minutes. The students are broadly sympathetic to the welfare state in the abstract and have almost no contact, personal or professional, with any benefit-claiming process.

**What I want them to come away seeing.** Not that conditionality is cruel, and not that fraud is or is not widespread — both of those are conclusions they could reach from an armchair. I want them to have *performed* the impossibility. Specifically, they should discover through play that the line between the genuinely unable and the merely unwilling is not a fact about claimants that a carefully drafted rule can track, but a residue of three things they themselves will have set: how much verification resource exists; which kind of error the political sponsor of the system prefers to absorb; and whether a claimant can be made to disclose the very thing that would exonerate them. And I want them to run into the perverse loop in which pressing harder on fraud degrades the information the system has to work with.

**Structural requirements.**

1. *The failure must be load-bearing, not incidental.* If the activity's punchline can be produced by participants simply playing badly, arguing poorly, or misreading a card, it does not work. Sorting must fail because of the design. State explicitly, for each of the three mechanisms below, why competent and well-intentioned players cannot avoid it.

2. *Drafting phase.* Before any group meets a claimant, each table writes its own operational conditionality rule and sanction schedule, in checkable terms, from a constrained menu you provide (allowable condition types, evidence formats, sanction magnitudes and durations) plus a verification budget it must allocate. Give me the template, the menu, and one worked example of a badly specified rule to prime them against vagueness. Groups must not be allowed to write a rule that simply says "reasonable effort" — force them to specify what the caseworker looks at.

3. *Hidden-type cards.* Full printed texts for at least twelve claimant cards. Each has an observable file — what a caseworker sees without spending anything — and a hidden truth on a fold-over or reverse side. Calibrate the deck so that a substantial share of hidden truths are precisely the sort of thing the groups' own rules would accept as good cause if it could be evidenced, but which the budget cannot reach before the decision deadline. Include at least two cards where the hidden truth is adverse to the claimant and leaves no trace in the observable file; at least one where the claimant has done everything correctly and is sanctioned anyway by a rule artifact such as a threshold cliff or an evidence-format requirement; and at least one where two cards interact (a couple, or a claimant and an informal carer) so that the unit of assessment itself does damage.

4. *Verification economics.* Caseworkers hold a small number of checks — an employer letter, a phone call, a home visit, a GP note, a jobcentre attendance record. Each costs budget and yields partial and sometimes misleading information. Give me the yield table: what each check type returns for each card type, including the cases where it returns nothing or returns something misleading.

5. *Asymmetric scorecard and a minister.* Caseworkers are scored for correct decisions, penalised for wrongful sanctions, and penalised more heavily for missed fraud. The weights are not theirs: design a "minister" role — one participant, or you if you prefer — who holds the weightings and changes them between rounds under visible political pressure. Print the scorecard. The minister should be given a private brief with instructions that are not villainous, only myopic.

6. *Endogenous disclosure.* Make it rational for some claimants to withhold the fact that would exonerate them, and make disclosure itself risky — escalation if the disclosure is rejected, or being recorded as having made a false statement. Then, in a short paragraph, tell me the equilibrium you expect: which cards go silent, and how the observable file consequently looks to the caseworker. This is the mechanism I most want to work, so be precise about the payoffs that generate it.

7. *Appeal stage.* Sanctioned claimants choose whether to appeal; the award is withheld pending the outcome and appealing carries a real cost. A tribunal with better information hears only those who come forward. The room must then compute an appeal-success rate, and you must explain to me — for the debrief, not for the participants — why that number is a systematically biased measure of first-instance accuracy, and in which direction.

8. *Aggregation and reveal.* Specify exactly which numbers go on the board, in what order, and who writes them: fraud caught, fraud missed, genuine claimants sanctioned, appeal-success rate, verification units spent per correct decision. The hidden cards are revealed only after the numbers are up. Tell me what the reveal should look like physically, since that moment is doing much of the work.

9. *Round two.* A shock that breaks whatever the most successful group has built — a budget cut, or a surge of claims after a large local employer closes. Tell me honestly whether round two fits in 110 minutes; if not, give me the cut-down version and say what is lost by the cut.

**Also give me:** a minute-by-minute timeline; a printable materials list with the full text of every card, sheet, and slide; facilitator script only where scripting genuinely matters (the opening pretext, the reveal, and the transition into debrief), since I want to improvise elsewhere; at least five predictable failure modes, each with the intervention to deploy *at the moment it starts* rather than after the fact — including the loud student who enjoys playing a fraudster and skews the room's sense of base rates, the tribunal that turns merciful once it has seen hidden information and thereby contaminates the next round, and the strong group that drafts a rule it believes is watertight and then gloats; a follow-up question to use when a group insists it would simply have written a better rule; a short variant I could run in a 50-minute session if the room runs late.

**Two things I want handled with unusual care.** First, a frank section on what this activity cannot reproduce — the points are not money, the claimants have no history and no waiting, the randomness makes hardship look like luck rather than structure, and, most awkwardly, by turning conditionality into an optimisation problem with a scorecard the activity itself performs the move that the rhetoric of the undeserving poor performs: it relocates a political and moral question into a technical one. Tell me how to name that in the debrief without turning the last twenty minutes into a lecture that undoes everything the first ninety achieved. Second, a safeguarding note: in a group of 24 at least one student has claimed benefits themselves or watched a parent do it. Build me an opt-out that does not mark the person out, and tell me which roles you would refuse to allocate by lottery.

**Housekeeping.** Begin with a planning section of 250–400 words in which you list four candidate insights this activity could be built to deliver, choose one as the spine, say what gets demoted to a passing remark in the debrief, and identify the single most likely way your design fails to produce that insight together with the structural feature that prevents the failure. Then give the activity. Write in markdown with clear headings, in polished prose rather than bullet fragments where the reasoning matters, and aim for 1,800–2,600 words. Everything must be self-contained and immediately usable: do not give me a bibliography, a reading list, or any citations, and do not refer me to sources I would have to go and find. The words *deserving*, *undeserving*, *conditionality*, *workfare*, and *sanction* must not appear on any participant-facing card, sheet, or slide until the debrief; the opening pretext should be administratively neutral and should not tip the moral hand. Participants should collide with the problem before anyone names it.
