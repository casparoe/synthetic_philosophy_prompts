**DRAFT: "The Moving Floor: A Size-Dependent Critical Level and the Repugnant Conclusion"**

**1. The problem.** I take welfare to be measured on a scale on which 0 is the level at which a life is exactly neither worth nor not worth living, and 50 is an excellent life. Let A be a world of ten billion people, all at welfare 50. Let Z be a world of ten trillion people, all at welfare 0.5, so that their lives are still worth living but only barely. Under a total view, A is worth 5 × 10¹¹ and Z is worth 5 × 10¹², so Z is better: this is the repugnant conclusion. Following Parfit, the argument runs through a mere-addition step — adding a large group of people with lives well worth living is not worse than doing nothing — plus transitivity, plus a trade-off step. I want to know where to block it. Two errors I have tried to avoid. The first is saying that Z is bad because the people in it are badly off; by stipulation they are not badly off, their lives are worth living, and if they were miserable the puzzle would collapse and would not be interesting. The second is retreating to an average view, which does make Z worse than A but implies that it is wrong to bring into existence a person with a very good life whenever that person would fall below the current average, and that seems to me clearly false.

**2. The Diminishing Returns Principle.** My first proposal is to value a population as the sum of f(w) over its members, where f is increasing, strictly concave, and f(0) = 0 — for instance f(w) = √w. On f(w) = √w, A is worth about 7.07 × 10¹⁰ and Z about 7.07 × 10¹², so concavity narrows the ratio but does not reverse it for this pair. Still, I think the direction is right, because f rises ever more slowly as welfare grows: the contribution of each additional unit of welfare shrinks, so there comes a point beyond which piling on more people cannot add enough value to overturn A. Diminishing returns in quality should therefore discipline quantity.

**3. The Moving Floor.** Concavity alone is not enough, so I add a critical level: a life contributes f(w) − k only if it clears a threshold. My proposal is that the threshold is not a constant but moves with the size of the population. Let c(n) = 5 + n / (2 × 10⁸), and cap c(n) at 40. Then value is Σ (wᵢ − c(n)). For A, n = 10¹⁰ gives c = 55, capped to 40, so A is worth 10¹⁰ × 10 = 10¹¹. For Z, c = 40 and each life contributes −39.5, so Z is worth about −3.95 × 10¹⁴. Z is far worse than A, and the repugnant conclusion is blocked. The cap is needed: without it, c(10¹⁰) = 55 would exceed the welfare of the people in A and make a world of ten billion excellent lives worth less than an empty world, which is absurd. I also think the moving floor avoids the conclusion that it would be better for some people to suffer horribly than for further people to live lives worth living, because that conclusion depends on holding the threshold fixed while populations grow, and mine does not.

**4. Two supporting claims.** First, because the floor moves with the number of people, no individual's contribution to value is settled independently of how many others there are. I take this to capture the person-affecting insight, that value has to be about how things go for particular people rather than about impersonal totals. Second, because I block the argument at the mere-addition step — on my view adding a large group of people at welfare 5 is not neutral but positively bad once the population is large — the continuum route to Z, which trades small amounts of quality for large amounts of quantity step by step, never gets going either.

**5. Conclusion.** The repugnant conclusion is not a discovery that population ethics is impossible. It is a discovery that a threshold is needed, and that the threshold should be indexed to population size.
