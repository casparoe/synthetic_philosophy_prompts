Last night a friend and I argued until nearly two in the morning about Frankfurt-style cases and the principle of alternative possibilities, and by the end we had talked our way out of the familiar examples and into a variant I have not seen discussed anywhere — which is why I want it written up carefully rather than argued about across a kitchen table again.

Let me describe the variant as precisely as I can, since the details carry the whole weight.

Black wants Jones to kill his business partner by poisoning her, and he is prepared to guarantee the outcome. The device he implants does not monitor Jones's deliberation: it reads no neural precursor and responds to no sign, symptom, or flicker of hesitation. It works on the action itself. Jones's plan requires a temporally extended act — he must hang an infusion bag containing the poison on the stand beside her bed and let it run to completion. The device is set so that a fixed interval after Jones begins the act, his capacity to break off is disabled: from that point he cannot take the bag down, cannot call for help, cannot stop. If he never begins, the device does nothing. As it happens, Jones decides on his own, hangs the bag, and stays until the infusion is finished. He never attempts to abort and would not have attempted to.

The wrinkle is the length of the interval — call it the abort window — and I want the essay to work through a family of cases that differ in nothing else:

- **Window A:** five minutes. Jones begins, and for five minutes he retains an unimpaired ability to remove the bag, wake the household, or telephone for an ambulance; the device then locks him in for the remainder.
- **Window B:** five seconds.
- **Window C:** fifty milliseconds — the window is genuinely open, but it is shorter than the time required to form and execute any intention to abort.
- **Window D:** zero. The lock engages at the instant the act begins, so there is no window at all.

My friend's position is that only D is a Frankfurt case, and that A, B, and C are ordinary situations in which the agent had alternatives and used his own will — so they refute nothing and are merely padding around the interesting example. He also holds that the actual sequence (what Jones does, why he does it, how it unfolds) is identical across all four, and that since moral responsibility tracks the actual sequence the verdict on Jones must be identical across all four; the shrinking window is therefore cosmetic. On his view, PAP is refuted by D and untouched by my slider.

My position is that the slider cannot be cosmetic, because it shows that possession of a *robust* alternative — one the agent could actually have used, and whose use would have made a moral difference — is a matter of degree, whereas the Frankfurt-style argument requires it to be a matter of kind. In A, Jones plainly has such an alternative: stopping would have saved the woman and would have made him far less blameworthy than the man who completes the poisoning. In D he has none. If there is no principled cutoff between them, the Frankfurtian cannot say which nearby cases are counterexamples to PAP and which are not; if there is a cutoff, he must say where it lies and why, and every candidate looks arbitrary. I also think he is caught by his own actual-sequence claim: if A and D receive the same verdict precisely because their actual sequences coincide, then robust alternatives have been shown to make no difference to responsibility whatsoever — a far stronger and far less defensible thesis than the denial of PAP.

Write an essay of roughly 2,500 to 3,500 words adjudicating this dispute. It should:

1. State PAP and say what a Frankfurt-style case must accomplish in order to refute it — not merely that the agent lacks alternatives, but that he lacks them *and* is clearly responsible — and identify where in the family that burden is actually met.
2. Take up directly whether desisting mid-action counts as doing otherwise in the sense PAP requires. The objection is that by the time the window opens Jones has already formed his intention and begun the attempt, so what the window offers is not an alternative action but an alternative completion; the reply is that voluntary abandonment of an attempt is among the best-attested loci of diminished blame we have. Assess both, rather than noting both.
3. Decide whether the windows in A, B, and C are robust alternatives, nominal ones, or something in between, and whether robustness admits of degrees. If it does, explain what becomes of a principle stated with a threshold, and whether a graded notion can bear the weight PAP asks of a binary one.
4. Confront the dilemma usually pressed against cases of this shape: either the trigger condition is causally sufficient for what the agent does, in which case he seems unfree in the sense incompatibilists care about, or it is not, in which case he seems able to do otherwise after all. Explain whether a device triggered by the agent's own overt behaviour rather than by a neural precursor escapes that dilemma, inherits it, or merely relocates it.
5. Address the sorites charge head on. Decide whether a vague boundary between responsibility-preserving and responsibility-destroying interference is fatal to the Frankfurtian, fatal to me, fatal to both, or fatal to neither, and argue for the answer.
6. Test the coherence of the case itself. A device that removes the capacity to stop while leaving the capacity to continue may not be switching off one ability but conflating two that were never distinguishable; if so, say what repair is available and whether the repair changes any verdict.
7. End with a verdict on PAP, stated and defended. A conditional verdict is acceptable provided you specify the condition and argue for it; an evenly balanced non-conclusion, or an observation that the cases underdetermine the question, is not.

Write for readers who know the terrain but have never encountered this case. Define your terms on first use and keep the prose tight. Do not build a survey of the literature into the essay and do not append a bibliography — I want the argument, not a report on who has said what. Where you think one of us is simply wrong, say so plainly, and say what turns on it.
