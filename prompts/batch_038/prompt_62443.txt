can you write me a parody of one of those consumer-electronics quick-start guides + warranty booklets, where the product is a pet — specifically a spaniel — sold by a startup that is totally sincere about being Cartesian? as in: the animal is a machine, full stop, no soul, and the marketing department thinks thats a *selling point* (no moral-status paperwork, guaranteed not to have an inner life that disappoints you, and the price reflects it).

format it like the real booklets: a what's-in-the-box list, a getting-started section, a feature spec table, a troubleshooting FAQ, a warranty section with the exclusions in small print, and one sidebar. around 700-800 words.

the important thing is that the jokes come from getting the philosophy right rather than from winking at the reader. so:

- the spec table should list language support as zero, and explain it the way Descartes actually does — the thing makes plenty of sounds, but it never arranges signs to express a thought, and that arrangement is the only thing that would show there's a mind in there
- the FAQ should have entries for whining when you step on its paw, begging at the table, and the guilty look after it chews something. every one of them gets answered without ever conceding feeling, using the reflex-and-disposition machinery and the point that a sensory response can run through a body with nobody home to be aware of it
- the diagnostic procedure for checking whether your unit is a counterfeit (i.e. secretly ensouled) should be the genuine Cartesian test: a real automaton never acts against the arrangement of its own parts, so behaviour that exceeds the machine is the giveaway
- the warranty exclusions should do the reductio work — what a company that sincerely believes all this would refuse to cover, and what that lets the reader infer about how it thinks of its customers

one more constraint: at least one passage has to make the Cartesian case sound genuinely strong instead of strawmanned. someone who actually likes the view should read the spec table and think "yeah, ok, that IS the argument." if the whole piece is just a hit job it stops being funny.

also, somewhere in the fine print there should be a pending class action from customers who say their unit looked them in the eye and asked for help — and the company's response should be the correct Cartesian one, calm and slightly condescending, not a panic.

no footnotes, no bibliography, no real book titles. and if you want to nod at the dissident faction of customers, their founding text is by a Renaissance essayist who wondered whether he was his cat's pastime rather than the other way around — trust the reader to catch that without naming him.
