You are to act as a philosopher-economist with genuine working expertise in interactive epistemology — common knowledge, common priors, agreement and no-trade results — combined with enough feel for applied social science to know which formal niceties actually bite once you put forty real people in a room and ask them for numbers.

Background, so that you can judge what matters. A charitable foundation has given my small institute eighteen months and two researchers — one with a measure-theoretic background in the philosophy of probability, one a survey methodologist — to produce a memo on the preconditions of 'productive' disagreement. I came at this from the forecasting side: years of prediction tournaments, calibration threads and the usual arguments about whether rationalists converge, long before the foundation came calling. The object of interest is a concrete institutional design. It is a standing citizens' assembly of forty members, drawn by lot and serving three-year terms, which deliberates a fixed list of policy questions over repeated rounds. Three features of the design strike me as philosophically load-bearing, and the memo has to say which of them genuinely threatens convergence and which are harmless:

(1) Members revise privately, then publish a posterior rounded to the nearest five percentage points. The rounding is deliberate: it is meant to discourage false precision and to stop members having their exact figures quoted back at them in the press.

(2) Publication is simultaneous but lagged. In each round you see everyone else's rounded figure from the previous round, never their current one, and no one announces anything else — no evidence, no arguments, only numbers.

(3) A substantial minority — on some questions perhaps a quarter of the room — cannot supply a single number at all. They are ambiguity-averse in something like the technical sense: for a certain class of questions, mostly long-run technological risk, they will say only that some outcomes are worse than others, and refuse a point probability. The design has to accommodate them rather than score them as non-responsive.

Now the request. I do not want a survey of the literature on agreeing to disagree, and I do not want the well-worn rationalist-community riff about how Aumann's theorem sets an ideal that mere humans systematically fall short of; I have read that many times and it does not help me allocate two researchers for eighteen months. What I want is a defended research agenda: which open questions, in and immediately around the agreement-theorem neighbourhood, most directly determine whether this assembly converges, and in what order we should prioritise them.

Please think carefully, and do not begin with the ranking. Start with a short brainstorming section — bullet points are fine — in which you generate candidate questions, including ones I have not thought of, and say in one line each why you are keeping or discarding them. The candidates should cover, at minimum: which assumption of the theorem the rounding violates, and whether iterated announcement of coarse-grained figures can converge at all; what the one-round lag does to the iterated structure, as opposed to a design in which everyone sees current figures; whether anything like agreement is even definable when part of the room holds non-additive or incomplete beliefs, and what the right convergence target would be if not equality of posteriors; and whether the absence of a common prior across forty strangers is a real obstacle here or a red herring. Then give the ranked agenda, five to seven items, and for each: the question stated precisely enough that someone could tell whether they had answered it; why it is upstream of the other items rather than downstream of them; how tractable it is for a philosopher and a methodologist in eighteen months with no new mathematical machinery; what would count as progress (a proof, an impossibility result, a simulation, a measurable prediction about this assembly, a conceptual distinction that dissolves the problem); and what evidence or argument over the next year would move it up or down the list.

Two further things, which matter as much as the ranking. First, name two or three questions that are fashionable or intrinsically interesting in this area and that we should nonetheless refuse to fund, and say what it is about the design that makes them irrelevant. Second, identify the single question which, if answered in the worst possible way, would make the whole assembly design indefensible: I need to know the crux the funder is implicitly betting against, and I would rather hear it now than in the final report.

No citations, no bibliography, no reading list — I will supply those myself. Refer to named results and standard positions only where you are confident of them, and otherwise describe the argument rather than the attribution. Where you think one of my three design features has a consequence I have not anticipated, say so plainly: I would rather be corrected than reassured.
