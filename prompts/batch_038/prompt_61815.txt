I'm doing some informal advising for a foundation that wants to fund roughly four people for two years on the theory side of citizen assemblies, juries, and expert panels — basically everything that hangs off the Condorcet jury theorem. The catch is they're allergic to math-for-math's-sake. Every deliverable has to be something a panel organizer, a constitutional reformer, or a court administrator could actually use when making a design choice: how big the group should be, whether to let people talk before they vote, whether to weight votes by measured competence, whether to require a supermajority, whether to break the question into sub-questions and aggregate those instead. What they want from me is a defended research agenda, not a survey of the literature, and definitely not a reading list — I'm not going to be able to chase down citations and I'd rather you just reason it through.

Here's the candidate pile, roughly as I've scribbled it. (1) The correlation problem: the theorem's headline result assumes votes are independent conditional on the state of the world, but real people talk, read the same newspapers, and get recruited from overlapping pools, so errors line up. How much correlated error can you swallow before majority accuracy stops climbing with size, and is there a non-asymptotic version a practitioner could actually plug numbers into? (2) Weighted voting: if you can estimate individual competence from past accuracy, does competence-weighted majority beat simple majority, or does the noise in your competence estimates eat the whole gain? (3) Deliberation: does structured discussion push error correlation up or down, and can that be modeled at all rather than just hand-waved at? (4) Beyond binary: what survives when there are more than two options, or when you aggregate rankings, or when you split a decision into premises and a conclusion? (5) Asymmetric error costs: for some decisions a false positive is way worse than a false negative, which is where supermajority and quorum thresholds come in — is there a principled way to set them from the cost ratio? (6) Strategy and preference falsification: what happens when voting isn't sincere, either from incentives or from social pressure? (7) The framing objection, which a friend of mine thinks is the real issue: the theorem presupposes there's a procedure-independent right answer, and for value questions rather than factual ones that presupposition may just be false, in which case the whole apparatus is being misapplied whenever assemblies decide policy rather than diagnose a problem.

My own instinct — possibly wrong, tell me so — is that (1) is the crux and most of the others are downstream of it, since if you can't bound correlated error then the jury-size and weighting answers are all artifacts of an assumption nobody believes. But I can see the counterargument that (1) is intractable for a group of four in two years and (5) is the one with real near-term policy bite. So: rank these for this specific funder, defend the ranking explicitly in terms of how much other work depends on the answer, how tractable it is for people with formal training but not a full-time probability theorist, and how much it would change what an organizer does on Monday morning. Flag any that you think are overrated, any that are secretly the same question wearing two hats, and any important open question I've left off the pile entirely. Then tell me what would move things up or down — what result in the first year, or what failure to make progress, should make them abandon a line and reallocate. I'd rather have three well-argued priorities than seven hedged ones.
