Take two agents who share a common prior, update strictly by conditioning, and communicate fully publicly — but whose message space is coarse: each may announce only which of ten pre-specified intervals its posterior falls into, never the posterior itself. Aumann's agreement theorem does not bite on them, and they can exchange messages indefinitely without converging.

Using that case as a calibration point, rank the following candidate explanations of persistent disagreement among honest, well-informed, non-strategic people from most to least explanatory, and assign each a rough share of the disagreement it accounts for (the shares may total more than 1 if you judge the explanations to overlap):

1. Coarse message spaces of the kind described above.
2. Priors that were never common, having been assembled from largely non-overlapping evidence.
3. Non-Bayesian responses to ambiguity — hedging, imprecise credences, or inertia in place of conditioning.
4. Private information that is small in magnitude but amplified by the common-knowledge structure of dialogue.
5. Divergent carvings of the state space, so that the agents' events do not coincide even when their priors nominally do.
6. Growing unawareness: agents who come to entertain possibilities for which they had no slot at the outset.

For each, give one or two sentences of justification and a note on what observation would tell you that you had weighted it too heavily. I want a committed ordering: no ties, no declining to compare items that seem close, and an explicit statement of which explanation you think is doing most of the work in actual public disagreement, even where the evidence for that judgment is thin.
