So, three weeks from now I'm giving my first proper workshop talk, at this weird interdisciplinary thing on self-reference in decision-making. The audience is maybe 25 people and it's an unholy mix: half are ML/alignment types who signed up because the call for papers mentioned Newcomb, and who think about agents in terms of "your decision procedure is the thing being predicted" rather than anything causal; the other half are decision-theory philosophers, including a senior Jeffrey-style evidentialist who im pretty sure thinks all talk of causation in this area is a category error, a game theorist from the econ department who will absolutely tell me I've reinvented some folk theorem without knowing the proof, someone who works on imprecise probability, and a person who's published on the idea that Death in Damascus is really a story about an agent who can't coherently hold the credences the story stipulates. My slot is 25 minutes plus 15 of Q&A and I'm on third, right after a talk about prediction markets, so the room will already be warmed up and slightly irritated.

My thesis is deliberately annoying. I claim that Death in Damascus — the Gibbard–Harper case, where a man meets Death in the marketplace, Death looks startled, the man rides all night to Damascus, and Death is waiting for him there explaining that he only looked startled in Baghdad because their appointment was in Damascus — is not a counterexample to evidential decision theory at all, because EDT doesn't actually deliver a verdict in the case. Sketch of the argument: write the evidential calculation as a function of the agent's own credence state, then ask which states are stable, where stability means the recommendation you get survives conditioning on your having made it. In this case there's no stable state; every candidate oscillates, because leaning toward Damascus makes Baghdad look like the safe bet and leaning toward Baghdad makes Damascus look safe. So the right description isn't "EDT gets it wrong," it's "EDT is silent here, and the case is a genuine no-win that shouldn't be booked against any theory." I then build a ratifiability constraint out of this, and I claim — this is the part my advisor says will get me eaten alive — that the same machinery gets you one-boxing in Newcomb without ever mentioning causation. I also have a variant case of my own that I think is the best thing in the paper: instead of choosing, the agent commits in advance to letting a fair coin pick between the two cities, on the grounds that a genuinely stochastic act is evidentially transparent in a way a deliberate act isn't, so the oscillation stops and a fixed point comes back. Everybody still dies, obviously, but the point is that the theory regains a verdict, and that's supposed to show my diagnosis was tracking something real rather than just being a way of shrugging at the case.

What I need from you is a prep sheet, not a defense of my view. Give me the eight or so objections this particular room will actually raise, ordered by how much damage each one does to the talk rather than by how interesting it is, each labeled with who raises it and phrased in that person's own register — the ML people and the evidentialist will not say the same thing even when they're gesturing at the same problem, and I want to hear both versions. For each objection: a reply I can deliver standing up in about two minutes, a blunt note on where that reply is softest, and whether the person will be satisfied by a formal answer or is really after a philosophical one. Then tell me what I should concede in the first five minutes so I don't get ambushed with it in minute 38, which single reply absolutely has to land for the talk to survive, and the question I should be praying nobody asks. I have a suspicion about what that last one is — something like "if you're conditioning on the agent's own act, you've assumed the deliberation is already settled, which is exactly what the evidentialist denies" — but I'm probably wrong and I'd rather you find the worse one. Also flag any objection where the honest answer is "I don't know yet," because I'd much rather say that than try to bluff past the game theorist.
