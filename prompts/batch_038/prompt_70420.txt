I'm part of a small group at a teaching hospital that reviews patient-facing decision aids — the one-page sheets that spell out the odds of a procedure's outcomes so people can make an informed choice. Several of us have been reading about risk-weighted expected utility, the family of views on which a rational agent's decision is governed not by the plain expected utility of her options but by a weighted sum in which the probabilities themselves are transformed by a fixed risk function, so that risk attitudes enter separately from, and in addition to, the shape of her utility function. One member of the group, a statistician, is a deflationist about the whole enterprise. His line is that no such theory can ever genuinely disagree with ordinary expected utility maximization: either the apparent divergence is really just concavity in the agent's utilities, which plain expected utility handles on its own, or the probabilities are ambiguous rather than sharp, which is somebody else's problem, or else the choice can be reconceived as a sequence of repeated decisions, in which case the risk-weighted verdict loses. He also thinks cases like this are always cooked: the numbers get chosen so that the desired verdict falls out, rather than the verdict emerging from independently plausible utilities and probabilities. He has offered to retract his view if we can hand him a single well-built case that survives all three escapes at once. That is what I want from you.

Build me that case, under the following specifications. It should be a realistic decision by an ordinary adult — medical, occupational, or financial, with stakes a normal person could actually face, expressed in money, health, time, or functioning. No urns, no dice, no decks of cards, no lotteries, no betting slips, no casino games, and no gambling devices of any kind; no science fiction, no exotic physics, no demons, and no predictor better than a shrewd human. The decision must be genuinely one-shot and irreversible, with no opportunity for hedging, insurance, or repetition, and that must be part of the situation rather than a stipulation bolted on. The probabilities must be sharp and the agent's credences well defined, so that ambiguity, vagueness, and ignorance play no role at all. State the utilities over final outcomes explicitly, and make them linear enough, or at least transparent enough, that no part of the disagreement can be attributed to outcome-level concavity. Name the risk function you are using in closed form, keep it fixed throughout, and check your own arithmetic.

Within that setting I want two divergences running in opposite directions. First, a choice that risk-weighted expected utility endorses but that plain expected utility ranking forbids — the risk-weighted agent is permitted, or required, to take an option with the lower expected utility. Second, a choice in the same direction of stakes but going the other way: plain expected utility favors an option that risk-weighted expected utility rejects. If you can manage it with a single scenario presenting the agent with two related decisions, do that; if it needs two scenarios sharing an agent and a risk function, that is acceptable. In neither case may the verdict be stipulated: the same numbers, fed into plain expected utility, must produce the opposite ranking, and I want to see the sums.

Then do the audit. Show the arithmetic for each theory on each decision, with the outcomes, utilities, probabilities, transformed probabilities, and both totals laid out so a reader can check them. Say which structural feature of the case is doing the work, and show what happens when that feature is removed — does the disagreement vanish, and does it vanish in favor of one theory or merely into agreement? Give me the two strongest attacks an expected utility defender would launch on the case: the redescription attack, in which the utilities are said to be defined over a richer set of outcomes than you admitted, so that concavity alone explains the pattern; and the dynamic attack, in which the choice is said to be improperly framed as a single isolated decision. Say for each whether it succeeds here, and if it does, say what has to be true of the world for it to succeed. Finally, tell me whether your case is description-relative in a damaging way — whether a coarser or finer partition of the outcomes changes the verdict — since that is the one objection my statistician has not yet raised and I would rather meet it than be ambushed by it. I'll supply any references myself, so don't include a bibliography; I just need the scenario, the demonstration, and your honest assessment of how much weight the case can bear.
