A friend of mine reviews novels for a living and calls himself a relativist on the strength of 'beauty is in the eye of the beholder.' His argument runs: when he writes that a book is exquisite he is only recording how it struck him, and when I write that it is tedious I am only recording how it struck me, so our verdicts cannot conflict, and a reader who picks up his review therefore learns nothing about the novel itself. I suspect a slide somewhere in that chain but cannot name it.

Could you set out the distinct claims the slogan might be making — a bare observation that tastes in fact vary; a denial that there are any facts about beauty; a claim that beauty is a disposition to please, so that reports from the right sort of beholder are reports about the object after all; a claim about whose sensibility sets the standard and whether some judges are better placed than others; a claim about what testimony can and cannot transmit, including whether it might convey that a work merits admiration without conveying the seeing of it — and grade each as a platitude, a substantive thesis worth defending, or simply false? Then tell me which reading, if any, supports each step of his argument, and which step breaks even if the slogan is granted outright. Please give me this as a numbered menu I could bring to him, rather than one favoured interpretation defended at length.
