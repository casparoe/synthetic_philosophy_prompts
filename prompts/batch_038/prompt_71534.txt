I am grading a midterm for a third-year philosophy of probability course. The question below was worth 20 points and students were told to answer all three parts in roughly 400 words. I have not yet formed a view of the script I am sending you; I want an independent, part-by-part assessment rather than a confirmation of an impression.

**The question**

(a) State the single-case objection to a long-run frequentist analysis of probability.
(b) Explain how a propensity interpretation is meant to answer that objection.
(c) Say whether the propensity interpretation in fact escapes the objection or inherits it, and argue for your verdict.

**The student's answer**

*(a)* The long-run view analyses the probability of an outcome as the limit of its relative frequency in a long, ideally infinite, series of repetitions of the same conditions. But we routinely attach probabilities to events that are not, and could not be, members of such a series: that this particular atom decays within the hour, that this coin toss lands heads, that a certain election is overturned. On the analysis a claim about such a case is either meaningless or else a roundabout way of talking about the series. So the account cannot make sense of a large class of probability claims we plainly make and that science plainly needs. It also gets the relation between evidence and content wrong: the limit is not something any finite series could establish, so every probability claim we test is tested only approximately, and the exact value of a probability is never observable at all.

*(b)* Popper's response is to treat single-case probability as primitive and objective. A propensity is a disposition of the whole repeatable experimental arrangement — not of the individual event — to produce a given outcome, as solubility is a disposition of a lump of salt. Propensities are objective features of the world but are not frequencies; frequencies are rather the evidence by which we estimate them. Because the propensity attaches to the single case, we can now say what we meant about this atom and this coin: the claim reports a real disposition of the physical set-up that would be manifested here, true or false independently of anyone's opinion. This also explains convergence: the propensity calculus entails that observed frequencies approach the underlying propensity with probability approaching one, so convergence is a consequence of the interpretation rather than a stipulation built into it.

*(c)* The propensity interpretation escapes the objection, and decisively. First, the reference-class problem — that a single event falls under many classes with different frequencies, so which frequency is its probability looks arbitrary — arises only for a view that defines probability in terms of frequencies. Since propensities are not so defined, the problem cannot arise for them: the propensity of this atom to decay is fixed by the physical arrangement itself, and there is no question of choosing between classes. Second, subjectivism cannot escape either, and in effect concedes the point. De Finetti's representation theorem shows that for an exchangeable sequence an agent's probabilities are uniquely determined by the limiting relative frequency, so at the limit the subjective and frequentist accounts coincide; the theorem therefore establishes that the limit is an objective single-case fact about the world, which is what the propensity theorist says. Propensities supply the metaphysics both rivals lack: they say what makes the frequency claims true rather than merely reporting them.
