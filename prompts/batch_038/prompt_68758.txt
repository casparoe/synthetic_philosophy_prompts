I'm a novelist, not an academic, so bear with me — this is a bit of a strange request.

Last month I published a novel set in Florence over roughly fourteen months in the mid-1490s. In six weeks' time I'm giving the opening address at a historical fiction festival in a converted chapel, about 400 people who paid to be there, and the organisers have given the whole weekend a theme: *Making the Past Real*. I want the talk to argue with that title without being rude to the people who chose it or the people who came expecting to be told they'd get to visit the Renaissance.

The problem is that the book was built on the opposite of the theme, and I dont know how to say so out loud to a room that's mostly there for the costumes.

The book had a forbidden-words list. No character says "Renaissance," "medieval," "the Middle Ages," "the modern world," or "science." Nobody uses "art" as a category — they're painters and goldsmiths and stonecarvers and they'd think I was mad. Only one character ever says "Italy" and he's showing off, and I'm honestly not sure I was right to let him.

Then two things cut against the clean version of that rule, and they're the interesting bits.

One: Florence ran its year from the 25th of March, the Feast of the Annunciation, which meant its dates lagged a year behind ours every January and February. So the last chapter is dated the 24th of March 1494 by the characters and the 24th of March 1495 by every reader, and the last line is about the New Year coming tomorrow — and tomorrow, for once in the whole book, we mean the same year. That convergence is the final page and I have no idea how to talk about it without sounding like I'm doing a party trick. Also the day there began at sunset, not midnight, so when someone in my book says "two hours of the day," he means two hours after dark, and the date I've printed for him is already the next one by your reckoning.

Two: the three-part scheme — ancient, middle, modern — was worked out by a Florentine, Bruni, in the 1440s, and the phrase for the middle stretch first turns up in Latin in the 1460s as *media tempestas*, a middle season. So my characters are alive in the city where the word for their period is being coined, decades before their own births, and not one of them would recognise it as being about him. I gave it to a single character: Baccio, a second-rate copyist who's spent his life making fair copies of other men's books, and who tells his wife once that they're in the middle season. He means his marriage. He means his career. He means nothing whatsoever to do with history.

So the talk has to hold both ends. The three academic historians sitting in the front row — one of them writes about periodisation specifically, I'm told — will not let me get away with "periods are just stories we tell afterwards," because they know perfectly well that printing, plague, prices and a bunch of other things really did change and really did change together. And the other 397 people will walk out if I give them a seminar. I need it to concede that something real happened without conceding that the 1490s was a room anyone standing in it could have named.

What I want you to write:

- The speech itself, first person, in my voice, timed for about fourteen minutes read aloud — so roughly 1,700 words, short sentences, places to breathe.
- One joke that lands with the ticket-buyers and the front row at the same time, and a marked beat after it where I can shut up and let it happen.
- Never once the words "construct," "constructed," "construction," or "discourse." If you need that idea, say it in plain speech like a person.
- At most two proper names and at most two dates in the whole thing. This is not a lecture on where the vocabulary came from, and I'm not going to be able to field follow-up questions about it.
- One genuinely uncomfortable admission, in the middle rather than at the end, about a cheat I allowed myself. Specifically: there's a scene where a printer's apprentice holds a finished sheet and feels that his trade is new and that newness has a shape, and that is my own hindsight wearing his clothes — and it's the scene readers keep quoting back at me, so I'd have to be lying if I said I regret it. Make that ambivalence real rather than resolving it.
- Don't mention the book's title more than once, and don't end on the book. End on the room: on the fact that everyone in it is inside a period whose name hasn't been chosen yet, and on what Baccio would make of that if he could hear me. My mother will be there and she hasn't read a word of it, so the ending has to work for her.
- Put one sentence in italics — the one I could fall back on in the Q&A afterwards if a historian asks me something I can't answer.

And the thing I'm most worried about: I don't want it smug. If it comes out sounding like a man who's outsmarted the past, it's failed, however clever it is.
