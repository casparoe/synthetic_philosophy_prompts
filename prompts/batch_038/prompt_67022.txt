I am not an academic. I am a retired civil engineer who reads on his own, and for the past year I have been working through the quarrel between Christopher Browning and Daniel Jonah Goldhagen about who the perpetrators actually were. Browning's Hamburg reserve police battalion: middle-aged men, most of them not Party fanatics, ordered to shoot Jews in a Polish village in the summer of 1942, told by their commander beforehand that anyone who did not feel up to it could step aside, and only a handful did; his explanation runs on conformity, careerism, deference to authority, and the way killing becomes routine once it is organized. Goldhagen's reply: the question is not why these men conformed but why so many were willing, and why so much of what they did went past what any order required, which he traces to an antisemitism that had become German common sense. I follow the shape of that dispute reasonably well, though I would not claim to know either book page by page.

I have also read Bernhard Schlink's The Reader several times and seen the film, and I keep being struck by the fact that the novel seems to be assembled out of the very pieces the two historians argue over, while refusing to fit either picture. I would like a serious reading of it through that debate, and I want to tell you what I have noticed so far, because I suspect some of it is wrong and I would rather be corrected than flattered.

First, Hanna's way in. She is a tram conductor who is about to be promoted, and she volunteers for the SS instead, because the promotion would expose the fact that she cannot read. Browning has a category for careerism, but this is careerism in reverse: she flees upward. Goldhagen has a category for conviction, and the novel gives her none that I can find. Neither book, as far as I can tell, has a slot for the person who enters the killing apparatus in order not to be found out about something else.

Second, the selections. The satellite camp sends a fixed number of women to Auschwitz each month to keep the population down, the other guards let Hanna do the choosing, and there is testimony that she singled out certain prisoners to read aloud to her in her quarters. That looks exactly like Goldhagen's favorite kind of evidence — conduct in excess of the order, with an element of personal arrangement — and yet the novel's explanation for it is a hunger for stories and an inability to read them herself. I do not know what to do with an act that has Goldhagen's shape and neither historian's motive.

Third, the trial. At the church, nobody unlocked the doors; the co-defendants say Hanna was in charge and wrote the report afterward; Hanna accepts authorship rather than produce a handwriting sample, and takes a life sentence where the others take a few years. What the novel thereby stages, I think, is the evidential predicament underneath the whole dispute: we never observe motives, only accounts, produced decades later by people under threat, contradicting one another, and shaped by who has the most to gain from seeming ordinary. But then the novel cheats, because Michael knows the secret and so can know Hanna's motive in a way no historian ever can. And he withholds it from the court. I cannot decide whether that is a portrait of proper restraint about other people's inner lives or of a lover protecting himself.

Fourth, the absences. No character in the book states a belief about Jews, and the victims exist as two witnesses and a memoir. I have read that critics accuse the novel of exculpating Hanna by substituting a private shame for a political crime. My suspicion is that this accusation is itself a Goldhagenite accusation — that you cannot write about these killings at all without antisemitism in them, and that if you replace it with a secret about literacy you have written a love story where a history belonged. Tell me whether that is a real argument or a confused one.

So, concretely, what I am after:

Which historian would have to concede something if Hanna were a real case in his data, and what precisely would he have to give up? Where does my reading break down — and I mean this sincerely: if your view is that Schlink's book is about the second generation, about law and shame and reading, and that using it as a test case for Browning against Goldhagen is a category mistake, say so plainly and then tell me what is still worth doing with it. What does the novel put at the disposal of this argument that argument alone cannot supply — something, I think, about what it is like to be a person who does the thing without holding the belief, and about whether that description is a mitigation or just a further fact to be explained. And two or three scenes I could point a reading group to, most of whom saw the film and half of whom read the book, with what to look for in each.

Three constraints. Please do not quote verbatim from either historian or from the novel; I cannot check quotations and I do not want invented ones, so describe and paraphrase instead. No bibliography or reading list — I am not in a position to chase sources. And assume I know the plot, so do not spend words summarizing it; where the film and the novel differ in a way that matters to this question, say so rather than blending them.
