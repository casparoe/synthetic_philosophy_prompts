I'm a second-year in a philosophy of biology program, and this semester I've been sitting in on the metaphysics seminar to fill an elective, and I've come away feeling like the composition debate has been parked in the same spot since before I was born. Everyone knows all the moves: composition is either unrestricted, or never happens, or happens exactly when the activity of the xs constitutes a life; every restriction proposed in between gets pressed with the charge that it's arbitrary or vague; then somebody says the whole dispute is just quantifier variance, somebody else says there's a perfectly joint-carving way to quantify and the debate is substantive after all, and nobody budges. And half the seminar time goes to statues and lumps, ships of Theseus, cats with tails removed.

What got me worked up is that in the biology seminar across the hall we handle cases where the artifact intuitions don't seem to buy you anything at all. A Portuguese man o' war is a siphonophore: a gas-filled float, stinging tentacles, feeding polyps, reproductive bits, all of them genetically identical zooids physically fused together, none of which can survive alone. Is that one object or a few thousand? Pando in Utah is a single root system with something like 47,000 aspen stems, regularly described as the heaviest living thing on the planet, but parts of it have been cut off from each other by roads and fires and half the stems are dying. A lichen is a fungus and an alga growing as one thallus, and it turns out there's often a third partner, a yeast, tucked into the outer layer. Physarum is the mirror image of all this — one cell, thousands of nuclei, spread over a square meter or more, an organism by any biological count but with barely any of the internal part-structure that makes a cat obviously composite. Coral colonies, biofilms, your own gut microbiome. Nobody has a pre-theoretic intuition about these, because nobody has ever met one in ordinary life, and that seems to me like it might be exactly the problem with the debate.

So here's my hypothesis, and please push hard on it rather than just agreeing: the composition debate is stalled because the two sides aren't disagreeing about the same question. The metaphysician is asking what there is strictly speaking, in a sense that's supposed to be free of anyone's interests, but the cases being used to get traction are all human artifacts, where our interests and conventions are precisely what's doing the work — so the argument is running on data that's already contaminated, and the biological cases show there may be no interest-free answer sitting there to be found. On that reading the dispute is largely verbal, or really a disguised disagreement about which explanatory job composition is supposed to do. Is that right? Or is it a substantive disagreement that's merely hard — a hidden empirical question about biological individuality, a clash over which cases count as data, or bedrock intuitions about arbitrariness that neither side can argue for beyond a certain point? I want the diagnosis argued through what each side actually says (the arbitrariness argument, the vagueness move, quantifier variance and the objections to it, brute composition, the idea that mereology is ontologically innocent), not a tour of the positions — I've read the tour versions. And then, given whatever diagnosis you land on, tell me what would count as progress: a new distinction, a formal result, some empirical finding about colonies and clones, or an explicit decision to change the question. If your honest answer is that no progress should be expected, I want to know why, and what you think the people who keep working on it are actually doing.
