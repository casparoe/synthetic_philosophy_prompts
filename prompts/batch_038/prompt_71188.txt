I am a software engineer by training and trade — I work on distributed systems infrastructure, and my exposure to linguistics consists of an irregular reading group that some colleagues and I keep half-alive, plus several years of arguments about language models that invariably stall at the same place. Those arguments have left me with a handful of fairly firm intuitions about the claim that children come to know more about their language than the evidence they are given could support, and I would like to know what principle, if any, those intuitions add up to, because I suspect they do not add up to anything coherent. Let me say at the outset what I am not asking for: I do not want a reading list, a survey of who holds what, or pointers to papers, since I have no way of following those up. I want the reasoning laid out and the criterion stated precisely enough that I could apply it myself to a case neither of us has mentioned.

Here are my verdicts. First: I can tell instantly that from the statement the man who is tall is happy, the corresponding question is Is the man who is tall happy? and not Is the man who tall is?, and I can tell you this without being able to state the rule that decides it, without ever having been taught the rule, and — so far as I can recall — without ever having heard a child produce the bad version. That case feels to me like the paradigm of a learner getting more than the data licenses. Second: my parents corrected me relentlessly for saying things that were not true and essentially never for saying things that were not grammatical; I find that suggestive, though I could not presently say of what. Third: my niece learned giraffe from something like four encounters at a zoo and never subsequently applied it to a camel, although nobody told her what a giraffe is not; I find it very hard to see how that is less underdetermined than the question case, and yet I feel no pull at all toward saying that the meaning of giraffe is innate. Fourth: my daughter went through a months-long phase of saying goed and foots after a period of saying went and feet correctly, and I take that as evidence she had extracted a rule rather than stored forms. Fifth, and this is where I know I am being inconsistent: I believe a sufficiently large model trained on a sufficiently large corpus handles the question contrast above without anyone having built the relevant generalization into it, which suggests to me the evidence was never all that impoverished; but I also think the fact that such a model needs an enormous corpus to reach what a child reaches on a few thousand hours of noisy input is itself the strongest evidence available that the child brought something to the task. Sixth: my infant looked reliably longer at a display in which a ball appeared to pass through a wall than at one in which it stopped, and I am inclined to treat that as the same kind of case as the question one, though a friend insists the two are obviously different and I have not been able to say why.

Is there a single criterion for when a body of evidence is too poor to license what is learned on the basis of it — one that delivers all six of these verdicts, or nearly all? If there is, please state it clause by clause, say which of my cases motivates each clause, and tell me what it implies about two further questions I cannot resolve: whether the fact that much of the speech children actually hear is fragmentary, ungrammatical, or full of false starts undercuts the whole enterprise rather than merely complicating it; and whether a system that arrives at the right generalization only because its training data was vast should be described as having faced an impoverished stimulus that it overcame by brute force, or as having faced no poverty at all. If no single criterion survives, give me the two or three closest candidates and state plainly which of my verdicts each one forces me to abandon — I would much rather be told that one of them is incoherent, or that two of them cannot both be kept, than be handed a principle with six escape hatches in it. Since I have no formal background here, please define any technical term you need that an engineer or mathematician would not already possess. Finally, a formatting request: write the whole thing as continuous prose, the way one would write a long email or a letter, with no headings, no bullet points, no bold text and no tables. I find I actually read that kind of thing, whereas structured documents I only skim.
