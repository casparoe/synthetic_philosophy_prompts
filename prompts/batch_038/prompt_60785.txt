I am preparing a handout for a graduate seminar on the foundations of probability, to be distributed at the opening of our unit on indifference. The deliverable is a decision tree, not an essay: it should route a student from the raw data of Bertrand's chord problem to a named position on what, if anything, has gone wrong.

The starting point, which every student in the room already grants, is this. Take a circle with an inscribed equilateral triangle and ask for the probability that a randomly chosen chord is longer than a side of the triangle. Choosing two points uniformly on the circumference and joining them gives 1/3. Choosing a radius uniformly, then a point uniformly on it, and drawing the perpendicular chord through that point gives 1/2. Choosing a point uniformly in the interior and taking it as the chord's midpoint gives 1/4. All three procedures look like equally legitimate applications of indifference, and they disagree. The handout's job is to sort students by what they think about that disagreement, not by which label they would pick for themselves.

Please build it in the following order.

**Step one: the logical space.** Before any position is named, lay out five or six questions whose answers are independent enough to generate genuinely distinct positions but constrained enough that some combinations are ruled out. Consider at least these; keep the ones that do real work, cut the ones that do not, and add any I have missed:

- Does a probability attach to a proposition, or to a proposition together with a parameterization of the space it concerns?
- Is indifference an a priori requirement of rationality, a defeasible default, or no part of probability theory at all?
- Is a problem that specifies no randomizing procedure simply ill-posed, so that the three answers are not competitors but answers to three different questions?
- Is invariance under a group of transformations — scale and translation invariance in the chord case — a constraint strong enough to determine a unique prior, or does it merely relocate the ambiguity from the parameter to the symmetry?
- Is the trouble specific to continuous parameters, or does it equally afflict finite partitions?
- If indifference must be restricted, which restriction is principled rather than ad hoc: finitude of the alternative set, indivisibility of the alternatives, or the presence of a physical symmetry?

Mark the combinations of answers that are jointly incoherent, with a clause saying why.

**Step two: the tree.** Each internal node is one of these questions, phrased so that a student can answer it from an intuition or a commitment they already hold, without knowing where the answer leads. No node may use the vocabulary of a school of thought — subjectivist, frequentist, objective Bayesian, maximum entropy, transformation group — in the question text itself. Those words belong at the leaves only.

**Step three: the leaves.** I need seven to nine. Among them there must be:

- a Jaynes-style position on which the problem is under-specified until invariance requirements are imposed, and the prior then falls out of the symmetry group rather than from indifference applied to an arbitrary parameterization;
- a restricted-indifference position of the Keynesian kind, on which indifference binds only over finite sets of mutually exclusive, indivisible alternatives;
- a position on which the three answers reflect three equally concrete refinements of a description that is too abstract to privilege any of them — the diagnosis usually associated with van Fraassen;
- a subjectivist position on which nothing has gone wrong, because no prior was ever rationally mandated;
- a frequentist position on which the question has an answer only relative to a specified chance setup;
- an objective-Bayesian position that retains a norm of indifference but grounds it in a requirement to equivocate rather than in symmetry;
- a deflationary position on which the paradox is unresolved and shows there are no non-trivial objective priors over continuous parameters.

Add any further leaves the space demands. I expect the Jaynes-style leaf and the frequentist leaf to threaten to collapse into one another; either separate them sharply at some node, or report the collapse under Step five.

At every leaf give: a name for the position, or "unnamed" if you cannot think of anyone who defends it; two sentences stating it; its verdict on each of the three test cases below; and one sentence naming its most awkward commitment.

**The three test cases**, against which every leaf must be scored, so that students can check their own path:

1. *Bertrand's chord problem.* What does the leaf say the answer is — or that the question lacks one?
2. *The cube factory.* A factory produces cubes with edge length between 0 and 1 cm. What is the probability that a given cube's edge is between 0 and 1/2 cm — uniform over edge length, uniform over volume, or neither? Does the leaf's answer to case 1 force this answer, or can the two come apart?
3. *A discrete repartitioning.* A prize lies behind exactly one of three doors. Indifference over the doors gives 1/3 each; repartitioning into "the first door" and "not the first door" gives 1/2. What does the leaf make of that pair? This case exists to test whether the leaf's diagnosis depends on continuity at all.

**Step four: the empty cells.** Some combinations your questions permit will correspond to no leaf. List them, and diagnose each in one line: is the combination incoherent; is it coherent but unattractive, and what does it cost; or is it merely unexplored, in which case what would have to be true for it to be worth occupying?

**Step five: inseparabilities.** If two leaves take the same branch at every node, say so rather than manufacturing a distinction. Either supply an extra question that would separate them or concede that, for this handout's purposes, they are one position.

**Format and constraints.** Plain text that survives printing: an indented outline or an ASCII diagram, your choice, with node questions numbered so a student can record a path as a short string of answers. No bibliography, no reading list, no citations, and no page references; where a position has a well-known proponent, give the name only if you are confident of it, and otherwise describe the position purely by its content. Target roughly two pages.
