I'm six months into a novel and I've hit a wall on a scene, so this is a writing problem more than a philosophy problem, but I need the philosophy done properly or the scene won't work.

The book's narrator is a home health aide whose hours got slashed and who's trying to get the other aides to organize. Her brother-in-law Doug is a regional sales manager who listens to a lot of Stoic podcasts, and his whole verbal tic is 'control what you can control.' He says it at the family dinner when she brings up the schedule cuts. He says it again later, about the strike fund, in a way that's obviously doing some self-exculpatory work. My problem: right now he's a cardboard cynic, and my editor is right that the scene is too easy. I want him to sometimes be saying something true and even useful, and I want the reader to feel the exact moment the sentence changes meaning under him without anybody announcing it.

So can you lay out, as a menu I can keep next to me while I revise, the distinct precise claims that one sentence could be making? I want each reading stated as a definite thesis — what's supposed to be in the control box, what 'control' is doing (causal power? the proper object of my concern? the thing I can be blamed or praised for? the thing I'm allowed to have feelings about?), and whether it's binary or a matter of degree — and then a verdict on each: which are platitudes nobody would deny, which are substantive and defensible, which are shaky, which are just false. For every one, say whether it would actually license Doug telling her to stop going to the meetings.

A few things I especially want teased apart, because I suspect the scene turns on them:

- the strong classical version, where the only thing genuinely up to us is assent and desire and aversion, so her hours, her body, her reputation, her brother and even her own future choices all land outside the box — I think that makes the slogan far narrower than Doug thinks it is, and I want to know what happens to it under that reading. Does it become nearly empty, nearly unbearable, or something else?
- the objection from the modern revival that the dichotomy needs a middle category of things only partly in our control, and whether that patch actually rescues the dismissive use or just relocates the fight.
- the reserve-clause reading — go all out for the outcome while holding it as 'if nothing prevents it' — which as far as I can tell is the opposite of what Doug means and would make him wrong in an interesting way rather than a boring way. Say clearly whether that reading is compatible with his usage or kills it.
- the therapy/attention reading, where the sentence isn't metaphysics at all but a rule about where to point your worry, and whether that version can coexist with collective political action.
- the responsibility slide, where 'not in your control' quietly becomes 'not your problem' or 'nobody's fault,' and 'in your control' becomes 'entirely your fault if it goes wrong.'

Then tell me where the equivocations are — which specific pair of readings Doug would have to swap between mid-sentence to get from a truism to telling her to sit down — because I'd like to write the dialogue so the swap is right there on the page and deniable. And give me your best guess at which reading a person in Doug's position actually means when he says it, since that's the one I need to be able to inhabit.

Last thing: I want the narrator to say the identical sentence back to him near the end and mean something completely different by it. Which reading would be true in her mouth, and what would have to be true about her situation for it to be true? Keep it practical, no citations or reading lists — I'm not writing a term paper, I just need the distinctions sharp enough to build dialogue on.
