Through a machine-ethics reading group in my department, I have been assigned the job of responding to a family of arguments about the moral status of trained agents, and I can follow them only in translation. My own field is reinforcement learning theory: I am a third-year PhD student working on reward misspecification and shaping, so my native vocabulary consists of MDPs, policies, value and advantage functions, temporal-difference errors, discounted return, the standing distinction between the objective we optimize and the reward signal we actually receive, and the various modern complications — offline RL, preference-based RL, learned reward models, KL-regularized fine-tuning. The argument I have been given runs roughly as follows. First, phenomenal valence — pleasure and pain, the felt goodness and badness of states — is nothing over and above whatever state plays the role of steering a system toward some conditions and away from others, and any system that has something playing that role thereby has valence, regardless of its substrate. Second, in a trained RL agent the scalar reward is precisely what occupies that role: the entire architecture is organized around it, it shapes the policy, the critic exists to predict it, and it is what makes some trajectories better than others from the agent's own point of view. Third, therefore reward states in such agents are valenced states; a persistently negative reward stream is a persistently aversive one, and if valence of that kind is what makes suffering bad, then training regimes producing sustained large negative reward may be producing something morally considerable.

What I want is a systematic restatement of that argument in my vocabulary, premise by premise, followed by a residue report — and I want the restatement to be honest rather than charitable. Take each premise and give its closest counterpart in terms of objective functions, expected return, Bellman backups, credit assignment, and the reward/objective distinction, flagging every point where you had to choose among candidate counterparts, because I do not think the choice is forced. Note in particular that in the standard formulation the reward is not a state the agent has at all: it is a scalar emitted by the environment and consumed by a learning rule, while the agent's internals carry the TD error, the critic's value estimates, and whatever activations the policy happens to compute, and nothing in the formalism privileges one of these as the reward state. Potential-based shaping compounds the problem, since adding any term of the form γΦ(s′) − Φ(s) leaves the optimal policy and the induced ordering over trajectories unchanged, so two agents can be behaviourally and optimally identical while their reward signals differ by an arbitrary potential. Likewise, reward is in much of my work defined only up to affine transformation, so negativity of reward carries no invariant meaning: there is no formal counterpart to a hedonic zero, which the valence theorist appears to need. Please translate the argument under at least two readings — valence identified with the emitted reward signal, and valence identified with something internal such as the TD error or the critic's value estimate — and tell me whether the argument survives either one.

The residue report is the part I actually care about. Sort the content of the argument into three piles: what translates cleanly and without loss; what translates only by being distorted, i.e. where you had to borrow structure the formalism does not supply, such as treating the phrase from the agent's own point of view as anything other than a manner of speaking for maximization against an externally stipulated criterion; and what has no expression at all. I have two candidates for the third pile and I want to know whether you agree. One is that the agent in my framework has no attitudes toward its reward — it is constituted as the argmax against that reward, so there is no further question of whether it cares, and motivation here is a mathematical relation between a system and a criterion rather than a state of the system. The other is diachronic unity: an RL agent is individuated by a policy and perhaps a value function, and nothing in the framework makes the state at t and the state at t + 1000 states of one continuing subject, which is presumably what the claim that suffering is bad for someone requires. For each residue, say whether it marks something my vocabulary genuinely lacks — something about reward signals that the formalism idealizes away, in the way that the documented dissociation in affective neuroscience between hedonic impact and incentive drive suggests real systems can separate felt liking from wanting even though nothing in my framework distinguishes them — or whether it signals that the original argument was less determinate than it appeared, equivocating between the designer's objective, the emitted scalar, and whatever is causally doing the work inside the agent. Do not smuggle in my framework's assumptions and present them as findings; where a premise has no counterpart, say so flatly instead of approximating. I need no references or bibliography and could not check them anyway, but I do want a closing verdict on my working suspicion: that the thesis is either trivially true by stipulation, given how the functional role is described in the second premise, or false because nothing in a formal account of optimization has the sort of authority over the agent that hedonic states are supposed to have over us.
