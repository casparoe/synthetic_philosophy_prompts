I am preparing a handout for an advanced undergraduate seminar on practical rationality, and I would like help disambiguating a piece of folklore that has become load-bearing in our discussions. Last week I put the following case to the students: a department has spent six years and a little under two million dollars constructing a bespoke corpus and instrument for a research program that has yielded nothing publishable; the apparatus has no resale value and no plausible alternative use; the two postdocs trained on it have no fallback project; the donors who funded it are watching closely and will read any withdrawal as a signal about how the department treats long commitments; and, of course, the six years of failure are themselves evidence bearing on how hard the remaining problem is. When I asked whether continued funding could nonetheless be rational, one student closed the matter with the proverb "don't throw good money after bad," adding that the six years are gone whether we continue or not and therefore cannot enter the calculation at all. Several others nodded, and I found myself unable to say precisely what I objected to — which is exactly the predicament I want to remedy before the next session.

My suspicion is that the proverb is not one claim but a family of them, and that its persuasive force comes from sliding among them: something close to a tautology about unrecoverable outlays is quietly doing the work of something much stronger about what may figure in a rational deliberation. I would like you to lay the readings out as a menu rather than settling on the one you find most interesting. For each, state the thesis precisely enough that it could be assessed, making explicit the parameters the proverb leaves open — whose costs are at issue, against which counterfactual baseline the money counts as "bad," at what time the assessment is made, whether the decision is one-shot or embedded in a repeated interaction, whether it is taken under certainty or under uncertainty, and whether the objects of rational evaluation are being assumed to be outcomes or whole histories of outcomes.

For every reading in the menu, then, give me a verdict — platitude, substantive but defensible, false, or not really a claim at all — and say whether that reading, on its own, licenses the student's dismissal of my case, or what auxiliary premise would have to be supplied to get him there. I would also like to know which reading you take ordinary users of the proverb to have in mind, and where exactly the slide between readings occurs. Please address two further points directly: first, whether any of the readings depends on a contentious thesis about whether rational preferences may be sensitive to history — for instance, whether the waste of a sacrifice already made can be an object of disvalue in its own right, or whether an agent may rationally care about the fit between what she has already given up and what she now pursues; and second, the distinction I suspect the proverb collapses between an outlay's being unrecoverable and an outlay's being evidentially or strategically inert, since the informational content of six years of failure, the signal withdrawal sends to future funders, and the value of a reputation for honoring commitments all look like ways in which past expenditure can bear on a decision without ever being treated as a recoverable cost. The deliverable should be a numbered list I can hand out as-is: for each item, a one-sentence precise statement of the thesis, its verdict, and a line saying whether it supports the dismissal. You are under no obligation to vindicate the proverb — if its strongest reading is false, say so plainly — and you should write for readers who know expected utility theory but no formal decision theory beyond it.
