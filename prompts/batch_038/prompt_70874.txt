I draw letters for a living — small foundry, mostly display faces plus one variable family I've been grinding on for two years. Someone at a design school asked me to do a critique session and suggested I first write down what I actually believe about letterforms, so the students could attack the list instead of my taste. Here it is, roughly:

1. There's no set of features — no stroke inventory, no proportion, no geometry — that's both necessary and sufficient for being an 'a'. I've drawn forms that break every rule you could write down and are still unmistakably 'a'.
2. What makes an 'a' an 'a' is the way it's made: the family of transformations you can put a shape through and still get an 'a' back. Style isn't decoration poured over an invariant skeleton.
3. Identity is settled by the writing system, not by the drawing. An 'a' is whatever fills the a-slot in a system of contrasts — it's an 'a' partly because it isn't an 'o', isn't an 'e', isn't a 'd'.
4. Uptake matters but doesn't decide everything. If a whole community agreed to call one of my shapes an 'a', there are still forms that would be wrong — forms that aren't letters at all no matter what anyone says.
5. The a-slot has real structural limits. Some shapes can't fill it, and a face that fills it with those is defective, not just unconventional.
6. Legibility is not the test. Plenty of highly legible things aren't the letter in question, and alot of my display work is barely legible and still perfectly good letterforming.
7. There's no such thing as the essential 'a'. I think "what is an 'a' really?" is a badly formed question.
8. When I tell a student "that's not an 'a', that's a blob with a stem on it," I mean it as a factual claim, not a preference report.

One concrete case, because it's where I get in trouble: at the lightest end of my variable axis the 'a' loses its closed bowl entirely and collapses into basically a single curved stroke with a little foot. I still insist that's an 'a', and I think someone who disagrees is mistaken rather than differently-tasted.

So: which of these actually fight each other? I suspect a couple of pairs look bad and aren't, and one or two are genuinely bad and I can't see them. Rank the real tensions by severity, say which commitment has to go in each case and what giving it up costs me in practice — I still have to ship faces, not just win arguments — and explicitly name the pairs that are fine on inspection so I know you checked rather than flagging everything. Start with a one-line verdict on whether the package hangs together, then the ranked audit. Under 450 words total please.
