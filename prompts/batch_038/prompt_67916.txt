You are a professional moral philosopher whose specialty is the ethics of praise, blame and moral worth: what makes a right action creditable to the agent who performs it, and whether that turns on acting from duty, on acting from sympathy, or on being moved by the features that make the act right. Approach what follows as a colleague's request for a careful audit of a stated position, not as an occasion to survey a debate. Think carefully before answering.

I have drafted my own account of moral worth for a departmental workshop. Before I circulate it I want to know whether the commitments below are jointly tenable, or whether they are a collage of positions whose usual motivations pull in opposite directions. Please start with a short orientation pass: sketch, in a compact form, which commitments entail, constrain or presuppose which others, and where you expect the fault lines to lie. Then deliver the audit.

My commitments:

1. What confers moral worth on an action is the agent's being moved by the features that make it right — the child's distress, the promise she made, the danger to the person she could rescue — and not by the thought that acting this way is right. An agent whose operative motive is rightness itself strikes me as defective even when her action is correct.
2. Sympathy and natural fellow-feeling are not the only motives that can confer moral worth. In cases where inclination runs against duty, an agent who does the right thing while feeling nothing for the person she helps is not thereby less creditable than one who helps gladly.
3. Moral worth comes in degrees. Mixed motives are the norm, and an agent who acts partly from the relevant features and partly from self-interest deserves partial credit; there is no threshold below which the question simply fails to arise.
4. Moral worth requires counterfactual robustness. An action is creditable only if the agent would have done it anyway had the particular feature that actually moved her been absent, so long as some other right-making feature was present. A lucky one-off alignment of motive and circumstance is not moral worth, however good it feels from the inside.
5. Moral worth is not a matter of luck. It depends only on the agent's motivational set and on how she responds to the situation in front of her, never on features of her circumstances, her upbringing or her temperament for which she is not responsible.
6. Praise and blame are asymmetric. Blameworthiness does not require a bad motive: negligence, indifference and merely self-regarding motives can render an agent blameworthy. Praiseworthiness does require a good motive. Doing the right thing from a merely non-moral motive is not praiseworthy, though it need not be blameworthy either.
7. Deferring to someone I have good reason to trust can itself confer moral worth on my action. If my friend's judgement about what a patient needs is reliable, then acting on her advice because I take her to be reliable is a perfectly good way of being responsive to the patient's needs, and I lose no credit for failing to see those needs myself.

My verdicts on three cases:

(a) Huck Finn helps Jim escape, from friendship and fellow-feeling, and against his own sincere conviction that he is doing wrong. The action is creditable, and creditable in the way it actually happened; his false moral beliefs subtract nothing from its worth.

(b) An anaesthetist who is exceptionally careful with her own patients — and who, I stipulate, would not have been nearly so careful with a stranger she had never met — is more creditable for her care than a colleague whose scrupulousness is impartial and would have been exactly the same for anyone. Particularity is not a defect.

(c) Someone betrays a confidence out of loyalty to a friend in distress. She deserves praise for the loyalty and criticism for the betrayal, and the two assessments do not cancel each other out.

The question I expect from the workshop is whether this is one coherent position or several incompatible ones held at once. So please:

- Identify the pairs or clusters that are genuinely in tension, and rank those tensions by severity, most serious first, rather than presenting them as equally bad. For each, state precisely where the collision lies — which commitment's rationale cannot survive the other — and give the minimal revision that would remove it, together with what that revision would cost me elsewhere in the set, including in the case verdicts.
- Say explicitly which apparent tensions dissolve under inspection, and why they only look like problems. I expect at least one or two of these commitments to seem contradictory at first pass and not to be; do not report those as difficulties.
- Finally, tell me which single revision to the set would clear the largest share of the remaining trouble, and what sort of position I would be left holding if I made it.

Keep the answer to the audit. Do not turn it into a general essay on moral worth, and do not give me a reading list or citations; you may name a position or a case where a name genuinely helps, but otherwise argue from the content of the commitments themselves. Use clear headings for each tension and severity ranking, and write the whole response in British English.
