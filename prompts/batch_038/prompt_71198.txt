For a philosophy of language seminar, write a dialogue between two theorists of slurs who disagree about where derogatory force is located. One maintains that a slur contributes derogatory content to what is said, so that a sentence containing it has different truth conditions from the corresponding sentence built out of a neutral counterpart; the other maintains that the derogation is a matter of pragmatic conveyance — a presupposition, or expressively marked content, or something of that kind — which is precisely why it survives negation, conditionals and questions, never being at issue in the assertion.

Do not use a real slur, and do not name actual authors, books or papers: the two should argue from the logic of their positions and from the following invented data. In a fictional jurisdiction, the group in question is the Senn, and "vask" is a slur for them with exactly the same extension as the neutral term. Four observations are on the table:

(a) Senn hearers take offence at the utterance of "There is nothing derogatory about calling someone a vask".
(b) Younger Senns use "vask" of one another as a term of solidarity, and that use is not resented within the group, though the same words in the same tone offend when uttered by a non-Senn.
(c) A Senn woman raised abroad, who knows "vask" only as the ordinary word for her own people and is unaware of its history, is nonetheless resented by other Senns when she uses it in good faith.
(d) In the country's statute book "vask" is the only expression that picks out the class, so a Senn legislator arguing that some benefit should extend to her own people cannot avoid uttering it.

Both characters should be equally competent — no strawmen on either side — and each should press the other using the datum their own account handles worst, not merely by tallying their own successes. The exchange should contain real back-and-forth: each response should engage the move actually just made. Have them finish without agreement, but with an explicit statement of which observation, if any, would settle the dispute, and of what each concedes their view owes on (d). Roughly 800 words.
