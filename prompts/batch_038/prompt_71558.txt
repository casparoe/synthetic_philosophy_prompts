I have just finished a novel whose final eighty pages are engineered to grieve the reader, and I cannot justify that enterprise to myself by intuition alone. I want a decision tree, for private use while revising, that sorts the available answers to the question of why sorrowful art is worth making. Please build it with eight to twelve branch nodes and a leaf for every coherent position you can identify — catharsis and its descendants, the make-believe or quasi-emotion account, the view that sorrow is transmuted into pleasure by the eloquence of its presentation, mastery- or control-based accounts, the response that treats the sadness as itself a kind of pleasure, and anything else you think belongs — and mark as unoccupied any leaf no serious writer defends, saying whether it is empty because the position is incoherent, merely unattractive, or simply untried.

Three constraints govern the drafting. First, the tree must be navigable by someone who has never encountered the literature: every node should pose a concrete question about a case or a reaction, never a question about which label one prefers. Second, and this is what I most need, the nodes should keep apart three inquiries that the debate seems to collapse into one — what makes the sadness tolerable or welcome while it lasts, what makes sadness a fitting response to persons who never existed, and what justifies choosing in advance a work one knows will devastate one. A given position may address only one or two of these, and each leaf should record which it actually undertakes to answer. Third, please make at least some nodes turn on these observations from my drafts and my readers: those who wept hardest were the most emphatic that their sorrow was not a displaced version of anything in their own lives; on rereading, the sorrow came noticeably weaker and the pleasure noticeably stronger; and the death I moved offstage, so that it is reported by a servant rather than enacted, was consistently judged more devastating than the staged version. Tell me which leaves each of these observations is meant to rule out, and flag every pair of leaves the tree cannot genuinely separate — I suspect at least two positions differ only in vocabulary, and I should like to know which two, and what would have to be true for their dispute to amount to something.
