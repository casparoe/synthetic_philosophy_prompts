You are to act as a professional epistemologist and philosopher of language whose specialty is the norms of assertion and knowledge-first epistemology. Treat what follows as an audit of a stated position, not as an occasion to survey the debate.

**Context.** My department runs an annual workshop at which each candidate circulates a one-page statement of core commitments in advance, on the express understanding that the session will consist of attacking them. Mine is reproduced below. My supervisor read it and said only that she doubted it would survive contact; she declined to indicate where the trouble lies, so I genuinely do not know which of these I would abandon first, and I would rather not have that decided for me by which one I happened to list last.

**My commitments.**

(i) Assertion is governed constitutively by the knowledge norm: one may assert that p only if one knows that p; and, in the other direction, knowing that p is what entitles one to assert it. I take this to be neither a rule of thumb nor a conversational ideal, but the norm in virtue of which an act is the act it is.

(ii) Classical invariantism: whether S knows that p is settled by S's evidence and by the truth of p. What is practically at stake for S plays no role in whether S knows.

(iii) Case verdict, matched pair. Two surgeons have exactly the same evidence — self-reviewed imaging, chart, colleague's confirmation — about which kidney is diseased. In the routine case I hold that announcing "we are taking the left" to the team is plainly proper. In the second case the patient's anatomy is such that an error would be unrecoverable, and I hold that the announcement would be improper there, even though, by my own lights in (ii), she knows just as much in one case as in the other.

(iv) Case verdicts on statistical and on luck cases: a man holding a million-to-one ticket before the draw may not assert "my ticket has lost"; in fake barn country one may not assert "that is a barn." Intuitions of this kind — about which assertions are proper — are my primary evidence for (i), and I take competent speakers' sense of impropriety to be a generally reliable guide to which norm governs the practice.

(v) Belief, by contrast, is not governed by a knowledge norm. Its norm is truth, or justification, and it can be rational both to believe that one's ticket has lost and to act on that belief — bin it, stop checking — without thereby knowing it.

(vi) Assertion is the externalization of belief: to assert that p is to express one's belief that p, and the norms on assertion are inherited from the norms on the state that is expressed. This is also how I want to account for why insincere assertion is always defective; I do not want a separate sincerity norm.

(vii) Speakers need not have any conception of the norm they follow. Children and wholly unreflective adults assert properly and improperly all the same, and the inability of competent speakers to articulate reliable criteria for proper assertion is no threat to (i).

(viii) Pretense is not a violation. Asserting in fiction, supposing for the sake of a reductio, delivering a scripted line, joking: these fall outside (i), and I do not regard treating them as exempt to be ad hoc.

(ix) A lie always violates (i), since if you believe p is false you do not know p. I count this as a welcome consequence rather than a problem.

Two objections have already been pressed on me, and I suspect both are bad, but I cannot say precisely why. First, from a colleague of my supervisor: if (i) is constitutive, then a speaker who flouts it is not making an assertion at all, so there is no such thing as an improper assertion, and (iii) and (iv) describe nothing. Second, from a fellow student: (iv) and (vii) cannot both stand, since I am using the intuitions of speakers who, on my own account, have no grip on the norm as evidence for that very norm.

**The task.** Think carefully before writing. Begin with a short, deliberately unranked brainstorm — every candidate tension you can locate in the set, including ones you expect to dismiss — and only then produce the deliverable:

1. Rank the genuine tensions by severity. For each, state which commitments generate it, why they cannot all stand as formulated, what the cheapest revision is, and what that revision costs elsewhere in the list. Where a tension can be dissolved by drawing a distinction I have not drawn, name the distinction and say whether it comes free or whether it damages some other commitment of mine.
2. Say explicitly which candidate tensions dissolve on inspection, and why. Do not flag everything as equally bad.
3. Assess the two quoted objections: does either land, and are the replies available to me consistent with the remainder of the package, or does each available reply itself create new trouble given (i)–(ix)?
4. Tell me whether any of my case verdicts is one my stated principles cannot in fact deliver, and in which direction the failure runs.
5. Close with the single revision that buys the most consistency for the least cost, and state plainly what I lose by making it.

**Constraints.** Work only from what is given above: no citations, no reading list, no reconstruction of the wider literature, and no attributing my views to named authors. Where you judge one of my commitments to be doing no damage at all, say so in a sentence and move on. I want an audit of these nine items and nothing broader.
