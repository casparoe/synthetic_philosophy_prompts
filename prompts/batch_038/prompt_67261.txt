Six months ago my partner took a job in Strasbourg and we dragged our 14-year-old over here, and I have discovered that Alsace is possibly the weirdest place in Europe to try to understand French secularism from. The 1905 separation law apparently doesn't apply in these three departments, the state still pays clergy under something dating back to Napoleon, four faiths get recognised and Islam isn't one of them, the President of the Republic apparently has a hand in naming bishops, my daughter's official timetable had an hour of religion class on it that we had to formally opt her out of, and she gets Good Friday and the 26th of December off. Meanwhile my sister in Ohio, who has never been to France, forwards me articles about the headscarf law and the abaya thing from a couple of years back and is furious on my behalf. So I'm getting both versions at once and neither of them matches what's in front of me.

Here's what I actually want to pick apart though, and it's a phrase, not a country. At the parents' meeting last week "religion is a private matter" got used at least twice, and I'm now fairly convinced it was doing two incompatible jobs each time.

One: a mum asked whether the canteen could offer a pork-free option alongside the regular menu, and a dad shot it down — religion is a private matter, so the school has no business organising its kitchens around anybody's dietary rules, whatever you eat is something you sort out at home.

Two: the same meeting, later, about a trip to the Haut-Kœnigsbourg, where a woman who wears a headscarf offered to chaperone and someone objected. And the defence of her was *also* religion is a private matter — what she believes and what she puts on her head is nobody's business, the state has no right to police it, of course she can come.

My brother-in-law in Toronto uses the identical sentence to argue the opposite of both, roughly: religion is *not* a private matter for the people who have it, it's an identity you live in public, and that's exactly why a decent state accommodates it instead of asking people to check it at the door.

So: lay the readings out for me as a menu, each one stated precisely enough that I could tell whether a given person accepts or rejects it, and labelled — platitude, substantive but defensible, or just false. I've got a vague hunch that there's a jurisdictional reading (the state has no competence over belief or salvation) and a visibility reading (religion belongs behind a door, not on the street) and that people slide between them, with the first one being near-universally accepted and the second doing all the controversial work, plus some descriptive reading about religion having faded out of public life that's doing quiet work as evidence. But find the others, there are clearly more. Then tell me which reading each of my three people was leaning on, whether any single reading makes all their uses consistent, and — the part I keep going round in circles on — which reading, if any, the canteen dad actually needs to get from "it's private" to "so no pork-free option", or whether he's smuggling in a seperate premise about what equal treatment even means. And if you think the phrase isn't making a claim at all in some of these mouths, that it's a badge or a conversation-ender, say so and tell me what it's ending. Not looking for a history of laïcité or a survey, I just want the sentence cracked open.
