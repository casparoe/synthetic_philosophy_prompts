I'm a third-year philosophy PhD student working on coercion, consent, and the ethics of influence, and there's a configuration I keep running into — in the literature and in real life — that I don't have a clean label for. I'm not after a lit review. I want to know whether this specific thing has a name, and if it doesn't, whether you can coin one that cuts at the joints.

The pattern: someone wants you to do X, but instead of giving reasons for X they strap a flattering description of *you* onto the request, so that the only available way to turn it down is to reject the description. A supervisor circulates a draft with "you're the only person in this department who'll actually read this properly" — now declining isn't declining a task, it's announcing that you don't read things properly. A fundraiser writes "you're not the kind of person who scrolls past this." A teacher tells a kid "you're someone who finishes what they start."

What I find interesting is where the pressure sits. It isn't aimed at the merits of X at all — nobody ever argues X is worth doing, and often the requester would concede it isn't. It's aimed at the cost of saying no, and that cost is that refusal has to be articulated in a vocabulary that indicts the refuser. Which makes it self-concealing in a way threats aren't: to object you either deny the compliment (which sounds like false modesty, or worse) or accept it and comply. There's no phrasing of the complaint that doesn't make you look like the problem. And note it's frequently benevolent — the teacher's line probably worked, nobody was deceived, the kid may well endorse it in hindsight. That's part of why I'm stuck: it doesn't look like the standard cases of manipulation, where the worry is that you got your rational capacities bypassed. Here your values and self-conception are exactly what's being engaged, and engaged pretty directly.

So: is this a variety of emotional blackmail? Of flattery? Of the loaded-question / complex-question move, since the request smuggles in a presupposition you can't deny without denying something about yourself? Does it drop out of Nozick-style accounts of coercion as worsening someone's alternatives, given that the "worsening" here is entirely self-reputational and nobody's announced a penalty? Or is it its own thing that the manipulation literature just hasn't carved out? Give me the nearest established concepts, say precisely where each one stops fitting, and if nothing fits, name it and give me the boundary cases — what clearly would and clearly wouldn't count, including whether the benevolent teacher version belongs inside the category.

Two things on format: keep it under 350 words, and lead with a single line stating your verdict on the naming question before you do any carving. No references or suggested readings, please — I'll find those myself.
