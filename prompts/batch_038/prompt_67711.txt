**Progress as residue: why the cyclical and linear philosophies of history aren't rivals**

The standard setup puts two families of account against each other. Cyclical: Polybius, Ibn Khaldun, Spengler, Toynbee. Linear: providential history, Hegel, Marx, and the vaguer modern confidence that things accumulate. The families are usually presented as competitors, on the grounds that history either has a direction or has a shape that repeats. My claim in this paper is that they aren't competitors at all. The linear story is what a cyclical process looks like when you plot only what the cycles leave behind.

Start with the engine, because all four cyclical theorists turn out to be running the same one: a group's success destroys the capacities that produced the success. Polybius, in Book VI, has monarchy harden into kingship when rule stops being merely the strongest and starts being accepted; kingship decays into tyranny when the ruler's heirs get the position without the struggle that made it acceptable; the aristocrats who kill the tyrant found an aristocracy that decays into oligarchy by the identical route, heredity; and democracy decays into ochlocracy when a people who no longer have to defend anything get used to being flattered instead. Ibn Khaldun's version is more sociological. Asabiyyah — group feeling — is manufactured by the harsh conditions of the periphery, it is what lets a peripheral group take the centre, and the centre then dissolves it: luxury, and the substitution of paid soldiers and clients for kinsmen. He thinks a dynasty has a natural lifespan the way an individual does, about three generations. Toynbee has civilizations growing while a creative minority actually solves problems and is followed voluntarily, by mimesis, and breaking down when that minority stops creating and starts compelling — becomes a dominant minority — the failure he traces to its worship of its own former self. And Spengler's culture passes into civilization at the moment it stops asking questions because it believes it already has the answers.

Call that shared engine inheritance without predicament. The second generation receives the position, the wealth, the institutional form, but not the predicament that made those things functional. It's a mechanism, and it comes with a clock, because positions are transmitted by succession while predicaments are transmitted by experience, and succession is faster. That is what I think Ibn Khaldun is doing with his forty years to a generation: he isn't numerologising, he's dating the lag.

Once you see the clock, the quarrel changes shape. Ibn Khaldun's unit is a dynasty, three generations. Polybius's is a constitution, decades. Spengler's is a culture, something like a millennium. Toynbee's is a civilization, longer still — he counted nineteen or twenty-one fully fledged ones, plus abortive and arrested cases. These men are not disagreeing about the same object; they are describing differently sized objects with the same engine. So the real question isn't whether history cycles or progresses. It's which units have a clock, what the clock runs on, and what happens to the stuff the unit was sitting on when the unit dies.

That last question is where my thesis lives. What survives a unit's death is not its form but its substrate. Ibn Khaldun's dynasties succeed one another in the same cities, collecting the same taxes in the same language through much the same apparatus. Toynbee says it outright: the universal state that a dominant minority builds to hold itself together becomes the scaffolding for the universal church of the internal proletariat, and he devotes a volume to renaissances, contacts across time, in which a later society picks up an earlier one's artifacts. If substrate outlives unit, then a plot of substrate against time can look monotonic even though every individual unit's plot is a closed loop. Progress is residue. It isn't the opposite of the cycle, it's the cycle's exhaust.

The place this bites hardest is Spengler, who is my best counter-example and knew it. His cultures are effectively monads, each with its own prime symbol — Apollonian body, Faustian infinite space, Magian cavern — and he treats inheritance across cultures as mostly illusion: what looks like a young culture receiving an older one's forms he calls pseudomorphosis, and it is a distortion, a young culture made to speak in a grammar that isn't its. When a culture finishes, what's left over isn't a substrate, it's a fellah people, humans without history. If Spengler is right about that, residue doesn't accumulate, it just piles up, and my thesis is a euphemism for wreckage.

So the question the paper should answer is: under what conditions does substrate transmit cumulatively rather than as debris? My guess is that cumulative transmission needs a dead-hand medium, something that stores a solution in a form independent of the people who found it — writing, law, codified technique. And here is the case that has me stuck, because the guess seems false. The Rule of St Benedict is about the most durable dead-hand medium Europe ever produced: fixed, copied, argued over clause by clause. Yet Cistercians reform the Benedictines, the Cistercians grow rich and relaxed, and Trappists reform the Cistercians. The medium preserved the text perfectly and the predicament not at all. Which suggests residue only accumulates where the medium stores not the solution but the means of regenerating the predicament, and I have no idea what such a medium would look like or how you'd recognise one from the outside.

A final worry, which is that the whole thing may be too accommodating. Any sequence whatsoever can be re-described as a cycle whose residue is the next cycle. Toynbee's arrested civilizations, in particular, look suspiciously like a valve for the cases where the cycle never starts. I would rather have an account that could fail than one that explains everything, and at the moment I think mine explains everything.
