Longtime reader of the rationalist blogs here — LW and the old Overcoming Bias archives for like fifteen years — and I've drifted into actual philosophy of mind on the side, mostly by arguing with a small reading group. That group has basically settled on thinking multiple realizability is a dead argument. What killed it for us was the grain point, the Bechtel and Mundale move: when you look at how neuroscientists actually individuate brain states, the "same" cognitive state across species usually turns out to be homologous machinery, and the apparent many-realizers-one-function picture was an artifact of describing the function too loosely. Grant that. Grant all of it.

Here's the position I want you to argue for as hard as you can: even if the grain point is right in every actual case — even if it turned out that every mental kind has exactly one physical realizer kind in this universe, no exceptions — multiple realizability would still be doing all the work functionalists ever wanted from it. The version I have in mind makes a swap: what should make a higher-level kind multiply realizable isn't that it *has* many realizers, but that its generalizations stay true under interventions that vary the realizer. So the real question isnt "how many realizer kinds are actually out there?" but "which changes to the substrate would leave the higher-level generalizations intact?" That's a counterfactual question, and it can have a wide answer even when the actual answer is one-to-one.

So build the case. I want the strongest form of the argument, an explicit account of why it survives the grain objection rather than being defeated by it — my intuition is that grain is about how you carve the actual taxonomy, whereas this is about which carvings are modally robust, and those just aren't in competition — and at least one worked example. Pick something concrete, like a generalization about memory consolidation or about nociceptive withdrawal, and show me what an intervention on the realizer looks like and why the higher-level generalization survives it. Then tell me where its softest, and be specific about this one: does the whole thing collapse into reductionism with extra steps? I.e. would Kim look at it and say fine, that's my point, once you fix the realizer the mental kind is nothing over and above the physical kind, so your "counterfactual multiplicity" buys no autonomy for psychology? If the answer is yes, say yes instead of defending the position past where it's honest — I'd rather have a clean account of what the view costs than a victory lap.

Don't give me a bibliography or a reading list. I'm not going to look any of it up, and the reasoning is the part I actually care about.
