I'm 73, retired three years ago after running a soil microbiology lab at a state school for thirty-some years, and I've taken up philosophy the way other people take up woodworking — slowly, badly, but with great enjoyment. Right now I'm stuck on Strawson's essay on freedom and resentment. I think I've got the machinery: the reactive attitudes, the way we switch them off when the other person is a child, or ill, or acting under duress or unusual strain, or simply didn't know what he was doing, and the way we can drop into the objective attitude and start treating somebody as a thing to be managed, treated, or avoided rather than as someone we're dealing with. Also the bit where he says taking up that objective attitude can be a way of getting relief from the strain of being involved with a person at all, which he seems to think is a symptom that the relationship is dying.

What I want from you is a case built to order, not an essay about Strawson, and not a reading list — I have a library card and no patience for bibliographies.

Here's what the case is for, so you know what counts as cheating. My hunch is that the exemption list is about the person you're looking at, but that there are also reasons for dropping into the objective attitude that live entirely on my side of the ledger, and I want to see whether that hunch survives contact with a properly constructed example. So the verdicts have to come out of the theory. The specifications:

No illness, no addiction, no trauma reveal in the last sentence, no coercion, no unusual strain, no accident, no ignorance of consequences. - Setting: a lab, a field station, a small department, a family firm. Ordinary life. One paragraph for the scenario itself, and no more. No neurology, no psychiatry, no sci-fi.
