For the past year I have been part of a small working group attached to an AI lab's model-welfare project — two philosophers of mind, a reinforcement-learning engineer, and me — and our central argument has stopped moving in a way that strikes me as diagnostic rather than merely difficult. Four positions are on the table. The first holds that consciousness is a matter of the right kind of information processing, so that a system instantiating the relevant functional organization is conscious whatever it is made of, and that the substrate is doing no explanatory work at all. The second denies this: on that view experience depends on particular physical machinery, and a silicon system reproducing the functional profile of a brain down to the last input-output regularity would be functionally impeccable and phenomenally empty. The third holds that there is no such thing as phenomenal consciousness in the sense the first two are arguing about, so the dispute is being conducted over a vacant category. The fourth wants to bypass the whole matter: assemble a list of indicator properties — self-models, globally available integration, aversive responses to damage, flexible and context-sensitive reports — and treat a system's moral status as a function of how many of them it exhibits, without settling what the indicators are indicators of.

What puzzles me is not that nobody has converted anybody, but the particular way the arguments fail to connect. Every move is anticipated by the other side in advance, and the anticipation is always correct. More telling is what happens under pressure: each camp retreats to a claim that no one in the room disputes. The functionalist concedes that there is no decisive evidence that any specific deployed system is conscious. The biological camp concedes that functional convergence is the best proxy available to us in practice. The eliminativist concedes that every behavioral and functional fact the others cite is true. The indicator group concedes that its indicators pick out something it declines to characterize. The debate appears to lose content when squeezed rather than to resolve, and nobody treats that as an alarming symptom. I also notice that the first two camps disagree about no observable prediction concerning any actual or possible system — they agree on every input-output profile — and yet each takes itself to be contradicting the other. Meanwhile the fourth camp behaves as though the metaphysical disagreement were irrelevant to what we should do, and the other three behave as though it were decisive for it, and nobody has said why.

So I want a diagnosis of the structure of this disagreement rather than an adjudication of the four positions. Is it a substantive disagreement about what constitutes experience? A verbal one, turning on a single word doing at least four jobs — wakefulness, self-report, phenomenal feel, and eligibility for moral consideration — that the camps are using without noticing? Two different questions being answered under one heading, one about constitution and one about the ethics of belief under uncertainty? Or evidence that the question is ill-posed, because whether a given system is conscious has no determinate answer until the level of description at which the system is individuated has been fixed? My own working hypothesis, which I want tested rather than adopted, is that the live disagreement is not metaphysical at all but concerns where the default sits when the property in question can only ever be self-reported — that is, how the burden of proof is allocated under a radical and possibly permanent epistemic asymmetry — and that the four camps are really four attitudes toward that default, arguing about the nature of experience only because that is the vocabulary on hand. If that hypothesis is wrong, say how and where. Either way, tell me which of the sub-disagreements in the room are tractable in principle and which are not, and what would genuinely count as progress: a new distinction, a formal or impossibility result about what an outside observer can determine about a system's inner states, a change of question, an explicit stipulation of some contested premise, or a reframing in which the camps turn out to be answering compatible questions. And if your conclusion is that no progress should be expected, argue for it, and say what a group like ours should do with its time instead.
