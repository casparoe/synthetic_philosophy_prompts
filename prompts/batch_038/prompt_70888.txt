Our department runs a small internal seed-grant thing — one semester of teaching relief in exchange for a one-page proposal, roughly 700 words. I need help turning a hunch into a proper application.

The hunch is about the Hart–Devlin debate, specifically Devlin's claim that a shared public morality is as necessary to a society's continued existence as, say, a recognized government, and that loosening it risks disintegration. The standard post-Hart treatment is to read that as a causal-empirical prediction, then observe that no society has dissolved becuase its sexual morals got more permissive, and call the thesis refuted. My sense is that this reads it as the wrong kind of claim entirely. Suppose instead that a shared moral practice behaves like a common-pool resource or an assurance device: worth having to each person only if enough others keep buying in, individually cheap to defect on, and very slow to rebuild once it erodes. Then Devlin isn't forecasting collapse, he's making a claim about erosion conditions — and Hart's no-evidence objection just doesn't reach it.

Turn that into the proposal. It needs: (1) the question stated precisely enough that it has a determinate answer — I think the real question is something like whether the collective-action reconstruction yields a principle for when criminal law may enforce a moral norm that isn't just the harm principle in disguise, but sharpen that; (2) why the standard readings of Devlin (moral conservatism, straight-up social-cohesion sociology, the treason analogy) leave the question open rather than settling it; (3) the method — what I'd actually be doing, month by month across the semester — including how the reconstruction is meant to generate a sorting criterion for norms. My guess is that enforcement earns its keep only for norms with threshold or assurance structure, where defection is visible, imitable, and self-reinforcing, and not for norms whose violation is genuinely private and non-contagious, but I want the proposal to treat that as a hypothesis the project tests rather than a conclusion I've already reached; (4) why this method and not the obvious alternative of going and looking for sociological evidence about whether moral change destabilises societies — I need a real answer here, since on the face of it that looks like a better project and just isn't a philosophy one; (5) what a result would look like and what would count as failure. Be specific on failure, because I think there are two distinct failure modes the proposal should own up to: the reconstruction makes Devlin trivially true but normatively inert, or it collapses his thesis into a harm-prevention argument that Hart could have signed off on, in which case the whole exercise bought nothing; and (6) an honest read on scope — is this a single paper or am I overreaching for one semester of relief.

No bibliography and no lit review by citation, I'll add those myself. Refer to positions and arguments however you want, just don't invent references. One more thing: the panel is two philosophers and a law prof who thinks Devlin is a joke, so it can't read like the rehabilitation of a dead judge for its own sake. It has to say what turns on getting the answer right.
