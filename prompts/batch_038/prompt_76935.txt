So — I've finally got a shot at the internal research leave, the year-long one. Committee is two historians, a sociolinguist, a business ethicist, and the deputy dean. Two pages, 1200 words, and it genuinely has to read as a real project to people who have never met the phrase expected choice-worthiness, without coming across as though I'm explaining colour to the blind.

Here's the half-formed thing I want to build the application around. The moral uncertainty literature has spent a lot of ink on the comparison problem: how do you get utilitarianism's choice-worthiness onto the same scale as Kantianism's, given different units, different ranges, different attitudes to risk. Fine, that's the acknowledged headache. But there's a prior problem that I think gets waved through, which is the individuation problem. Before you can put credences on moral theories you need to know what the theories are, and that partition of theory-space looks arbitary in a way that actually bites. Utilitarianism and prioritarianism — one theory with a different weighting, or two? Total versus average utilitarianism once population size varies? Is Ross-style deontology a theory or a family with no determinate boundaries? My worry is that the ranking of options by expected choice-worthiness can flip depending on how you carve, and that there's no anchor telling you which carving is right — which is unlike the empirical case, where the world and your evidence do most of the carving for you.

So the question I want funded is: does that break expected choice-worthiness maximisation as a decision rule, or can the rule be repaired — say by demanding invariance across carvings, or by some robustness-across-partitions criterion?

Write me the two pages. I want:
