I cover neuroscience and philosophy for a general-interest magazine, and I'm three weeks out from a feature on the readiness-potential experiments and the free will arguments built on them. The honest problem with my draft is that the debate has calcified into two rehearsed moves, and my editor has now told me twice that a piece consisting of nothing but those two moves will read like every other anniversary retrospective. The first move belongs to the deflationists: the slow negative shift over motor cortex, they say, is not a decision being made at all but the visible tip of an accumulation of subthreshold neural noise drifting toward a movement threshold, which is why it appears to start so early when the trials are aligned to movement onset rather than to the decision. The second belongs to the compatibilists, and it concerns the more ambitious decoding work: even where a scanner or a classifier predicts which of two buttons a subject will press several seconds before the subject reports any urge, the accuracy is barely above chance, so all that has been shown, they argue, is that the coarse outcome is prepared in advance while the decision itself is still made consciously. Between them, these two replies absorb almost any experimental result you can describe, and I want to know whether that is a sign that the stalemate is metaphysical or merely an artifact of what the existing paradigms happen to be able to measure.

So I would like you to build me a case to order. Design an experimental scenario, one paragraph long and readable by an intelligent layperson, in which both escape hatches are closed simultaneously: the preparatory signal has to be one that the noise-accumulation account genuinely cannot absorb, and it has to carry information about the content of the choice rather than merely about when the choice will occur. Here are the constraints I want honored. The setup must be something a well-funded cognitive neuroscience lab could plausibly run within the next few years and an ordinary institutional review board would approve: scalp recordings only, no implanted electrodes, no stimulation of the brain, no drugs, no hypnosis, and nothing that coerces, deceives, or otherwise reaches around the subject's own psychology, because if the subject's action is unfree only because someone else is steering it, the case tells my readers nothing. The classifier must be capped at roughly two-thirds accuracy on any single trial; it has to be wrong about a third of trials, and your scenario should say what its errors look like rather than treating it as an oracle. The subject must be choosing between two options that carry a small real stake, not pressing a button in a sensory vacuum. And here is the hinge I care about most: at least once during the session the subject must withhold the prepared action, exercising the power to veto, and that act of withholding must itself have been predictable in advance by the same capped classifier. The purpose of the case, so you know what counts as cheating, is to test whether the two standard replies are available in principle or only in practice; a scenario that stipulates its verdict, by quietly building the classifier to track the decision rather than its neural antecedents, fails, and the verdicts have to come out of the design itself.

After the scenario, show your work. Walk me through how the design defeats the noise-accumulation reading and why it does so, rather than asserting that it does; say what a compatibilist should say about a veto that was itself predictable, and in particular whether predictability alone undermines control or whether something further is needed; and state plainly what you take the case to show about the difference between an action's being caused and its being bypassed. Then identify the single feature of the design that is doing the work, and tell me what the case collapses into if I delete, one at a time, the content prediction and the veto prediction. Finally, give the strongest available counterattack from whichever position comes out worst in your scenario, and say whether you think it succeeds. No references, no reading list, no named studies; I will fact-check the background separately and I would rather you not guess at citations. I do want one thing of that kind, though: end with a candid statement of how confident you are that the design actually delivers the verdicts you claim, and single out the one step in your reasoning that you are least sure of, since I intend to put exactly that much pressure on it when I run it past the neuroscientists I have on speed dial.
