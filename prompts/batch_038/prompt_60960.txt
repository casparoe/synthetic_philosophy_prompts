so i help run a small comms thing for a regenerative-farming outfit and we're putting together a short public explainer on crop protection. one of our agronomists keeps reaching for the same comparison and i want to stress-test it before it goes in print.

his version: spraying a broad-spectrum insecticide is basically medicine, not warfare. the crop is the patient, the pest population is the infection, the spray is the antibiotic, and the non-targets that get caught in it — the hoverflies, the parasitoid wasps, the bees foraging in the neighbouring field, the aquatic invertebrates down the drain — are collateral damage, or maybe just side effects, like a drug that wipes out your gut flora while clearing the infection. and on his telling the analogy does a lot of work: it's supposed to show that the whole fuss about insect welfare is a category mistake, because you don't need to know anything at all about whether a bacterium minds being killed in order to justify the course of antibiotics, so likewise you don't need to settle anything about insect sentience in order to justify the spray programme.

that last move is what i'm suspicious of. he's using a comparison about microbes to make a question about insects disappear, and i can't quite tell whether the analogy genuinely earns that or whether it's smuggling in the premise that insects are microbe-like.

can you go through it properly? i want the correspondence laid out explicitly — what plays the patient, the pathogen, the drug, the resistance, the side effect, the dose, and the prescriber, since someone has to occupy that last role. then the disanalogies, but sorted: which ones actually undermine the use he's putting it to, and which ones are real but beside the point (i'm sure there'll be some of both, e.g. plants don't have immune systems in the relevant sense, that sort of thing). then what features of the source it's quietly importing that the target doesn't have — i'd guess something about sentience, something about resistance being a shared-resource problem rather than an individual-patient problem, and something about who the patient even is here, given it's not clear whether that's the crop, the field, the farm business or the food system. and finally: does his conclusion survive once the disanalogies are on the table, and if not, is there a better comparison that would do the honest work without begging the question?
