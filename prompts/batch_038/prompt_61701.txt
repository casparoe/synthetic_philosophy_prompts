I write a weekly column on science and ideas — mostly psych-ish stuff aimed at a general audience — and this week's is on hindsight bias and the narrative fallacy: how knowing the ending rewrites your memory of what you predicted, and how badly we crave a compact causal story once we know how things turned out. I've got the research side handeled. What I don't have is an example that isn't a stale business-school anecdote about Nokia or the 2008 crash.

So I want to use Citizen Kane, and I want you to read it as a machine for generating hindsight bias and narrative fallacy in its audience — and as a film that arguably knows it's doing that.

Play the role of a philosopher of psychology who actually watches movies: someone who knows the Fischhoff-era hindsight work (including the study where people estimated the likelihood of various outcomes of Nixon's trips to Peking and Moscow and then were asked later what they'd predicted), Kahneman and Taleb on the narrative fallacy, creeping determinism, and also the more recent argument that hindsight bias is partly a functional sense-making process rather than a pure defect. And someone who won't fudge either side to make my column work. Think carefully before you write — do a little brainstorming first about which parts of the film are doing which psychological work, then settle on the reading.

The thing I want you to actually grapple with: Thompson is sent out to assemble one coherent story about a dead man out of five witnesses (Thatcher's memoir, Bernstein, Leland, Susan, Raymond the butler), every one of whom is reconstructing the past with the ending already in hand, plus a newsreel that gives the official tidy version first. Then the film hands the audience Rosebud — one object that seems to explain the whole life — which every single character pointedly fails to find. So the film denies its own reporter a narrative and hands the viewer one instead. Is that a diagnosis of the narrative fallacy, or a particularly elegant instance of it? I want you to take a position, not both sides.

Also be careful with the psychology.
