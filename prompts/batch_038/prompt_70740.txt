I have recently joined an interdisciplinary working group on the codification of administrative discretion, and I find myself repeatedly unable to answer a senior member's favorite argument. My own training is in public policy, so I lack the technical vocabulary to say precisely what is wrong with it — if anything is. Whenever the conversation turns to whether a statute can be drafted so that its application is fully determinate, he says some version of: "Gödel proved that any consistent formal system rich enough to do arithmetic is incomplete — there are statements that are true but that the system cannot prove — so no body of rules can ever settle every question its own rules raise." He then deploys the same sentence in two further places. Against a proposal to replace case-by-case adjudication with an automated eligibility procedure, on the ground that any such procedure is a formal system and therefore cannot capture everything that is correct. And, most recently, in favor of a constitutional design that deliberately leaves certain matters unenumerated, on the ground that a fully self-contained charter would be unable to vouch for its own consistency and would therefore be hostage to paradox.

What I want from you is a disambiguation rather than a tutorial. Please lay out, as a numbered menu, the distinct precise theses that one slogan could be expressing, ranging from the unexceptionable to the wildly overreaching, and for each tell me three things: exactly what it asserts and what it presupposes about the system in question — that its axioms be effectively enumerable, that it be able to represent enough arithmetic to encode its own syntax, what counts as a proof, and whether we are now discussing the first theorem or the second; whether it is a platitude, a genuine theorem, a contested philosophical extrapolation, or simply false; and whether it would in fact license any of the three uses my colleague makes of it. Please treat the following confusions explicitly, because I cannot tell them apart on my own: whether "incomplete" in the technical sense bears any relation at all to a legal code failing to anticipate every case that falls under it; whether the judgment that the undecidable sentence is true is available to someone reasoning inside the system or only from a stronger standpoint, and what it costs to claim that standpoint for ourselves; how much work is being done by the word "true" as against "provable," and whether the theorem says anything about the former; what becomes of the whole line once we note that the entities in question need not be effectively axiomatized, and that adding an oracle, an infinitary rule of inference, or simply a court of last appeal may amount to completing them by non-mechanical means; and whether his consistency remark is a distinct result, and what that result does and does not license — in particular, whether it implies anything about a system's being unsound, or about our having any reason to doubt it.

It would also help to be armed with counterexamples I could put on the table: at least two substantial formal systems that are nevertheless complete and decidable, one of which I dimly recall involves arithmetic with addition but no multiplication, along with a statement of what feature of those systems blocks the diagonal construction. Then give me a short diagnosis of why the transfer from formal arithmetic to statutes, agencies, and constitutions fails — or, if you judge that it sometimes succeeds, the exact conditions under which an institution would have to be structured for a genuinely analogous result to apply, and whether any real institution meets them. My deliverable should be something I can carry into Thursday's session: for each of his three uses, one or two sentences stating the strongest claim he could legitimately be making, and an identification of the weakest link between that claim and the conclusion he wants.
