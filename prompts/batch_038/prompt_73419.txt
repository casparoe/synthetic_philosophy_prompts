Forty-odd years in accelerator physics, retired about six years now, and I've fallen into reading philosophy plus this whole algorithmic-ethics literature — half to keep the old grey matter busy, half because my daughter works in policy and forwards me things. Last month I went to a public talk at the library by a sharp young woman from one of these data-ethics outfits, and she made a move that's been itching at me ever since. She said the impossibility results in algorithmic fairness — the ones showing that a risk score can't simultaneously be calibrated within each group and also equalize false positive rates and false negative rates across groups, except in those degenerate special cases where the prediction is perfect or the base rates happen to coincide (a Kleinberg-and-co. paper I think, plus a separate one by a statistician named Chouldechova) — are essentially the uncertainty principle for algorithms. And she leaned on it hard: just as Heisenberg tells you the fuzziness isnt a defect of your apparatus but a feature of the world, the fairness trade-off isn't a defect of the engineers or the training data, it's a hard mathematical limit, so the grown-up response is to stop demanding a fairer algorithm and start working on the social conditions that produce different base rates in the first place. Somebody in the audience objected that a particular bail tool in their county was just badly built, and she waved him off — his complaint, she said, was like arguing with the value of Planck's constant.

Here's my problem: I spent a career around that relation, and I can see the analogy has some real grip. Both results are theorems rather than empirical accidents; both concern a trade-off between two things you'd naively want at once; both get mangled in popular writing into a story about measurement disturbance or about sloppy instruments. But I also have suspicions I can't quite articulate, and my daughter says I'm just a physicist who resents seeing his toys borrowed. So I'd like a proper audit. Could you take the role of someone genuinely expert in both quantum foundations and the fairness literature, and who has taught this to non-specialists? Think carefully before you write — brainstorm out loud first, list candidate correspondences and candidate disanalogies, then commit to a structure rather than dumping everything.

What I want out of it, concretely: first, the mapping spelled out — what plays position, what plays momentum, what plays the non-zero commutator, what plays hbar, what plays the quantum state, what plays the measuring device, and what plays the observer's choice of which quantity to measure. Second, the places it breaks, but sorted: I don't care about cosmetic mismatches, I want to know which disanalogies actually bear on the use she put it to. Things I half-suspect and want checked: in quantum mechanics the trade-off is fixed by a universal constant and no state escapes it, whereas here the slack seems to depend on empirical base rates and on how good the predictor is, and apparently disappears in special cases; the uncertainty relation is about a single system and non-commuting observables, while these fairness criteria are about populations and about properties of a chosen decision rule; and in fairness you get to decide which criterion to privilige, which looks like a values question rather than a discovery. Third — and don't end on a both-sides shrug — tell me whether her two conclusions survive once the disanalogies are on the table: (a) that nobody is to blame and complaints about a specific tool are a category error, and (b) that the only sensible target is base rates and upstream social conditions. If the analogy is doing illegitimate work, say what it's smuggling in. And if there's a better analogy for what those theorems actually establish — some limit result from statistics or economics or thermodynamics that fits the shape of the thing more honestly — propose it and say how far that one holds instead. No references or bibliography needed, I'm not writing a paper; I just want the reasoning laid out so I can argue about it at dinner.
