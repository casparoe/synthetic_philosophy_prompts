I am preparing a talk for a departmental work-in-progress series on the metaphysics of games, and the central argument has developed a conclusion I cannot defend in front of an audience. I would like a diagnosis. The argument, as it currently stands:

(1) Something is real just in case it is a locus of causal powers: it can bring about, prevent, or modify events.

(2) A virtual sword sitting in a game's inventory brings about, prevents, and modifies events: equipping it changes what the engine computes on the next frame, and it is what stands between the player-character and death in the encounter that follows.

(3) So the virtual sword is real.

(4) The same goes for the events it figures in. The wolf's death was brought about, and bringing about is not a matter of degree, nor of the perspective from which one describes it.

(5) So the wolf's death was a real event.

(6) Consider now a novel. Anna Karenina's death was brought about — by her decision on the platform at the station — and it modified, within the story's own causal order, what happened to nearly everyone around her.

(7) By (1), (4), and (6), Anna Karenina's death is a real event as well.

(8) If a death is a real event, then someone really died; and a real death is a real harm.

(9) So Anna Karenina was really harmed, and grief for her is grief at a genuine loss.

(10) But nobody is harmed by what happens in a novel, and grief for Anna Karenina is not the grief of loss — at most it is a rehearsal of it.

(11) Therefore (1) is false: being a locus of causal powers is not what it is to be real.

I am attached to (3), since it is the thesis of the whole talk, and I am equally attached to (10). What I cannot accept is (9), and I cannot see how to get from (2)–(5) to (7) without it. So: which step do you reject, and why did it look innocuous when I wrote it down? I want the diagnosis to be specific rather than a general warning about analogies, and I want to know what each of the three positions I expect to be pressed on from the floor — fictionalism about virtual worlds, the reduction of virtual objects to physical patterns in the hardware and the code, and realism about virtual objects as genuine digital entities — has to pay in order to block the argument at that step. Finally, tell me whether any repair preserves (3): if the argument can be fixed only by weakening the sense in which the virtual sword is real, I would rather know that now than discover it at the talk.
