I'm a doctoral student in history drafting a methods critique of cliodynamics, and the argument I've built seems to prove far more than I want. I'd like a diagnosis.

The setup. In a four-paragraph correspondence in *Nature* in February 2010 (vol. 463, p. 608), contributed to a special collection on the coming decade of science, Peter Turchin argued that the next decade would be one of growing instability in the United States and western Europe. His stated grounds: stagnant real wages, widening inequality, an oversupply of young people with advanced degrees, and rising public debt, all of which had turned in the 1970s; US instability spikes around 1870, 1920 and 1970, implying another was due near 2020; a downturn in the long economic wave; and a coming youth bulge. He attached this to the demographic-structural account of secular cycles he had developed with Nefedov.

The argument:

(1) A theory earns substantial credit from a forecast if the forecast was issued before the outcome was known, the theory's parameters were not re-fitted afterwards to produce that outcome, and the outcome came about as forecast.

(2) The 2010 correspondence satisfied all three conditions with respect to US instability around 2020: it was published a decade in advance, and every indicator it cited had its turning point in the 1970s, long before the events.

(3) What happened from 2020 onward matches what was forecast.

(4) So demographic-structural theory earned substantial credit. (From 1–3.)

(5) Now construct theory R: a large, wealthy, unequal democracy with a polarized elite facing a contested leadership transition will experience heightened instability in the following decade. Fix R's parameters on pre-2010 data as well; R also implied instability in the US around 2020.

(6) R is compatible with demographic-structural theory's being false, and with mechanisms that contradict it — purely ideological, informational, or stochastic dynamics.

(7) So by (1)–(4), R earns the same credit from the same event.

(8) The construction can be repeated without limit: for any mechanism one likes, one can write down a theory whose parameters were fixed on pre-2010 data and which implies instability in the US in the 2020s.

(9) So indefinitely many mutually incompatible theories earn equal credit from this forecast's success.

(10) If indefinitely many incompatible theories earn equal credit from a prediction, that prediction discriminates among nothing, and a prediction that discriminates among nothing is evidence for none of them.

(11) Therefore (4) is false: the 2020 forecast is no evidence for demographic-structural theory.

(12) And since nothing in (1)–(11) was specific to cliodynamics, no long-run historical or social theory has ever earned credit from a forecast.

I want to keep (1), which I had taken to be uncontroversial in the philosophy of science I know, and I want to reject (12). Tell me whether I can have both, and if not, what I am paying.

Please use these sections, in this order:

**The break** (about 250 words): identify the step or steps that fail, and say precisely what each one equivocates on or assumes without support.

**Why it looked sound** (about 150 words): for each failing step, explain what true principle it is a distortion of, so that I can see why I wrote it down without flinching.

**Pricing the repairs** (about 300 words): take the candidates in turn — tightening (1), denying (2), denying (3) as a hindsight judgment about a vague outcome, denying (6), blocking (8)–(10), refusing (12) — and say what each repair costs and which is cheapest.

**What survives** (about 150 words): once the reductio is blocked, what the 2010 forecast is actually worth as evidence, and what the strongest true version of the criticism of cliodynamics' forecasting is.

**Settled and contested** (about 100 words): separate what is settled among people who work on this from what is genuinely contested — among both philosophers of science and cliodynamicists and their critics — and do not present a contested position as the consensus view.
