I cover social science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and last month I ran a long piece arguing that the multidimensional poverty indices everyone treats as the heir to income poverty don't actually measure freedom. They count achieved functionings — years of schooling, whether there's a latrine, what the household cooks on — and then get sold to readers as if they'd captured the capability approach, which is supposed to be about what people can do and be, not what they happen to have done. My sharpest claim is that the weights on those indicators are a choice dressed up as a finding, and that the people defending them have no way to tell someone deprived of something from someone who chose to go without it.

The magazine has now sent me to a workshop where I'll do twenty minutes plus Q&A on that argument, in a room of applied economists who build and use these indices, some stats people from a development agency, and two philosophers who work on measurement. None of them are hostile to the capability approach — most of them think they're the ones implementing it — which is exactly the problem.

Give me the prep sheet: the five objections this room will actually raise (not the ones I'd get from a philosophy seminar), in their vocabulary, with my quickest honest reply to each and a line on where the reply is soft. One of them should be the question I'm praying nobody asks — I think its the one about what I'd have them measure instead. And be straight with me about which of my premises are settled among people who know this literature and which are genuinely live disputes; don't hand me a contested reading as if it were the consensus.

Numbered list, exactly five items, a couple of sentences each — objection in their words, then my reply and its soft spot — nothing before or after.
