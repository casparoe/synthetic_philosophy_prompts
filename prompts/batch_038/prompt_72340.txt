I'm in the second year of a philosophy PhD and need to submit a 1,200-word project outline for my department's dissertation review next month, along with a ten-minute presentation of it. My topic is the ethics of infinite populations, and my half-formed idea is that the literature has quietly slid between two different kinds of infinity: an infinite temporal horizon, where the moments come pre-ordered by time, and an infinite population at a single time, where no ordering of the individuals is privileged. Devices invented for the first setting then get carried into the second without anyone noticing that they have lost their justification. Consider the overtaking-style rules that compare infinite welfare profiles by looking at finite initial segments and taking a limit, or by comparing two profiles whenever one is eventually always better than the other: these presuppose an enumeration, and in the temporal case the enumeration is handed to us by the arrow of time, whereas for a population spread out in space any enumeration is an arbitrary choice, so a rule whose verdict changes when we relist the same individuals is either incomplete or covertly indexical. My working hypothesis is that a strict index-invariance requirement — the comparison of two infinite populations must not depend on how their members are listed — forces us to give up full comparability and settle for a partial ordering on which two profiles are comparable only where they differ at finitely many positions. What I most want to test is the decision-theoretic payoff: does restricting attention to finite differences actually dissolve the familiar paralysis whereby, in an already infinite universe, no finite improvement to anyone's welfare can be shown to make things better on the whole, or does it merely relocate that paralysis from global choices to the local, finitely scoped choices an agent actually faces?
