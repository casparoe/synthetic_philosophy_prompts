For the next two award cycles I am serving as chair of the jury of a British fiction prize, a post I accepted partly because the trustees wanted a philosopher in the room and partly because I thought the year would be a useful test of whether anything in aesthetics can be made to bear weight in practice. I have come to doubt it. The structure is as follows: some one hundred and thirty eligible novels are read by a readers' panel of twelve paid non-specialists, who reduce them to a longlist of sixteen; five jurors, of whom I am not one — I deliberate but do not vote — reduce that to a shortlist of six and then choose a winner; and the recommendation must then be endorsed by two-thirds of the trustees, most of whom are publishers, agents and donors rather than readers of any kind. Eligibility is fixed by the founding instrument: first publication in Britain or Ireland, in English, original rather than translated. The prize carries forty thousand pounds and, more consequentially, a change in the winning author's fortunes that no one on the jury is really equipped to authorise. We meet six times between November and April, no juror reliably finishes everything on the longlist, the chair has no tie-breaking power beyond the rules, and each year the same three complaints arrive in the post — that one juror is a newspaper critic with an interest in certain imprints, that another openly does not finish books he dislikes, and that delicacy of taste has become a courteous name for the panel's shared preferences.

What I want from you is a decision procedure the jury could actually run, grounded not decoratively but substantively in Hume's essay of 1757 on the standard of taste and in what his account of the true judge really commits us to. I would like his marks — the delicacy of the palate, the practice in a given art, the habit of comparison, the freedom from prejudice, and good sense — turned into tests that can be applied to a juror and to a verdict, in a stated order, with a specified standard for what counts as passing each: what evidence establishes delicacy in a person we have not watched read; how much practice, and in what, before someone is competent to judge a particular book; what a sufficient comparison-set looks like when nobody has read everything; how one distinguishes a disqualifying prejudice from an honest limit of one's palate, given that Hume himself seems to allow both a duty to survey a work from its own point of view and the legitimacy of finding some things repellent; and what good sense is being asked to do at each stage, since it appears to be carrying more weight than the other four marks together. Two difficulties must be faced directly rather than papered over. First, the circularity: the qualified judges are supposedly identified by their agreement on an established canon, and the canon is established by the qualified judges — I need a way of admitting and discounting jurors that does not simply assume the answer. Second, aggregation: Hume offers us the concord of true judges as the standard but no rule for reaching a decision from a plurality of them when they disagree, and our deed of trust requires a single winner, which is an ordinal demand laid on a standard that concerns the correctness of a sentiment. Please mark explicitly every point at which the procedure runs out of Humean resources and hands the matter to me or to the trustees as a question of institutional choice rather than fact, and be candid about which of the steps are judgment calls dressed in procedural clothing. I should also like the procedure to work forward as well as backward: it should give me selection tests for the three jurors I must nominate for the coming cycle, not merely tests for weighting the verdicts of jurors I already have.

Then run it on three cases, and please make the third one an outcome I shall not welcome. (i) A juror who is an eminent stylist and essayist declares at our first meeting that he cannot read fiction in a non-realist register, and asks whether he may abstain from the two fantastical entries or must attempt them; if he abstains, does his vote on the remaining entries stand at full weight, at reduced weight, or not at all? (ii) A shortlisted novel is written in an invented register built out of a Northern English dialect; two jurors report it as unreadable, stopped at about sixty pages, and are prepared to vote against it on that basis, while three report that the register is the source of its power. Are the two non-finishers qualified judges of this work at all, may their verdicts enter the count, and what does the procedure do if it turns out that no juror has the practice and comparison the work requires? (iii) Suppose the procedure, applied honestly, concludes that the readers' panel — which I have defended publicly for two years, and which the trustees like because it is cheap and looks inclusive — is the least qualified body in the process and must be stripped of its filtering role, or, worse, that my own non-voting but deliberative chairmanship is a disguised sixth vote. Tell me what the procedure then requires of me. Finally, state what it cannot do: I want the limits specified rather than implied, including whether a jury-level procedure can cure an eligibility rule that pre-filters for nationality and language, and whether Hume's standard can generate any guidance at all about a body of jurors who share a blind spot none of them can detect. I am not after a bibliography, a reading list, or a survey of the secondary literature, and you need not quote the essay — cite nothing you cannot stand behind — but do say, at the end, how confident you are in the main claims you have made about what Hume's account permits and forbids, and single out the one point in your construction of the procedure on which you are least sure. Please write throughout in British English.
