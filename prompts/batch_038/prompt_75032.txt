I am preparing the position statement that opens the final seminar of my philosophy of cognitive science course, and before I commit it to paper I would like an audit of whether my commitments can be held jointly. The seminar's topic is the frame problem and its descendants, and what follows is an honest inventory of where I have landed.

(i) In its original technical form, the frame problem is a solved matter of engineering. Once one adopts the commonsense law of inertia — that a property persists unless something brings about a change in it — and formalises it in a nonmonotonic apparatus such as circumscription or an event-calculus-style treatment of fluents, a planner can reason about thousands of properties without writing a frame axiom for each action–property pair. What is left is representational economy, not epistemology.

(ii) I nonetheless accept the broadened reading, on which the deeper difficulty is relevance determination: how any system, biological or artificial, arrives at the small subset of what it might take account of that actually bears on its present situation. I think this problem is unsolved, and unsolvable in principle, for any system whose contact with its situation is mediated by a representation that the system must consult.

(iii) I am broadly Dreyfusian about expertise. Skilled coping is not inference over a stored model of the world, and what shows up as relevant for an expert is disclosed by the situation rather than selected from an internally represented candidate set.

(iv) I take large language models to be the best available evidence bearing on (ii) and (iii). They settle what is relevant to an input with no body, no persisting world model, and no explicit representation of what has stayed the same. I read this as showing that relevance can be acquired from distributional structure alone, and I think it substantially defuses (iii).

(v) I draw a sharp line between the qualification problem, which I take to mark a genuine limit on formalisation, and the ramification problem, which I take to be a tractable nuisance that a better ontology absorbs.

(vi) My methodological principle is that a puzzle counts as a real problem only if it yields an exhibitable failure — some system that, absent the purported solution, gets things wrong. Where no such failure can be produced, I treat the dispute as verbal.

(vii) Two case verdicts. A chess program that recomputes the position from scratch after every move, and so never represents what stayed the same, does not face the frame problem at all. A domestic robot that must decide whether making an omelette also alters the room's temperature, the cat's location, and its owner's mood plainly does.

(viii) I am a naturalist about the mental. I do not think there is any relation between a system and its environment that physics and biology cannot describe, and I hold (iii) literally rather than as a piece of Heideggerian poetry.

Please tell me which of these pull against one another and which merely look as though they do. For each tension, rank it by severity, identify what would have to be given up or reformulated to remove it, and state what the cheapest revision costs me. Then tell me which single revision would repair the most damage across the set, and whether either of my verdicts in (vii) is one that my own principles are unable to deliver. I have no view about where the trouble lies and no prior attachment to any of the eight, so please do not assume that (i) and (ii) are the only pair worth examining. What I want is the audit itself, not a survey of the literature and not a reading list.
