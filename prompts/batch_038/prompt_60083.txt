Thirty-five years as a comparative physiologist, and philosophy only since retirement — largely the literature on determining death. One institutional practice puzzles me, and I should like help seeing what it takes for granted. In donation after circulatory death, life support is withdrawn in the operating room; once the heart stops, the team observes a mandated few minutes of hands-off time, declares death on the ground that circulation will not return, and then begins procurement — in some protocols reconnecting a pump that restores oxygenated blood flow to the chest and abdomen, after first clamping the vessels that would carry that blood to the brain. The same arrest in a patient who is not a donor would be treated as reversible for far longer, and nobody would call him dead. And the whole procedure is governed by a rule that procurement must not be what kills the donor.

What conception of death does this package presuppose: death as the irreversible loss of the organism's integrative work, as the loss of the brain specifically, or as a status conferred once certain conditions and intentions are in place? Which rival conceptions does it rule out, and do its parts point in the same direction — the hands-off interval, the clamping, the asymmetry with the non-donor? I should like a clear separation of what the practice strictly requires from what merely sits comfortably with it, and an account of where it is philosophically underdetermined, such that several incompatible views would license it. And if the presupposition were replaced, what in that operating room would have to change?
