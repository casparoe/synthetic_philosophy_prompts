I cover neuroscience and philosophy of mind for a general-interest magazine, and I have been assigned a long piece on whether first-person reports can serve as data in a science of consciousness. Before I start putting researchers on the record, I want the specification rather than the positions: the list of requirements any proposed *method* for gathering and interpreting evidence about conscious experience must satisfy in order to be taken seriously.

Anchor the exercise on Dennett's heterophenomenology, since that is the proposal my piece will treat as its reference point — the idea that a subject's sincere verbal reports are collected as a corpus describing a world the subject seems to inhabit, that the theorist stays neutral about whether the items in that world actually exist, and that the remaining job is to explain, by ordinary cognitive-scientific means, why the subject says what they say. But write the desiderata so that they are not a restatement of that proposal's own commitments; a good number of them should be things heterophenomenology is itself accused of failing.

For each requirement, give me: its statement; the case or consideration that motivates it, in concrete experimental terms wherever possible (confabulation about one's own reasoning, blindsight, partial-report paradigms, change and inattentional blindness, split-brain interpretation, colour-phi-style timing puzzles, and the like); a verdict on whether it is non-negotiable for anything calling itself a science of consciousness or merely a preference of a particular camp; and the single question I could put to a researcher in an interview that would reveal whether their method satisfies it.

Then three things I want most. First, identify any subset of the list that cannot be jointly satisfied. I have a suspicion that strict neutrality about whether reported items exist is in tension with the demand that a method's deliverances actually discriminate among rival theories of consciousness — confirm, repair, or replace that suspicion, and if the tension is real, say which horn a working scientist has to choose. Second, tell me which single requirement does the most to eliminate popular approaches. Third, tell me which of the things people routinely demand in this literature are genuine requirements and which are the imported prejudices of one side of a fight I am about to report on — for instance, whether reports must be treated as presumptively authoritative, or whether a method must be applicable to creatures and systems that produce no reports at all.

Two complications need to be absorbable by the list, because both are in the piece:

1. One lab has built an apparatus that, on some trials, silently replaces a subject's typed report with a fluent but different report generated by a language model, and returns it to the subject as their own. In debrief, subjects endorse the substituted text and elaborate freely on the experience it describes. I need the desiderata to be able to settle whether that substituted text counts as data about that subject's experience, and to say what provenance constraint, if any, a method must impose on its inputs — or why provenance is a red herring.

2. A second group argues the method should be applied reflexively: that protocol write-ups, researchers' accounts of what they take themselves to have observed, and a theory's own motivating thought experiments are texts of exactly the same kind as the subjects' and deserve the same neutrality. Whether this is a legitimate requirement, a reductio of the method, or a category mistake, I want the list to deliver an answer rather than a shrug.

Close with a brief scorecard — three or four lines each, no more — for heterophenomenology, for a trained-first-person neurophenomenological method, and for a report-free approach that infers consciousness from neural or behavioural structure alone. No bibliography or reading list, please; I will source the piece separately.
