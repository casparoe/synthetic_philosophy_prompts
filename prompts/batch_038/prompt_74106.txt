So I'm sitting in on a reading group about logical empiricism and its politics, and halfway through it turned into an argument I can't settle. Half the room reckons Carnap would have been on board with automated welfare administration — it's exactly what he wanted, they say: swap the foggy judgement of individual caseworkers for an exact, publicly checkable procedure, which is just explication applied to the state, and his socialism plus his faith in scientific planning points the same way. The other half says his own criteria would have made him one of the earliest and sharpest critics, and that the planning enthusiasm is doing all the work in the first argument. Carnap died in 1970 so nobody can win this by quoting him; I want to know what he'd most plausibly have said.

The case we were arguing about: from 2016 an Australian agency matched its welfare records against annual income figures from the tax office, and where the two disagreed a program spread the annual figure evenly across every fortnight of the year, computed an overpayment from that averaged amount, and issued a debt notice automatically. The recipient then had to disprove it, usually by producing payslips for a stretch years in the past, for work that was often casual and erratic — which is precisely the pattern that averaging erases. A federal court later went against the government on the legality of the averaging, the scheme was wound back in 2020 with hundreds of thousands of debts voided, and a royal commission in 2023 described it as an expensive failure of public administration in both human and economic terms.

Where would Carnap land, and why? I'd like you to actually work through his toolkit rather than just picking a side on vibes: the four marks he wanted from a good explication (similarity to the rough original concept, exactness, fruitfulness, simplicity) applied to a construct like an evenly-distributed fortnightly income; the principle of tolerance, i.e. that a framework is a stipulation judged on pragmatic grounds with no external fact about which one is right — and what happens politically when a stipulation gets mailed to someone as if it were a finding; his anti-metaphysics, especially his diagnosis of value quarrels as pseudo-disputes until the terms get explicated, and so what he'd do with the deserving/undeserving distinction and with shifting the burden of proof onto the person least able to carry it; and his later inductive logic, since the whole scheme is an inference from annual data to particular fortnights. Give me your verdict in the very first sentence — which half of the reading group is right, or whether there's a third position neither of them had — and then defend it. Under 500 words please.
