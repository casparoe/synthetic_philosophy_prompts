I write for a small policy outlet that mostly covers public finance, and last month I was sent to a two-day workshop on the funding of higher education in a city I'd never visited before. At the reception on the first evening I ended up standing by a window with a lukewarm glass of wine between two people I'd only just met: a public-finance economist attached to a treasury-adjacent research institute, and a researcher at a charity that campaigns on widening access to university. They got into a long argument about whether undergraduate study should be free at the point of use, and I typed up the exchange on my phone afterwards as best I could from memory. I don't have a view of my own to defend here — I've written pieces sympathetic to both positions — and neither of them ever quite lost an argument to the other, which is what I'd like your help with. Call them A (the economist) and B (the access researcher).

The argument ran roughly as follows. (1) A: free tuition is a transfer from taxpayers to future high earners; graduates out-earn non-graduates over a working life and still disproportionately come from above-median-income families, so a universal subsidy takes from a general pool that includes people who never attended and never will, and hands it to those with the best earnings prospects; means-tested grants at least put the money where the need is. (2) B: that reasoning treats today's enrollment pattern as fixed, and the enrollment pattern is precisely what's in dispute; price deters people at the margin not through cool calculation but through sticker shock, misperception of net price and debt aversion, all of which are strongest among first-generation and low-income applicants, so the "regressive transfer" is measured on a population that fees have already filtered, and you cannot use the distribution under fees to condemn the distribution without them. (3) A: then don't abolish the price, make it contingent — repayment as a percentage of earnings above a threshold, with no fixed balance and no default, the design Australia pioneered and Britain later copied; that removes the ruin risk B is worried about while recovering the subsidy from graduates who do well, and it dominates both a fee system and a free one, since low earners effectively pay nothing and high earners pay it all back. (4) B: an income-contingent loan is a graduate tax with extra paperwork and worse psychology; it imposes a high effective marginal rate on graduates exactly when they are starting families and trying to buy homes; people demonstrably fail to process contingency, so the deterrent effect survives the redesign because the word loan does the damage; and since a large share of contingent loans get written off anyway, the state is still subsidizing, only less visibly and at higher administrative cost — if most of the money will be forgiven, say so and give it as a grant. (5) A: fine, compare the honest versions then, a large graduate subsidy against a price, because prices do work subsidies don't; institutions respond to whoever pays, and when the state pays the money tends to be captured by administrators, amenities and slow productivity growth rather than teaching — that is the old Bennett hypothesis about student aid and cost growth, and universal free tuition is just that aid made universal. (6) B: the mechanism didn't operate anywhere you'd have to point to; the German states that reintroduced modest fees repealed them and now charge undergraduates nothing, the Nordics have not charged their own students, and none of those systems display runaway cost growth, while cost growth has been worst exactly where loan volumes are largest — and anyway skin in the game is the wrong instrument, because a student makes a one-off purchase under radical information asymmetry where the credential is much of the point and quality is nearly unobservable beforehand, whereas prices discipline repeat purchases of goods whose quality you can learn by experience. (7) A: the Nordic comparison smuggles in the rest of the fiscal system; those countries fund free tuition with high, progressive general taxation, and graduates repay it across their whole careers, so it is not free but a lifetime cross-subsidy that looks neutral only because the tax system beneath it is doing the redistribution — you cannot import the subsidy without importing the tax base, and if you did, you would achieve the same thing more cheaply by taxing graduates directly, which is my move (3) again, so the debate has gone full circle back to income-contingent repayment. (8) B: it has gone in a circle only because you keep assuming the beneficiaries are whoever graduates under the system we currently have; the question is not who pays for the graduates we have but what happens to the number and the composition of graduates once the price disappears, and that is a question your incidence arithmetic is not even addressed to.

Could you go through this move by move and tell me who is right at each step, rather than only who wins overall? For each of the eight, give a rating from clearly mistaken to clearly right (with mixed in the middle) and one or two sentences of justification. Then do three further things. First, my suspicion is that they are sliding between at least three distinct questions — who ultimately bears the cost, who is deterred from enrolling, and what a price does to institutional behaviour — and that several replies answer a different question from the one they were aimed at; say for each move which of the three it is actually about and whether the reply meets it or dodges it. Second, list the empirical premises each of your verdicts depends on, and mark which of them could in principle be settled by evidence and which are value commitments wearing an empirical costume. Third, tell me what the two of them are jointly taking for granted and never argue about — I noticed neither of them ever asks what a degree is worth if everyone has one, nor whether the case for public subsidy rests on fairness or on spillovers to the wider economy, and I suspect those omissions matter more than anything they actually said. End with an overall verdict on who came out ahead and a statement of what would have to be true for that verdict to flip the other way. Please don't ask me for more detail about the evening; work with the argument as I've reported it.
