Acting as a political philosopher who also knows enough First Amendment doctrine not to embarrass yourself, work through the following case study with me. Think carefully before you start typing, and begin by listing — in a couple of lines, for yourself and me — the three or four facts of the episode that are actually load-bearing for the analysis, so we know what the whole thing is going to turn on. Then do the analysis.

The episode is Skokie, 1977–78. The facts as I have them (flag it if you think I've got any of this wrong rather than quietly going along with it): Frank Collin's tiny National Socialist Party of America, which had been marching in a Chicago park, wrote to a bunch of North Shore suburbs in the fall of 1976 asking for permits to demonstrate. Most ignored him. Skokie — a village with a large Jewish population including a lot of concentration-camp survivors — first decided the best response was to ignore the group too, on the theory that no publicity meant no platform. Local Jewish organizations objected loudly to that approach, the mayor and village board reversed course, and in the spring of 1977 the village went to court for an injunction barring the marchers from wearing Nazi uniforms or displaying swastikas, then passed three ordinances: a ban on military-style uniforms at demonstrations, a ban on distributing material that incites hatred, and a $350,000 insurance bond requirement for any demonstration. The ACLU took Collin's side, assigned two lawyers to it, took a public beating for it, and lost members and donations over it. The courts eventually gutted the restrictions, holding among other things that marching in a swastika through that community wasn't unprotected fighting words, and the Skokie march itself never happened — the group demonstrated in Chicago instead.

The theory is Mill's harm principle: the only legitimate ground for coercing someone against his will is preventing harm to others, with his own good, physical or moral, not sufficient warrant.

What I want is the mapping done properly and then the two-way test.

First, run the machinery. Who is the party whose liberty is at issue here, and against whom — the state, or also the local community whose pressure changed the village's mind? What is the harm being alleged, and does it clear Mill's bar? Run it twice, deliberately: once on a narrow reading of harm as a setback to interests, and once on a reading that takes in dignitary and status harm, the harm of being publicly marked as fit to be exterminated in the town where you live. Which reading does Mill himself plausibly hold, given the rest of the book rather than just the famous sentence?

Second, deal with the escape hatches. There's the bit in On Liberty about an opinion that grain merchants are starving the poor being unobjectionable when circulated in print but punishable when delivered to an angry crowd assembled outside a merchant's house — say whether anything in Skokie resembles that, given months of advance notice, a permit application, and no mob. And there's the "tyranny of the majority" worry, which Mill treats as the central danger of democratic self-government: what happens to that machinery when the people pushing for suppression are a traumatized local minority with real political influence in one village, and the people being suppressed are a despised handful with none? Does the principle still fire, fire backwards, or just go quiet?

Third, tell me where the harm principle is silent, and don't force it to speak. I'm thinking of at least: symbolic conduct that makes no proposition anyone could argue with; the state's own interest in what it expresses by permitting a march; and whether an institution like the ACLU defending a right it detests is Millian consistency or a way of outsourcing a moral judgment. If you think there are better examples of silence than mine, use yours.

Bottom line first, in two or three sentences, then the analysis. Hard cap of 900 words — I'd rather you cut something than pad it. No reading list, no citations, no throat-clearing about how complicated free speech is. And end with your actual verdict: does this episode confirm the harm principle, embarrass it, or expose that it was never built to decide cases like this one?
