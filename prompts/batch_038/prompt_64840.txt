So I just came off a three-hour argument with my friend Dana about the second of Nietzsche's Untimely Meditations, the one on history for life, and I've lost my voice but not my nerve. Her position: the three species of history are just a tidy triad with no teeth, and the essay is really a case against the past in general, since Nietzsche ends up on the side of forgetting and the grazing animal. My position: each mode answers a different need of a living thing — the one who's trying to act, the one who's trying to hold on, the one who's suffering and needs to be freed from it — and each one goes rotten in its own particular direction, and the deepest move in the whole thing is that you can't step outside your inheritance to judge it, because the judge is made of the stuff he's sentencing. Neither of us budged, and now I want to test whether my version can actually be *shown* rather than argued.

Here's what I want: a crown of exactly five stanzas, six lines each, loose four-beat lines, numbered 1 through 5, and nothing else — no title, no introduction, no note at the end explaining it. Set it in a small town I'll seed for you. There's a bronze soldier in the square being re-gilded by a sculptor who works from an old photograph and keeps touching up the jaw because he wants the man to look like someone you'd follow. There's the historical society, which keeps every reciept going back to 1911, including the ones for cleaning that statue, in a room that smells of wet wool. And there's a woman with a petition to have the thing melted down, who found out her own grandmother chaired the fundraising committee. Stanza 1 is the sculptor, stanza 2 the society's secretary, stanza 3 the woman with the petition — and in stanza 3 she should realize partway through that the indictment she's writing has her grandmother's name at the top of it. Stanza 4 is someone who can see all three clearly, sympathise with all three, understand every age of the town equally well, and therefore never once does anything: the fair judge who's fair to the point of paralysis. Stanza 5 is the town in early spring, life itself, the thing that grows out of what it was handed and heals its own wounds.

The formal constraint is the whole point, so please don't drop it: stanzas 2 through 5 each have to open by repeating the previous stanza's *last* line, but damaged — exactly one word removed or swapped — so what gets handed down is never quite what was given. By stanza 5 that carried line should be a worn-down fragment. Then stanza 5's final line must be stanza 1's opening line, whole and unchanged, closing the ring — and it has to land as inescapability, not triumph, the town discovering it never got outside its past but only got a horizon to see it from. Two more things: don't use the words monumental, antiquarian or critical anywhere, and don't quote Nietzsche or put sentences in his mouth — I'd rather have the ideas rendered in brick, bronze and paper than cited, especially since I can't check quotes. Somebody who knows the essay cold should be able to say which stanza is which mode and where each one starts to rot; somebody who's never read it should just get a good poem about a statue in a town.
