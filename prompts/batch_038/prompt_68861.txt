I'm one of nine people on the board of a small AI safety nonprofit — about a dozen staff, mostly technical, plus a couple who write. At next Thursday's meeting we have to vote on a proposal from one of our researchers, and I've been asked to circulate a short written opinion beforehand so the board isn't hear it cold for the first time in the room. Can you draft it for me?

The proposal has two parts. Part one: we adopt, on our public policy page, a standing precommitment to something like a surrogate goal. Concretely, we designate one arbitrary-seeming thing we agree to care about *for threat-response purposes* — the researcher's suggestion is the complete historical archive of an old rationality forum we've been mirroring for years, roughly four million comments, which costs us real storage plus a fraction of one persons FTE. We commit in advance that if anyone tries to move us by threatening something we care about — including anyone we'll never meet, never observe, who might be reasoning about us rather than acting on us, at any distance in the future — we treat the threat as though it's aimed at the archive, and we don't pay out of anything else. Part two: we publish a writeup of the reasoning so other orgs can copy the pattern.

The logic, roughly as the researcher pitched it: a threat only has force if the thing threatened is something you'd actually trade to protect. If the set of things you'd pay to protect is bounded and publicly fixed ahead of time, threats aimed at your real values lose their grip, and the best available move against you is to attack the decoy — which is comparatively cheap to lose. But this only works if we *do* care about the decoy, not merely say we do; anything that models our dispositions well will see straight through a policy that never shows up in how we behave. So the proposal includes deliberately investing in the archive: budget it, celebrate it, treat its loss as a real institutional harm, so the caring isn't a lie.

Board composition, because who objects matters more than the abstract arguments here. Two ML people think the underlying decision theory is fine but the org-level version is unfalsifiable theater. One philosopher thinks talk about agents we can't causally interact with is empty and wants to know what it could even mean for such a threat to be credible. Our lawyer worries a public precommitment document is a written record of what we'd trade away, and of the fact that we have a policy about being threatened at all. Our ops director points out that "invest in the decoy until we genuinely care" means spending maybe $60–80k of a $3M budget to manufacture something we can then be squeezed over. One member holds her seat at the pleasure of our largest funder and says that funder will read the public writeup as evidence we've gone around the bend, with a $400k renewal coming in the spring. Two of us, me included, lean toward some version of yes but can't say what would make it work.

One wrinkle I want handled rather than ignored: the archive isn't neutral. A few staff met on that forum, one researcher's earliest work is in there, and the ops director has said privately she'd be devastated to lose it. So our "decoy" is something several of us already care about for unrelated reasons. Does that defeat the whole mechanism, or is pre-existing caring the only kind that could ever be real enough to work?

The memo: recommend one concrete course of action — adopt both parts, adopt part one without publishing, adopt a stripped-down version, table it pending something specific, or reject it — and give reasons a nine-person board can actually act on Thursday. Take the ops director's objection seriously as the strongest thing on the losing side: we'd be spending real money and real credibility to build a hostage and then advertise where it's kept. Tell me what, if anything, would make this kind of precommitment credible rather than decorative, what the minimal version looks like if a minimal version is worth having, and where our own reasoning is softest, so I'm not blindsided. Also give me a paragraph or two I could basically read aloud if the philosopher says the whole thing is incoherent.

Keep it under about 1100 words — nobody reads past that in the packet. No references or reading list, nobody here is going to look anything up and I'd rather you not gesture vaguely at papers. Assume no one on the board knows the jargon: explain the mechanism in plain language, and if you do need the term, define it once and move on. Write it as me, since I'm the one circulating it, not as an outside consultant advising me. And don't make it too stiff — small org, we all know each other, a memo that reads like it came out of a law firm just gets eye-rolled.
