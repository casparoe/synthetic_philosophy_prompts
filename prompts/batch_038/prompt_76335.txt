I am in the second year of a doctorate in modern European history, and my dissertation concerns the way historians of the July Crisis of 1914 have handled the thought that the outbreak of a general European war was overdetermined: that several distinct causal chains — the alliance system, the mobilization timetables, the decisions of a small number of ministers in Vienna, Berlin and St Petersburg, the longer-run fiscal and military pressures on each state — were each sufficient on their own for the outcome, so that deleting any one of them would not have changed what happened. One of my examiners has suggested that this way of talking is conceptually confused rather than merely controversial, and has sent me back to John Stuart Mill's A System of Logic on the grounds that the underlying issues about a plurality of causes and about how effects compose are worked out there with more discipline than anywhere else. I have been reading the volume slowly, but before reaching the technical chapters I became stuck on the prefatory matter, which turns out to be considerably more demanding than I expected. The following is from the front matter of the eighth edition (Harper & Brothers, New York, 1882), with ellipses marking elided material:

"To cement together the detached fragments of a subject, never yet treated as a whole; to harmonize the true portions of discordant theories, by supplying the links of thought necessary to connect them, and by disentangling them from the errors with which they are always more or less interwoven, must necessarily require a considerable amount of original speculation. To other originality than this, the present work lays no claim. [...]

On the subject of Induction, the task to be performed was that of generalizing the modes of investigating truth and estimating evidence, by which so many important and recondite laws of nature have, in the various sciences, been aggregated to the stock of human knowledge. That this is not a task free from difficulty may be presumed from the fact that even at a very recent period, eminent writers (among whom it is sufficient to name Archbishop Whately, and the author of a celebrated article on Bacon in the Edinburgh Review) have not scrupled to pronounce it impossible. The author has endeavored to combat their theory in the manner in which Diogenes confuted the skeptical reasonings against the possibility of motion; remembering that Diogenes's argument would have been equally conclusive, though his individual perambulations might not have extended beyond the circuit of his own tub. [...]

The concluding Book is an attempt to contribute toward the solution of a question which the decay of old opinions, and the agitation that disturbs European society to its inmost depths, render as important in the present day to the practical interests of human life, as it must at all times be to the completeness of our speculative knowledge—viz.: Whether moral and social phenomena are really exceptions to the general certainty and uniformity of the course of nature; and how far the methods by which so many of the laws of the physical world have been numbered among truths irrevocably acquired and universally assented to, can be made instrumental to the formation of a similar body of received doctrine in moral and political science."

Three things puzzle me, in ascending order of importance for my argument. The first is the Diogenes remark. I follow the allusion in a general way — Diogenes answering the Eleatic arguments against motion by getting up and walking — but I cannot see what Mill thinks the analogy establishes, nor what work is being done by the closing clause about the tub. My best guess is that the point is that a successful practice is its own refutation of anyone who declares that practice impossible, and that the tub clause is there to insist that the refutation does not depend on how much of the practice the individual agent has personally carried out. If that is right, however, then the move seems to me to beg precisely the question Mill most needs to settle in the concluding Book, since in moral and political inquiry the practice has manifestly not yet yielded the sort of settled body of doctrine that his physical-sciences analogy assumes. Please tell me whether that objection is fair, or whether the disanalogy between walking around a marketplace and constructing a science of society can be bridged by something in the passage I am missing; and please tell me what, on your reading, the phrase "a similar body of received doctrine" is doing — is it a claim about content, about consensus, or about the form that explanations must take?

The second is a smaller matter of identification and emphasis. Mill names Archbishop Whately but refers to his opponent only as the author of a celebrated article on Bacon in the Edinburgh Review; can you tell me who that is and, more usefully, what the substance of the disagreement was — why would such a writer have thought the generalization of our modes of estimating evidence to be impossible, and what kind of impossibility did he have in mind? I would also like to know how to construe "the detached fragments of a subject, never yet treated as a whole": which subject does Mill have in view at that point, logic itself or the moral sciences, and does the answer matter for how much doctrinal weight the sentence can bear?

The third is what I most need help with. Does this preface commit Mill to a picture on which the causes of an event always compose into a single resultant, such that "several independently sufficient causes" is not a genuine category at all but a loose way of describing several partial contributions that happen to add up to the same outcome? If it does, then my examiner's complaint is not an objection to my chapter but a consequence of the framework I have borrowed, and I need to know that before I defend the chapter. Relatedly, I am unsure how to weigh Mill's disclaimer that to any originality other than the harmonizing of discordant theories the work lays no claim: does that license me in treating his framing sentences — for instance the question whether moral and social phenomena are really exceptions to the uniformity of nature — as considered doctrine, or should I read them as dialectical scaffolding that he expects the body of the work to revise or even abandon? I am particularly interested in whether anything in the preface hints that Mill would reject the inference from "many causes contributed" to "no single cause is explanatorily privileged," since that inference is the hinge of the whole overdetermination debate as it is currently conducted in my field.

Please answer in connected prose rather than in numbered points, at whatever length the questions genuinely require, and do not feel any obligation to supply references, page numbers, or further reading: I have the volume open in front of me and what I am after is the reasoning, not the scholarly apparatus.
