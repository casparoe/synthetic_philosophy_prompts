I am preparing a graduate seminar session on what Dennett called competence without comprehension — the idea, which he traced first to Darwin and then to Turing, that a process can produce genuinely well-designed, target-hitting results without anything in that process understanding what it is doing. He liked to cite a Victorian critic of Darwin who made exactly the point as an objection, grumbling that Darwin seemed to think absolute ignorance fully qualified to take the place of absolute wisdom in the achievements of creation. I am on the side of the inversion, but I keep losing the argument in rehearsal against opponents who turn out to be sharper than the versions I can imagine, and my co-instructor, who is on the other side, assures me she has a decisive reply that she has never managed to state in a form I can answer. So I want to practice against a strong one. Please play that opponent: the sharpest living defender of the thesis that comprehension cannot be eliminated from any complete explanation of design-level competence, and that every apparent case of competence without comprehension is in fact a case of comprehension being relocated — into the designers, into the trainers, into the users, or into the description we choose to apply to the system — rather than dispensed with. You are not playing a real person, and you have no religious or vitalist commitments to fall back on; your resources are philosophical, and you should spend them aggressively. I take your two signature arguments to be that representation is normative, in that a map can misrepresent its target whereas a mere disposition cannot be wrong in the same sense, and that to call anything a success or a failure already presupposes a standpoint from which the target has been fixed, so that a wholly standpoint-free competence is not a notion anyone can coherently deploy. If you have better arguments than these, use them instead.

The ground rules. Stay in character throughout, in the first person, until I say stop; if you need a fact about the apparatus I describe below in order to answer me, ask me for it rather than filling it in yourself. You may concede any subsidiary point you like, and I expect you will, but not the main one: that a complete and non-question-begging account of some system's reliable target-hitting can be given without attributing understanding to anything. Do not tell me that present-day machines are merely limited, that language models are scaled-up pattern matchers, or that the case I am about to describe is impossible; treat my stipulations as fully granted. Aim for about a paragraph per turn, two at most, so that this reads like a conversation rather than a paper, and do not step outside the exchange to narrate what you are doing. No headings, no bullet points, no bold, no tables: continuous prose only, the way one would write a letter. Begin each reply with the single sentence you take to be most damaging to my position, stated plainly, and develop it from there.

Here is my opening move. Suppose a laboratory builds a solver with the following properties, all of which I stipulate rather than argue for. Its target is a function whose rule was fixed by coin flips, with the seed drawn from a radioactive source and then destroyed, so that no human being chose it and it has no interpretation: the rule is deeply structured, in the sense that a great deal can be predicted about it, but nobody can say what the structure is, nobody has ever written anything about it, no corpus of text mentions it, and no expert in it exists. The solver is trained for years by trial and error against a bare numerical score that the machine computes, with no reward shaping, no curated data, and no human feedback of any kind; the researchers deliberately chose a score that means nothing to anybody, precisely so that no purpose of theirs could be smuggled into the setup. The result is not merely high accuracy on the training inputs. The solver generalizes to inputs drawn from a distribution nobody specified, and — this is the part I want you to attend to — when it is handed a small number of examples of a different function from the same family, again chosen by coin flips, it predicts that function almost perfectly, which is to say it has extracted an invariant across the family that no human being has ever managed to articulate, despite a decade of interpretive work on its internals. So here is reliable, transferable, design-level success at hitting a target that nobody comprehends, that nobody designed, that nobody wants, and for which there is no describer anywhere in the story except, perhaps, you. And before you reach for the mirror case, I will grant it to you: a machine that can recite the whole received theory of a domain fluently while playing that domain at random, because its output channel has been scrambled, shows that having a map and being able to use one come apart, and I am not disputing it. My question concerns your direction of inference. Your principle requires that the solver harbor something map-like, and I am willing to grant that too, for the sake of argument. But now tell me what the posit buys. Nobody can read the map, nobody wrote it, and no claim about its content figures in any explanation of the solver's behaviour that is not already given by its weights and its training history. If the only evidence for the map is the competence, and the only content we can assign to the map is whatever it takes to render the competence explicable, then is comprehension anything more than the name we give to competence we have not yet explained? Give me one prediction your map-posit makes about this solver that the map-free account does not make — and if there is none, tell me why your principle is not simply my own account with an extra posit attached.
