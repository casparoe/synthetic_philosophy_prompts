I teach a philosophy-of-biology seminar whose middle weeks are devoted to the species problem, and every year the same thing happens. The students can recite that the biological, phylogenetic, ecological, and cohesion concepts pull apart from one another; they can list the standard objections to each; and then the discussion dies, because the whole business starts to feel like a quarrel over a word. What it never feels like to them is a thing that has to be decided by particular people on a particular afternoon, with consequences attached. I'd like you to write a short story that supplies that missing texture — not an essay in narrative clothing, but a piece of fiction in which the conceptual difficulty arrives as an obstacle the characters have to get around, and in which getting around it costs someone something.

Here is the situation I want you to build from, which you may develop and rearrange but should keep recognizable. In an invented northern jurisdiction, a Standing Committee on Nomenclature and Listing must determine whether a small cold-water fish — call it the Kessan dace — occurs in the lake district as one variable species or as several. The populations are arranged in a chain around a retired ice sheet: each lake's fish breed freely with their neighbors in the connecting streams, and the chain closes in a terminal lake where the two end forms meet and do not interbreed, although the water between them is a few hundred meters wide. The genomicist on the committee has data indicating that the terminal forms are not each other's closest relatives, so the ring is not a clean ring; the morphologist has four decades of tray work showing that the terminal forms differ in gill raker counts by more than any other pair of populations in the chain; the committee's oldest member holds that all of this is a category mistake, and says so at length. What makes the case live rather than academic is that the statute permits the listing not only of species but of a distinct demographic segment of a vertebrate, which appears to let the committee declare a protected entity without ever settling what kind of thing it is protecting — and an upstream diversion project turns on the answer.

Work in this complication, or replace it with a better one of comparable weight: the type specimen behind the original description is a juvenile; its type locality is now inundated behind a dam built in the 1960s; and the collector's field labels — the only record of where along the chain each sample was actually taken — were re-sorted by a museum intern decades ago and may not be in the order he wrote them. The historical anchor of the entire nomenclature may therefore be a fiction, and the committee has to decide whether to notice. As for craft: third-person limited on the morphologist, between 1,500 and 2,200 words, with at least one scene among collection drawers and one scene in which the committee's bylaws — a tripartite test requiring separate findings on interbreeding, monophyly, and ecological distinctness, governed by a tie-breaking rule nobody likes — are actually applied rather than described. No character may deliver a speech explaining what a species concept is, and no paragraph may step outside the scene to gloss the ideas for the reader; the theory must be legible from what people do with specimens, ballots, and each other. End on the ballot or immediately before it, without telling us how the vote went or what the fish turned out to be. Keep the register dry and institutional, closer to a well-run public inquiry than to nature writing.
