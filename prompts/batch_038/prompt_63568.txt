I'm auditing a decision theory seminar this term, mostly to pick up math I skipped as an undergrad, and this week's packet is on prospects that don't have a well-defined expectation. Alongside the assigned papers someone in the reading group passed around a long essay from a rationalist-adjacent blog that tries to explain the whole literature to outsiders. It's honestly the clearest thing I've read all week, which is exactly what worries me — I can't tell if it's clear because it's right or clear because it's sanding off the parts that are hard. Here's the central stretch, more or less as written:

> "The Pasadena game, introduced by Harris Nover and Alan Hajek in 2004, is what you get when you give the St. Petersburg game a minus sign. A fair coin is flipped until it lands heads. If the first head comes on toss n, you win 2^n/n dollars when n is odd and lose 2^n/n when n is even — so the first toss is worth +$2, the second -$2, the third +$8/3, the fourth -$4. Weight each payoff by its probability, 1/2^n, and every toss contributes exactly ±1/n, so the expectation is the alternating harmonic series 1 − 1/2 + 1/3 − 1/4 + ..., which converges to ln 2, about 69 cents. But it converges only conditionally: sum the absolute values and you get the harmonic series, which blows up. And a conditionally convergent series can be rearranged to converge to anything you like, or to nothing. So the correct statement isn't 'the Pasadena game is worth about 69 cents,' it's 'the Pasadena game has no expected value.' This is the same trouble the St. Petersburg game is in, only worse — there the expectation was infinite, here it's undefined, and infinity is just a very big undefined. The obvious repair is what Easwaran called the weak expectation: cut the series off at some large term and treat the leftover tail as negligible, which gets you ln 2 back and lets you price the game again. Hajek and Nover's follow-up adds the Altadena game, Pasadena's twin with the terms in a different order, where every finite truncation of one looks better than the matching truncation of the other. The lesson is that these 'gambles' were never objects out in the world; they're series, and a series is only as determinate as the order you happen to write it in. Decision theory spent three centuries assuming a gamble is a thing that has a value. Pasadena shows it's a thing that has a notation. So expected utility theory isn't merely incomplete at the margins — it's refuted, and no pile of extra axioms can rescue it, because the problem was never the axioms. It's that the quantity the axioms quantify over fails to exist."

Can you go through this claim by claim and sort it into four piles: what a decision theorist who actually works on this would sign without qualification, what's a legitimate simplification for a general audience, what's a stretch that a specialist would want hedged, and what's just wrong? Four places I specifically want pressure on: the infinite-versus-undefined line, since the essay treats infinity as a variety of undefined and I have a vague sense that's backwards; whether the rearrangement theorem really licenses the move from "no strong expectation" to "no fact of the matter about its value" to "any price you like is defensible"; whether the gloss on weak expectation is accurate, because that term does all the load-bearing work in the second half and I suspect truncating a series and taking a weak expectation aren't the same operation at all; and whether the Altadena paragraph gets the point of that example, or inverts it. Last thing — the essay's closing move is that the theory is refuted rather than incomplete, and that axioms can't help because the trouble is upstream of them. Does that conclusion survive once the earlier claims are corrected, or was it leaning on the errors? I'm inclined to think the essay is basically fair and the specialists are just being precious about terminology, so tell me straight if that instinct is wrong. Answer in American English.
