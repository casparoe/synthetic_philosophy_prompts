Hey — I'm a third year in political theory, but my background is early modern stuff (Hobbes, Locke, the contract tradition), so I came to Aristotle late and I'm still shaky on the Greek-side details. Our reading group just did Politics Book I and somebody passed around a couple of paragraphs from a trade-press history of philosophy, not our assigned reading, just something another guy found. Honestly it's the clearest short account of natural slavery I've read, and that's what worries me: I can't tell whether the clarity is earned. Here's roughly what it says (typed up from a photo I took, so any weird punctuation is mine):

"Aristotle's defense of slavery in the first book of the Politics is refreshingly blunt. Some human beings, he says, are born without the capacity for rational deliberation; they have bodies suited to labor and souls suited only to obey. Because nature made them this way, they are property by nature, tools belonging to another person, no different in kind from an ox or a spade. Aristotle does draw one useful distinction: the master rules the slave despotically, as the soul rules the body, while free citizens rule one another politically, as reason rules appetite. This gives him a justification for empire as well. Since barbarains lack the spirited element that makes Greeks unwilling to be ruled, enslaving them in war is not merely expedient but just, and Aristotle says so without hedging. It is worth noting that his argument quietly undoes itself: he concedes that nature often produces free souls in slavish bodies, and that a great many actual slaves are slaves only because they were on the losing side of a war. For Aristotle slavery is natural in principle and unjust in practice, a distinction he never seems to notice he has made."

Can you audit this for me? Go through it more or less line by line and tell me what a classicist or an ancient-philosophy person would just sign off on, what's a legitimate trade-book simplification, and what's an actual distortion. A few specific things I keep going back and forth on:

1. Is "born without the capacity for rational deliberation" right? I have a feeling Aristotle's formulation is more specific than that — something about the slave's relation to reason being different in kind from simply not having it — and that the difference matters for whether the view is the crude thing this passage describes. Also does "born" smuggle in a heredity claim he doesn't make?

2. Does the ox-or-spade comparison survive contact with what he says about instruments of action versus instruments of production? I remember that distinction being load-bearing for him and I can't see how a spade fits it.

3. He's presented as endorsing wartime enslavement of the defeated. My vague memory is that he actually argues against the "the stronger must be the better" move that would license it, and that he grants that plenty of people who are enslaved are enslaved unjustly. Is that memory right? And where does the ethnic material genuinely enter, since the passage treats it as part of the definition of the natural slave?

4. The two analogies — soul/body for despotic rule, reason/appetite for political rule. Did the author line those up correctly, or has something been reassigned between the two pairs? I ask because if reason's rule over appetite is the model for rule among equals, the whole master-slave contrast looks less clean than the passage wants.

5. The closing move, that the tension between natural-in-principle and unjust-in-practice is one Aristotle "never seems to notice." Is that a fair hit, or does he flag the difficulty himself, maybe even say something generous about the people who disagree with him?

I don't need a bibliography or a list of where to look — I'll chase down the Greek myself. I just want to know how much of this passage I can trust going into the next session, and whether its overall verdict on the argument is fair even in the places where the details turn out to be wrong. If the verdict survives the corrections, I'd rather know that too.
