Second-year epistemology PhD here — dissertation is going to be on epistemic value (why knowledge is worth more than mere true belief, that whole territory), and I've spent the last month in the knowability literature as background: verificationism, the Church-Fitch argument, the whole cottage industry of responses. But the thing that's actually stuck with me isn't the paradox itself. It's a shape I keep seeing in arguments that aren't obviously related, and I don't have a word for it, which is annoying because I want to say something about it in my lit review and right now all I can do is gesture.

The shape is this. Someone puts forward a general principle that looks totally harmless — every truth is such that it could be known, say. Then you refute it by building a sentence: take some truth nobody knows, and consider the conjunction of that truth with its own unknownness. That conjunction is a truth, and it can't be known, so the principle is false. But notice what actually happened. The principle was never in any trouble over any sentence anyone had managed to write down beforehand. It gets refuted only by a sentence you could construct *because* you already had the principle in front of you and knew how to diagonalize with it. So the defender restricts — only apply it to sentences whose being known is consistent, or relativize it so the troublesome instance isn't in scope, or ban the offending vocabulary — and now the principle comes out true again of everything that was available before the refutation. And then the whole dispute collapses into whether that restriction is ad hoc or principled, with the critic saying you're just walling off the counterexample and the defender saying no, the entire class of counterexamples was only ever reachable through the argument itself, so walling it off is exactly what a correct account should do. Same thing with the verification principle of meaning: it isn't itself verifiable, and the standard reply is that it isn't a factual claim at all but a proposal or a definition, so it doesn't have to satisfy its own standard. And Popper's falsifiability criterion isn't itself falsifiable, and his reply was roughly that adopting the criterion is a decision rather than an empirical hypothesis. I'm genuinely unsure whether the unexpected exam belongs in this family — there the announcement seems to destroy the conditions that made it meaningful once the student reasons about it, which feels adjacent but maybe importantly different.

So: is there an established term for the phenomenon where a general principle is refuted only by instances you can build *using* the principle, so that it looks true of everything antecedently available and the remaining disagreement is entirely about whether a restriction to that pre-refutation class is principled or ad hoc? I know the neighbourhood — self-refutation, revenge problems, diagonalization, Dummett's indefinite extensibility, hierarchy solutions — and I know about Tennant's Cartesian restriction and Edgington's actuality-operator move, but none of those seem to name *this*. They name the sentence, or the proof technique, or one particular repair. What I want named is the situation, plus its edges.
