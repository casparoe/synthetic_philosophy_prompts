I'm a backend engineer with no philosophy background to speak of (one ethics podcast and half a book is the extent of it), and last Thursday I ended up sitting through an arguement at our team offsite that I keep turning over in my head. We were doing the yearly review of our incident process — we've run blameless postmortems for about five years now — and Dana, our SRE lead, got into it with Ravi, an eng manager from another org, over whether blamelessness is actually a good idea, which somehow turned into a fight about guilt versus shame that was way over my head. I happened to be at the whiteboard keeping notes, so I've got their moves roughly in order:

(1) Ravi opened by saying guilt is clearly the better moral emotion, because guilt is about the act — I did a bad thing — so it points outward, at whoever got hurt and at repair, whereas shame is about the self — I am bad — so it points inward and makes you want to hide. (2) Dana said the distinction is real but doesn't survive contact with an org chart: a postmortem doc is public, searchable, and linked from three Slack channels, so whatever you label it, the engineer experiences the exposure as shame. And blamelessness removes the repair channel, since you can't apologize for something the process insists wasn't your fault, so guilt with nowhere to go just ferments into shame — meaning blameless postmortems don't eliminate shame, they manufacture it. (3) Ravi said that conflates the emotion with the publicity around it. A culture can't choose people's feelings directly, but it can choose how much is public and to whom, and publicity is precisely the lever that converts guilt into shame, so a culture can choose indirectly, and indirect choice is still choice. (4) Dana said fine, but you don't actually control publicity — DMs, the rumor mill, the incident resurfacing in a promo packet six months later. On top of that, guilt presupposes an internalized standard you don't control either; if the engineer doesn't already have the conscience, shame's reputational teeth are the only lever you've got. So even if guilt is the nicer emotion, shame is the one a manager can deploy. (5) Ravi's closer was that this is self-defeating: if the standard lives in the self, the cheapest way to satisfy it is to disappear — don't report the near miss, don't volunteer that you touched that config last Tuesday. Shame is information-hiding, and an incident process is fundamentally an information-gathering machine. (6) Dana answered that Ravi is smuggling in the assumption that guilt carries standards, when actually guilt lets you pay the fine and move on; shame is what makes someone want to be the kind of engineer who'd never ship that, and that's the only thing that produces real excellence rather than compliance.

Both of them seemed to accept that act-focused guilt versus self-focused shame is the right cut, and they disagreed about (a) whether that cut stays seperate once publicity is involved and (b) whether a culture can choose. Nobody conceded anything and I honestly couldn't tell who'd won. Can you referee it move by move — a short rating for each of the six moves, something like clearly right / partly right / clearly wrong, plus two or three sentences of justification — and then say who's more right overall and what both of them are missing? To be clear this isn't advice about our process, I just want the exchange adjudicated as argued. Please lead with a one-sentence bottom line before you go move by move, keep the whole thing under 600 words, and write it in American English.
