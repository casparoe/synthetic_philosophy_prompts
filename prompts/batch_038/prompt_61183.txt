I'm writing a seminar paper for a philosophy-of-mind course, and the position I've ended up defending is a specific version of the thesis Schrödinger took over from Vedanta in his later philosophical work — the epilogue "On Determinism and Free Will" in *What Is Life?* (1944), *Mind and Matter* (the 1956 Tarner Lectures, published 1958), and *My View of the World* (1961). Not the slogan that we are all one, which I find useless, but the more precise claim I take him to be pressing: that experience has no principle of individuation. On my formulation, mind behaves like a mass term; the plural "minds" counts bodies, or mutually inaccessible informational channels, rather than distinct subjects, and there is no further fact that could make one stream of consciousness numerically different from another over and above the fact that the streams are differently situated in the physical world. I explicitly reject the reading on which your experiences are literally mine, since that either makes the view absurd or makes it a claim about memory and self-ascription that is trivially false.

Tell me what would count against this. I want the defeaters sorted by how much damage they would actually do, and I want you to be strict about separating what would falsify the thesis, what would leave it intact but drain it of interest, and what merely sounds like an objection. Begin with the empirical candidates I keep getting in seminar and say whether I'm right to wave them off: split-brain patients in whom the two hemispheres can be shown to hold different information; dissociative identity disorder with its apparently discrepant reports and skills; the fact that anaesthetizing one body leaves another untouched; and the developmental and behavioural divergence between humans and other animals, which seems to require many subjects maturing at many rates. Then give me the ones I have not thought of. Two questions especially: is there any conceivable observation, experiment, or third-person finding that would demonstrate that streams of experience come in a determinate number — and would a successful naturalistic account of what binds experience *within* one organism, some integration or global-broadcast criterion that yielded a principled cutoff between organisms, count against me, or would my thesis simply absorb it as a claim about bodies and channels rather than about subjects? And on the argumentative side: does the asymmetry between my direct acquaintance with this body's experience and my merely inferential attitude to yours require that there be two subjects, or does that objection presuppose what it needs to establish?

Finally, assess the exposure honestly, including the objection I fear most — that the view has been defined into compatibility with every possible world, since whatever the neuroscience delivers a defender can say it concerns channels and organisms, never the number of subjects. Is that fatal here, or can a metaphysical thesis of this kind be legitimately insulated, and if insulated, is that a defect or simply the wrong kind of thing to demand falsifiability of? If it is insulated, sketch the nearest version that keeps the Vedantic inspiration but risks something: a claim about which physical configurations are accompanied by experience, or about the moral attitude that identity — as opposed to mere impartiality — would license, or about the significance of death. What does my version predict that a pluralist rival does not? And because I will be defending this to people who want to know how much weight your analysis carries: state how confident you are in the ranking you give, and single out the point in your own argument where you are least sure you are right, whether that is a claim about the empirical literature or a claim about what the thesis is even committed to.
