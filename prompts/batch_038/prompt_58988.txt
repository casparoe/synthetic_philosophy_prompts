I sit on the model-behavior review board at a company that ships a customer-facing assistant, and we vote Friday on a rewrite of one of our standing policies. The current policy requires the assistant to flag its own status: when it offers a substantive judgment, it appends language to the effect that it holds no beliefs and is only producing text from its training. QA has flagged a large class of turns where the same response does both things at once — it states a confident conclusion (the discrepancy is almost certainly a timezone bug in the batch job) and then, two sentences later, disclaims having any view at all. The board has split three ways. One camp says the disclaimer is legally required candor about what the product is, and that dropping it would mislead users. A second camp says the pairing reads as the system contradicting itself, that users lose trust in it, and that the fix is simply to delete the disclaimer. A third camp wants to keep a disclaimer but reword it so it never mentions belief or opinion.

My own instinct, for what it's worth: the badness here isn't an ordinary contradiction, because both halves seem like they could be true — the output genuinely carries information about the bug, and the system genuinely has no beliefs in whatever sense we mean. Something else is going wrong, and I suspect it concerns what an agent commits itself to in flatly asserting something, as opposed to what is true. I'd like the memo to take that intuition seriously rather than treating assertion as information delivery with extra steps.

Please draft the memo I'll circulate, roughly one page: recommend one of the three options, give reviewers a test they can apply to a flagged turn in a couple of minutes, and engage the candor camp's strongest argument on its merits instead of assuming it away. Write for an audience of engineers and one lawyer, none of whom have a philosophy background.
