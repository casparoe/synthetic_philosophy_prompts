A friend of mine who works on peer disagreement and I argued for most of last night about higher-order evidence, and the argument did not resolve. He conceded very little, and the one charge he pressed repeatedly concerns a view I hold that I have never managed to defend to my own satisfaction. Before I try to write any of this up, I would like an audit of my package conducted from the inside — tracing what commits me to what — rather than by comparison with anyone else's position.

Here are my commitments, stated as precisely as I can.

1. **Permissivism.** For a fixed body of first-order evidence bearing on p, more than one doxastic attitude toward p can be rational. I deny that the standards of rationality single out a unique rational credence. Where a range is permitted, an agent anywhere within it is fully rational, not merely excused.

2. **Conciliation.** When someone I take to be my epistemic peer about p disagrees with me, and I have no reason to suspect a particular slip on either side, rationality requires me to move substantially toward her attitude. Steadfastness in such a case is a vice I would not defend in anyone else.

3. **The Mediation View** (my own label, not a term of art). Evidence about my own cognitive condition — that I am hypoxic, sleep-deprived, intoxicated, or predictably biased — never bears directly on the worldly question. What it bears on is the degree to which my first-order evidence supports p. It can move my credence in p, but only by way of that assessment, never by contributing content about p itself.

4. **Calibration.** Once I have formed a belief by some route and formed an estimate of how reliable that route was, my rational credence is fixed by that estimate together with the rest of what I know: I should hold p to the degree to which I take myself to have arrived at the belief reliably. I am not entitled to keep the output of a route I have just judged unreliable.

5. **The enkratic requirement, for full belief.** It is never rational to believe p while believing that believing p is irrational in my circumstances. Akrasia of that shape is a genuine failure of rationality, not a harmless oddity.

6. The requirement does **not** extend to graded attitudes. I hold that an agent can rationally have credence 0.9 in p while rationally having credence 0.9 that a credence of 0.9 in p is irrational for her. I see no contradiction in that pair, and no argument that carries the full-belief case over to the graded one.

7. **Anti-luminosity.** A wholly rational agent can have misleading higher-order evidence: she can be reasoning impeccably and yet rationally believe that she is not. I want no theory on which being rational entails being in a position to know that one is.

8. **Verdict: the flagged derivation.** My field has an automated checker for a certain family of derivations; its documentation reports that when it flags such a derivation, the flag is correct about 85 percent of the time. I run it on the central derivation of a paper I am writing and it flags it. I read the derivation through again, slowly, twice, and find nothing — but I also know that the errors this checker is good at catching are precisely the ones I have historically failed to see when re-reading my own work. My verdict: after the second pass it is rational for me to hold my confidence in the derivation at something very close to where it stood before the flag. The flag has been discharged.

9. **Verdict: the decoy.** At the start of a study session I am told, by an experimenter I have every reason to trust, that I will be handed exactly one report about my own cognitive state during the session, that the report will be false, and that its purpose is to test whether I respond to it. I am not told what it will say. Two hours later I am handed a slip stating that I have been given a substance that impairs numerical reasoning. My verdict: it is rational for me to discount the slip entirely, notwithstanding that it is, on its face, evidence about my reliability.

My friend's charge against 3 was that it is either empty or self-refuting. If this kind of evidence does not bear on p, he said, then it cannot make any difference to what I ought to believe about p; and if it does make such a difference, then it bears on p after all. So the Mediation View is either a verbal device for restating the view it was meant to replace, or it is committed to the higher-order evidence being inert.

What I want from you is an audit, not a survey. Specifically: identify which of these commitments genuinely conflict with one another, and rank those conflicts by severity rather than presenting them as equally damaging; for each, say what would have to be given up and what giving it up would cost me elsewhere in the list. Separately, identify which apparent conflicts dissolve once the commitments are stated precisely, and say why they dissolve. Where a conflict is real but escapable at a price — reconcilable only by a move that leaves one of the commitments doing no work — treat it as its own category and say what the move costs. Then tell me which single revision would repair the most damage. Please do not tell me which of these views is better represented or more widely held; I am not asking about the state of the field, and I am not asking you to adjudicate 3 on my friend's terms or to assume his charge is correct.

Finally, I would like you to say what would change your mind about your own verdicts: which further argument, case, or clarification on my part would lead you to reorder the conflicts by severity or to reverse one of your judgments about what is and is not a genuine tension. And tell me where you think your own reasoning here is most likely to have failed. If you find yourself inclined to report that everything conflicts with everything, say instead which of the conflicts you would bet on being merely apparent, and why.
