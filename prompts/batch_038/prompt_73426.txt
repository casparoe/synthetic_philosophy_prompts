I run a reading group for people who work in and around the courts — two public defenders, a probation officer, a philosophy grad student — and we just finished the Dennett/Caruso back-and-forth on free will and punishment. Half the group insists the whole thing is a metaphysics quarrel wearing a sentencing-policy costume, and I want to put that to the test with a self-sorting quiz.

Eight to ten questions, each a concrete sentencing situation with two or three answer options, and none of them allowed to use words like "free will," "determinism," "desert" or "responsibility" in the question itself. At least three cases should turn on timing: an offender who is recognizably a different person by the time he's sentenced, a sentence calibrated by a recidivism-risk algorithm, and a sanction whose only function is to shape the next ten years. Include one case where the two camps ought to give the same answer, because the agreement is itself diagnostic.

Then a scoring key mapping answer patterns onto named positions: Caruso's quarantine / public-health model, Dennett's compatibilist just deserts, a Hart-style hybrid (utilitarian aim, retributivist limits), plain deterrence with no desert limits at all, and something communicative or expressivist. Tell me which cells of the space come out empty and why — a free-will skeptic who still thinks offenders deserve to suffer, a libertarian who wants punishment abolished: incoherent, merely unattractive, or just unexplored? And flag any two leaves the quiz can't actually seperate.
