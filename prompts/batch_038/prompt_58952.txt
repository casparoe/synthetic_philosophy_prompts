I am a staff software engineer on a reliability team. I have no formal training in philosophy, but for the past year I have been running an eight-week internal seminar for twelve engineers on causal inference. We approach the subject mostly from the machine-learning side — structural equation models, DAGs, the do-operator — and, more tentatively, from the philosophical side, where I have tried to give them both a Lewis-style counterfactual account of causation and the interventionist response to it (Woodward's philosophical thesis, which I understand to be a different thing from Pearl's formal apparatus even though the two are usually mentioned in the same breath). My participants are strong formalists and weak metaphysicians: they can manipulate a causal graph fluently but tend to treat any philosophical question as either trivially settled or unanswerable, and they have a habit of ending discussions by saying that causation is just about interventions, full stop. For our final session I need one essay question, answerable in sixty minutes and worth thirty marks in three parts, that they cannot pass by reciting that correlation is not causation, that Pearl formalised interventions, or that Lewis analysed causation as counterfactual dependence between possible worlds. What I want the question to do is force them to decide whether the two frameworks ever genuinely disagree — about truth, not about notation — and, when they do, to say what kind of disagreement it is: a substantive metaphysical one, or a dispute about what causal talk is in the business of doing.

Here is the scenario I want the question built on. I drafted it myself and I am not confident I have described it correctly. Our deployment pipeline ships a canary build and two independent checks watch it. The first is an automated error-rate monitor that issues a rollback when the error rate crosses a threshold. The second is the on-call engineer, who watches the same dashboard and can also issue a rollback. A deduplication policy in the pipeline accepts only one rollback command: if the monitor has already issued one, any later command from the engineer is discarded before it reaches the deployment controller. On the night in question a bad build ships, the monitor fires at 02:14 and the rollback succeeds; the on-call engineer noticed the same spike, reached for the same command at 02:15, and was silently suppressed. Two further features matter and I want students to have to reason about both rather than pick the easy one. First, the monitor's threshold had been lowered the previous afternoon by a configuration change made by an unrelated team, so the rollback arrived roughly ninety seconds earlier than it otherwise would have; students should have to say what caused the rollback, what caused the rollback's timing, and whether these are two causal facts or one fact under two descriptions. Second, the incident review afterwards blamed our deployment frequency — we ship about forty times a day, and the reviewer's claim is that this rate is what made a bad build reach the canary at all, though nobody can describe an intervention on "deployment frequency" that is not simultaneously an intervention on team size, release tooling, on-call staffing and the product roadmap. A colleague who has read some philosophy told me my first feature is textbook early preemption and that the deduplication layer turns it into late preemption instead; I could not tell which of us was right, and that is part of why I want the last deliverable below.

Please produce four things. (1) The question stem, entirely self-contained: every detail a student needs must be inside the stem, with no required background reading and no expectation that they know any particular paper, although the standard labels for the two positions are fine to use. Three parts, with mark allocations that add to thirty and that I can rebalance. (2) A model answer of roughly nine hundred words, written as a strong participant would write it in the time available. (3) A marking scheme that names, part by part, the specific moves that earn credit rather than the topics that must be mentioned — for instance, distinguishing at the right point between the two accounts agreeing on the truth of a causal claim and merely agreeing on how to symbolise it — followed by a separate list of five errors that should cost marks, each with a one-line diagnosis of the misunderstanding it reveals. I expect at least one of those five to be the error of treating the do-operator as a metaphysical relation rather than an operation defined relative to a model, and at least one to be the error of assuming that because the two accounts can agree on the verdict in my scenario they cannot disagree anywhere; but please choose the rest yourself, and make them errors my engineers would actually commit. (4) A short instructor's note addressed to me, since I cannot evaluate the philosophy on my own: tell me whether my scenario is malformed or ambiguous in any way that would let a student answer a question other than the one I intend, adjudicate the early-versus-late preemption dispute between me and my colleague, and say which verdict on the blocked engineer is the defensible one and why. Two further constraints. No bibliography, reading list, or citations anywhere, and nothing that depends on a specific quotation. And please do not let the model answer retreat into a both-sides shrug: the question should have a crux, and the model answer should take a position on it and defend it.
