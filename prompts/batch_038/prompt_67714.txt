I want to run the "is Buddhist ethics consequentialist?" argument against a real episode instead of against thought experiments, and I've settled on the Burmese monks' boycott of the junta in September 2007 — the overturned alms bowls. Please analyse that through the two main readings, and then tell me which one the case actually puts under pressure.

The facts, so you don't have to hedge about them. August 2007: the SPDC abruptly removes fuel subsidies and diesel/petrol prices jump by roughly two thirds to double within a week, with bus gas up several times over; small protests, arrests. September 5: several hundred monks march in Pakokku, the army fires warning shots and then beats monks and bystanders with bamboo staves, militia groups join in. Next day a government delegation goes to the main monastery to apologise to the abbot and ask him to call his monks off; a crowd of monks and locals burns their cars and holds the officials inside for hours. September 9: a new group calling itself the All Burma Monks Alliance gives the regime until September 17 — pointedly the anniversary of the 1988 coup — to meet four demands: an apology to the monks for Pakokku, price reductions, release of all political prisoners including Aung San Suu Kyi, and dialogue with the democratic forces. Or else a religious boycott. The regime doesn't comply; it does try handing cash gifts to individual monasteries, which are mostly refused. September 14: the Alliance announces that monks will refuse alms from SPDC officials and their supporters, and across the country monks turn their bowls upside down. Late September: huge marches in Yangon, Suu Kyi comes briefly to her gate to take the monks' blessing, then the crackdown — somewhere between a dozen and a few hundred dead, the exact number never established, thousands detained, monasteries raided, monks beaten and some forced to disrobe. The regime survives. There was a precedent: monks did the same bowl-overturning in Mandalay in 1990.

Here's the part I find interesting and I want you to dig into it. The Vinaya does contain a formal procedure for this — the "turning down of the bowl" — and it's not a spontaneous gesture: the community meets, agrees, formally proclaims the sanction, notifies other communities and the person concerned, and turns the bowl back upright if the person reforms. And the grounds it lists are all offences against the monastics or the Triple Gem: seeking the monks' material loss, their harm, or their expulsion, insulting and reviling monks, causing a split in the Sangha, disparaging the Buddha, Dhamma or Sangha. Nothing in that list is about fuel prices, political prisoners, or whether a regime beats people. So in 2007 an ad hoc alliance announced a boycott whose targets were accused of economic cruelty and violence against civilians, using a ritual whose canonical warrant is about protecting the Sangha's own dignity.

What I want:

1. A disciplined mapping first. Who is the moral agent here — an individual monk, a monastery, an alliance that didn't exist a fortnight earlier — and does the tradition even have a slot for a collective agent, given that kamma attaches to individual mind-streams? What exactly was the act: no contact, no speech, no injury, just a refusal? Whose consequences are supposed to count, and over what time horizon, when the tradition thinks in terms of rebirth?

2. Then run both readings over it. The consequentialist reading has the skillful-means machinery — the famous case of the compassionate ship's captain who kills a would-be mass murderer to save the merchants and spare the murderer a worse rebirth, taking the karmic cost on himself. Does the boycott look like that? Does it license precept-bending for the sake of outcomes, and does it get the outcome right, given that the predictable result was a massacre and the regime stayed up for another four years? The virtue/intention reading says kamma is cetana, that the moral quality is fixed by the mental states expressed — the stock illustration being the blind monk who crushes insects underfoot in walking meditation and commits no offence because he didn't mean to. But then the case turns entirely on wether the monks' minds were full of compassion or of contempt, which nobody outside can see. Say what each reading has to say, and where each of them is simply silent rather than just uncomfortable.

3. The two puzzles I can't resolve. First: is refusing a gift a harm? In a merit economy, giving alms to a monk is one of the best investments available to a layperson, and the regime's own legitimacy rested partly on being seen as a lavish patron — it was handing out cash to monasteries precisely then. So the refusal withholds something real. Does Buddhist ethics have any way of counting that as harm, or of counting it as a benefit to the soldiers (sparing them the kamma of propping up a regime), and which way it cuts seems to depend on the metaethics you already brought with you. Second: the Vinaya's procedure is public, rule-governed, and justified by reference to what a layperson does and the damage it causes. That looks a lot like institutionalized rule-consequentialism sitting inside a tradition whose moral theory is supposedly all about inner states. Does that help the consequentialist reading, undercut the virtue reading, or show that both sides are arguing in a taxonomy the tradition never used?

4. Finally, tell me what the episode does to the debate — confirms one side, is an anomaly for both, or reveals that the debate was never really about cases like this one. And if you think a third description of the case beats both readings (the boycott as a speech act, or as a withdrawal of religious legitimacy, or as something the Vinaya simply doesn't cover), say so and argue for it rather than defaulting to the two I handed you.

I know the standard write-ups of the Saffron Revolution and I know the Keown/Goodman/Garfield debate, so don't recieve-recite either back to me. Also don't force the theories to speak where they don't; a clean statement of where each one has nothing to say is worth more to me than a stretch.
