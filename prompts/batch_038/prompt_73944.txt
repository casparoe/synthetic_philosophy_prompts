In about six weeks I'm giving a 20-minute paper at a small ancient philosophy workshop, followed by nearly an hour of discussion, and what I want from you is a Q&A prep sheet — not a defense of my view, I've got that part.

My thesis: the Athenian's treatment of wine in the early books of the Laws is not a moral position on drunkenness at all, it's a dosing policy. He's proposing the supervised administration of a psychoactive substance in graded amounts to produce a disposition that untrained exposure would never produce. The hypothetical drug that would induce fear is doing the real normative work: that's the ideal instrument, and wine is only the imperfect stand-in available to an actual legislator, worth having to the extent that it approximates that instrument's function. So whether citizens should drink is, on my reading, the wrong question for Plato; the questions are what, how much, under whose supervision, and at what age.

Sketch of the arguement, so you know what to aim the objections at:
1. The fear-drug hypothetical shows him thinking in terms of controlled provocation and measured response — the substance as both a test of character and a training device.
2. Wine does the opposite of the fear-drug, it makes people confident and pleasure-seeking rather than afraid, so the training it supplies is in holding on while the pull gets stronger. That's why the puppet material with the golden cord of calculation sits in the same stretch of Book I: the golden cord has to stay taut while the pleasure and pain cords are deliberately being strengthened.
3. The age-graded rules about who may drink later in the dialogue read like titration rather than prohibition — what's withheld is the dose and the unfit subject, not the substance as such.
4. Therefore the symposiarch isn't a censor of pleasure but something closer to a supervisor of exposure.

The room: maybe 25 people, mostly working ancient philosophers, plus (a) one historian of Greek medicine who will smell anachronism a mile off, (b) someone who works on the archaic symposium in lyric poetry and thinks Plato is reacting to a social practice, not designing a psychological intervention, (c) two political theorists who read the Laws as a blueprint for authoritarianism and will resist any reading that makes the Athenian look subtle, and (d) a grad student who knows the Symposium cold.

Give me the eight or so objections I'll actually get from this room, in their vocabulary, roughly in the order they'll arrive and ranked by how much damage each does. For each: a two-minute reply, a note on where that reply is soft, and a flag for anything I should concede up front instead of fighting it. Then tell me which single reply has to land for the paper to survive. And include the question I'm praying nobody asks — my guess is either that dosing makes the thesis unfalsifiable, since any passage can be recast as a rule about quantity, or that Socrates drinking everyone under the table at the end of the Symposium wrecks the supervised-dosing picture — but tell me which of the two is worse and why.
