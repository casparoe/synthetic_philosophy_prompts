I am a high school junior on a student advisory panel that has been asked to review the risk score our district uses to assign students to mandatory summer tutoring. The vendor's one-page summary claims the score is fair in three senses at once: students with the same score go on to fail a course at the same rate regardless of demographic group; among students who would not have failed, the same share is flagged in each group; and among students who would have failed, the same share is flagged in each group. The same summary concedes that failure rates genuinely differ between the groups in our data.

Before I write anything, I want the specification rather than a verdict on this vendor. Lay out the requirements any statistical notion of fairness for a predictive score must meet to be worth defending, with the motivating case or consideration behind each: what it must settle about score-level versus thresholded-decision-level claims, which distinctions it cannot collapse (calibration versus error-rate balance, group versus individual, the label predicted versus the harm at stake), and which requirements are non-negotiable rather than the commitments of one camp — such as insisting the protected attribute be ignored at the moment of decision. Then tell me which subsets of the requirements are jointly unsatisfiable when base rates differ across groups, and which single requirement, if pressed, rules out the largest number of otherwise attractive candidates. Two points I especially want addressed: whether any of the requirements can be stated at all when the predicted label — a teacher-assigned course failure — is only a proxy for the harm we care about, and whether a real deployment like ours forces a requirement that these debates usually leave out.
