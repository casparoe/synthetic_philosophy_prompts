ok so I've been going around in circles on an Orwell question and I want an essay that picks a side instead of surveying one.

The question: is Orwell actually committed to strong linguistic determinism — the view that deleting a word deletes the thought it named, so the speakers who come after literally can't have it — or is that a misreading the texts don't support?

For the strong reading you've got the obvious stuff. Syme, the philologist compiling the Newspeak dictionary, tells Winston that the whole aim of Newspeak is to narrow the range of thought, and that thoughtcrime will end up impossible because there won't be words to commit it in. The appendix to 1984 goes further: Newspeak's purpose wasn't just to express the Ingsoc worldview but to make all other modes of thought impossible, with only the small hedge that this holds insofar as thought depends on words — and it claims the vocabulary was built to exclude heretical meanings and also any possibility of arriving at them by indirect methods. Plus the free example: free survives, but only in sentences like "This dog is free from lice."

Against it you've got the structure of the book itself, which I think is the more interesting half. The appendix is written in the past tense, in ordinary English, by what reads like a later academic, and it says outright that in 1984 nobody yet used Newspeak as their only medium and that full replacement wasn't expected until about 2050. Meanwhile Winston keeps a diary and thinks rebellious thoughts constantly, in fluent Oldspeak. So either the regime fails at the one job Newspeak was built for, or the strong reading is being imposed on a text that's quietly arguing against it.

Here's a three-way split I've been playing with — attack it if you think it's junk, but I want it used:
- strong: delete the word, delete the concept. The thought can't be formed at all.
- medium: delete the word, keep the concept recoverable by description but make recovery expensive — you can assemble the thought out of other material, almost nobody bothers.
- weak: words don't add or remove thoughts, they just change the default route thoughts take, like a settings menu that buries an option three levels down rather than removing it.

My claim, argue for or against it but land somewhere: Orwell is only coherent on medium, and the appendix proves it against itself. Its opening insists heretical meanings can't be reached indirectly, but the section on compound words concedes that most of them can be translated into Oldspeak, if your willing to accept a long paraphrase and lose certain overtones. That's a price, not an impossibility. And Appendix C is more clearly medium still: it admits you can say BIG BROTHER IS UNGOOD in Newspeak, it just can't be developed into an argument, because the words for arguing aren't there. That's a gradient, and gradients aren't determinism. Which is why I suspect the real engine in Newspeak isn't the deleted vocabulary at all — its duckspeak, the word for speech that issues from the larynx without involving the higher brain centres, which the appendix says was ambivalent and approving when the quacking was orthodox. That's a claim about what people spontaneously say, not about what's sayable.

Things I want the essay to actually do:

1. Run the circumlocution objection to ground rather than just naming it. If Newspeak keeps full compositional syntax and only strips the political senses, a speaker without free can still build something like "a state of affairs in which the authorities are unable to compel you." Does the fact that this takes effort and sounds awkward do any philosophical work — is cost a genuine barrier — or is the strong theorist right that a thought you'd have to construct by hand isn't a thought anybody has? Spell out the mechanism, don't shrug.

2. Take the compound words seriously as a counterexample to the whole deletion story. crimethink, goodthink, bellyfeel, thinkpol, duckspeak, doubleplusgood: these are additions. They pack batteries of ideas into two syllables and come with valence pre-installed, and the appendix says the function of some of them is less to express meanings than to destroy them. Is the Party's real win compactness-plus-valence rather than absence? If that's a distinct thing from all three of my tiers, name it as a fourth and defend it.

3. Look at the chess analogy the appendix leans on: someone who had never heard of chess wouldn't know that QUEEN and ROOK carried those secondary meanings. Is forgetting-by-disuse a linguistic phenomenon at all, or just ordinary historical amnesia wearing a language costume? This feels like the crux to me and I can't decide.

4. Verdict, and then the bill. If the strong reading is wrong, does Politics and the English Language lose its rationale or gain one? The prescription there — short words, active voice, no pretentious diction, concrete imagery — is usually read as resting on a loop where sloppy speech and sloppy thought feed each other. Does that loop need determinism to run, or does it run better without it? Definite answer please. Not "Orwell held both views at different times", not "it's more complicated than that" — I want a position and the argument that gets you there.

Don't give me citations or a reading list, I have no way to check them and I'd rather you didn't guess. Your reasoning is the point. Two or three thousand words, or whatever it takes.
