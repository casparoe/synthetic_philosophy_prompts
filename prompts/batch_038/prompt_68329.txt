I'm on a hospital ethics committee and we have to rule on a "dementia directive": a woman in her late sixties, early-stage Alzheimer's, still competent, has written that once she no longer recognizes her daughter staff should not treat her pneumonia and should not hand-feed her even if she takes food willingly. Half of us want to honor it, half think we'd be letting her past self kill someone who is currently content and enjoys her meals. Before we vote I want to know what were actually presupposing.

Write out the desiderata for a theory of when an earlier self gets to bind a later one. Which cases does it have to come out right on — the mast-and-sirens kind where the person regains competence, the manic-depressive who pre-refuses medication she'll beg for while manic, someone who changes her mind while still competent and then reverts to her old views after the dementia advances, a directive written on a prognosis that turned out wrong? Which distinctions can it not paper over — identity versus authority over a life, refusing versus requesting, the competence needed to write it versus the competence needed to cancel it? And which facts about how people actually change is it not allowed to wish away? Give me the case or consideration behind each one.

Then say which of these are non-negotiable for a policy and which are one camp's pet principle, which single one does the most work ruling candidates out, and which pair you think can't both be satisfied — my suspicion is that "it's her life so it's her call" and "what matters is how it goes for her now" are on a collision course, and I want to know if that's really the shape of it. End with a few lines on how identity-based accounts, precedent-autonomy accounts and best-interests accounts do against the list. No sources please, this is for a 30 minute discussion, not a paper.
