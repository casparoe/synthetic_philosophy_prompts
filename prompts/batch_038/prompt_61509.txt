I retired four years ago from an experimental biophysics group, having spent some thirty-five years largely in the company of amplifiers, and I have been reading philosophy seriously only since. My mathematics is not a difficulty; the vocabulary is. I should like to ask you to explain a conceptual problem I have run into, on the assumption that I can follow an argument but cannot be presumed to know the literature. Please do not offer me references or a bibliography — I have no means of checking them, and I do not want them. What I want is the reasoning itself, set out in order, with your terms defined at first use.

The problem concerns von Neumann's unfinished lectures comparing the computing machine with the nervous system, published posthumously in 1958 from notes prepared for a Yale series he never delivered. I have read them twice, and my impression is that he argues for a disanalogy between the two on two quite different levels, and that the levels are not visibly connected.

The first is quantitative and thermodynamic, and it is the sort of argument I was trained to respect. The nerve element is orders of magnitude slower than the electronic components of his day, yet the system does far more; he accounts for this by parallelism. Its numerical precision is low, its elementary events being coarse, fatigable and noisy, so whatever arithmetic it performs must be carried in very few significant digits and over short logical chains rather than long ones. Its memory is of another kind entirely — diffuse, not addressable as a machine store is — and vastly greater in capacity. Its energy cost per elementary operation is enormously smaller. All of this is measurement, and it invites a conclusion of degree: the brain is a machine with different engineering constants.

The second seems to say something stronger and of a different character: that the nervous system does not use our language at all. The significance of an impulse is not intrinsic to it but depends on context and on the statistics of the traffic; the coding is statistical rather than arithmetical; the system is mixed analog and digital in a manner that cannot be cashed out merely by noting that continuous quantities coexist with discrete events, since timing and frequency make one and the same discrete event serve both roles; and he warns that the logical organization at work here may sit at a lower level than arithmetic, so that importing our notation onto it may be a mistake of category rather than of detail.

Four things follow from this that I want explained.

First: the quantitative strand, taken alone, appears to license nothing stronger than a difference of degree, and I cannot see how the second strand is meant to follow from it. What precisely would have to be added to a list of engineering constants — precision, speed, parallelism, memory architecture, energy — to yield the conclusion that the system speaks a different language? If nothing, then the second strand is doing independent work, and I want to be told what kind: is it a hypothesis awaiting measurement, or a claim about how descriptions of physical systems are chosen?

Second, and this troubles me most, von Neumann had already shown how to dissolve the first strand. In his work on synthesizing reliable organisms from unreliable components he gave a redundancy scheme — multiplexing, with a majority organ — and showed that provided the failure rate of an individual element stays below a critical threshold, which I believe is well under one percent, any desired reliability can be purchased at a definite and computable cost in redundancy. But imprecision and noise are exactly the obstacles that scheme was built to defeat. If unreliability is convertible into reliability at a price, then the brain's coarseness looks like a term in a cost function rather than a barrier, and the entire weight of the disanalogy shifts onto the second strand. Did he not notice this? Or does his redundancy result presuppose something the nervous system does not supply — a fixed and known wiring, an external specification of what is being computed, a stable separation between the organ and the signal it carries? Tell me which, and why.

Third, I want the second strand made concrete, because as it is usually reported it seems to me either trivial or question-begging, and I cannot tell which. Consider a case of my own construction. Two investigators record the same population of spike trains from the same animal performing the same task. One reads the trains as a rate code and describes the tissue as computing an average; the other reads them as a temporal code and describes it as computing a comparison, and predicts the behaviour about as well. Each decoding is internally consistent, neither is more parsimonious in any way I can make precise, and the dispute is not settled by better electrodes — only perhaps by better theory. Is there a fact of the matter as to which computation occurs? If so, what fixes it: something in the tissue itself, or something in the downstream structures that read the traffic and are themselves constrained in what they can read? If not, in what sense is talk of the nervous system's language an empirical claim at all? State what conditions a candidate code must meet to be more than an interpretive convenience — compositionality, locality, error correction, the existence of a reader that cannot help but interpret it one way rather than another — and then say which of these the nervous system plausibly satisfies and which it plausibly fails.

Fourth, a smaller matter. If my memory serves, von Neumann distinguished automata made by nature from automata we construct on their model, and had names for the two classes, something along the lines of primary and secondary. Correct me if the terminology is wrong or the distinction is not his, and tell me whether it bears on the problem: does the fact that a constructed automaton has a designer who knows what it is for change whether its internal states carry significance in the sense he intends?

Be explicit throughout about where you are reporting an argument and where you are reconstructing one to make it hang together, since the lectures are unfinished and I cannot check them. If a later philosophical debate — I gather there is one concerning whether the attribution of a computation to a physical system is relative to an observer — bears directly on this, explain the connection in your own terms, but do not build the answer out of names and dates.

Something in the region of twelve hundred to fifteen hundred words, organized under headings corresponding to my four questions, with a worked example for the third small enough to be checked by hand. Continuous prose, please, rather than a telegraphic list of points; I am old-fashioned enough to want sentences.
