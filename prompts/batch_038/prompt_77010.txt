I teach a third-year metaphysics module and I'm writing the final paper. We did a fortnight on causation by omission and I want one long question they can't blag by summarising the readings, because half of them will have memorised "contrastivism" as a label without knowing what it's supposed to fix.

Here's the case I've made up for it — please use it verbatim as the stem, and build the question parts around it:

A small museum keeps a fragile 19th-century watercolour in a sealed case whose humidity is held steady by a tank that has to be topped up once a day. On the Tuesday in question Priya, the conservator on shift, sees the gauge reading low at 8:40am, tells herself she'll deal with it after her 11am meeting, and then forgets entirely. The tank runs dry around 3pm, the humidity in the case crashes, and by that evening the paper has cockled permanently. Also on that Tuesday: Tomás, a retired conservator who lives two hundred kilometres away and has never set foot in the museum, did not top up that tank. And the backup humidity sensor, which would have set off an alarm, had been disconnected three months earlier by an outside contractor who was nowhere near the building that day. And in March the museum board had voted down a proposal to install a redundant humidification loop.

What I want the question to do:
- Part (a), 8 marks: get them to state the problem the standard counterfactual account runs into once absences are allowed as causes — using Tomás, not just Priya — and then explain how making causation three-place, relative to a contrast, is supposed to deal with it.
- Part (b), 14 marks, this is the real crux: the contrastivist owes us a non-arbitrary way of fixing which contrasts are relevant. I want them to press the objection that the only candidate principles look normative — the relevant contrast is the one Priya was expected, or well-placed, to bring about — so that contrastivism quietly turns into the expectation-based view of absence causation rather than being an alternative to it. Then they have to say what a contrastivist can reply that isn't just an appeal to what happens to interest us.
- Part (c), 8 marks: have them give the strongest version of the flat denial — neither Priya nor Tomás caused anything, the felt difference between them tracks responsibility rather than causation — and then say what that view costs them, especially about preventions and about the contractor's disconnected sensor.

Please write the question as it would appear on the paper (with the case, the three parts and the marks shown), then a model answer of about 700 words, then a marking scheme. The marking scheme is the bit I care about most: I want it to say what an A answer contains that a B answer misses, in terms of specific moves — e.g. spotting that contrast-selection and norm-selection are the same job, or noticing that the board's March vote and the contractor's disconnection are different kinds of absence and shouldn't get the same treatment. And for each part list two or three mistakes that should cost real marks, like sliding from "caused" to "was blameworthy", or thinking the profligacy worry is just a preemption problem in disguise.

It's closed book and there's no bibliography — don't make any of it depend on exact quotations or on remembering publication details, and say in the scheme that naming authors gets no marks. Oh and I need to hand the draft to the external examiner on friday, so keep it to something I can drop straight into a Word doc.
