I'm TA-ing an intermediate philosophy of mind course and I've got to hand back the practice essays on mental causation tommorow. The question I set was:

"State Kim's causal exclusion argument against nonreductive physicalism as carefully as you can, making explicit every premise it needs. Then say whether the nonreductive physicalist has a good reply."

Here's one of the answers, which I have to mark out of 100:

---

Kim's argument tries to show that nonreductive physicalism can't keep both mental causation and physicalism. It needs three premises.

(1) Causal closure: if a physical event has a cause at all, it has a physical cause. This isn't really a philosophical thesis so much as something physics backs up.
(2) Exclusion: if an event e already has a sufficient cause c at t, nothing distinct from c can also be a cause of e at t, unless we have a genuine case of overdetermination.
(3) Supervenience: mental properties depend on physical ones, so whenever a mental property M is instantiated there's some physical property P instantiated that it supervenes on.

Now suppose a mental event m causes some behaviour, a physical event p. m causes p in virtue of instantiating M. But by closure p already has a sufficient physical cause, and by supervenience there's a physical property P that realizes M. So P is doing all the causal work and M is left with nothing to do: if M counted as a cause too, then every action would be overdetermined, meaning our mental and physical lives are constantly double-caused the way two people shooting someone at once double-cause his death. Nobody thinks that's what's happening, and overdetermination is supposed to be a rare coincidence anyway. So exclusion kicks M out and keeps P, and Kim's conclusion is that mental events are causally inert — they don't cause anything, they're epiphenomenal.

The nonreductive physicalist doesn't have much room. She can't drop supervenience without ceasing to be a physicalist, and she can't drop closure without dropping physics. Her best move is to attack (2) and insist mental and physical causes really do overdetermine. There's a version of this from Yablo, who says mental properties are determinates of physical determinates, so the mental is the more specific property and specificity is what wins causal competitions — the mental isn't a rival to the physical, it's a better candidate than it is. Kim would just say a determinate and its determinable are still distinct properties, so exclusion applies. And anyway Kim's own preferred solution is the old type identity theory: mental properties turn out to be identical to brain properties, which is why he's happy to be a reductionist. So the argument goes through.
