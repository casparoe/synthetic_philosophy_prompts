I have just completed my first year as chair of a seven-person tenure evaluation committee, and I am writing up the procedure I defended — and the philosophical rationale I gave for it — so that colleagues can test whether the package is coherent. The operative case: appointment requires all three of research distinction, teaching excellence, and an adequate service record; each premise is put to a simple majority vote and the conclusion is then read off the premises. In the case that prompted the write-up, the premises each carried 4–3, while a direct vote on appointment failed 3–4; my procedure appointed her, the conclusion-based procedure would not have, and I told the faculty that the former was the right way to decide. Here is what else I told them I am committed to:

1. The committee is a genuine agent with beliefs of its own, not a shorthand for a tally of ours; that is precisely why its judgments must be held to the same standards of consistency over time that we hold one another to.
2. Even so, whenever the committee asserts a judgment, at least one member must be able to say sincerely that she holds that very judgment herself. I promised the faculty that no one would ever be the instrument of a committee finding she personally denies.
3. The premise-based route is what secures unanimity: if we ever all vote alike on the premises, the committee cannot come out the other way.
4. Members should vote their sincere view of each premise, without calculating what the resulting committee verdict will be; I regard such calculation as a corruption of the process.
5. Responsibility tracks the premises, not the verdict: a member who votes yes on all of them cannot disown the appointment on the ground that she voted against it, and a member who voted against it is answerable for it if she accepted the premises that entail it.
6. I did not restrict who may serve on the committee or what configurations of member votes are admissible; the procedure is supposed to work for any seven colleagues and any profile whatsoever.

Audit this set rather than defending any part of it. Identify which commitments genuinely pull against which, ordering the conflicts by severity; say for each whether it is a real incompatibility or only looks like one, and what would have to give; state the cheapest available revision and what it costs; and identify the single revision that would resolve the most trouble at once. Where a conflict depends on facts the numbers alone do not settle — the composition of the majority, say — say so explicitly rather than assuming the favorable case. Please do not guess which of the six I would be most willing to abandon; I have not decided that myself.
