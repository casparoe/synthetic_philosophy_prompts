My school's philosophy club records a podcast in front of a live student audience, and I am one of its two hosts. Next week's guest is a philosopher who has spent his career developing and defending the argument from divine hiddenness in the form associated with J. L. Schellenberg: a perfectly loving personal God would see to it that everyone capable of a personal relationship with God, and not resisting one, believes that God exists; there are people who are capable of such a relationship and are not resisting one, and who nevertheless do not believe; therefore no such God exists. I have read the argument and the standard replies to it, but my listeners have read only the one-page summary I wrote for them, so every question has to carry enough context to be followed aloud, without technical vocabulary.

One feature of his style needs to be planned around. Give him a concrete person who appears to be seeking God sincerely and yet not to believe, and he almost never engages the case directly; he reclassifies it — either the person is not as open as she reports, or what she is missing is not the belief the argument actually requires. I would like a sequence of nine questions in the order I should ask them, arranged so that this escape route narrows with each turn. He is a gracious guest and I am myself a theist, so the pressure should come from the content, not from anything that feels like an ambush.

For each question, give me: (a) the wording I would actually read aloud, short enough for a thirty-minute recording; (b) one sentence on what the question is really testing; (c) the answer he is most likely to give; and (d) a follow-up keyed to that answer. Between them, the nine should cover: whether perfect love must aim at conscious reciprocal relationship rather than at something else, such as the moral formation of persons or the good of creation as a whole, and what survives of the argument if it need not; the reply that an unmistakable divine presence would overwhelm human freedom, and why that reply does not prove too much given that mystics report vivid awareness of God without describing themselves as coerced; the suggestion that divine silence is itself deliberate and aimed at changing the seeker's will, which would make a person's own report of her openness a poor guide to it; those who are entirely open to God but devoted to a god other than the one the argument has in view; people who die young, who cannot form the concept of God at all, or for whom the idea of God is inseparable from cruelty they suffered; whether the promise of a relationship after death removes the force of hiddenness during this life; and whether the argument establishes that there is no God or only that there is no perfectly loving personal God, which would leave room for something more like deism. Please include one question constructed so that both available answers commit him to a claim he would rather not hold, and close with a question about what a genuine case of non-resistant nonbelief would have to look like for him to concede it, and what he would do with his argument if such a case existed.

No warm-up questions about how he came to philosophy, and nothing that asks him to recommend books or articles — my classmates have no way of looking anything up.
