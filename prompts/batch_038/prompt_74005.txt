I'm four drafts into a novel and one scene keeps collapsing. It's a two-hander in a kitchen, and my beta reader's verdict on the last version was "two pamphlets shouting at each other over a kettle." Fair. Can you write it properly?

The book's one weird premise: about a third of the way in, it becomes credible to the characters — and I want it to feel credible to the reader, not like a gimmick — that space is infinite, so that every arrangement of matter that can happen does happen, over and over, out past the horizon. Which means there are uncountably many people who are, for all practical purposes, my protagonist: same memories, same headache, same chipped mug, same decision in front of her tonight.

**The scene.** Nadia, 40s, a process engineer at a regional water utility. Three weeks ago she noticed that a set of compliance readings from a downstream town were being quietly re-run until they came back clean. She has the originals on a thumb drive in her coat pocket, and tonight is the night she either mails them to the regulator or doesn't. In the kitchen with her is Elias, her husband, who is a cosmologist — and this is the thing, he's the one who got her hooked on the infinite-space business in the first place. He believes the physics completely. He just thinks what she's doing with it is garbage.

**Nadia's position, which I need argued at full strength:** the copies don't have to hear her. Nothing travels. It isn't influence, it's that she and they are the same kind of thing running the same procedure in the same circumstances, so her choice is *evidence* about theirs — not a lever on it. When she weighs the decision she shouldn't be weighing "what happens in this valley," she should be weighing "how does this situation go, for everyone who's in it." She's not choosing for them; she's finding out which thing she is.

**Elias's position — and he has to draw blood, three separate wounds:**

1. If they're genuine duplicates, they do what she does automatically. There's no coordination happening, no gap for her to close, no credit to earn. The "we" in her sentence is decorative. A thermostat doesn't cooperate with other thermostats.
2. The arithmetic one, and it's the good one: if space is infinite, then the drive gets mailed by infinitely many Nadias *and* buried by infinitely many Nadias, whichever she picks. Both totals are the same size. She thinks she's changing a sum and she isn't changing anything, because you cannot add to infinity by behaving well.
3. Her view makes her answerable for what people she'll never meet and can't affect actually do — the exact structure he'd call corrupt in any other context. If a man in the next valley poisons a reservoir, is that on her because she was the sort of person who'd have done it?

**What I need from the scene.** She has to answer 2, and the answer has to be a *distinction*, not arithmetic — nobody does maths at a kitchen table in my book. I want her to find it live, mid-argument, and I want it to be this: the duplicates aren't exact. There are infinitely many near-Nadias in near-kitchens where the details are off — the drive is thinner, the husband isn't home, the town is bigger, she's more tired — and those are the ones her behaviour is really evidence about, because they're running something close to her algorithm rather than her algorithm. And it has to cost her: the moment she concedes the copies aren't identical, she loses the guarantee and has to admit she's placing a bet on the character of strangers she can never check on. Let that land.

He gets to press once more after that, and she doesn't get the last real word. Even granting all of it — why is *how situations like this go* the thing to care about, rather than what she causes? She should have something to say that is obviously not an answer. The scene ends with that one still open on the table between them, and I want the reader to be able to tell which of them is more bothered by it.

**Craft constraints.** 900–1100 words, mostly speech. They're married and they've had this argument before, so nobody explains the premise to anybody — both of them are sick of it, and it should come out sideways, through old grievances and the objects in the room. Interruptions, at least one non sequitur, one point where somebody is flatly wrong about a fact and doesn't get corrected. Give me one recurring prop — I've been assuming the thumb drive, but the printed email, the kettle scale, whatever — that means something different on its last appearance than its first. No name-dropping philosophers, no papers, no terms of art; if either of them needs a label for the other's view, make it a bad homemade one they coin on the spot and then have to live with. Neither of them wins.
