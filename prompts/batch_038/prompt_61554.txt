Six weeks ago I moved out of a research role and onto a product team that builds systems which infer what users prefer from what they do — which offers they accept, what they scroll past, how long they hesitate before deciding — and I have been asked to write the design memo that either justifies or declines that approach. Two of my colleagues keep reaching for the same historical example to defend opposite conclusions, and before it goes into the memo I would like the example examined properly. The example is the early-2000s Oakland Athletics front office: the subject of Michael Lewis's 2003 book *Moneyball* and the 2011 film.

Here are the facts I take to be settled, so that the analysis does not depend on recalling them. Oakland, a low-payroll club — roughly $44 million in player salaries in 2002 against the Yankees' $125 million-plus — was run by general manager Billy Beane together with a quantitatively minded assistant, Paul DePodesta. The front office held that the market for players systematically overpaid for conventional measures and impressions — batting average, runs batted in, stolen bases, physical "tools," and on the scouting side appearance and demeanor — while underpaying for what actually produced runs, above all the ability to get on base. It bought accordingly, signing players other clubs had discounted; Scott Hatteberg is the best-known example. The 2002 team won 103 games, took its division, put together 20 consecutive wins between mid-August and early September, and was eliminated in the first round of the playoffs by Minnesota. Over the following years most of the league's front offices hired their own analysts, the on-base discount narrowed by most accounts, and Lewis has said the publicity surrounding the book eroded Oakland's advantage.

Please analyze that episode through the framework of inverse reinforcement learning and, more broadly, the project of inferring human preferences from observed behavior. I want the mapping made explicit and then interrogated, and I want you to say where it fails to map rather than forcing it to speak.

1. Fix the terms first. Whose preferences are being inferred here — the market as an aggregate, rival front offices, individual scouts, the players themselves? What exactly counts as the demonstration data: salaries, draft selections, roster moves, contract refusals? What environment were those data generated in, and what does the analyst get to observe of it?

2. State the episode in IRL's own vocabulary and then test the statement. Standard IRL assumes a known environment model and recovers a reward function that renders the demonstrations approximately optimal. My hunch, which is what I most want checked, is that Oakland never actually disagreed with the rest of baseball about the reward: everyone wanted wins per dollar. What Oakland had was a better model of the *dynamics* — how player attributes turn into runs and runs into wins. If that is right, then a naive IRL analyst pointed at a scout's behavior would have recovered a distorted reward function, a preference for jawlines and home runs, precisely because reward misspecification is the cheapest way to explain behavior produced under a mistaken world model. Assess whether the reward-and-dynamics confound is really what happened, or whether I am overreading, and say what evidence would distinguish the two readings.

3. A related complication: the market was not a single agent. The people writing the checks answered to owners and to their own career prospects, and a scout's behavior plausibly optimized blame-avoidance — signing the player everyone else liked — rather than organizational wins. What does an IRL system recover when its demonstrations come from a composite of differently incentivized sub-agents, and does that reading explain the persistence of the mispricing better than simple irrationality does?

4. Identifiability. Given that a reward is recoverable only up to transformations that leave the optimal policy set unchanged, with potential-based shaping as the familiar example and maximum-entropy IRL folding in a rationality parameter instead of pinning down a unique function — what exactly did Beane's office claim to know that the market did not? Was that claim falsifiable in advance, or did the 103-win season and the eventual narrowing of the discount do all the falsifying after the fact?

5. Non-stationarity. The advantage decayed once other agents learned the same model. Does inference of preferences from behavior have any principled account of the fact that its target moves when the inference becomes public, or does the framework simply have nothing to say about this?

Then run the analysis in the other direction. What does this episode do to the general warrant for reading preferences off behavior: does it support the warrant, since Oakland demonstrably outperformed the revealed preferences of the entire market, or undermine it, since that market's behavior turned out to be a poor guide to what the market wanted? And tell me what the episode licenses for a case like mine, where the behaviors are clicks and hesitations rather than contracts. Identify the specific features of baseball — repeated play, a publicly and precisely measurable outcome, a competitive market that punishes error and rewards correction — that made the inference checkable there, and say plainly which of those are missing in my setting and what follows from their absence.

Two notes on form. Write in American English, organized under headings corresponding to the numbered parts, and aim for something I could hand to a colleague without further editing. I do not want citations, a bibliography, or a reading list; I want the analysis. Where the film's dramatization departs from what the front office actually did, do not let the film carry the argument, and if you find you need to rely on something from it, flag that you are. Finally, if any fact I supplied above strikes you as wrong, or as a distortion that would change the conclusion, say so before building on it.
