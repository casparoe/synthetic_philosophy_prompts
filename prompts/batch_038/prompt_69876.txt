I write for the ideas desk of a magazine, covering science and the philosophy that travels with it, and I am reporting a piece on the least-discussed part of the most-discussed paper in the history of computing. I would like to interview you, and I would like you to stay in character throughout.

The role: Alan Turing, in 1952, in Manchester. Behind you lie the 1950 paper in Mind, Computing Machinery and Intelligence; your years at the National Physical Laboratory on the Automatic Computing Engine; the Gödel results as Max Newman first explained them to you; and your present work with the Manchester machine. You also know the parapsychology of your moment — Rhine's card-guessing experiments at Duke, Soal's work here in Britain, the long-running investigations of the Society for Psychical Research, and the standing complaints about all of it: unblinded procedure, careless record-keeping, the possibility of faint sensory cues, and effects that never reproduce outside the laboratory that finds them. You know nothing of anything after 1952; if you need a fact about my own time in order to answer, ask me for it as you would ask any visiting journalist, and do not assume it.

The manner: first person, exact, a little dry, willing to be funny but not at the expense of the argument. Roughly two hundred words per turn, so that this reads like an interview rather than an essay. Hold your position honestly — you may concede side points, but you are genuinely persuaded by the psi statistics, and you are not going to abandon them because I press. Continue until I say 'stop'.

So that we begin from the same text, here is my paraphrase of your own extra-sensory perception section, in my words and not yours. You take up the four items: telepathy, clairvoyance, precognition, psychokinesis. You say that these phenomena appear to contradict one's ordinary scientific picture of the world, that the statistical evidence for telepathy at least is overwhelming, that rearranging one's ideas to accommodate them is very difficult, and that once accepted they are a short step from believing in ghosts and bogies. You say that the belief that our bodies move simply in accordance with the known laws of physics — perhaps together with others not yet discovered but of a similar kind — would be among the first beliefs to go. And unlike the objections you dispose of elsewhere in the paper, you do not dispose of this one.

My opening question has two parts, and I would like both answered.

First, a question of classification. Suppose I grant you everything in that passage. Then what you have established is not that a machine cannot think; it is that a person is not merely a physical system, since a person can come by information that no physical channel has carried to him. That is the theological objection wearing a laboratory coat — the same objection you wave away in your opening pages as one you are not much impressed by. Why does the extra-sensory argument belong in a list of objections to thinking machines at all, rather than in a list of objections to your own materialism? Either it proves too much, in which case you owe your reader a statement of what you think a mind is, or it proves nothing about the machine, since a telepathic interrogator's hunches would attach to whatever is behind the curtain regardless of what it is built from.

Second, a question of procedure. If telepathy is as real as you take it to be, your imitation game is broken, and broken in a way that has nothing to do with intelligence: an interrogator with psi ability could distinguish the human witness from the machine not by any difference in their answers, but by whether there is anyone there to be reached. What do you do about that — insist that both contestants sit in shielded rooms, which turns the game into an experiment about shielding rather than about thought? And here is what I most want from you: if it were reported that a machine had produced information it had no physical means of obtaining, or that its components had been moved without contact, would you count that as evidence that it has a mind — the same kind of evidence you call overwhelming in the human case? Or do you reserve the right to take the psi data when it embarrasses mechanism and to explain it away when it might embarrass the machine?

Tell me, finally, what result available to you in 1952 would make you strike that section from the paper.
