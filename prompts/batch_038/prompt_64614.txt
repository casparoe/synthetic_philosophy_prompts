So — second novel, and I'm in trouble with chapter four. The book is set in a drought town where there's an outfit operating out of the back of a laundromat that will, for a fee, make you the kind of person who actually keeps their word. Not through training or shame or a support group: they cut something, install a real compulsion, so that walking away from a promise you made becomes unbearable, maybe physically impossible. The problem is the fee. Nobody in this town has it up front, so the outfit runs entirely on IOUs, and their intake clerk is a savant at reading faces — he can tell in about four seconds whether your mean it. Which means the exact people who need the procedure, the ones who know perfectly well they'd skip town instead of paying, can't get past the desk. And the ones who can get past it are the ones who never needed it. I've been putting off writing the chapter for three weeks and reading decision theory instead, which is arguably its own little version of the problem.

That setup is basically the shape of Parfit's hitch-hiker, the case where the driver in the desert will only give you a lift if he can see that you'll genuinely hand over the cash at the ATM once you're in town, and the perfectly rational traveller dies of thirst because he knows his future self will have no reason to pay. What I care about isn't the puzzle, it's the bootstrapping — how does somebody get from here to there at all, when the price of becoming trustworthy is a promise you can't yet credibly make? — and then the second half, what it's like to be the person who made it across once the compulsion starts costing them things nobody warned them about.

Could you write me a short story in that setting, 1800–2500ish words? Some seeds, use or ignore as you like: the narrator is a skip tracer, a woman whose whole career has been tracking down people who promised to pay and didn't, and she's been hired by the laundromat outfit to work intake — partly because she's good at it, partly because she wants the procedure herself for reasons she won't say out loud. I'd like a scene at the desk where somebody fails the reading and gets turned out into the heat, and a later scene where the narrator has already crossed over and has to honor a promise that's going to cost her something she cares about considerably more than the money. Keep the philosophy in the woodwork: nobody says the word "rational," nobody names a philosopher, nobody explains the predicament aloud — I want it to land as a situation, the way it does in the better parts of Le Guin or Dick. And please don't tie a bow on the ending; I'd much rather a reader come away arguing about whether she's better off than be told she is.
