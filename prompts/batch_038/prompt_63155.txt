I am opening a graduate seminar on the metaphysics of agency with Samuel Beckett's Waiting for Godot read against Buridan's ass, and I would like help getting the pairing right. The obvious reading casts Vladimir and Estragon as the starving donkey: facing a choice between leaving and staying with nothing weighing on either side, they are paralysed by equipoise. I suspect this is backwards. They are not indifferent; they avow a preference to wait, again and again, and even the one alternative plan they do form — hanging themselves from the tree — is dropped on an explicitly instrumental ground (they have no rope) and postponed to the following day, after which they nevertheless fail to move at all as the act ends. Buridan's paradox is that a tie blocks choice; theirs looks like a preference that fails to generate action, which is a different and arguably harder problem.

Please develop this into criticism rather than plot summary. I would like: a statement of what a genuinely Buridan-style diagnosis of the play would look like and which structural features of the text support it; an assessment of where my inverted version fits better, and where it overstates the case; and an account of what each of the two standard escapes from Buridan's paradox — that a rational agent may break a tie arbitrarily, and that exact ties never obtain because no two futures are alike — would predict about these characters, together with a verdict on whether the play refutes those escapes or merely depicts agents who never attempt them. Close by saying what the play contributes to the debate about choice without preference that argument alone cannot supply, and by flagging honestly any point at which the work resists the reading. Please write in British English.
