I am preparing a seminar exercise for a graduate class on self-knowledge and would like a case built to order rather than a stock one. Please design a single scenario, set in an ordinary environment of your choosing — a hospital ward, a family firm, an emergency crew — with no implants, no brain surgery, no pharmacology and no scanning technology, containing two subjects who self-ascribe the same kind of occurrent emotion and whose situations come apart in both directions at once.

- Subject A's self-ascriptions are reliably true, in the actual situation and across the nearby variations you describe, but the accuracy is produced by a trained capacity to pick up her own behavioural and physiological cues. She is unaware of using cues, sincerely denies inferring anything, and reports her state in the ordinary first-person way.
- Subject B has precisely the vivid present-tense feel that A lacks, yet her self-ascriptions of that state are systematically false in a motivated, non-random way — a standing pattern rather than a careless slip.

Place both in the same setting, so that the contrast cannot be explained by differences in environment, and specify each mechanism concretely enough to show why A's accuracy is not luck and why B's error is not sloppiness. Do not simply label one alexithymic and the other self-deceived: show what each attends to, what each would say, and what a colleague would say of them. Two paragraphs at most for the scenario. Then:

1. For each of four accounts — (i) an inner-sense or acquaintance account, on which self-knowledge requires a distinctive first-person mode of access; (ii) a reliabilist or tracking account, on which it requires a process that reliably yields true self-ascriptions; (iii) a constitutivist account, on which the self-ascription partly constitutes the state ascribed; (iv) an expressivist or avowal account, on which present-tense first-person self-ascriptions are not reports of an inner observation at all — give the verdict for A and for B with the reasoning, and say in each case whether the subject has self-knowledge, has mere true belief, or has something that is not a candidate for knowledge at all.
2. Identify the single feature of the case doing the work in each direction, then make the smallest change to A that would restore first-person access on the most restrictive of the four accounts, and the smallest change to B that would restore reliability, holding everything else fixed. Say whether either change also repairs the other accounts or only that one.
3. Explain how a defender of the account that fares worst would attack the case — by redescribing A's cues as themselves introspected, by denying that B's mechanism is belief-forming, or otherwise — and whether that redescription is available without collapsing the account into one of its rivals.
4. Finally, use your results to sort the four accounts into those that treat introspection as a process kind, individuated by the mechanism, and those that treat it as a success or achievement kind, individuated by the outcome; note any account that cannot give a coherent verdict on both subjects, and flag explicitly any point at which an account needs a posited unconscious state to deliver its verdict rather than smuggling one in.

Argue from the structure of the accounts: no citations, no attributions to named philosophers, no reading list. Please write the answer in British English.
