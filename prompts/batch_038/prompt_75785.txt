I have no academic training in philosophy. I left school at sixteen, spent my working life on boats, and for twenty-two years volunteered on my town's lifeboat crew, the last six as helm. Since retiring I have kept a notebook of the calls we actually made and the ones we only narrowly avoided making, and I have become convinced that the question of whether the greater number should be saved is not one question but several which people run together. My book group — twelve of us, none with any philosophy behind us — is devoting an evening to it, and what I want from you is a diagnostic instrument rather than an essay.

Please write me a decision tree that can also be used as a quiz, sorting a reader by what they say about cases rather than by what they call themselves. Four parts.

**One: the axes.** Before any questions, summarise in a table the distinctions you take to genuinely cut the logical space — for instance, whether a choice is bad because of how many people are made worse off or because of how much worse off people are; whether claims are matched one against another between individuals, and whether anything is left over after the matching; whether a tie is broken by chance, must be broken by chance, or never arises; whether the numbers count only inside a single decision or also across decisions and across years; whether one's own projects and promises change the answer. Tell me which axes you think are independent, which collapse into one another, and whether I have failed to recognise any that matter. Only then should positions appear.

**Two: the questions.** Nine to twelve nodes. Each node is one concrete case with two or three options, each option no longer than two sentences. No node may ask the reader what they think about aggregation, and no term of art may be used without a plain gloss in the same sentence. Every node must be answerable by someone with no idea where their answer leads. Use at least five of the following six cases, which are my own, and add others if you need them:

(a) One lifeboat, two harbours: three people clinging to a wreck to the north, one to the south, and time to reach only one.
(b) A rescue that would certainly save one person, or a winch attempt with an even chance of saving all five and an even chance of saving none.
(c) Five who will each lose a hand unless you go to them, or one who will die unless you go to him.
(d) Five villagers you can pull from a flooded house today, or the same effort spent on a scheme that will keep fifty alive ten years from now.
(e) One person's paralysis cured, or a million people spared a week of headaches, with nothing else on offer.
(f) Five units of antidote: five people need one unit each, a sixth needs all five.

One node must concern what happens after the choice — whether the person left behind is owed an explanation, and whether tossing a coin instead would have been better, worse, or beside the point.

**Three: the leaves and the key.** Six to nine leaves, each given a plain-English name of your own devising, with the name it usually goes by in brackets only if it has one; the tree must not be assembled out of names. Then a scoring key giving, for each leaf, the pattern of answers that lands there and one sentence on the commitment its occupants find hardest to defend in public. I want these reachable: the view that each person's claim must be considered separately and never summed; the view that outcomes are simply added up; the view that claims are compared pairwise and the greater number wins only where the harms are comparable; the view that a threshold must be crossed before numbers count at all; and the view that a coin toss is not a last resort but the procedure fairness requires. Where a combination of answers is coherent but unoccupied, say so and tell me why nobody is there — whether the view is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored. Where two leaves cannot be separated by any question you could honestly put to a lay reader, flag that pair rather than papering over it.

**Four: two short judgements.** Which single question does most of the sorting work, and which of my six cases is the weakest — the one you would cut.

I do not want references, a reading list, or suggestions about what to look up afterwards; I want the instrument. Number the nodes, letter the options, and put the key at the end.
