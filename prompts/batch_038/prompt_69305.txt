Trying to pin down when it's OK to just take somebody's word on a moral question, and my intuitions are a mess. Here they are, roughly in the order I thought of them.

1. My sister-in-law is a philosophy prof who works on this exact stuff. I ask her about putting my dog under anesthesia for a procedure that's mostly for my convenience, she says it's wrong, doesn't elaborate, and I decide not to do it. Fine. She knows more than me about this.
2. Same sister-in-law, same question, but she walks me through why — the dog suffers, the benefit to me is trivial, there's an alternative. Now I see it myself. Also fine, and obviously better, but I don't think #1 was bad in any way that #2 fixes.
3. A coworker who's read everything on labor law tells me our overtime practice is illegal, and I go to HR on the strength of that. No weirdness at all. That's just what testimony is for.
4. A friend tells me a mutual acquaintance's behavior at a party was sexist, gives no reasons, and I start treating the guy differently. I feel bad about this one in a way I didn't feel bad about #1, and I can't locate why. She's not an expert, sure, but the thing that bugs me is that I wouldn't have felt better if she WERE one. If my sister-in-law had told me the same thing about the same guy, I'd still feel the same twinge. So expertise doesn't seem to be the variable.

Those two — 1 and 4 — look structurally identical to me and I have opposite feelings about them. That's the main thing I want explained.

5. When I was nine I did what my parents said was right without asking why, and I think that was the correct posture, better than a kid trying to reason it all out. But an adult doing the same thing about a serious question looks like a failure of some kind. Same structure, flipped verdict.
6. A surgeon unsure whether to proceed asks the hospital ethics committee and does what they recommend: fine. The same surgeon ignoring the committee and going with her own judgment after a night of thinking: also fine, maybe even more admirable. I can't get both of those out of one principle and I'd like to.
7. The guy who donates a kidney because a mentor he trusts told him it was the right thing gets less credit in my eyes than the guy who donates because he sees the need. But also I'd much rather have the kidney than the credit-adjustment, and I'm not sure what that says about me.
8. Purely factual stuff — is this bridge sound, did that study replicate, what's the actual mortality number — I defer constantly and think that's just being rational, zero compunctions. My guess going in was that the whole problem is that moral testimony is about wrongness itself rather than about the facts, and factual testimony is unproblematic. But 4 kind of kills that, since the party story is a factual-ish claim about what he did plus a normative gloss, and I'd feel weird about deferring on either half.
9. If some bot turned out to be demonstrably more reliable than me on hard cases I'd take its verdict over mine. But if it told me something I suspect is wrong about my own conduct was actually fine, the relief I'd feel seems like the wrong kind of relief.

So: is there a principle that captures most of this, or a small set of candidates? State them precisely and tell me what each clause is buying you. Then run it on cases I haven't listed — a judge following a legal advisor's recommendation on sentencing, me asking a friend whether it's OK to lie to my mom about her cooking, a doctor in a country whose medical norms differ from hers going along with local practice, someone accepting a priest's word on a question the priest is admittedly not an expert in. And be straight with me about which of my verdicts it can't save, because I'm fairly sure at least one of 5 or 6 has to go. Bonus question: is the pure/impure distinction (they just tell me it's wrong vs. they give me the reasons) actually doing the work I assumed it was in 1 vs 2, or is something else going on there?
