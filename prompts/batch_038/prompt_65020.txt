Six weeks ago I became the person accountable for the merged clinical knowledge base of a hospital system that has just absorbed a neighboring network. The two vocabularies were reconciled mechanically, and the result now contains pairs of assertions that flatly contradict each other — for instance, two contraindication rules governing the same drug combination, each traceable to a source our clinicians regard as defensible. I have been given two pieces of advice and cannot tell whether they amount to the same commitment. Our informatics lead says we should stop trying to purge the contradictions and instead reason over the data with a paraconsistent system, so that a single bad pair does not license any conclusion whatsoever. A philosopher on our clinical ethics board says that once we keep contradictory assertions in place and go on treating them as correct, we have already accepted dialetheism, and that we owe an account of the metaphysics we have thereby purchased.

Please compare the two commitments — adopting a paraconsistent consequence relation, and accepting that some contradiction is true — precisely enough that I can see where they come apart and where they genuinely bind each other. In particular, I want the comparison to settle: what the objects of the two commitments actually are, given that one sounds like a property of an inference system and the other like a thesis about truth, and whether that difference in kind already decides what my colleagues are arguing about; whether refusing ex contradictione quodlibet commits anyone to a true contradiction, both in systems that reject that principle for reasons having nothing to do with gluts and in light of the possibility of a system that blocks explosion while still validating the law of non-contradiction; whether the dependence runs in the other direction — that is, whether someone who holds a contradiction to be true must abandon classical logic, or whether there are strategies for housing a true contradiction inside a system whose internal reasoning stays classical, and what separates such a position from trivialism; whether my own situation is available to me as a merely epistemic predicament (I do not know which of two records is right) rather than an alethic claim that both are right, and whether running non-explosive machinery while regarding the offending pair as simply false is a stable middle position or a piece of self-deception; and what the Curry paradox does to the assumption that negation is the only troublemaker, since as I understand it a sufficiently expressive conditional can trivialize a system without any negation appearing in the derivation.

Give me exactly five numbered items, one for each question above and in that order, each of one or two sentences and dense enough to stand on its own. No introduction, no conclusion, no preamble about how the debate is usually framed, and no references, reading lists, or suggestions of further reading — I am working through this away from my desk and cannot look anything up. No item may merely restate a distinction already drawn in an earlier one.
