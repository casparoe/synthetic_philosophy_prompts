So I'm a second-year MPH student, and I've been sitting in on a philosophy-of-medicine reading group that keeps circling back to epistemic injustice, and I got frustrated enough to start a running log of real cases from my own life — mostly from the two years I worked as a research coordinator in a hospital, plus some family stuff. For each one I wrote down my gut verdict: injustice, not injustice, or "injustice but of a different kind than the last one." My advisor's position is that the phrase has inflated into a catch-all and that half my log is just people being wrong or unlucky, which is not the same as being wronged. I want to know whether my verdicts actually add up to a principle before I argue with him about it.

Here's the log, more or less in the order I wrote it:

1. Woman in her thirties, three ER visits for abdominal pain, sent home as anxious each time; endometriosis diagnosed four years later. Injustice, obviously, no debate.
2. A psych inpatient's complaints about medication side effects get typed into the chart under "insight poor / noncompliant." Every clinician who reads her after that is primed by the label. Injustice, and I think worse than #1, because there's no single bad hearer anymore — the paperwork does it for them, indefinitely.
3. A postdoc whose first language isn't English hedges everything in lab meeting ("this might be nothing, but..."), gets no uptake; ten minutes later a native speaker says the identical thing flatly and gets the nod, and eventually the citation. Injustice — but note nobody disbelieved her. She was believed, she just wasn't credited. That doesn't fit the credibility story cleanly and it bugs me.
4. My PI lifted a junior person's study design and ran with it, no attribution. Here's the problem: I coded this as NOT an epistemic injustice, just credit theft, plain ordinary professional misconduct. It's structurally almost the same as #3 and I cannot tell you why my gut diverges.
5. A wealthy alumnus tells the hospital board that a new scheduling policy "destroyed morale." No evidence sought, board accepts it, policy killed, and the staff who'd actually filed objections never get asked. Injustice against the staff, even though the mechanism is somebody being over-believed, not under-believed.
6. In a peer support group for a badly-understood condition, members couldn't get anyone outside to take their experience seriously until they coined a name for it; once the name existed, outsiders got it immediately. Injustice during all the pre-name years — and nobody was individually blameworthy, they literally lacked the concept.
7. A teenager tells her parent the friend group is toxic; parent has met them, finds them charming, waves it off. Parent turns out to be wrong. NOT an injustice, just a mistake. This is my line-drawing case, but I can't articulate the line.
8. Elderly man is confused at triage. Version A: brushed off by an ER resident carrying fourteen patients. Version B: brushed off by his daughter, who has a documented dementia workup on file and is following her clinician's advice. Identical from the speaker's chair. I coded A as injustice and B as not. But in #6 I said blamelessness doesn't get you off the hook, so I'm clearly running two different rules.
9. A hospital rolls out a triage score trained on records in which certain groups' pain was systematically under-documented, so it under-prioritizes them; clinicians from those groups report they can't override it because the number reads as objective. Injustice, I think, but I don't know who the perpetrator is or whether it's even the same genus as the rest.
