Read that sequence against David Lewis's paper *Do We Believe in Penal Substitution?* (the Princeton philosopher, not the novelist). Lewis's central move, as I read it, is that the standard objection — that a punishment is essentially personal and so cannot be borne by anyone but the offender — depends on picturing penalties as things like imprisonment, whereas the fine shows that a penalty can be discharged by a substitute provided the authority entitled to exact it accepts the substitution in the offender's stead. Does Narnia's transaction meet Lewis's conditions, or does it turn out to be something structurally different from the atonement he is defending?

Work through the mechanics rather than the mood: who owes what to whom, and in what capacity.
