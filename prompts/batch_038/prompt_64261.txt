So context: I cover science and ideas for a mid-size nonprofit outlet, and somewhere along the line I got talked into being one of the two outside members on our five-person standards committe (three staff, two of us, we meet roughly monthly). We're in the middle of rewriting the policy document that governs both corrections on our own copy and the labels on our fact-check vertical — the little verdict boxes, "accurate," "misleading," "false," that kind of thing. It's drier work than you'd think and mostly it's arguments about wording.

The wrinkle is that a philosophy professor at the state university, who's been doing pro-bono consulting for our little "trust initiative," circulated a twelve-page proposal last week saying we should strike the words "true" and "false" from the policy entirely. His case, as far as I can follow it: the deflationists — starting with that redundancy point Ramsey made, and then the later line that "true" is basically just a device for taking the quotation marks off a sentence and for generalizing over a bunch of claims you can't list one by one — have shown that truth isn't a substantive property with some hidden nature behind it. So when a newsroom puts "true" on a claim, we're implying we detected some deep fact-of-the-matter we didn't actually detect; the honest thing is to label our verdicts in terms of what we can cash out, like "supported by the evidence available to us" or "not supported by the evidence available to us." Fine, I get that part. But then in the FAQ at the back he makes what seems to me a seperate move: he says that since adding "is true" to a claim adds nothing to the claim itself, our verdict labels aren't carrying any extra commitment anyway, so there's no real cost to being generous with them — he suggests we could stamp a claim "true" whenever a majority of the panel judges it more likely than not, without pretending we tracked anything. Two of us think at least one of those two moves is a con job and I can't articulate which or why. Meanwhile our one staffer with a philosophy MA says the professor is right that the Liar sentence shows a deflationist can't even keep their own predicate from blowing up, which also doesn't sound right to me, but I've given up on that thread.

Could you draft the memo I'll circulate to the committee before Thursday's meeting? Bottom line up front — adopt as written, adopt with specific edits, or reject — and then the reasoning, written so the professor could actually answer it rather than feel strawmanned. I'd like you to sort out whether his two moves are consistent with each other and with what deflationism actually claims, including whether any of this has anything to do with realism about the world, because he keeps sliding between those. Then give the losing side a real hearing: I genuinely think there's something in the proposal about what our labels track in day-to-day practice, and separately, in a libel-sensitive environment "false" lands much harder on a subject's reputation than "unsupported" does, which is not nothing. Finish with a short section of actual label language a copy desk could start using Monday. About 800 words, plain prose, no hedging everything into mush and no splitting the difference for its own sake — and please don't tack on a reading list or bibliography, I'm not going to have time to chase anything down and the memo needs to stand on its own.
