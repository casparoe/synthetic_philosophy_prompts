I teach a graduate seminar in decision theory — 18 students, one 100-minute session — and next week's topic is superrationality: the claim that a rational agent facing a symmetric multi-player game should reason that whatever she decides, every other rational agent will decide identically, and should therefore pick the strategy that pays best along the diagonal. The reading has gone well, but my students treat the view as a curiosity rather than a live option. Design a paper-and-pencil game they will actually play in class that makes them reason this way instead of merely discussing it. Two constraints: it must not be a Prisoner's Dilemma, which they have played often enough to perform the lesson rather than discover it; and the diagonal optimum must not be the option that looks most cooperative at a glance, so that they are forced to enumerate the diagonals in order to find it. Include one round in which the symmetry is quietly broken by a private instruction to an unknown subset of players, so that they learn afterwards that their diagonal reasoning had lost its warrant without their noticing. Give me the rules, the materials, a payoff scheme that requires no real money, and a debrief that maps each round onto the philosophical question — including the charge that the superrationalist is merely treating her own choice as evidence about other people's. Tell me what will predictably go wrong on a first run, and which feature of the view the game cannot reproduce.
