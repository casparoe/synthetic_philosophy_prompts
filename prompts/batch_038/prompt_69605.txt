Bit of background first. I'm taking on a PhD student this autumn who did a maths degree — solid probability, some measure theory, a Bayesian stats course — and essentially no philosophy, though she reads fast and isn't allergic to an argument. She's set her heart on the territory of updating when the evidence itself is uncertain, i.e. Jeffrey conditionalization and everything tangled up with it. She has four years, of which realistically two are useable before she needs a chapter in hand to keep her studentship, and after that she'll be on the market somewhere.

So I don't want to just hand her the canon and hope. And I definitely don't want a reading list from you — I know the classics and she can dig them out herself. What I want is your judgement on which of the open problems in this area are actually worth four years of someone's life, ranked, with the reasoning doing the heavy lifting rather than the list. Here's the menu I've sketched for her so far; feel free to tell me I've left something important off it:

1. The input problem. The rule tells you how to propagate a new distribution over a partition into the rest of your credences, but it's silent on where that new distribution comes from. Is that a genuine hole, or is it demanding something of perception that no formal rule could ever supply?
2. The status of rigidity. What's supposed to happen when rigidity fails — say she learns something about the reliability of her own judging — and is the fact that two successive soft updates generally don't commute a defect to be designed away or a feature to be interpreted?
3. Whether any of this actually touches the old-evidence problem, or just relocates it further down the corridor.
4. Imprecise credences: if she refuses to give a point-valued input distribution at all, does the rule turn into something else, and does that solve (1) or make it worse?
5. Something applied — soft evidence in graphical models, calibration under distribution shift — where a philosopher might be of real use to the people building these systems.

For each one, tell me how much else hangs on it, how tractable it is for a probabilist with no philosophical training, and how likely it is to collapse into either pure maths with no philosophical residue or pure exegesis with no content in it. I want an ordering I could defend to our funding comittee next month, and I want you to say plainly which of the five you'd steer her away from and why — she'll take a negative verdict better from you than from me. Finally: what would you want to see her produce in the first eighteen months that would make you reshuffle the ranking?

Please write the whole thing in American English, spelling and idiom both; she'll be job-hunting in the US long before I retire.
