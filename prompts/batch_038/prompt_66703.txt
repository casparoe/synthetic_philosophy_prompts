Having spent some thirty-five years in experimental biology and taken up philosophy only since retiring, I hope you will indulge a framing that may still sound more like a laboratory notebook than a seminar. I have been occupied with the thesis, associated above all with Dennett, that Darwin and Turing each performed the same sort of inversion: both showed that capacities which appear to require foresight or insight can be generated by processes containing none, so that comprehension is not the precondition of competence but at most a late and dispensable elaboration of it. Please write me a dialogue between two philosophers who accept that historical claim but dispute its reach. The first maintains that the two inversions are genuinely of a kind, and that Turing's achievement therefore entitles us to treat apparent understanding wherever we meet it as competence all the way down, with nothing left over to explain; the second maintains that the analogy conceals an asymmetry that decides the question, namely that Darwin's inversion was licensed because the process substituted for design — differential reproduction — could be measured, perturbed, and predicted independently of the very appearance of design it was meant to dissolve, whereas the process substituted for insight can be checked only as formal symbol manipulation, so that any move from it to the conclusion that a mind harbours no comprehension must already borrow the meanings from an interpreter outside the system. Anchor the exchange in two cases I can picture clearly: a bacterium steering up a gradient of attractant, and a program producing proofs no one had conceived before. Both speakers must be resourceful rather than merely obstinate: the second must answer the charge that insisting on an independent criterion of understanding simply reinstates the homunculus the inversion was meant to expel, and the first must answer the charge that the engineering triumph of computing establishes only that such machines are reliable, not that reliability is all that understanding ever amounted to. Let each concede what cannot be defended, but permit no comfortable merging of positions; instead, have them close by saying as exactly as they can whether what divides them is a claim about minds or a quarrel over the word comprehension. One practical request: do not put invented quotations from published works into either mouth, since I am interested in the argument rather than in the trappings of scholarship.
