I spent most of yesterday evening in an argument that neither of us won. My opponent is a historian of early modern science; I come at these questions from philosophy. The subject was whether the reflexive condemnation of "Whig history" has hardened into an orthodoxy that now does a good deal of the work it was supposed to prevent. Her view is that the critique remains necessary and that my objection is the familiar complaint of people who would like to go on writing comfortable narratives. My view is that the critique is a bundle of distinct claims with different logical statuses, and that the bundling, rather than the target, is where the damage is done. After she left I wrote my side up as a draft essay of roughly a thousand words. I want comments on the draft — not encouragement, and not a bibliography. I have no library access this week, and I would much rather be told that a claim is wrong than be pointed toward someone who has made it better.

**Four Sins Under One Name: An Unbundling of the Anti-Whig Critique**

Since Butterfield's short book of 1931, "Whig history" has functioned less as a description than as a reproach, and a remarkably portable one. Historians of science invoke it against narratives that trace a line from discarded hypotheses to present theory; political historians invoke it against accounts that end where the reader already lives; anyone may invoke it against anyone else's framing. What is rarely noticed is that the reproach carries at least four distinct charges, and that these charges do not share a logical status. Some are methodological vices. One is an inescapable condition of doing the work at all. One is a substantive thesis that could be true or false on the evidence. Treating them as a package is what allows a historian to dispose of a claim about the world by identifying a habit of composition.

I propose the following separation.

*(i) Evaluative anachronism*: assessing past agents against standards that were not available to them, or describing their conduct in the evaluative vocabulary of whichever side happened to win.

*(ii) Ancestral selection*: constructing the narrative by running causal lines backward from the present, so that the past is populated by ancestors on one side and extras on the other.

*(iii) Directionality*: composing the account so that the outcome appears as the point of the sequence — either by asserting its inevitability, or by treating what is really a heap of contingencies that happened to issue in us as though it were a line of causation.

*(iv) Progressivism*: the claim that the sequence constitutes an improvement.

My contention is that these should be judged separately, and that the standard treatment gets at least three of them wrong.

**(ii) is not a vice, and cannot be made one.** Every finite history selects, and the only selection criteria available are ours; a past that answers no question the present can pose is not thereby more faithful to the material, it is merely noise. More importantly, ancestral selection is substantially imposed on the historian rather than chosen by her. What survives survives through institutions that survived. The record of the defeated exists largely in the archives of the winners, organized by the winners' categories. A historian who forswears running lines backward has not escaped backward-running lines; she has inherited somebody else's. So the objection cannot be to selection as such. It must be to a further claim: that the selection is exhaustive, or that the selected lines carry all the causal weight. Call this the *completeness fallacy* — the slide from "these threads issue in the present" to "these threads explain the present." That slide is a genuine vice, and it is a much smaller target than (ii).

**(i) is condemned far too quickly, and the usual corrective is structurally defective.** The contextualist rule — judge the past by its own standards — produces what I want to call *asymmetric charity*: it grants the past coherence while withholding competence. Two difficulties follow. First, in a great many cases the relevant standards were shared. Whether a treatment cures the disease was a question humoral physicians asked themselves; answering it in the negative is not anachronistic, it is responsive to a standard they already held. A general prohibition on anachronistic evaluation would oblige us to describe them as having had good reasons, which is a distortion of its own. Second, the rule is self-undermining in scope. We take ourselves to be capable of moral error that will be visible only later, and we take that possibility seriously; to deny the same capacity to past agents is not respect but a kind of preservation, treating them as specimens of a culture rather than as people who got things wrong. The genuine vice is narrower than (i): applying a standard an agent could not have recognized *and* treating the failure to meet it as culpable. Evaluation and imputation come apart, and the bundled reproach hides the joint.

**(iii) is the real sin, and it survives in professional practice in a displaced form.** Butterfield's own target was closest to this: the abridgement, the moral verdict delivered because the story feels inconclusive until somebody has been found to have been in the right, the contingency flattened into a trajectory. But note what has happened to the prohibition. Denouncing teleology has become a disciplinary reflex, and the reflex now functions as a permit. A historian who has opened with the customary disclaimer may proceed to write an account whose architecture is entirely teleological — the framing, the chapter sequence, the choice of endpoint, the placement of the last page — while the disclaimer absorbs the criticism that the architecture would otherwise attract. Call this the *disclaimer function*: the profession's announced hostility to a narrative form licenses that form by making it unremarkable. The declaration is not a hedge. It is cover.

**(iv) is not a methodological matter at all.** It is a comparative judgment about states of affairs, and its truth does not entail (iii). One can hold that life goes better now, on most measurable dimensions, than it did in 1300, while insisting that nothing in 1300 was pointed at us. The bundling treats the assertion of improvement as though it committed one to the assertion of inevitability, with the result that a historian cannot report a comparison across time without being suspected of harboring a philosophy of history. This is the most consequential of the confusions, because it silences a claim that is sometimes true.

The bundle also generates its own inverse. If the anti-Whig reflex is applied as a rule — take the side against the future, look for what was lost, assume that the victors' self-description is false — it reproduces the three vices it was meant to police. It supplies a selection principle (ancestral selection run backward, with the losers cast as the point). It supplies a direction (decline, or the permanent moral blindness of whoever won). And it supplies a standing verdict, which is precisely the impulse Butterfield objected to. Call this *inverse Whiggery*. It is protected by the same disclaimer function, since suspicion of progress has itself become a way of not having to argue about it.

So the useful rule is not "do not write toward the present." It is three narrow rules. Do not impute culpability against standards the agent could not have recognized. Do not claim completeness for your selection. Do not let your endpoint do causal work in the explanation. Improvement may then be asserted — but it must be argued on the merits, period by period, and it may not be smuggled in as the direction of the narrative.

What I would like from you, point by point:

1. Stress-test the unbundling. My own suspicion is that the weakest joint is between (ii) and (iii): if selection is already backward-running, some directionality may be built in before the historian writes a sentence. Argue that case against me if you think it holds, or show me a case in which all four come cleanly apart.

2. On the asymmetry of charity: does the distinction between evaluating and imputating actually do the work I assign it, or does it quietly presuppose a picture of moral knowledge — that some standards are discoverable and binding regardless of recognition — which I have not argued for and which a contextualist would reject? Give me the strongest reply available to her that is not simply a restatement of the rule.

3. The disclaimer function is the most speculative paragraph in the draft, and it is an empirical claim about the conduct of a profession, which I am in no position to survey. Tell me whether it can be restated so that it is arguable rather than insinuating, or whether I should cut it.

4. Flag anything you believe to be factually or textually wrong, including my characterization of Butterfield's target, which is entirely paraphrase. I would rather be told I have misread him than be given a reference. Do not send citations or suggested readings; they are of no use to me this week.

5. Tell me which of the four sections is load-bearing: if a reader granted me only one claim, which one should she grant for the essay to be worth having written?

6. Be blunt about novelty. Is this an argument, or is it the familiar move of conceding that Whiggery is bad and then observing that its opposite is also bad, dressed up in new terminology? If the latter, say so, and tell me which single piece is worth keeping.

7. I have deliberately left out concrete cases. Should I add one, and if so which would best *test* the framework rather than merely illustrate it — the Luddites and their treatment across two generations of historians, the historiography of abandoned theories in science, or the narrative shape given to interwar Europe? I have about a third of the length to cut and I would rather spend what remains on a case that could falsify the distinctions than on one that flatters them.

8. Finally: does the closing move to inverse Whiggery earn its place, or is it rhetoric? If it is rhetoric, what would make it a claim?
