I'm co-teaching a grad seminar on chance, evidence, and outright belief with a colleague from across the hall, and before we sit down to hammer out the syllabus I'm trying to reverse-engineer the rule she's actually applying to cases — we agree on nearly everything and disagree about why, which is the worst combination for teaching a section together. Her rulings so far, all from margin notes on my draft handout:

1. Ten-million-ticket lottery, $5 tickets, $50,000 prize, exactly one winner. You may rationally believe of your own ticket that it will lose. She adds that you wouldn't *know* it will lose, and she's firm that that's a separate question — so treat every verdict here as being about belief, not knowledge.
2. Same basic structure, three tickets, one certain winner, $4 million prize, and a stranger offers you $100 cash for your ticket right now. Here she says you may not believe your ticket will lose.
3. The preface case: 200 claims in a careful scholar's monograph, each about 99% likely to be right on her own reckoning, so roughly an 87% chance that at least one is wrong; nothing much rides on any individual claim, it's a book read by about forty people. She says the author may believe each claim and may also believe that at least one of them is wrong, and that she's under no obligation to give any of them up — the inconsistency isn't a failure of rationality.

Candidate rules I've got, none of which she's endorsed by name: (a) a flat probabilistic threshhold for outright belief; (b) a resilience requirement, roughly that you may believe p only if your high confidence in p would survive the further evidence you're in a position to get; (c) a practical/stakes rule, on which you may believe p only if acting on p is admissible in the decisions that actually bear on it; (d) something about relevant alternatives — whether the not-p possibility is a live one for you. There may be a better rule that I haven't thought of, and I'd genuinely rather hear that than have my four handed back to me.

Next week's case, which she's going to rule on and I want to bet on first: a hospital lab tech runs a rapid screen on a batch of donor tissue and it comes back positive. Prevalence together with the screen's accuracy puts the probability that the batch is contaminated at 0.98. The confirmatory culture is already in the incubator, is definately going to be read, and reports in 36 hours; the batch is quarantined either way. The only thing hanging on what she believes in the meantime is whether she marks the log as positive and phones the surgical team to move a case — an hour of OR time, fully recoverable if the culture comes back clean.

So: what does she say, and which of (a)–(d), or something else, best explains the three verdicts above?
