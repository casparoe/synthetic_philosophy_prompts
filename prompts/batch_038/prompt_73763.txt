I'm in the fourth year of a history PhD, and roughly a third of my dissertation has turned into an argument with my own framing. The book is about agrarian crisis and fiscal unraveling in a river-valley state across the middle decades of the eighteenth century, including one bad aerosol year whose sulfate signature is legible in the ice-core record but whose local effects I can only see through tax rolls and grain-price series. My co-supervisor, who trained as an economic historian, has been pushing me toward what he calls the natural-experiment reading: a drought, a cold spell, or an eruption year is an exogenous shock that lands on a society from outside, so if I can pin down its timing and location, the differential outcomes across districts let me identify the causal effect of climate on revenue, prices, banditry, and out-migration. He's emphatic that this is what rescues the project from the charge of environmental determinism: I would not be claiming the weather wrote the script, only that it delivered a blow whose consequences I can measure against controls. The social and environmental historians I actually cite insist on the opposite vocabulary — pathogens, monsoon failure, and volcanoes are historical *actors*, not independent variables — and their standing complaint is that the shock framing reinstates exactly the split they wrote against, an active nature acting upon a passive society. I've been going back and forth, and what strikes me now is that both sides are leaning on the same borrowed image, the laboratory, and that nobody has said what that image is doing or how much weight it will bear.

So I'd like a careful audit of the analogy between a climatic or epidemiological event and a laboratory experiment, in the sense my co-supervisor needs it. First, the mapping explicitly: what plays the role of the treatment, the control group, randomization, the investigator's manipulation, the outcome measure, the pre-registered hypothesis, the sealed lab, and the replication — and for each, whether the historical case actually supplies it or only gestures at it. Second, the disanalogies, but sorted rather than listed: which of them are fatal to the narrower identification claim, which are fatal only to the grander claim about agency and historical actors, and which are merely unlovely but harmless. Third — and this is the part I most want pressed — whether "exogenous" is doing double duty between *exogenous to the model* and *exogenous to the society*, and whether the slippage between those two senses is precisely where determinism creeps back in. Because my own material undercuts the clean version of the analogy in a specific way: the districts that suffered most were the ones where a generation of prior revenue policy had drained floodplain wetlands into single-crop cultivation and monetized the granary reserves, so exposure was built rather than given; and the districts with usable series are the ones with better administration, which correlates with resilience. On top of that the units plainly interfere — grain prices, flight, and violence spill across district lines — and my treatment is never a discrete event but a slow accumulation with an arbitrarily chosen onset.

Tell me whether the analogy can be repaired into something defensible, for instance by restricting it to a local question with genuinely as-good-as-random variation in timing, or whether it should be retired in favor of a different source domain; and if you think a better analogy exists, propose one and state what it licenses and what it refuses to license, since I'd rather have an honest weaker warrant than a borrowed stronger one. Finally, give me your plain verdict on the question my co-supervisor and I keep circling: was the laboratory analogy carrying the anti-determinism conclusion the whole time, so that the conclusion collapses with it, or does that conclusion stand on its own once the analogy is stripped away? I'm not after references or a reading list — I want the argument worked through, and I'd rather you be blunt about which of my two camps has the weaker position than diplomatic about both.
