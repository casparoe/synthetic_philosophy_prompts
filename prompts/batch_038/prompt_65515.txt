Almost none of the arguments I hear about coordination failure are actually about coordination failure. Two people will agree that some competition is a Moloch problem, or a race to the bottom, and then part company completely over whether it can be fixed, by whom, and at what level of aggregation — because they have silently made different assumptions about what kind of structure a trap is. I am running a two-day workshop for participants drawn from technology policy, environmental regulation, labor economics, and public health, and I want to administer a diagnostic instrument before the first session that surfaces those assumptions rather than the labels people prefer.

The deliverable is a classification instrument of nine to twelve questions, each with two to four answer options, together with a scoring key that maps answer patterns onto named positions, and a short commentary on where the instrument fails.

A note on form before the content. My working hypothesis is that this debate is not tree-shaped: that it consists of at least two largely independent axes, a diagnostic axis concerning what kind of bad structure is at work and a remedial axis concerning where the leverage lies, and that presenting it as a single tree will falsely imply that a diagnosis determines a remedy. If you agree, build the instrument as a compact matrix or as a two-stage profile and say so explicitly. If you disagree, build the tree and tell me where my hypothesis goes wrong. Either way it must be scorable by hand from a printed sheet.

The questions must be concrete forced choices about situations, never invitations to self-identify. A participant who has never encountered the words Pigouvian, Coasean, or singleton must still be sorted reliably. Please ensure the instrument separates at least the following distinctions, which I take to be load-bearing; if you think I have stated one badly, restate it and explain why.

First, agency versus selection. Does the trap consist in the strategic reasoning of identifiable players, or in a filtering process that would produce the same outcome if every current player were replaced by a wiser and more public-spirited one? This decides whether the lever is better people or better structure, and whether the diagnosis is game-theoretic at all.

Second, the baseline. Worse than what? A reachable alternative equilibrium, or an ideal that no mechanism could deliver?

Third, who bears the loss: the players themselves, parties outside the competition, or only the aggregate, such that each participant is better off given what the others do?

Fourth, horizon. Does the trap require short-sightedness, or does it survive perfect foresight and unlimited patience on the part of every player?

Fifth, the roster. Is the set of competitors closed, or does the competition continuously admit entrants able to undercut those who have restrained themselves?

Sixth, relative versus absolute standing. I suspect this matters more than it looks: if what is competed for is positional, side payments and corrective taxes may be structurally incapable of resolving the problem, and only a level constraint will do.

Seventh, the locus of the remedy, if there is one: payoffs, the composition of the player set, information and verification, credible commitment, or the rejection of the diagnosis itself.

Please draw your questions from the following cases, using each at least once. Several laboratories deciding how much to spend on pre-deployment testing while a better-funded rival sets the pace. Jurisdictions competing for a mobile tax base. An open-access fishery. An industry's advertising expenditure, where each firm's advertising largely neutralizes its competitors'. Academic departments competing on publication counts. States arming against one another in such a way that the resulting mutual vulnerability produces a durable peace. And a common-pool resource that local users have in fact managed sustainably for generations without external enforcement. The last two are there deliberately: the instrument must be able to return "there is no trap here" as a verdict, and not merely as a failure to reach one.

The leaves should be named positions, and must include at least the following: a view on which these are agent-level dilemmas resolvable by altering payoffs; a view on which they are selection processes that no incentive reform will hold, because a player who opts out is simply replaced; a consolidation view, including its most ambitious variant in which the number of players goes to one; a view on which the binding constraint is measurement and verification rather than enforcement; an Ostrom-style polycentric or self-governing view; and a deflationary view on which several canonical examples dissolve once the baseline is stated precisely. Add any leaf you think I have omitted. Every leaf receives one sentence naming its most awkward commitment — the case from the list above that it handles worst.

Then report the logical space before you populate it. State the axes, list the combinations of answers they permit, and say for each unoccupied combination whether it is empty because it is incoherent, because it is unattractive, or because nobody has thought to occupy it. I am particularly interested in one cell: the position that a trap is agentless and selection-driven and yet escapable by persuasion. Is that combination stable, or does it collapse under scrutiny?

Finally, flag any pair of leaves your instrument cannot in practice separate, and describe what an additional question would have to ask in order to separate them. Do not paper over the limitation.

No references, bibliography, or reading list. I want the structure of the space, not its provenance.
