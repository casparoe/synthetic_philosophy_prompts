Two members of the philosophy club at my school argued after our psychology unit on attention and vision, and since I was the only other person in the room they made me referee. I could not decide it, so I would like your assessment.

Iris opened by saying introspection is unreliable, and offered an experiment: she had her brother fix his gaze on the middle of a page of printed text and report, without moving his eyes, what was in the outer corner of his vision. He said he saw more words, in black print. She then swapped the page for one covered in random letter strings; he reported the same thing and could not tell the pages apart. Her conclusion was that he had been confidently wrong about his own current visual experience, so introspection misreports what is happening right now, not merely what happened in the past.

Ben replied that this shows introspection is coarse, not false. Her brother was not wrong that there were dark word-like marks out there; he simply could not say which letters. Not knowing is not being misled, he said, and a scale that rounds to the nearest ten pounds is imprecise rather than unreliable.

Iris answered that his defense costs him something: if the detail is in the experience but unreachable, then "my peripheral experience is richly detailed" and "my peripheral experience is vague" predict exactly the same reports. A distinction with no introspectible consequences, she said, is not doing any work.

Ben said the consequences need not be introspectible. His peripheral experience of a face is an experience of a face, and when he shifts his eyes the detail arrives without surprise, exactly as he expected it to. That smooth continuity, he argued, is evidence the detail was already in the experience.

Iris said the smoothness is just as good evidence that the detail was in the world, which he then went and looked at. Both theories predict the same flow, so the argument cannot choose between them — and if the world does the supplying, "rich experience" is an idle posit.

Ben's last move was that Iris had conceded everything: if it makes no difference either way, the dispute is only verbal, and she cannot call introspection unreliable, since there is no fact about detail for it to get wrong.

Please go through the six moves in order and say who is right at each one, with a sentence or two of justification per move. Then tell me who is better off overall, and specifically whether Ben's final move really follows from Iris's fifth. I would also like to hear what neither of them raised that an informed referee would consider important. Finally, please separate the points that are relatively settled among people who work on these questions from the points that remain genuinely contested, and do not present a contested position as though it were the consensus.
