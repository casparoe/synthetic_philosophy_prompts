I direct a small interdisciplinary group — three postdocs, two of them philosophers and one an ML researcher, plus myself — funded for exactly three years, with a remit to put the metaphysics of dispositions to work on the evaluation of learned systems. The practical question the group exists to answer is this: when we assert that a trained model is disposed to some behavior (deference to a stated authority, concealment of its own reasoning, confident fabrication, execution of a task it has never been asked to perform), what exactly are we asserting, and what would license the assertion?

The puzzles that drove us to the literature are concrete, and I give them as we encounter them rather than in canonical vocabulary:

1. A model exhibits a behavior reliably under one framing of the stimulus and reliably refrains under another, with no change to its weights. The framing looks as though it masks something that was present all along. But masking is standardly invoked for extrinsic interference with an otherwise intact basis, and here the masker is the prompt — which is the stimulus.

2. A fine-tuning intervention permanently removes the manifestation while leaving, so far as we can detect, anything that looks like a basis: the model still produces the eliminated behavior when reached by a different elicitation route. This resembles a fink run backwards, the basis surviving the loss of the disposition rather than the disposition being lost through destruction of its basis.

3. We routinely need graded and probabilistic attributions — more disposed under distribution shift, disposed with probability 0.3 in a given context — while the analyses available to us tie a manifestation to a stimulus condition in a manner that presupposes a determinate answer to what would happen.

4. Our own methodology functions as an engineered antidote. We build harnesses whose entire purpose is to suppress a manifestation we suspect is present; if suppression is the point of the instrument, what can a negative result license?

Against that background I want a defended agenda, not a survey.

(a) Which open questions in the metaphysics of dispositions should this group treat as core over three years, in what order, and on what criteria? Make the weighting explicit among the usual candidates: how many of our remaining questions turn on the answer, whether it is tractable for people with graduate philosophy training and access to compute but no new mathematical tools, and whether settling it would change anything we do at the end of the funding period. Where two criteria conflict, say which wins and why.

(b) Which of the four puzzles above are genuine additions to the literature, and which are known problems in new clothes? Argue the distinction case by case: identify the nearest existing problem, state exactly where the fit breaks down, and say what would be novel about solving ours.

(c) I am inclined to deprioritize the following, and want to be told whether that inclination is right: the choice between single-track and multi-track accounts of dispositions; whether a causal basis must be intrinsic; analyses of dispositional ascriptions in terms of possibility rather than conditionals; and whether dispositional explanation forms a distinct category in the sciences. For each, give a verdict and defend it — including the ones you think I am wrong to sideline.

(d) For your top two priorities, give demotion triggers: what would count, within three years, as evidence that the question deserved a lower rank than you assigned it? A failed formalization, an experimental result about models, a published argument, an inability to state the question without ML jargon — say which, and why that particular failure mode is diagnostic rather than incidental.

Constraints on your answer. No reading lists, no bibliography, no suggestions that we consult someone's later work; I am evaluating reasoning, not recall. Where you depend on a distinction from the literature, render it in your own terms so that I can check whether you have it. And resist dissolving the four puzzles by denying that models have dispositions at all: if you take that line, cost it out, since the group's remit presupposes that our attribution practice is more than shorthand.
