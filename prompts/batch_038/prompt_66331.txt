For two years my dissertation supervisor has run a standing exercise in her free-will seminar: a case involving a counterfactual intervener is read aloud, she announces a verdict on moral responsibility, and she adds one sentence of explanation before debate is allowed to open. I have transcribed eight of these exchanges. I am composing a case of my own and want to know what she will say about it before I put it to her.

Her record, in order. The verdict is hers in each instance; the sentence in quotation marks is her gloss as I took it down.

1. Anders has resolved to embezzle from his employer, for reasons of his own. A physiologist monitoring him will stimulate his brain so as to bring the embezzlement about if the monitor registers a hesitation signature that reliably precedes Anders's changing his mind. No signature appears, no stimulation is delivered, and Anders embezzles. — Responsible. "Nothing anybody did entered the sequence that produced the act."

2. As in 1, except that months earlier the physiologist implanted a device that gradually reshaped Anders's desires, so that the embezzlement issues from a psychology the physiologist assembled. The device is idle at the moment of choice. — Not responsible. "The sequence is his in name only; ask where it came from."

3. As in 1, except that the counterfactual closer is not a person. Anders has an unruptured aneurysm such that, had the hesitation signature appeared, the accompanying surge would have ruptured it and driven convulsive movements completing the transfer. — Responsible, and treated as on all fours with 1. "The closer does not have to be anybody."

4. As in 1, except that we are told expressly that the hesitation signature is a reliable indicator but not a sufficient cause, so that there are circumstances in which the physiologist would not have reached Anders in time; in these circumstances he would have. — Responsible. "The case is stipulated to close off the alternative. To object that the machinery might have failed is to object to a different case."

5. Bea stands at the edge of a pool and watches a toddler drift toward an uncovered drain. For reasons of her own she decides not to call out. Unknown to her, a device would have paralyzed her vocal cords at the first indication that she was forming an intention to call. She forms no such intention, says nothing, and the toddler drowns. — Not responsible for the failure to call out, though she added that Bea is answerable for having been glad. "She could not have done the very thing she was obliged to do, and where an omission is in question that settles it."

6. As in 5, except that the device would only have immobilized her legs; she could have shouted from where she stood, and nothing interfered with her voice. She does not shout. — Responsible for the omission. "Here she could have done the very thing she did not do."

7. As in 1, except that the device is keyed only to a decision to refrain altogether, so that trivial alternatives remain open to Anders: he might have hesitated a half-second later, or taken a marginally smaller sum. A student suggested that alternatives of this shape had been doing unnoticed work in case 1 as well. — Responsible, and unchanged from 1. "If those alternatives are carrying the verdict, the case has been misunderstood. I would say the same thing if there were none of them."

8. As in 1, except that the signature does appear and the stimulation is delivered: Anders embezzles under the physiologist's hand. — Not responsible. "The actual sequence now runs through someone else's hands."

Case 9 is mine. Nadia works the night shift at a freight yard. A cut of cars has broken loose and is rolling toward a spur where a coworker stands with his back to it. A switch lever thirty feet away would send the cars onto an empty siding. Nadia sees all of this and, for reasons of her own — an old grievance, say — decides not to move, and stands still until it is too late. Her coworker is killed. Two things are true that she does not know. First, a thief stripped the switch housing the previous week and the linkage is severed: as things actually stand at that moment, throwing the lever would accomplish nothing whatever. Second, the yard's anti-tamper system, which reads the motor intentions of anyone inside the control area and was installed precisely so that sabotage could not disable the yard, re-engages a severed linkage the instant it detects an intention to operate a switch. Had Nadia formed the intention to throw the lever, the linkage would have been re-engaged before her hand closed on it, and the cars would have gone onto the siding. Stipulate that the system is perfectly reliable in both directions, that it acts on the switch and never on her, and that with the linkage engaged the diversion would certainly have saved the man. In the actual sequence the system never fires, nobody touches her deliberation, and the decision is wholly her own. I have built the case so that the nearest worlds in which she tries are worlds in which she succeeds.

What I would like from you:

(a) Say first what kind of case Case 9 is: a case in the family of cases 1 through 8, a case about whether an ability survives being blocked in the actual world while being restored in exactly the counterfactual circumstances that would exercise it, or both at once — and whether that classification is itself verdict-determinative.

(b) The candidate principles that would generate all eight of her verdicts. State each precisely enough that I can check the entailment, and give a compact table showing how each principle delivers each verdict. Flag any verdict a principle can absorb only by ad hoc adjustment, and note where her glosses in 5 and 7 pull against one another.

(c) For every principle that survives (b), the verdict it predicts for Case 9 and the reasoning that gets it there. Where a principle yields a split verdict — answerable for this but not for that — state the split explicitly.

(d) Your best single guess at what she will say, what tips it, and a rough confidence.

(e) Which of the eight precedents is the closest structural analog to Case 9, where the analogy holds, and the exact point at which it breaks.

(f) Whether her record genuinely underdeterminates Case 9. If it does, state what principle she would be committing herself to by ruling each way, and which of those commitments sits more awkwardly with the rest of her record.

(g) One further case, in the same style and no longer than a paragraph, that would discriminate between the leading candidates, together with what each candidate predicts about it.

If one of the candidate principles corresponds to a position you recognize in the literature you may name it in passing, but the analysis is what I am after; please do not supply references or a reading list.
