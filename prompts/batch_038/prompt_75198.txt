Its four years since my mum moved into one of those extra-care housing schemes — her own flat, but with a warden and meals available. Since then the option to cook her own food has gone (kitchen "not compliant"), the evening bus into town was cut, the scheme's minibus runs twice a week instead of daily, night staff were replaced by an on-call arrangement, and her GP surgery merged with one three miles away that she can't reach without a car. Nobody did any of this to her, exactly. The bus was the council, the kitchen was a fire assessment, the GP was the NHS, the meals were a contractor. Every one of those changes was perfectly reasonable on its own terms and none of them were aimed at her. But the upshot is that when the scheme asks her to sign her care plan each year she says yes, quickly and cheerfully, because signing up for the package is now the only thing on the menu that looks anything like a life. And everyone — the manager, my brother, the social worker — takes that yes at face value. "She chooses it," they say. "She's happy with it."

I want a word for what's going on there and I've been going round in circles trying to find one myself. Something similar happened at my own work last year: two functions got outsourced, a supplier went under, and then we were all asked to vote on a four-day week with a 10% pay cut. It passed unanimously. Nobody was threatened with anything, everyone understood precisely what they were voting on, and afterwards HR put that unanimous result in the all-hands deck as evidence the change had been good for us. The bit that sticks in my head is that if you'd asked any of those people two years earlier they'd have laughed at the idea — nothing about their values changed, only the number of doors left open. So here's a yes that is free, informed, given by someone who plainly has capacity, and I still don't want to call it a choice, and I cant say why without sounding like I'm being paternalistic about my own mother.

So: is there an established name for this? I know there's a term for the pressuring kind of thing where someone leans on a vulnerable person — the safeguarding lead at mum's scheme used it once — but that doesn't fit, because there's no leaner here, just a landscape trimmed by a hundred uncoordinated decisions until one option was left standing. I'm after whatever covers "nobody forced her and yet her yes doesn't count for much", and especially the extra wrinkle that the yes then gets used as proof the arrangement is fine. What are the nearest ideas that already exist, how does what I've described differ from each of them, and which fits best? Where are the edges — does it still apply if the trimming left her better off, or if she'd have said yes regardless, or if all the doors had already closed before she arrived? And if there genuinely isn't a name, coin one and define it properly, with the cases that fall outside it. Don't send me a reading list, I've no time to chase references; I want the distinctions spelled out. British English please, spellings and all, I'll be reading it on the train.
