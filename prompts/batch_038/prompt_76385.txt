Got volunteered to run the opening session at our team's offsite — 75 minutes, whiteboard only, about fourteen ML engineers and three PMs who've shipped considerably more models than I have. Official topic is "what are we actually measuring." What I actually want them to leave with is ontology identification: the question of which real-world property a trained system's concept picks out, and why that question isn't settled by the label column, by a good eval score, or by what the person who wrote the spec meant.

They know overfitting and distribution shift cold, which is my best asset and my worst hazard. Half will hear this as distribution shift wearing a philosophy hat; the other half will smell a compliance talk and open their laptops. They're also allergic to the usual AI-bias war stories, so I don't think I can open on a husky-versus-wolf classifier that keyed on snow — that failure is fixable with better data and it teaches the wrong moral, that this is a hygiene problem. But I need something concrete to hang the whole thing on and I'm torn between two:

(a) a fraud model whose label is "chargeback filed," so the training data records customer behavior rather than the property we care about, and no volume of extra data repairs that because the target was never written down anywhere;
(b) a moderation model whose label is "a user reported this," closer to home for them, but I worry they'll just say noisy labels, known issue, and move on.

Which one carries it, and what does the example have to have and have to lack? And where does the bit about the model changing the thing it measures go — once we gate verification on the score, who files chargebacks shifts. My instinct is to lead with it because it's visceral, but I suspect that frames everything downstream as an optimization story and the actual point gets lost. Rule on the order and tell me what depends on what, not just which order you prefer.

Misconceptions I'm braced for: "collect better labels," "if it predicts well the reference must be right," "just ask the model what it thinks it's looking at." Tell me where in the sequence each gets headed off, and which one is half-correct and shouldnt be flattened.

Then one closing question that separates people who got it from people who picked up the slogan — ideally one where "get better labels" is the wrong answer but not a stupid one. And what should I flatly refuse to go into at this level, and say out loud that I'm refusing? Does naming Quine's gavagai buy me anything with this crowd or does it just sound like lecturing.

I keep calling it "one joint off" internally — the model's concept sits one joint away from ours and the eval can't see the gap. Keep or kill that.
