I am a third-year PhD student in philosophy, and my dissertation chapter concerns the ascription of moral responsibility within historical explanation. My case study is the historiography of the origins of the Cold War, which means I have to be able to speak to historians as well as to philosophers. To get my bearings I read the general introduction to a course reader in the field, written by an established historian, and I want to know how much of its framing I can safely build on. What follows is my own paraphrase of that introduction, reconstructed from notes — not a quotation, and I have tried to keep the shape of the argument rather than the wording:

Debates about the origins of the Cold War came in three waves, and each wave changed what the question itself was. The traditionalists, writing in the first decade after 1945, took the question to be one of guilt: Stalin, driven by Marxist-Leninist ideology and expansionist wherever he detected weakness, was responsible, and the American response was a reluctant one, adopted only once it became evident that cooperation was impossible. The revisionists of the 1960s, writing against the background of Vietnam, inverted the verdict, and their central claim was economic: American policymakers were committed to keeping overseas markets and sources of raw materials open to American capital, read Soviet consolidation in Eastern Europe as a threat to that system, and used the new atomic arsenal in part to impress Moscow; on this account Washington's insistence on its own conception of a postwar order made the division of Europe unavoidable. Guilt migrated across the Atlantic. The post-revisionists of the 1970s and 1980s then abandoned the search for a culprit. Influenced by realist international relations theory, they relocated explanation from the intentions of statesmen to the structure of the postwar system: in a bipolar world with no arbiter above it, each side's defensive precautions presented themselves to the other as offensive preparations, and the resulting spiral made confrontation probable irrespective of who held which capital. What remains is therefore not a verdict but a mechanism; the moral vocabulary of responsibility has been displaced by a systemic one, and historians now treat the question of who started the Cold War roughly as they treat the question of who started the First World War — as one that dissolves on contact with the archive. The opening of Eastern bloc archives after 1991 confirmed rather than disturbed this picture, since the documents showed how little the confrontation owed to anyone's plan.

Two features of this framing make me uneasy, though I cannot yet tell whether my unease is justified or is merely the reflex of someone who has read too much philosophy and too little archival history. I suspect the third wave is being described in vocabulary imported from political science rather than from the historians who belong to it, and I suspect the final sentence is doing considerably more work in the argument than its casual tone suggests. I am also inclined to think — this is my own view, and I want it challenged if it is wrong — that the introduction quietly collapses two questions that ought to be distinguished: a causal question about how a confrontation arose, and a question about who is answerable for it.

Please audit the passage claim by claim. For each substantive claim, tell me whether a specialist in this historiography would accept it, would regard it as a defensible simplification for a teaching introduction, or would actively dispute it, and in the latter case what the more accurate formulation would be. I am especially interested in whether the three-wave periodization, and the characterization of each wave's central commitment, is something the field recognizes or something imposed on it from outside. Then tell me whether the introduction's conclusion — that the culpability question has been retired in favor of a systemic account — follows from anything accurate in the summary, and whether it survives correction of the parts you dispute. Finally, address my own suspicion about the two questions directly: is the distinction between causal explanation and responsibility ascription something historians of this episode actually contest, or am I importing a philosopher's distinction that does no work in their practice? Where you think the introduction is right and my suspicion is misplaced, say so plainly.

Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence — a single verdict on how fair this framing is and which of my two suspicions, if either, holds up — and then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restatement of my question. Throughout, please keep separate what is settled among specialists from what remains genuinely contested, and do not present a contested reading as though it were the consensus view; where you are extrapolating from the general shape of the literature rather than reporting an established point, mark it as such. Do not give me a bibliography, a reading list, or citations to specific works — I want your judgment on the claims, and I will do my own reading.
