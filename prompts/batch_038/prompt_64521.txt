For about two years I've been sitting in on an informal reading group — half philosophy grad students, half ML people, we meet on Sunday mornings in a bakery near campus — and this semester we've been grinding through the standard Kolmogorov complexity textbook together. The group has split into two camps over a question I assumed would take one meeting to settle and which has now eaten six: does Solomonoff induction actually justify Occam's razor, or does it just restate the razor in more expensive notation? The maddening thing is that nobody in the room disputes a single theorem. Both camps agree that Kolmogorov complexity is only defined up to an additive constant once you fix a reference machine, that the universal prior dominates any computable measure up to a multiplicative constant, that cumulative expected prediction error is bounded by something like the complexity of the true data-generating process, and that none of it is computable. They just draw flatly opposite conclusions from the exact same list of facts.

Camp A says the constant is the whole point: invariance means the arbitrariness is bounded, so "simplicity" isn't a matter of taste or of which notation you happen to like, it's pinned down by the math up to a fixed finite slack, and the razor stops being a heuristic and becomes a theorem — the closest thing we have to an objective account of which hypotheses are worth taking seriously. Camp B says that's a confidence trick: the constant is uncomputable, nobody can write down a bound for any specific pair of machines they actually care about, and for adversarially chosen pairings you can make it as large as you like, so "asymptotically irrelevant" is doing all the load-bearing work and the asymptote is precisely the part that never arrives for a finite agent with finite data. They add that fixing a reference language is fixing a human artifact, which means the Goodman-style relativity the formalism was supposed to escape comes straight back in through the choice of machine, and anyway a scheme nobody can run is a picture, not a method. The conversational pattern is completely stuck: when camp A produces a theorem, camp B says "sure, and that theorem is about a limit, or about a machine you can't build"; when camp B produces a finite counterexample, camp A says "you're complaining about a transient, the guarantee is asymptotic." Nobody has moved an inch and I've started to suspect they aren't arguing about the same thing at all.

My working hypothesis — which I want you to attack rather than just confirm — is that the stall is really about what would count as an answer to "why be simple?": camp A thinks a dominance or optimality result in the limit is a justification, camp B thinks only a finite-data guarantee or an actual track record could be one, so each side keeps scoring points the other has already ruled inadmissible before the argument starts. Is that the right diagnosis? Or is this a verbal dispute about "justify" or "objective"? Or is there a hidden empirical question both sides are skipping past — how large the translation constant really is for realistic pairs of languages, say, or whether description length in some natural language has ever tracked successful theory choice in the history of science? Or is the question just ill-posed, since there isn't any one universal prior to be objective about? I want the diagnosis argued for, keyed to what the two camps actually say, not a survey of the formalism — plus an honest answer about what would count as progress: a distinction, a new theorem, an experiment, a reformulation of the question, or a case for expecting no progress and why. Two extras: there's a third guy in the group, an ML PhD student, who thinks the whole fight is moot because whatever the prior turns out to be, gradient descent finds something that works — if that deflationary move is actually a good one, say so, and tell me whether it's the same move camp B is making or a diferent one. And please don't send me a reading list, I can't chase citations right now, I just want the argument worked out.
