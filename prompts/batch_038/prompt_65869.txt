I am a novelist, and I should like an accuracy audit of a passage of dialogue before it goes to press.

In my current book a retired don explains to his granddaughter, over one of those interminable Sunday lunches, why he has stopped believing in his own discipline. I drafted his speech from a chapter in a trade history of historiography that I read last winter, and my worry is that a reviewer who actually works in the field will read it as the confident blundering of a novelist who has half-remembered a secondary source. Here is what he says:

"Ranke's motto — *wie es eigentlich gewesen* — was a demand that the historian turn himself into a pane of glass. No selection, no theory, no moralising: lay the documents end to end and the past appears, as a landscape appears when you wipe a window. It was a scrupulously secular creed, and it worked; for three generations the archive was treated as though it spoke in its own voice, and any hint of interpretation was held to dirty the glass. That is the position Carr demolished. Carr's own definition of history — the historian's re-enactment, in his own mind, of the thinking of the men he studies — is worth memorising, because it puts the historian back inside the picture. There are no facts lying about waiting to be collected, only documents, and a document becomes a fact of history when some historian decides to make it one; Caesar's crossing of the Rubicon is no more factual than any other crossing of any other stream, it is simply the one somebody chose. Selection, therefore, is invention, and the honest historian admits that he is making a case rather than reporting a verdict. White went further still. In *Metahistory* he took the great nineteenth-century historians and philosophers and sorted them by the shape of the story they told — romance, tragedy, comedy, satire — and by the figure of speech in which they habitually thought. The choice of shape, he showed, is not dictated by the evidence, since nothing in the past is tragic or comic in itself. Which means a history is a novel about people who really existed, and one account can be no better supported than another, only better written. The quarrel, then, is settled: Ranke lost, and all that remains to the historian is to be a good storyteller and to say so."

My own instinct is that he is broadly fair on White and hopelessly unfair to Ranke, but I cannot tell whether that instinct is worth anything, so please do not take it as a hint.

What I need from you:

- Work through the speech claim by claim, and for each say what a professional historian of historiography would accept, what is a defensible popular simplification, and what is simply false.
- Tell me whether any doctrine, definition or example is credited to the wrong person, or made to carry a weight its author never gave it.
- Distinguish the errors a specialist reviewer would pounce on from those I may keep as a layman's licence — my character is intelligent but out of date, and some imprecision is characterisation rather than mistake.
- Say whether his conclusion, that the debate is over and only storytelling remains, follows from anything actually true in what precedes it, or whether it is a non-sequitur dressed as a verdict.

No reading list, please, and no citations: I want your judgement rather than your footnotes. Kindly write throughout in British English.
