I'm one of seven trustees at a small river-conservation nonprofit — roughly $9M invested, and we hand out maybe $400k a year in project grants. Our written policy has said for a decade that we fund whichever projects have the highest expected ecological benefit per dollar. Last month a trustee, a retired actuary, circulated a proposal that we switch to what he calls risk-weighted expected utility: instead of multiplying each outcome's value by its probability, you push the probabilities through a risk function first, and he wants ours to be the probability squared. He says the effect is a deliberately pessimistic scoring — a 1-in-1000 shot at a huge win would count for essentially nothing, while a 1-in-1000 chance of a project blowing up (a dam removal that wipes out a fishery, a lawsuit we lose) gets scored almost as though it were certain. His pitch is that this isn't just being careful with money, and he cites a calibration result to the effect that an expected-value maximizer who won't take even a small favorable bet is committed to refusing some enormous ones, so our current policy is quietly committed to something absurd. Another trustee thinks the whole thing is smoke and mirrors: if we're worried about ruin, just make the utility function over dollars concave and keep maximizing expected value — same answers, no new machinery. She also worries that scoring each grant on its own will produce decisions we'd reverse if we looked at the portfolio as a whole, and she asks who gets to pick the squaring anyway. Draft the memo I'll circulate before the January meeting recommending we adopt it, reject it, or adopt some modified version — I want a definite bottom line, reasons our actuary could actually push back against, and a real hearing for the smoke-and-mirrors objection.
