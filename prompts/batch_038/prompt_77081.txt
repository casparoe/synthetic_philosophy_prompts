our department's annual title ix session opened with that recycled animated video where consent gets explained as offering somebody a cup of tea. the version our trainer used ran through the obvious beats: you don't force tea on anyone, if they said yes and then changed their mind you don't make them drink it, someone who's passed out hasn't agreed to tea, and being very drunk isn't an invitation. then she used it to draw a conclusion, which is what I actually want you to look at. her line was roughly that consent is a clear yes or a clear no, and that all the philosophizing about what counts as pressure or whether someone was in a state to agree is people manufacturing gray zones so they can dodge responsibility — if it's as simple as tea, somebody who said yes meant yes.

can you audit the analogy rather than just take a side? i want the correspondences laid out explicitly — what's playing the role of the proposal, the acceptance, the act consented to, withdrawal, and the wrong of going ahead anyway — a table is fine. then the breaks. i'm curious about the features tea has that sex lacks and the reverse: refusing costs nothing and there's basically no history of being punished for refusing tea, the harm from ignoring a refusal is trivial and easily reversed, the offerer is the one doing the thing and the receiver is acted on, and what you're agreeing to is fully specifed and identical every time. and separately, which features are being smuggled in by the trainer's use of it rather than by the analogy itself — does the yes-means-yes reading follow from tea, or is that her gloss on it?

then the validity conditions the picture seems to have no slot for at all: deception about what the act actually is, and power asymmetry — the offer coming from the person who controls your housing, your grade, your visa, or your job. and capacity, which looks graded rather than a clean switch, whereas tea treats it as either on or off. tell me whether the analogy gets these wrong or simply never reaches them, since those feel like different failures. and is there a better standing analogy? contract law keeps coming to mind for me, because it already has doctrines for duress, undue influence, misrepresentation and incapacity doing exactly this work — so if that one is better, what does it smuggle in its turn? (exchange, consideration, parties bargaining at arm's length, the idea that the terms get fixed at signing.)

one epistemic ask: be clear about which parts of this are settled and which are live disputes among people who work on consent seriously. i know there's a real fight about whether consent is a mental state or something you perform, and another about whether coerced consent is a contradiction in terms or a genuine but defective consent, and i don't want either camp presented as the consensus. and if her conclusion survives the disanalogies in some weakened form, say what form it survives in — i'm not looking for a takedown, i'm looking for what the tea can and can't carry.
