My roommate and a friend of hers got into it at dinner about al-Ghazālī's occasionalism and I basically just ate and listened, but it got genuinely good and neither of them would concede anything, so I'd like an actual referee. Context: they were talking about the bit in the Incoherence of the Philosophers where he says all we ever observe is the fire touching the cotton and the cotton burning, never the burning happening *by means of* the fire.

Here's how it went, roughly in order:

(1) Nadia said this makes al-Ghazālī an early Hume — he's denying necessary connection between observed events, full stop, and the "God does the burning" part is just window dressing on a point about what observation can and can't show.

(2) Owen said that's a deflationary misreading. Hume stops at ignorance about the connection; al-Ghazālī names the connection. God creates the burning at the contact, and does so as a fixed habit. So this isn't skepticism about causes, it's a reassignment of them, and the two positions have opposite shapes.

(3) Nadia said reassignment is exactly the problem. Keep every regularity, add a will behind it, and you've explained nothing Hume hadn't already left unexplained. The habit does all the actual epistemic and predictive work; the will does zero. It's decoration you can't test.

(4) Owen said that's unfair, because the will isn't there to predict anything, it's there to settle a modal question: whether the sequence could have been otherwise. On the philosophers' view it's necessary, and if it's necessary there's no room for the fire not burning. That's the whole target of the chapter.

(5) Nadia said fine, then it reduces to a miracle-preservation strategy — if you don't already care about miracles you have no motive to adopt it, and the argument can't be run the other way. And worse, it spreads: if the fire isn't what causes the burning, then my looking at the fire isn't what causes my belief about the fire, so the view undermines the knowledge it's supposed to protect.

(6) Owen said the spread is harmless, because the same habit that links fire to burning links looking to believing, so beliefs are exactly as reliable as anyone else's account makes them — occasionalism doesn't make perception worse, it just relocates the guarantee.

Can you rate each of the six moves, from clearly wrong to clearly right, and say briefly why? I want to know who's right about what, not who wins on vibes, and also what both of them are missing — I had a sense at move 4 that they'd started arguing past each other but I couldn't articulate it. And separately: is there any way to settle (3) against (4) — whether naming God's will earns its keep over just noting the habit — without already deciding whether you're a theist? If not, say so plainly, because I think that's the interesting result.
