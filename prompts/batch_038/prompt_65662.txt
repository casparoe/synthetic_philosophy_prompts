Okay, three months ago I became the intake coordinator for a small mutual aid fund — six of us, all volunteers, we hand out emergency grants for rent and utilities and medical bills, somewhere between 40 and 60 applications a month. The person who wrote our handbook quit two years ago and nobody has touched it since, and I've now discovered its shot through with direct conflicts. Not vagueness, actual contradictions: one section says we never release an applicant's identity or paperwork to anyone outside the panel, another section says a declined applicant has the right to see everything the panel relied on. We got away with it for years because everyone just decided case by case and whoever was more confident won. Last week we got our first formal complaint — someone who was declined wants the file, and two panelists say we're required to hand it over and two say we're forbidden to, and both sides are quoting the handbook at each other, and both sides are right.

That's what sent me down the rabbit hole, and I ended up reading about paraconsistent logics, where a contradiction doesn't let you prove absolutely anything, and about dialetheism, which I think is the stronger claim that some contradictions are actually true. Felt like it was written about my Tuesday. But obviously I can't hand a volunteer a three-valued semantics and call it training.

So: can you write me the decision procedure my panel can actually run on a case? I need the questions in order, and for each one what counts as a sufficient answer. Specifically I need it to sort conflicts into the ones that are only apparent — drafting leftovers that a normal precedence or specificity move dissolves — versus the ones that are real and stay real no matter how you read the document, and say what to do differently in each. Then tell me which ordinary reasoning moves become unsafe once you admit the handbook contradicts itself. I have a feeling you lose something like "it's either A or B, it's not A, so B" but I couldn't explain to a panelist why or when. Give me the plain-English versions with an example of each. And mark clearly where the procedure stops and hands the call to a human because it's a values question and not an interpretation question, plus how we write that up so the applicant can see we didn't just invent an answer.

Constraints: about 30 minutes a case, no lawyer on retainer, every decision is appealable to the full panel and the written reason goes to the applicant, and the people running it have no logic training and won't read more than two pages.

Run it on three of ours:

1. The disclosure conflict above. I should say up front that I've been quietly resolving these in favor of confidentiality for months, so I'm braced for this one to go badly.
2. Section 2 says that where two sections conflict, the later section governs. Section 17 says no section may be read to override Section 5. Section 5 conflicts with Section 21. So does Section 21 govern, or does Section 17 block it — and does Section 2 itself govern Section 17, given Section 17 comes later? Nobody can tell me which section decides which section decides.
3. The handbook defines county resident two different ways in two different sections, and this applicant satisfies one and fails the other. Section 11 says an applicant is either a resident, in which case they get a full grant, or not, in which case they're declined. A panelist is arguing "she's not a resident under the second definition, so we decline." Is that move allowed?

Tell me which of those three gives an answer I'm not going to like, and be honest about which steps of your procedure are judgment calls wearing a procedure costume. Also: do we actually need dialetheism for any of this, or is paraconsistency doing all the work — and which parts of the formal machinery have to survive the translation into a two-page checklist and which can be dropped without breaking it. Finally, what can this procedure not do, and what's the repair loop for getting the contradictions out of the document over time?
