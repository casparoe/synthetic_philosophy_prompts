I have no academic training in any of this. I left school at sixteen, and what I know about drug policy comes from twenty-odd years of reading on my own, from two relatives who died of overdoses, and from eleven years of running a monthly discussion group out of our town library. The group has contained the same two people since the first meeting. One is a prohibitionist of a particular kind: he does not deny that enforcement ruins lives, but he holds that keeping supply illegal is what keeps the number of users down, and that a change in the law changes what the community is understood to approve of. The other is a legalization advocate of an equally particular kind: she does not deny that legal availability would raise use somewhat, but she holds that most of the present death and misery is manufactured by the black market and by the criminal record rather than by the drug. Both are intelligent, both are sincere, and in eleven years they have never once finished an argument, because each keeps answering a different question from the one the other asked.

Our town council votes in the spring on a bundled pilot, and I have been asked to produce one document that both of my members would be willing to sign in front of the council. That is what I want you to draft.

The pilot, as briefed, has four separable parts: (a) simple possession of any drug becomes a civil fine, with no arrest and no criminal record; (b) two supervised consumption sites; (c) a regulated retail market for cannabis — taxed, plain packaging, no advertising, two outlets per district, no delivery; (d) a prescribed-supply programme capped at 500 long-term opioid users. The briefing gives us these figures and no others: a population of 420,000; 63 fatal overdoses last year; roughly 1,100 arrests per year for simple possession; 400 residential treatment places, with a nine-week waiting list. Treat those numbers as given and complete. Do not introduce statistics, studies, named experiments, or other jurisdictions, and do not cite anything — I want a document that two non-specialists can read aloud without one of them reaching for a footnote.

One thing I have worked out myself, and I want it used. I think there are three distinct channels through which a policy change alters how much a drug is used: access (how easy it is to obtain), price (what it costs in money and in risk), and permission (what the law's stance signals about whether the community approves). My suspicion is that my prohibitionist argues almost entirely through permission and my advocate almost entirely through access and price, and that neither of them ever says which channel a given prediction runs through, which is why they never meet. If you think one of my three channels is not really separable from the others, say so plainly and say which, but use the framework wherever it does hold.

Please produce the joint statement with the following structure:

1. A numbered list of claims both can sign. At least two of these must be claims that would be uncomfortable in front of each side's own camp — agreement that costs something, not agreement on the weather. Include claims about what this pilot's design can and cannot establish, given that all four parts start at once.

2. A disagreements table. Columns: the issue; the prohibitionist's position in wording he would sign; the advocate's position in wording she would sign; whether the disagreement is genuine or verbal; and what would actually move it. For each row, say which component of the bundle it concerns, because a prediction about decriminalised possession and a prediction about a retail market are not the same prediction.

3. Three to five cruxes. For each: the sub-question such that settling it would move at least one of them; the quantity measurable inside this pilot, or the argument, that would bear on it; each side's prediction written down in advance and specific enough to be wrong; and which channel — access, price, or permission — the prediction runs through.

4. A short closing section stating which parts of the eleven-year argument were about words. Give the definitions the statement will stipulate for decriminalisation, legalization, harm reduction, and addiction, and say for each whether the stipulation is neutral between the two camps or quietly favours one.

Plain language throughout, no jargon, around 1,200 words. The test I will apply is whether each of my two members can read their own rows and say: that is what I believe, and I would not have put it better myself.
