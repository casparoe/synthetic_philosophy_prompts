I am putting together a seminar for a history-of-science course, one session of which concerns the prehistory of astrobiology, and I want to set an exercise that obliges students to reason through a whole worldview rather than recite a list of findings. The figure I have chosen is Alfred Russel Wallace (1823–1913). Students typically arrive knowing only that he co-discovered natural selection, and they are consistently startled to learn that in his later decades he wrote at length on whether the Earth is alone in hosting life; that he assembled a long inventory of astronomical, geological and atmospheric conditions and argued that our planet's endowment is so unusually well suited to living things that higher life is probably confined to it; that he treated the immense scale and intricacy of the cosmos as functionally required for the emergence of such life rather than as evidence for a plurality of inhabited worlds; that he publicly attacked the Martian canal hypothesis; and that he invoked a spiritual cause both for the first appearance of life and for the human intellect, holding that selection could not account for the mind of the savage — a position that cost him considerable standing among his scientific contemporaries.

Now suppose Wallace were confronted with Fermi's lunchtime remark of 1950 and with everything that has accumulated around it since: the Drake equation and its decomposition of the question into a chain of multiplicative probabilities; the principle of mediocrity, extended from the Copernican assumption, which treats Earth as a typical rocky planet around a typical star in a common sort of galaxy and infers that complex life should be widespread; the survey of thousands of exoplanets, which simultaneously vindicated the plurality of worlds and revealed how varied their circumstances are; the Great Filter reasoning, according to which our continuing silence implies that at least one step in the passage from habitable planet to galaxy-spanning civilisation is catastrophically improbable, with the further and rather unnerving question of whether that step lies behind us or ahead of us; the zoo, quarantine and planetarium proposals; directed panspermia; and — the thing nobody in Wallace's day could even frame — the possibility that whatever crosses between the stars is not an organism at all but an artefact: self-replicating probes, or the descendants of civilisations that long ago transferred themselves into machines.

What I want is a reconstruction, not a biography. Would Wallace take the Great Silence as confirmation of his own position, and if so, would his reasons be the reasons that contemporary advocates of the Rare Earth view give, or something structurally different — a claim about designed fitness rather than about low joint probability? How would his appeal to a spiritual cause for life's origin and for intellect interact with filter reasoning: does his framework locate the filter at a particular natural step, at the origin of life itself, or does it dissolve the whole enterprise by refusing to treat the relevant steps as matters of chance that admit of frequencies? What could he make of machine intelligence and of self-replicating probes, given his dualism — could he count an artefact as an intelligent agent at all, or would the spectacle of a machine civilisation capable of stellar engineering force him to abandon the claim that intellect cannot arise by natural means? And how would he answer the methodological objection that his uniqueness argument rests on a sample of one, and slides from the observation that conditions here are fit for life to the conclusion that they were fitted for it? Finally, please be explicit about where the reconstruction runs out of evidence: I want to be able to tell my students which parts of your answer are interpretation of documented commitments and which parts are extrapolation you are making on Wallace's behalf, and I would rather you flag a genuine gap than fill it plausibly.

Kindly write the whole answer in British English, and structure it so that what you take to be Wallace's own views is kept visibly distinct from the conjectures you advance on his behalf. No bibliography, reading list or citations are needed.
