I'm in year 13 and I run the politics society at my sixth form, which mostly means booking room 4 and dealing with the fact the kettle in there doesn't work. There's about eleven of us. Last Tuesday we did a session on whether breaking the law can ever be legitimate in a democracy where you already get a vote, and two of the committee — Nas and Ellie, who are both in my history set — had a massive arguement afterwards and have been weird with each other ever since, and I've got to chair the next one on Thursday so I'd quite like to know where it actually landed. Neither of them was talking to me, I was just sat there writing it down, so I'm trying to report it as it happened rather than as I'd have said it.

It went roughly like this. (1) Nas opened with the standard view: real civil disobedience has to be public, announced beforehand, non-violent, and you have to stay put and accept whatever punishment comes. His reason was that the willingness to take the penalty is what separates it from just breaking laws you find annoying — it's a costly signal that you're addressing your fellow citizens' sense of justice and still treating the legal system as legitimate even while defying it. (2) Ellie said "costly" isn't a fixed amount. For someone with no settled status, or who'd lose their job, or whose family depends on them, or who has good reasons to be frightened of the police, the price of the signal isn't proportional to how sincere you are, it's proportional to how secure your life already is — so the condition doesn't screen for conviction, it screens for privilege, and it quietly says the only proper law-breakers are the ones who can afford it. (3) She added that the whole framing of handing over your own punishment as a kind of collateral is morally grim, because it makes your suffering the currency you pay the majority in order to be listened to, which means a theory built on it rewards whoever is most breakable. (4) Nas came back historically: the American civil rights movement ran a deliberate jail-no-bail tactic where people got arrested in numbers and refused bail so the jails overflowed and the city couldn't cope, and King argued from a Birmingham cell that breaking an unjust law and accepting the penalty shows respect for law rather than contempt. So the punishment condition isn't an armchair invention, it's a tactic chosen by exactly the people Ellie says it excludes, and it worked. (5) Ellie said that proves her case, not his: filling up the jails works because it makes the punishment nearly free for the protester and ruinously expensive for the state, which is leverage, not a pledge — she said he'd described a bargaining chip as though it were a promise. (6) She also said King was addressing a particular audience, white churchgoers who needed convincing the protesters weren't dangerous, so a tactic calibrated to that audience can't become a universal requirement for movements that don't have it. (7) Nas then said: fine, drop the punishment condition, but tell me what's left to separate civil disobedience from covert law-breaking — if you can mask up, do the action, post the footage later and face nothing, you've got publicity with no cost, and publicity with no cost is just marketing. He said Ellie had no line at all, and that without one her position collapses into "law-breaking is fine when I happen to agree with the cause". (8) Ellie said she does have a line, it's just not the punishment one: covert action hides the deed, whereas a masked action publicises the deed and withholds the person, and those are different things — so the principle should be about transparency of the act, not about whose body ends up in a cell. She finished, half joking, that on Nas's account someone who reports their own forgotten tax return is a better citizen than someone who blocks a road, which tells you the condition is tracking obedience rather than conscience.

Can you go through the eight moves one at a time and rate each of them — clearly right, partly right, or clearly mistaken — with a short reason for each, rather than just telling me who won at the end? I'm particularly interested in whether (3) and (5) are really the same objection or two different ones, and whether (7) is a genuine problem for Ellie or just a demand that she define things his way. Then say who you think is more right overall, and what both of them seemed to be missing — like whether they were even arguing about the same thing, since one of them sounds like they're arguing about what civil disobedience means and the other about who's allowed to do it.
