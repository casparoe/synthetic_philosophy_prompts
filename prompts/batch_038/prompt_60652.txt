You are a senior moral philosopher specializing in distributive ethics — in particular the three-way debate among egalitarianism, prioritarianism, and sufficientarianism — and you have been asked to prepare an analytical brief for an advanced graduate seminar. Work through the analysis carefully and in order; do not substitute a stock rehearsal of the debate for the specific comparisons requested below. Assume throughout that well-being levels are cardinal, interpersonally comparable, and positive.

**The two objects of comparison**

(A) *The leveling-down objection to telic egalitarianism*, as pressed by advocates of the priority view. Because such egalitarianism treats equality as intrinsically valuable, it appears committed to the claim that an outcome in which the best-off are made worse off so that everyone is equally well off is, in one respect, better than the unequal status quo — even though the change benefits literally no one.

(B) *What we shall call the priority-monster objection to prioritarianism*, constructed by analogy with Nozick's utility monster. Because prioritarianism assigns greater weight to benefits the lower the recipient's absolute level of well-being, it appears committed to licensing arbitrarily large aggregate losses to a great many better-off people for the sake of a trivially small benefit to the very worst off.

**Tasks**

1. Open with an explicitly labeled brainstorming section (roughly a dozen candidate points of contact and disanalogy between (A) and (B)), before you commit to any line of argument. Include at least: whether each objection targets a verdict the theory actually issues or a mere silence; whether each admits of a bite-the-bullet reply; whether each is fatal to the view as such or only to a particular weighting of the value the view recognizes; and whether each survives translation into the language of choice between options rather than ranking of outcomes.

2. Distinguish a *weak* formulation of each objection, on which the target theory is only committed to a specified outcome being better *in one respect*, from a *strong* formulation, on which it is committed to that outcome's being better *all things considered*. Set out how the dialectic changes under each formulation, and in particular explain why the standard egalitarian reply to (A) — that leveling down is never claimed to be better overall — is available at one level but not the other.

3. Compare (A) and (B) directly on the basis of that distinction. Are they structurally analogous, in the sense that a single underlying complaint about how each view aggregates does the work in both cases? Or is one of them a substantive objection to an axiology while the other is really an objection to a weighting parameter? Say which, and argue for it.

4. Test your answer against a stipulated view. Define **Gap-Penalized Summation (GPS)** as follows: the value of an outcome is

  V = Σᵢ xᵢ − λ · Σᵢ (x_max − xᵢ),

where xᵢ is person *i*'s well-being, x_max is the level of the best-off person in that outcome, and λ ≥ 0 is a fixed weight.

 (i) Consider a two-person outcome in which one person is at level *h* and the other at level *l* < *h*, and in which *h* is then reduced to some *h'* with *h* ≥ *h'* ≥ *l*, *l* being held fixed. Determine precisely for which values of λ the reduced outcome scores higher under GPS, and show your reasoning.

 (ii) Explain why GPS nonetheless remains vulnerable to the *weak* formulation of the leveling-down objection for every λ > 0, however small.

 (iii) Draw out the significance of (i) and (ii) for the comparison in task 3: does the leveling-down objection really discriminate between relational and non-relational axiologies, or does it discriminate only between degrees of weighting? Answer with respect to both formulations.

5. Conclude with a stated verdict on which of the two objections, (A) or (B), is the more damaging to its target, and why. Do not end on a non-committal note; a qualified verdict (for example, that one objection is decisive under the strong formulation while the other is decisive under the weak) is acceptable, provided you defend the qualification.

**Constraints:** write in polished, formal prose of roughly 1,200–1,600 words, with clearly marked sections corresponding to the tasks. No citations, bibliography, or apparatus of any kind — argue from reasoning and from the stipulations given here, not from appeals to authority. Do not pad the brief with general background on distributive justice that the seminar has already covered; assume your readers know what prioritarianism and telic egalitarianism are.
