Last night a friend and I spent something like four hours on Chesterton's fence, and I have not been able to think my way out of the corner he left me in. I would like you to act as a professional philosopher who works in political philosophy but is equally at home in the analytic literature on status quo bias, precaution under uncertainty, and normative uncertainty, and to diagnose an argument for me rather than to adjudicate the politics behind it. Please think carefully before answering: begin with a short, explicit brainstorm in which you list every step of the argument that could plausibly be the locus of failure and every premise he could weaken without giving up anything he cares about, and only then commit to a diagnosis.

The argument is his, though I have tried to restate it in my own numbering and as charitably as I can. He presents it as nothing more than an unpacking of Chesterton's principle, which he read out to me:

"There exists in such a case a certain institution or law; let us say, for the sake of simplicity, a fence or gate erected across a road. The more modern type of reformer goes gaily up to it and says, 'I don't see the use of this; let us clear it away.' To which the more intelligent type of reformer will do well to answer: 'If you don't see the use of it, I certainly won't let you clear it away. Go away and think. Then, when you can come back and tell me that you do see the use of it, I may allow you to destroy it.'"

He also insists that the passage continues by stressing that the gate "did not grow there" and was not set up by people who built it in their sleep, and that this is the warrant for the second step below: persistence is evidence of purpose precisely because somebody put the thing there.

1. Nobody is entitled to abolish a long-surviving practice until he knows how it arose and what purposes it was supposed to serve.
2. A practice that has survived a long time has survived because it did something for the community that kept it; practices that did nothing worth their cost do not survive.
3. So every long-surviving practice performs at least one function, which we may be unable to identify.
4. Abolishing a practice destroys every function it performs, including the unidentified ones; and there is no reason to expect an unidentified function to be a minor one, since importance is not correlated with visibility.
5. Where the costs of an act cannot be identified they cannot be bounded, weighed, or insured against, and no agent is justified in acting on a cost estimate she knows to be arbitrarily incomplete.
6. Therefore abolition of a long-surviving practice is permissible only for an agent who can state its full function in advance.
7. By (3), the full function of any long-surviving practice includes components nobody has identified, so no agent can state it in advance.
8. Hence no long-surviving practice may ever be abolished — including the ones he and I both think should have been: duelling, coverture, footbinding, the workhouse.

I said (8) was absurd and that the argument must be unsound somewhere. He said that calling (8) absurd is not an objection but a restatement of what he denies: I am treating the conclusion as a reductio only because I already assume reform is presumptively permissible, and (1) through (5) is a road with no non-arbitrary stopping point short of (8). When I pressed him on (7), he offered a repair: (2) is meant probabilistically, not deductively. Long survival raises the probability that a practice has some unidentified function; if that probability is high enough, (5) still rules abolition out, because one would be gambling on the low-probability branch with stakes one cannot size. He was willing to give up the word "never" — the conclusion becomes that abolition is permissible only where the probability of an unidentified function is low, which for anything that has survived several centuries it effectively never is. He also said that if I keep (1) and (5) while rejecting (8), my position is just conservatism with a thumb on the scale: I accept the epistemic principle and then apply it only to the practices I happen to dislike.

My own sense, which I do not trust, is that something slips either between (2) and (3) or between (4) and (5), and that (7) may be reading "full function" in a stronger sense than (1) licenses. But I cannot tell whether that is a diagnosis or wishful thinking, and I cannot answer his stopping-point challenge at all.

So: which step or steps do you reject, and what does rejecting each one cost? Take the argument seriously as valid-but-costly rather than assuming it is a heap of equivocations — I would rather learn that I have to surrender something expensive than be told what I want to hear. If I have to choose, I would most like to keep (1) as a genuine presumption placing the burden on the reformer, and I would like to keep something in the neighborhood of (5), since I rely on precautionary reasoning elsewhere and do not want to abandon it here alone; I would sooner give up (2) or (4). Tell me what those two commitments actually buy me, whether they can be held together with the rejection of (8) without a thumb on the scale, and whether his probabilistic repair escapes the problem or merely restates (8) with a hedge. Mark clearly which parts of your diagnosis are load-bearing and which are speculative, and tell me where you would expect a well-informed Burkean to attack your reply first, since I intend to bring it back to him.

Finally, do not humor me. If my framing is confused — if you think (8) is in fact the correct conclusion and my request for a flaw is question-begging, or if the argument as I have stated it is not the argument he gave and the whole exercise rests on my paraphrase rather than on anything interesting — say so plainly and explain, instead of politely supplying the diagnosis I asked for. And do not give me a bibliography or a reading list; I want your reasoning, not references.
