I'm TA'ing a philosophy-of-medicine seminar next term — about ten grad students, mostly first years who've had one bioethics course and nothing else — and my week is the naturalism vs. normativism fight over what disease even is. I've taught the standard moves a couple times now and the usual examples are dead on arrival: everyone's already seen left-handedness and the old myopia-is-only-a-disease-because-we-built-a-world-full-of-texts line, and half the room just files the whole thing under "philosophers quibbling about statistics." So I want a case I can put on a slide and have people actually argue over, and I'd rather you build it to spec than hand me one of the evergreens.

Construct a thought experiment where Boorse's biostatistical theory and the ability-to-achieve-vital-goals account give *opposite* verdicts on the same person, in the direction people find hard to build: the naturalist says this patient is healthy and the normativist says they aren't. Constraints. The scenario itself is one paragraph, set somewhere boring and real — a clinic, an occupational screening, a school physical — and the condition has to be one that actually exists and that an actual clinician would recognize and treat. No invented physiology, no made-up syndrome, no sci-fi. The statistical normality driving the naturalist's verdict has to be measured against a reference class Boorse would genuinely endorse, the ordinary age/sex kind, not a subgroup you gerrymandered to get the answer you wanted — and you should say out loud which class you used and why it's admissible on his own terms rather than mine. On the other side the normativist's verdict can't be cheap: it can't come from poverty, from discrimination, from the person's social role, or from any fact about their circumstances that isn't their biology. It has to attach to what the body does. And please don't give me a screening number with no consequences, the kind where the patient feels fine and everyone agrees nothing should be done — a clinician reading the case should think the finding matters.

Then show your work, because that's the part I actually need. After the paragraph, name the part-function and its statistical standing and walk through why the biostatistical theory says no disease; then spell out this person's vital goals and how the standard-circumstances clause is doing work, and walk through why the ability account says unhealthy. Tell me which single feature of the case is doing all the lifting, and prove it with the minimal edit — same patient, same biology, one thing changed, ideally just the prevalance — where the two theories stop disagreeing, and say whether they converge on healthy or on diseased. Also tell me where Wakefield's harmful dysfunction analysis lands on the same case, and whether that's a genuine third verdict or just a collapse onto one of the other two. Finally give the Boorsean their best shot: narrowing or widening the reference class, the species-typical-environment move, disease versus injury, whatever they'd actually reach for here, and be honest about whether the rescue works or only works by making the theory compatible with anything. So you know what counts as cheating: I'm not trying to show that naturalism is stupid, I'm trying to make the prevalance-relativity of the account feel like a live cost instead of a technicality, so a case that just announces its verdicts in the description ("a condition nobody considers an illness") fails — the verdicts have to fall out when you actually apply the theories. One last thing: flag anywhere in your scenario where a real doctor would say I've got the medicine wrong, since I'll have one in the room and I'd rather be embarassed now.
