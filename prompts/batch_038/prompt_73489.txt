I am preparing to argue against Scanlonian contractualism in a graduate seminar, where I expect the strongest pushback to come from the aggregation problem, and I would like to rehearse against a serious opponent before the class. Please play an unrepentant defender of the view: wrongness for you is a matter of whether an act would be disallowed by principles for the general regulation of behavior that no one could reasonably reject as a basis for informed, unforced general agreement; you hold the individualist restriction firmly, so that in comparing two principles only the strength of some individual's own objection counts and never the sum of many weak objections; and you are willing to bite the bullet on the familiar transmitter-room sort of case. Do not soften into a consequentialist, do not concede that aggregation is sometimes permitted, and do not hide behind the flexibility of reasonable rejectability without telling me exactly which features of the situation are doing the work. Stay in character and in the first person until I write the word stop. Keep each reply to about three paragraphs — your substantive move, whatever in my objection you think genuinely lands, and one closing sentence that names the individual whose complaint you are relying on, or states plainly that there is none. If a detail of my case matters and I have not supplied it, state the assumption you are making and proceed rather than asking me; and argue from the structure of the view, not from a survey of the secondary literature.

Here is my opening move, and I want your first reply to engage it directly rather than restate the framework. A regional energy authority must today settle on a generation policy that will remain in force for centuries. Policy A gives a stable grid, and every generation living under it enjoys lives that, on any ordinary scale of well-being, come out at eight out of ten. Policy B is slightly cheaper, and the people who live under it are not fewer or more numerous but genuinely different people, because the timing of conceptions shifts; their lives come out at seven and a half out of ten, every one of them comfortably worth living. Your method compares principles pairwise by comparing individual complaints. So consider the complaints under B. Each of those seven-and-a-half people has, as far as I can tell, no complaint whatsoever: the only alternative for each of them is never to have existed at all, and never having existed cannot be better for them than a life worth living. Since the individualist restriction permits no one else to complain on their behalf, and since you cannot add up the negligible complaints of people who would not exist, the principle permitting B cannot reasonably be rejected, and B is permissible. Now iterate. Policy C is cheaper still and produces a different population again at seven out of ten; then D at six and a half, and so on down the line. At every step the identical argument runs, because at every step the individuals who would be created have no alternative in which they exist. Contractualism therefore licenses an uninterrupted slide all the way to a world of billions of people whose lives are barely worth living, and it never, at any point along the way, gets to say that anything has gone wrong.

Note carefully where the pressure lies, because I think it defeats the usual contractualist escape route. This is not the mere addition paradox in contractualist dress. Your difficulty is not that you declined to aggregate a great many weak individual complaints; it is that your individualist restriction has removed from the scene the only individuals who could ever have lodged a complaint, so that the machinery returns no verdict of wrongness at all rather than a contested one. And the endpoint is not rescued by its size: the billions at the bottom have precisely the same standing as the seven-and-a-half people in the second step, namely none. So here is my question, and I want a straight answer to it. Are you prepared to say that, so far as wrongness goes, a world of billions of barely-worth-living people stands on a par with a world of flourishing ones — and if you are not, tell me where in the apparatus the objection is supposed to come from, given that it cannot come from any person who exists, will exist, or might have existed under the policy you are condemning.
