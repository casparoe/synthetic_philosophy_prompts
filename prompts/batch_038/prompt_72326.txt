I teach a graduate seminar on self-knowledge and rational agency, and I keep running into a structure I can't name — I suspect I'm reinventing something, but I'd rather check with you than with Google Scholar at 1am. Describe-back is welcome: tell me whether this is a known thing, and if it's only sort-of known, tell me which known thing it's closest to and where the fit breaks.

Here's the setup. Take an agent — a person, a committee, a piece of software, doesn't matter — that's careful enough to want to check its own reasoning before relying on it. Fine. But the check is itself a piece of reasoning, produced by the same equipment, so to rely on the check the agent has to check the check. Regress. Everybody's seen the regress, and I'm not asking about the regress, that's a seperate matter.

What I'm after is the stopping point, and why it's worse than a mere stopping point. When the agent finally stops and says, in effect, I'll trust my reasoning here — that act of trust isn't a neutral placeholder it can revise later on better evidence. It's load-bearing for everything downstream, and there's no position outside it from which to revise it. Any argument the agent produces in favor of trusting itself is another output of the very thing under examination. So it can't provisionally adopt the premise and then discharge it, because adopting it just is adopting the authority of the source recommending it. The evaluation inherits the evaluated thing's authority, all the way down.

Second feature, and this is the bit that makes me think it isn't ordinary circularity. A *strong* agent here seems worse off than a weak one. A dumb checker that never questions itself just goes ahead and acts. A sharp checker that demands a justification of its own reliability before it acts will never act, since the only justifications it would count as acceptable have to come from itself. The obstacle gets worse as the agent gets more rational, which is backwards from what you'd expect of an epistemic virtue.

Case I use in class: a student proves a theorem and wants to know whether to trust the proof. She re-reads it very carefully and convinces herself. But she also knows she has, in the past, convinced herself of wrong things by careful re-reading. So now she wants a reason to trust careful re-reading — and any reason she can come up with is itself something she arrived at by careful re-reading.

I know the neighborhood: Agrippa's trilemma and the Munchhausen retelling of it, the problem of the criterion, Alston on epistemic circularity, the bootstrapping / easy-knowledge arguments against reliabilism. But I don't think it's any of those, exactly. Those all seem to be about whether a *source* — perception, memory, testimony, a reliable process — can vouch for itself, and the verdict there is dialectical: it's about what counts as a legitimate justification and whether a subject can gain warrant that way. What I'm describing feels less like a bad argument and more like a structural wall: not "you shouldn't accept that inference" but "no inference of the right kind is available from where you're standing," something like a system being unable to certify its own reliability using only its own resources. If that's right, it's a formal result wearing an epistemology costume, and I want to teach it as one rather than as another entry in the skepticism unit.

So, concretely:

1. Does this have an established name?
2. If yes, how does the standard formulation differ from my sketch — did I smuggle something in, or leave out something that does real work?
3. If it sits on a seam between epistemic circularity and some formal theorem, draw the seam exactly. What's the extra ingredient that converts an unsatisfying circular justification into an outright impossibility? Is it self-reference, is it the strength of the system, is it the requirement that the certification be internal, or something else?
4. Give me one case that looks just like this but isn't, and one case that is this but doesn't look like it — ideally not the student-proof case, I have that one already.

Please don't just hand me a label and stop. The label is the least interesting part; I want the boundaries, because I need to know whether the thing I keep gesturing at in seminar is one thing or three things I've been conflating.
