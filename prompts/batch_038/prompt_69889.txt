I am putting together a session for a graduate seminar on literature and practical reason, and I want a reading of *Macbeth* set against the decision-theoretic problem usually called Death in Damascus — the case in which Death's appointment is already fixed, so that the traveler's flight has no causal purchase on whether he dies, while remaining entangled with the evidence about how and where he does. The point of the case, in the debate between causal and evidential decision theory, is that efficacy and auspiciousness can come apart: an act may be causally impotent yet evidentially significant, and Lewis's charge that evidentialism is a policy of managing the news rather than causing it is supposed to bite exactly there.

I want the reading to test a specific thesis rather than rehearse the familiar claim that the play is about self-fulfilling prophecy. My thesis is that *Macbeth* inverts the Damascus structure. There, the act is causally useless and evidentially loaded; in the play, the content of the prophecy is evidentially settled and beyond Macbeth's causal reach — he treats the promises as news about a fixed future — while his acts are precisely what causally determine the manner of their fulfillment. On this reading his central error is a category mistake about the witches' pronouncements: he converts an evidential guarantee into a causal shield, taking the assurance that no man born of woman can harm him as though it constrained what can happen to him, when its truth was always compatible with, and indeed dependent upon, what he does next. Banquo's warning about instruments of darkness that traffic in honest trifles to betray in the deepest consequence would then be a remark about manufactured evidence — accurate predictions deployed so as to corrupt the hearer's reasoning — and Macbeth's failure would be a failure to distinguish being given good evidence from being given a good outcome.

Please work through at least the following, by scene rather than by quotation (paraphrase; I do not want you reconstructing lines from memory):

- The first encounter on the heath, and whether Banquo and Macbeth draw different inferences from identical evidence.
- The soliloquy in Act 1, scene 7, which reads to me like an unusually clean expected-utility calculation — the weighing of consequences here and hereafter, the worry that violent dealing returns to plague its inventor — interrupted by Lady Macbeth's shift of register from consequences to consistency and resoluteness, capped by her claim about what she would do to her own nursing child had she so sworn. I want you to take seriously the possibility that this is a precommitment display of the kind the toxin puzzle makes problematic: an agent insisting she could form and hold an intention whose execution she has no independent reason to perform, and thereby shaming a man whose intention keeps dissolving under his own evidence about himself.
- The apparitions in Act 4, scene 1, as the point where the inversion becomes explicit, and the equivocation that follows — the moving wood, the man not born of woman — as cases where a prediction is true in a way that gave the hearer no usable evidence about what he should do.
- The report of the queen's death and the news about Birnam in Act 5, and what happens to Macbeth's deliberation once the shield fails.

Then be candid about where the reading strains. Are the witches causal agents in a way that simply destroys the analogy, given that the play leaves open whether the suggestion Macbeth recoils from came from them or from him? Does the theological register — providence, the porter's equivocator, the Jacobite politics of equivocation — make the decision-theoretic vocabulary anachronistic rather than illuminating? And does treating Macbeth as a defective calculator flatten a play that may be about the passions and about ambition rather than about inference at all? I would rather you say so plainly than protect the thesis.

Finally, tell me what the play contributes that argument alone does not. My suspicion is that the instability some evidentialists try to fix by requiring that a decision remain choiceworthy once the agent supposes she will make it — that no act be self-eroding — is dramatised in the play as a matter of character rather than of bookkeeping: Macbeth's probabilities shift because his self-knowledge shifts, and the play thereby suggests that the ratifiability of a choice depends on the stability of the deliberator's evidence about herself, which is not a quantity decision theory treats as endogenous. Argue with that if you think it wrong.

Aim for something between 1,500 and 2,000 words, no citations and no reading list, and organized so that the philosophical apparatus and the dramatic evidence alternate rather than sitting in separate halves.
