I have come to hold a strong version of the claim that climate and disease are historical actors rather than backdrops. Specifically: the volcanic eruptions of 536 and 540 CE, the cooling that followed them, and the arrival of plague in the eastern Mediterranean in 541 changed what was possible for the societies they struck — they did not merely accelerate transformations already under way, they closed options that no contemporaneous institution was built to absorb. My test for this kind of actorhood has two parts: counterfactual dependence (the political outcomes of the following century would have been significantly different without the shocks) and constraint novelty (the shocks imposed demands that existing fiscal, grain-supply, and public-health arrangements could not have been redesigned in time to meet).

Tell me what would count against the position, ranked from fatal to merely awkward. Include the defeaters critics have actually pressed — the palynological and settlement records said to show no demographic collapse, the recovery and continued fiscal strength of the eastern empire into the seventh century, disputes about how far ancient DNA evidence really reaches, the roughly ninety-year gap between the cooling and the Arab conquests, and the charge that our chronologies are too coarse to establish the ordering my claims require. Say where defenders of the general thesis would themselves concede that my formulation is exposed. Then assess two structural risks: whether a two-part test of this shape is satisfied by any large catastrophe, making actorhood a label for contingency rather than a claim that could fail; and whether the environmental-determinism objection is a genuine threat to this version of the thesis or a straw man aimed at a weaker one.
