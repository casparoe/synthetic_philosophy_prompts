You're a philosopher who runs adversarial collaborations — someone who is unusually good at writing sentences that neither party has to disown. Think of yourself as the referee who has to produce the document, not as a commentator summarizing a debate. Think carefully before drafting: sketch out, in a few scratch lines, what each side would concede under real pressure, and only then write the joint statement.

Here's the setup. I run a reading group on individual responsibility for collective harms and two members have been circling each other for months. One is an inefficacy skeptic: she thinks that in big diffuse harms — emissions, factory farming, maybe even voting — a single person's act makes no difference to any outcome, so the everyday story that your steak is what caused some particular bird to suffer is simply false. She also thinks most attempted repairs just take a fact about what all of us together do, relabel it as a fact about one of us, and pretend the problem is solved. The other is a marginal-impact theorist: he thinks she's sliding back and forth between an act making no difference at all and an act making a difference far too small for anyone to detect, that expected-value reasoning about threshold-triggering supply chains gets you a small but genuinely individual contribution, roughly your share of the harm, and that once you have that you don't need any exotic participation or complicity story to make the act wrong.

They've finally agreed to sit down and produce a joint statement. Write that statement.

Because they keep fighting over toy cases where goods divide perfectly, I want them to work through one shared running example. Treat all of the following as stipulated — it's fictional but meant to be realistic, and neither party gets to dispute the numbers, only their interpretation:

- Coldwater Eggs is a regional producer supplying one grocery chain through a single distribution center. It ships to the DC in fixed lots of 27,000 eggs per truck, and it plans flock replacement around the contracted lot size, not around what actually leaves the DC on any given week.
- The DC feeds 45 stores. Each store's replenishment system reorders when projected stock drops below four days of sales, and every store order is rounded up to the nearest crate of 360.
- The supply contract contains a tolerance band: in any month the DC may take plus or minus 15 percent off the contracted lot with no change in price or penalty, and production does not flex inside that band. The band's midpoint resets quarterly from a three-year demand forecast.
- That forecast comes from an algorithm ingesting aggregate point-of-sale data, so retail purchases do flow into it — but with a lag of up to fifteen months and a smoothing window wide enough that one carton is not recoverable from the input.
- One carton of twelve maps onto a known amount of feed, and hens are culled and replaced in batches, not individually.

Both parties answer these on the record: does buying one extra carton change the expected number of birds brought into existence and killed, and by how much, with the arithmetic shown; does the tolerance band change the answer, and is that a contingent quirk of this contract or a structural feature of how food supply chains are actually built; and if the honest answer to the first question is zero or indeterminate zero, is the purchase still wrong, and if so in virtue of what.

Now the deliverable.

Section 1 — numbered agreed claims. I want at least twelve and they should have teeth, not things like both parties care about animal welfare. Include explicit agreements about what the skeptic is *not* claiming and what the marginal-impact theorist is *not* claiming, because I suspect half their fights have been about that. Include agreements about the Coldwater structure itself, and at least one agreement about what would make the whole dispute pointless.

Section 2 — a disagreements table, columns: the issue | the skeptic's own preferred wording of her position | the theorist's own preferred wording of his | what the two formulations share | whether the residual disagreement is substantive or verbal, and why. Each side's wording has to be one that side would sign without wincing, and neither may presuppose that the other is confused.

Section 3 — cruxes. For each: a neutral one-sentence statement of the question, the case or argument or data that both agree bears on it, and each side's prediction written down in advance in a form that would be genuinely embarrassing if it came out the other way. Use the Coldwater case wherever it will bear the weight. I expect at least one crux to be conceptual or normative and at least one to be empirical; don't let them all collapse into we just need better data about supply chains, and don't let them all collapse into we just need to agree on a moral principle.

Section 4 — facilitator's note: which parts of this dispute you think were about words all along, what the two should have said to expose that, and what is left standing once the verbal part is stripped out.

Appendix — both parties state whether the voting case is structurally the same as the egg case or different, since pivoting probability and marginal share don't look like the same kind of quantity to me. If they disagree, that goes in as a crux rather than getting fudged.

Ground rules: don't adjudicate, nobody wins, and don't write it as a dialogue or a transcript — it's a document both can put their names to. No citations, no bibliography, no recommended reading, no name-dropping of philosophers; the reasoning is the point. Avoid strawmen in both directions, including the subtle kind where a side gets described using vocabulary only its opponent uses. And watch the length: seperate the scratch work from the document, and keep the document tight enough that a busy person would actually read it.
