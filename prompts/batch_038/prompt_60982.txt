I study how democracies erode, and I keep needing a distinction I cannot find a name for. Here is the pattern. A governing party takes a step that critics call undemocratic — replacing the leadership of the public broadcasters, say, or rewriting the procedure for selecting judges so that its own nominees come to dominate a constitutional court. When challenged, it does not defend the step on the merits. It points instead to an earlier, structurally similar step taken by the party now doing the challenging: you also filled seats in the last weeks of a term; you also invoked the same emergency powers when you held office. The critic usually cannot deny the precedent and is left with two bad options — condemning both sides, which reads as false balance and costs them their own constituency, or accepting the precedent, which raises the baseline for what the next government may do. The following round starts from that raised baseline, and the same move is available again, now citing the round just completed. Erosion thus runs through this reciprocal licensing, and it is one-directional: the party with the longest catalogue of grievances has the most ammunition, and the baseline only moves toward it.

Is there an established concept for this? It feels adjacent to whataboutism and to discussions of decaying norms of restraint between rivals, but those seem to describe either a single exchange or the violations themselves, not the cumulative one-way shift in what counts as acceptable. Tell me which existing concepts come closest, where each falls short of what I have described, and if nothing fits, coin a term and define it with boundary cases — including what would look like this but not be this. A bare label will not help me; I need the carving.
