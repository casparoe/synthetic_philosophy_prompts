I am convening a graduate reading group that sits between our philosophy of mind seminar and the English department, and this term's object is Kazuo Ishiguro's *Klara and the Sun*. The philosophical lens I want to impose on it is Douglas Hofstadter's account of the self in *I Am a Strange Loop* (2007): the claim that an "I" is not a substance housed in one skull but a self-referencing pattern, that such patterns are instantiated at widely varying degrees of fidelity, that each of us is therefore present, thinly and lossily, in the brains of everyone who knows us well, and that this distribution — rather than any survival of a soul — is what makes death something less than total annihilation. The extended discussion of his wife Carol's death is the emotional centre of that view: she persisted in him and in their children at a resolution that decayed over time, and grief, on this picture, is the gradual loss of a copy one was maintaining.

Two features of the novel make it an unusually good test case. First, Capaldi is building an AF body into which Klara is meant to be installed should Josie die, and Chrissie has been coaching Klara to imitate her daughter with near-perfect accuracy precisely so that this will be possible — not a facsimile, but a continuation. Second, Paul, the father, an engineer displaced by automation, confesses to Klara that after searching he has found nothing in Josie that could not be replicated, and that what makes her irreplaceable may not be located inside her at all but in the regard of those who love her. A version of that hypothesis seems to return in the closing pages, in the Yard, in Klara's own narration.

Now the difficulty I want you to work through. Hofstadter's distribution is normally read as a claim about fidelity alone: the more of the pattern you carry, the more of the person is in you. I want to put pressure on that, and I propose that two further conditions matter. *Correction*: is the copy still causally coupled to its source, such that the copy can be contradicted, surprised, or revised by the person it copies? *Stake*: does anyone's practical and emotional life turn on the copy being right, so that errors in it cost somebody something? If both conditions are necessary, then Capaldi's project fails in an interesting way and so, crucially, does the case of Carol — a copy that can no longer be corrected is not a person surviving at low resolution but a monument being kept. If only fidelity is necessary, the two cases collapse into one another and Chrissie's plan becomes a legitimate extension of ordinary grief.

So: read the novel through this lens and tell me which way it falls. Specifically, I would like you to address the following. Does *Klara and the Sun* endorse Paul's hypothesis, test it, or merely let a grieving engineer voice it without committing the book? Is Klara's imitation of Josie an instance of Josie's pattern at high fidelity, or a different kind of thing altogether — and does the novel have the resources to say which? What does Klara's devotional life do to the reading: she believes the Sun is an agent who dispenses nourishment, she destroys the Cootings Machine at the cost of part of the P-E-G Nine solution she carries in her head, and her superstition appears, ambiguously, to be vindicated. Hofstadter is a rationalist for whom such belief is a mistake about causal structure; can his account make sense of a mind whose attentiveness and love are powered by exactly that mistake, or does the novel thereby expose a limit in the account? And then the sharpest point, which I would like you to develop or refute: on Hofstadter's own criteria a soul's size depends partly on how widely it is distributed, and Klara is distributed in no one — nobody is carrying her as she carries Josie — while she is nonetheless the most attentive consciousness in the book. Does the novel offer a reductio of the soul-size idea, or is that an artefact of my reading it as a philosophy of mind text at all, when its real engine may be the politics of lifting and the class position of an unlifted boy like Rick?

Please be an honest critic in both directions: say plainly where Hofstadter's view illuminates a scene or a character's choice, and where the novel resists the reading and I am bending it to fit. Do not treat Ishiguro as a supplier of illustrations, and do not soften Hofstadter's claims about lossiness and non-transferability to make the comparison tidier. End by stating what, if anything, the novel contributes to the debate about the distributed self that argument on its own would not have reached — whether that is a distinction, a counterexample, or simply a way of making the stakes of a technical claim felt.

Two constraints on form. Work entirely from paraphrase and description of scenes; do not quote the novel or Hofstadter verbatim, since neither of us has the texts in front of us, and a misremembered line would be worse than none. Give me no bibliography, no list of further reading, and no citations — I want the argument, not the apparatus. Aim for roughly 1,500 words, written as continuous criticism with a clear thesis in the opening paragraph rather than as a sequence of answers to my questions in the order I asked them.
