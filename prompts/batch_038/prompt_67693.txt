I've got about two years as a solo postdoc — philosophy/decision theory background, no compute, no lab, nobody to bounce things off — and I want to spend it on the ontology identification / inaccessible information cluster: the worry that an agent's own world-model doesn't cleanly contain the categories its goal is stated in, and that some of the info needed to fix that mapping was never in the channel it learned from in the first place.

Candidate lines I keep circling. I want a ranking of these for someone in my situation, with the reasoning defended rather than just listed:

1. Formalize the goal-ontology → learned-ontology mapping as its own object: what conditions make a pointer "correct," and what the failure taxonomy actually looks like.
2. The channel-capacity version: bound the mutual information between training data and the human-relevant referent, then work out where that bound genuinely bites vs. where it's a red herring that makes the problem sound more hopeless than it is.
3. Overinclusion instead of absence — assume the model *does* have something diamond-shaped in there, but its extension quietly sweeps in simulated diamonds, diamond-like lattices, tokens in documents about diamonds, so the goal gets Goodharted through a concept that looks fine from the inside. Is this even a separate problem from 1, or a special case?
4. Interpretability-first: treat the whole thing as an engineering question about reading concepts out of trained models, and see whether any conceptual content survives that reframing.
5. The dissolution move: argue it all evaporates once you stop assuming goals are fixed ahead of time, then pin down precisely what's left over after you make that move.

Criteria I care about, in roughly this order: how much downstream work depends on the answer, whether one person with no GPU can make real progress in 24 months, and whether an answer would change what anyone actually builds. Tell me which of these are secretly the same question wearing a costume, which one I should just drop, and what I'd plausibly see over the next couple of years — a theorem, a technique, a released model — that should make me re-rank the whole thing.
