I'm a senior, and next month I'll be defending a thesis at my school's research symposium. The thesis is that anatta is not a denial that persons exist and not an ancient anticipation of Parfit-style reductionism, but a claim about ownership. My argument is that the reasoning in the Not-Self Characteristic discourse turns on control and on affliction — the aggregates are unfit to be taken as self because they disobey us and because they are bound up with stress — so the operative criterion of selfhood is sovereignty rather than substance. On my reading the doctrine is therefore silent on whether persons persist, which makes the usual move of pairing the Buddha with Parfit a category mistake.

The panel is a Buddhist studies professor who reads Pali and has practiced for years in the Thai forest tradition, an analytic philosopher who works on personal identity, and a cognitive neuroscientist who studies narrative self-models. Give me the objections I will actually face, in the order they will come, phrased the way each of those three would phrase them, with a two-minute answer to each and an unflinching note on where that answer is softest. Tell me what to concede in my opening so it isn't dragged out of me, and name the question I'm hoping nobody asks. If you judge that one of these objections genuinely defeats the thesis, say which and why; I want a verdict, not a survey of considerations.
