You are a philosopher of language and social ontology whose specialty is David Lewis's theory of convention, coordination problems, and the emergence of norms. Please think carefully and work in two passes: first cast as wide a net as you can over candidate readings of the slogan, then prune, order, and evaluate. I want analysis rather than a survey — name positions and authors where that sharpens a point, but do not quote passages, do not cite page numbers, and do not hand me a reading list.

Here is my situation. I am auditing two seminars this term, and in both of them the same sentence did the same kind of work: "it's just a convention." Having been reading Lewis, I find that the sentence slides between things he would sharply separate, and I cannot yet say which slides are being exploited.

First seminar, philosophy of logic. A visiting student who works intuitionistically objected that our free use of excluded middle begs the question against her. A classmate replied, roughly: the law of excluded middle is just a convention, each side is entitled to its own, there is no fact of the matter which is correct, and so there is nothing to argue about. He added, in what he took to be her favor, that she loses nothing anyway: every classical theorem has a double-negation translation she can accept, while she keeps a distinction between a statement and its double negation that our logic collapses. The remark was treated as settling the dispute and we moved on. I am confident it did not settle anything, but I cannot say what it would have taken to settle it.

Second seminar, a social ontology reading group. The same phrase came up about the alphabetical-author-order convention in mathematics and theoretical economics — "it's just a convention" — said with the implication that the field could switch to contribution-ordering next year without anybody having been wrong about anything, and that people who resent appearing last are confused. Here I largely agree with the speaker's conclusion, and yet the claim feels very different from the first one, despite the identical words.

What I want is a disambiguation of the slogan, laid out as a menu:

1. Enumerate the distinct precise claims the sentence might be making. I expect at least five or six, ranging from something very near a platitude to something radical. State each as a single explicit thesis, and for each say what it commits the speaker to on the following points: whether some alternative regularity would have served equally well; whether the practice arose by design or by convergence; what reasons, if any, individuals have to conform to it; whether it is changeable and by whom; and whether a dispute about it is a factual dispute or a merely verbal one. Where Lewis's own machinery is relevant — regularity in a recurrent situation, common knowledge, the preference to conform conditional on others conforming, the general preference that others conform, common interest, and the arbitrariness clause — say whether that machinery actually applies here, and identify which of his conditions the case in question fails to meet.

2. Give each reading a verdict: platitude, substantive and defensible, substantive and questionable, or false, with a brief justification. Note explicitly where a reading is true of one of my two cases and false of the other.

3. Say which readings, if any, would license the dismissal in the logic seminar. In particular: does arbitrariness in Lewis's sense entail that there is no fact of the matter, that both parties are entitled to their logic, or that the disagreement is merely verbal? And does the genealogical point — that our logical practice was not designed but grew up — support any of those conclusions, or is that an independent inference? Address whether the logical case even supplies the coordination structure with common interest that Lewis's analysis requires, or whether what governs logic is something else (a constitutive rule, a norm of assertion, a semantic practice) that the slogan merely borrows the vocabulary of.

4. Say which readings are operative in the author-ordering case, and locate exactly where the two cases come apart.

5. Finally, give me the strongest single claim the philosophy-of-logic speaker could have made — the most defensible thesis available within a Lewisian framework that still leaves the intuitionist with something genuine to object to — and say what he should have said next in order to actually engage her.

Be explicit throughout about where you are exercising substantive philosophical judgment rather than reporting a standard view.
