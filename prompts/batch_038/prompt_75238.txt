I am at work on a novel whose central institution is a Ministry of the Past, and I need a prop document that could be tipped into the book as a found object: a parody of the folded patient-information leaflet that comes inside a prescription box, for a medicine whose active ingredient is history. Around six hundred words, in the standard bureaucratic register, with the customary headings — description, indications and usage, dosage and administration, adverse reactions, warnings and precautions, contraindications, overdosage, animal studies, patient counseling information, how supplied.

The comedy has to be clinical rather than merely absurd. Three formulations are on the market, corresponding to the monumental, the antiquarian, and the critical modes of engaging with the past that Nietzsche separates in the second of the Untimely Meditations, and the pharmacology of each must be exactly what he says that mode does: its genuine therapeutic benefit, the population it is indicated for, its ceiling dose, and the particular way it degenerates when the regimen is continued too long. Let the individual-dosing variable be what he calls plastic power — the capacity to metabolize a past without being poisoned by it — and let the overdosage section describe not one patient's collapse but an entire age's, with the historical sickness treated as a population-level condition rather than a personal one. The animal-studies subsection should carry the grazing herd that lives unhistorically and dies unhistorically as the comparator species, and the leaflet should be honest that the comparator is not proposed as a substitute therapy.

Two constraints on the target. First, the insert must not imply that any one of the three formulations is simply the correct drug: all three are indicated and all three are hazardous, and the satire is aimed at the temper of a culture that has come to treat its relation to the past as a dosage question — not at Nietzsche, and not at any living person. Second, please do not quote the Meditation, cite its sections, or lean on any translation's phrasing; work entirely from paraphrase, and let the piece turn on the accuracy of the distinctions rather than on recognizable wording.

Keep the register utterly deadpan and uninflected: no exclamation marks, no authorial nudge, no joke that depends on the reader noticing the joke. A reader who has never opened Nietzsche should still find it funny as a document; a reader who has should be able to tell that whoever wrote it had read it closely.
