I am a fourth-year doctoral student in philosophy working on skill and expertise, and over the summer I spent several weeks embedded in a motor-cognition laboratory, where the two PIs have asked me to put my theoretical commitments in writing before they decide whether to keep me on as a collaborator. I would like a rigorous consistency check on the resulting list before it goes to them. Here is what I have written down, roughly in the order the views came to me:

1. I accept an intellectualist analysis of knowing-how in the Stanley–Williamson line: to know how to φ is to know, of some way w of φ-ing that is a way for the subject to φ, that it is such a way, where w is entertained under a practical mode of presentation. Knowing-how is therefore a species of knowing-that.
2. I hold that knowledge is factive and that it is a mental state rather than an ability, an achievement, or a disposition to behave — knowledge-first, in that limited sense.
3. I hold that practical modes of presentation are individuated functionally, by the role they play in producing and guiding the subject's intentional φ-ing: two ways of thinking about the same way of φ-ing count as the same practical mode only if they play that guiding role alike.
4. I reject the Rylean entailment. Knowing how to φ does not require being able to φ; my standing example is a coach, formerly an elite gymnast, who is now paralyzed from the chest down and who on my view knows perfectly well how to perform a layout step-out although she can no longer execute one. I deny the converse entailment too.
5. I accept the linguistic case for intellectualism: "know how" embeds under negation, forms interrogative complements, and takes the same range of complements as "know that," "know where," and "know when," and the best explanation of this uniformity is that these are all instances of a single propositional-attitude verb.
6. I think the regress argument against intellectualism fails. Acting intelligently does not require first contemplating a proposition, and what blocks the regress is precisely that the proposition is entertained under a practical mode of presentation, which is not itself something one must know how to deploy in the same way.
7. I want to preserve Ryle's central insight: know-how is essentially bound up with skill, it is exercised and displayed in action, and it is had by non-human animals and by pre-linguistic children — the chapter of my dissertation on infant reaching depends on this.
8. I am a contextualist about ascriptions: whether a know-how ascription is true depends on the ascriber's standards, and in particular on which ways of φ-ing are relevant in the context.
9. Two case verdicts I would not abandon without argument. First, the amnesic virtuoso: a concert pianist who, after viral encephalitis, plays a Chopin ballade flawlessly and intentionally but has lost every capacity to recall, describe, recognize, or correct her own performance; I say she still knows how to play it while no longer knowing that any such way is a way to play it. Second, the armchair physicist: someone with no athletic training who has derived from first principles a complete kinematic description of the layout step-out; I say he does not thereby know how to perform it, even though on my own account there is a way for him to φ and he knows it.

My suspicion is that this is more a grab bag than a position, but I cannot locate where it actually breaks, and I am not going to tell you which commitments I would be most reluctant to give up, because I do not know that yet — part of what I want from you is an argument about which ones are load-bearing. So please audit the set as a set rather than lecturing me on the literature: identify which pairs of commitments genuinely pull against each other and which merely look as though they do, keeping in mind that I expect at least one pair that strikes me as scandalous to dissolve under inspection, and I do not want that one padded into the list of problems. For each genuine tension, state exactly what would have to give, rank the tensions by severity instead of presenting them as equally bad, and then identify the cheapest single revision that would restore coherence, together with what that revision costs me elsewhere — including whether it forces me to retract either of the verdicts in (9), and, if so, what I could say about the coach and the physicist instead. Please write your response in American English.
