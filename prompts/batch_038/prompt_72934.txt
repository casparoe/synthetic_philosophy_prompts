I am the standards editor at a mid-sized international news service. We file wire copy that is picked up by a few hundred small outlets, most of which will run our words unchanged, so whatever we decide to call a thing becomes what their readers call it. I have no academic training: I was a copy chief for eleven years before this job, and what I understand about genocide as a concept I have assembled on my own over the past year, mostly from the 1948 Convention and from things written about Raphael Lemkin, who coined the word.

The definition I keep pinned above my desk is this: "In the present Convention, genocide means any of the following acts committed with intent to destroy, in whole or in part, a national, ethnical, racial or religious group, as such: (a) Killing members of the group; (b) Causing serious bodily or mental harm to members of the group; (c) Deliberately inflicting on the group conditions of life calculated to bring about its physical destruction in whole or in part; (d) Imposing measures intended to prevent births within the group; (e) Forcibly transferring children of the group to another group." If that is not the text your procedure should be applying, correct me before you begin.

My difficulty is that the word arrives on my desk doing two entirely different jobs, and my desk can no longer tell them apart. One job is legal: there is a treaty definition, courts apply it, and there is a fact of the matter about whether a given finding has been made or not made. The other job is that it is the strongest word our language has for the worst thing one group can do to another, and readers, survivors, governments and advocacy organizations all hear it in that second sense. I suspect the two came apart a long time ago and that nearly every fight we have in the building is really about which one a particular sentence is doing. I have been trying to write the rule down for eight months and I keep failing.

Here is as far as I got. My draft rule is: we may print the word only if the same story could carry, without hedging, a sentence naming the protected group being destroyed and the act being done to it — and if we cannot write that sentence, we do not have a genocide story, we have an atrocity story. Four things about my own rule bother me, and I want each of them dealt with rather than smoothed over.

First, I am told that "in whole or in part" is what saves the definition from requiring a completed extermination, but nobody can tell me how large the part must be, and my reading is that the treaty simply does not say and that adjudicators have had to fill the gap themselves. If the threshold is a matter of judgment reserved to a bench, then my rule asks a reporter to make a judgment she is in no position to make.

Second, the Convention's list of groups omits political ones, and I have read that this omission was a compromise struck in 1948. If so, the vocabulary available to us in some of the worst stories of the last seventy years is being fixed by a bargaining position taken by states, several of which no longer exist.

Third, Lemkin appears to have meant something wider by the word than the treaty ended up carrying — including the destruction of a group's language, institutions, monuments, and capacity to go on being a group. We file a great many stories in which the killing is disputed but the dismantling of a community's means of continuing is not, and I have no word for that at all.

Fourth, if naming something genocide is supposed to trigger obligations in somebody, then declining to name it is also an act with consequences. I do not want a procedure that lets my desk hide behind a definition while a story runs for months.

What I am asking for: work as a philosopher of law with a genuine command of international criminal law and of the conceptual literature on genocide, and specifically as someone who has thought about what happens to a legal definition when people who are not lawyers must apply it under deadline. Think carefully before writing. Begin, and show me your work, with a short exercise: list the distinctions you believe must survive into a working desk procedure, then the ones a reporter can be allowed to lose, and for each of the latter say what is lost by losing it and what kind of error that loss will produce.

Then build the procedure. It must include:

1. An ordered set of questions the desk runs on a story before the word goes into copy — the questions in the sequence they should be asked, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, and where an insufficient answer stops the procedure at a definite no.
2. Each question labelled as one of three kinds: settled by facts we can gather; settled by a determination that someone else has or has not made; or settled by judgment that no procedure can remove. The third kind must be flagged openly, not buried in the wording.
3. The points at which the procedure hands the decision up or across — to counsel, to the editor-in-chief, to the regional desk — and what exactly is being handed over: a fact, a legal question, or a question about what we owe readers.
4. A vocabulary section for what the desk writes when the test fails. I want this usable rather than evasive, and I want you to be honest about which of the available fallbacks is itself a loaded term that a style guide has no business treating as neutral.
5. A ruling on the relationship between a legal determination and a descriptive use: whether my desk is permitted to describe something as genocide in the second sense while no competent body has ever made the first, and if so what the copy must do to keep the two from collapsing into each other.

Then run the procedure on three cases, and report what it produces rather than what I would prefer it to produce.

- Rwanda, in the third week of April 1994. We have roadblocks, eyewitness accounts of killings in churches, a body count rising faster than we can verify it, and no tribunal exists and none will for years. What goes into copy that week?
- Cambodia, 1975 to 1979, and the death toll of the regime taken as a whole. I am aware that particular minority communities inside Cambodia are argued about separately; I mean the total. I expect this case to produce an answer I will dislike, because I believe most of the dead were killed as members of categories the definition does not list. If that is correct, say so plainly and say what the desk writes instead.
- The destruction of the Herero and Nama in what is now Namibia between 1904 and 1908. A treaty that did not yet exist, a court that can never take the case, and a story we will file again every time two governments negotiate. What does the procedure answer when the legal route is closed forever and the moral question is still open?

Finally, tell me where the procedure will misfire. I want the classes of story in which running it honestly yields copy I believe to be wrong, and the classes in which a lazy desk can satisfy every step and still mislead its readers. Tell me also what it cannot do — the work people in my building will expect this rule to perform that no rule can perform.

Close with a version that fits on one side of an index card, in the imperative, that a reporter who has never read the Convention can run in three minutes.

Please do not supply a bibliography, a reading list, or citations to cases and scholarship. I cannot verify them and my reporters will not look them up; where you rely on something specific, describe it instead of citing it.
