**Keynes's Carrier Problem**

The usual account of *Economic Possibilities for Our Grandchildren* is that Keynes made two forecasts — a several-fold rise in living standards within a century, and a fifteen-hour working week — and that the first came true while the second did not. The failure is then explained by his underestimating the appetite for positional goods, the hedonic treadmill, or the durability of work as a source of status. I think that diagnosis is too shallow, and that the essay contains a structural problem that would have blocked the good life even if the short working week had arrived on schedule.

The problem is that Keynes runs two incompatible accounts of what the art of living is. Call them the suppression model and the production model. On the suppression model, the capacity to live well is a standing feature of human nature that economic necessity merely crowds out; remove the necessity and it flowers by itself, the way a plant recovers once you stop standing on it. Much of the essay's rhetoric works this way — the image of the lilies of the field, the confidence that once the struggle for subsistence is settled we shall value ends above means again, the suggestion that the love of money will be recognized as a morbidity rather than treated as something to be cured. On the production model, by contrast, the art of living is a stock of habits, skills, rituals, and tastes that has to be manufactured and transmitted by particular institutions: a milieu with genuine time on its hands, patrons, long-lived households, apprenticeships in leisure, and a visible class of people whose job description is to be good at living. Stocks of that kind depreciate. They do not regenerate spontaneously.

My claim is that Keynes is committed to the production model in fact and to the suppression model in argument. When he looks around for people who actually know how to occupy unforced leisure, he finds them in one social location: the Cambridge and Bloomsbury world he inhabited, and the rentier class whose accumulated capital had purchased for its members a century of attention to art, conversation, and the arrangement of a life. That class is the carrier. And his own policy program, most explicitly in the closing discussion of social philosophy in the *General Theory*, aims at its euthanasia: the rentier is to disappear, the scarcity value of capital is to be socialized away, investment is to be deliberately steered. I don't think this is hypocrisy, and I don't think he overlooked it. I think he assumed the cargo would survive the burning of the ship — that habits the rentier class had accumulated had by then become general cultural property, available to anyone who acquired free hours.

They had not, and the reason is not greed. It is that those habits were never encoded anywhere except in the practices of the people who had them. Keynes's own favorite instrument, compound interest, cannot be turned on this asset, because there is no instrument in which to hold it: the art of life yields nothing, has no market price, and has no natural investor, and it is the only thing in his story that a century of abundance is supposed to purchase. So the good life is orphaned rather than deferred, and the orphaning is caused by the same program that delivers the abundance.

A Keynesian has an obvious repair: the carrier need not be a rentier class. Any durable non-market institution can hold the stock — universities, public broadcasters, libraries, parks, the arts councils that mid-century social democracies actually built. On this version, the euthanasia of the rentier is fully compatible with the good life, provided the state inherits the patronage function. I think the repair is available and also that it is worse than the disease, because it converts Keynes's picture of the good life — private, spontaneous, answerable to nobody — into something administered, and administered leisure is precisely what the twentieth century proved adept at producing and inept at enjoying.
