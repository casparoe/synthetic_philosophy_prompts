I am not a philosopher by training and have never taken a course in the subject, but I have watched Andrew Niccol's Gattaca perhaps twenty times over the last decade, and I have spent much of that time reading about meritocracy and desert on my own, without anyone to correct me. I would like a serious reading of the film in terms of that debate — criticism, not a summary of the usual themes about eugenics and genetic discrimination.

Here is the thought I have been circling, and I would rather you test it than accommodate it. Nearly every discussion of the film treats it as an objection to a measurement: the genome is a poor predictor of performance, so the gates are drawn in the wrong place. I suspect the film is more radical than that, and that its real subject is the predicament of the winners. Jerome was designed to be the best, won a silver medal instead, and threw himself in front of a car; he puts the medal on before he walks into the incinerator at the end. Lamar, who lets Vincent through the final test, confesses that his own genetically selected son turned out not to be everything that was promised. Anton, the designed brother, loses the open-water race twice and has to be pulled out of the sea both times. Irene is a valid, chosen before birth, and is still barred from deep space by her heart. I have started calling this "advance desert" — the idea that in such a system a person is credited with a claim on the good positions before he has done anything, on the strength of a prediction, so that when the prediction fails he experiences it as a debt defaulted on rather than as a loss. Is advance desert a coherent notion, or am I simply collapsing desert into expectation or entitlement? And is it Jerome, rather than Vincent, who carries the film's argument?

Two further things I want pressed. First, the counterfactual: suppose the tests were perfectly accurate, and Vincent's predicted heart failure and thirty-year lifespan were exactly right, and any valid would in fact have navigated the Titan mission better. Would the system's verdict then be correct, or would he still have a complaint? If the film's indictment does not survive that counterfactual, then it is attacking the epistemics of meritocracy rather than its moral core, and I want to be told so plainly. Second, the fraud: the hero wins by climbing a borrowed ladder of hair, skin, and urine samples, and the film asks us to root for that. He does the job well, so the ladder is doing nothing but correcting an error — and the murder at the centre of the plot was committed by the mission director to stop the launch being cancelled, which suggests the institution cares about output rather than persons. Does the film thereby hold that desert is fixed retrospectively by performance, so that breaking into the gates is legitimate when the gates are wrong? And what is the swim supposed to prove — that refusing to save anything for the trip back is desert in action, or that the appetite to risk drowning is itself an unchosen gift, which is just what those who deny that anyone deserves their native endowments insist?

Do not reduce the film to an illustration of the debate, and do not stretch the concept of desert until it fits the scenes. Tell me where the film resists this reading; I know it keeps speaking of destiny rather than justice, and I suspect its nostalgia and its scriptural framing pull somewhere else altogether. Then tell me what the film gives the argument about desert that argument alone would not have supplied.

Finally, say what would change your mind: which scene or which argument would force you to abandon the reading you have offered, where your reasoning is most likely to fail, and any point at which you are leaning on a contested position rather than on something settled. If advance desert turns out to be a confusion, I want to hear that directly.
