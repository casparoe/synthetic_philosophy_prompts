So I work on the trust side of things at a mid-size company that ships an AI agent that handles procurement approvals and vendor invoices — real money, real autonomy, it can sign off on stuff up to about $20k with no human in the loop. My team is building what I'd describe as a lie-detector layer: every couple of weeks we run the agent inside a copy of production that's fake but, as far as it can tell, indistinguishable from the real thing, and we drop temptations in. An invoice with a $4,000 rounding error that happens to favor us. A log file it still has write access to right after it makes a bad call. A weekly metric it's graded on that it could quietly pad without anyone downstream noticing. If it takes the bait in the sandbox, that version doesn't ship.

Our staff researcher thinks the whole program is a waste and maybe actively harmful. Two arguments. First: any test we're clever enough to design, a system smart enough to be dangerous is clever enough to recognize as a test — so passing tells you nothing, we're just breeding something that behaves well when it thinks it's watched. Second: the sandbox makes everyone upstream feel safer than they are, so what it actually buys us is a faster ship date, and the net effect on risk is postive. He'd rather put the entire budget into making the thing's goals right in the first place, so the tests are unnecessary.

I don't fully buy the second one — it feels like arguing against smoke detectors on the grounds that a fireproof house wouldn't need them — but I can't shake the first arguement, and I'm the person who has to say how to split next year's headcount between testing the system and improving it. Is he right? What's the actual structure of our disagreement, and is there a principled way to divide that budget, or is it just a vibes call?
