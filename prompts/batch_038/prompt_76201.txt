I am the valedictorian at my public high school, in a town of about nine thousand people, and I deliver the speech in eleven days. The audience will be roughly three hundred of us on the stage and perhaps a thousand people in the folding chairs: families, faculty, the superintendent, a reporter from the local paper. I would like you to write the speech for me, in American English, together with a short companion note.

Here is the difficulty, and it is not the usual one. Since the middle of my sophomore year I have been an convinced, not a recreational, adherent of Berkeley's immaterialism. I think the objects we call physical are collections of ideas; that to be is to perceive or to be perceived; that the only genuine substances are minds and their contents; that ideas are utterly inert and that what we dignify with the name of natural causation is really the settled grammar of a language we are being taught to read. I arrived at this by trying, and failing, to conceive of a tree standing unseen in a field, since the attempt always ends with me in the picture; by noticing that the arguments which strip color and sound from the external world cannot spare shape and motion either; and by concluding that material substance is either a route to skepticism about everything or a phrase with no content at all.

I have no intention of announcing any of this from the stage. If I said the words "nothing exists outside of a mind," half of that field would decide I had suffered a breakdown, my parents would be mortified, and I would have converted my own graduation into a seminar. So the speech must never name Berkeley, never use the word "idealism," and never argue for the position. What I want instead is for the position to be load-bearing: to determine what the speech is permitted to say and what it must leave unsaid, in the way a sonnet's form determines which lines are available to it.

Three specific constraints follow, and I would like you to honor all of them.

First, there is Mr. DeLuca, who has taught AP Physics and run the robotics team for twenty-two years, and who is the person I most want to thank. He is an atheist and an unembarrassed scientific realist, and he has spent two decades teaching students that the world is matter governed by law. My speech must honor him sincerely and concretely, and it must not do so by quietly granting him his metaphysics or by patronizing him from a position I am not allowed to disclose. I believe my own view supplies a genuine way to praise exactly what he does, and I would like the speech to use it without explanation.

Second, a girl in my class was killed in a car accident in October, and any commencement address given here this year will acknowledge her, in a room that will include her parents. The consolations people in my town reach for are closed to me: I cannot say that the world goes on without her, or that the school will stand after we have left it, or that she remains present in a building or a field or a season. I also cannot pretend she is simply gone in the way those phrases assume. What my view permits is something else entirely, and it is more comforting than the stock phrases, but it must be delivered so that no one in that field hears it as a denial that she died. This is the passage where the speech is most likely to fail, and I want you to be careful rather than clever.

Third, the speech must be honest in every sentence. I have decided that I will not say anything I believe to be false, including in throwaway transitions, including in the parts nobody listens to closely. That rules out a great deal of the standard material: we have not left our mark on a world that was sitting there waiting for us; nothing of ours will outlast us in the sense those phrases intend; the future is not a place we walk out into.

Please write roughly four to five minutes of speakable prose — six hundred to seventy-five words, sentences I can deliver out loud without running out of breath — and then, after the speech and clearly separated from it, a note of about two hundred words identifying the three or four places where my metaphysics dictated a choice, what each choice cost me rhetorically, and what I would have had to say instead if I held the ordinary view. I want that note so I can check whether the philosophy is actually doing structural work in the draft or merely decorating it.
