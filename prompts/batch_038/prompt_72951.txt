Thirty-four years at a research station doing grassland ecology, mostly on the receiving end of other people's sampling designs, and now three years retired and, of all things, running the volunteer desk at our county biological records centre two days a week. What I actually do is digitise and re-label: old herbarium sheets going back to the 1890s, plus the modern sighting records that come in from walkers, a couple of school groups, and one very persistent man with a camera. Every record gets a status label from a fixed little vocabulary — native, introduced, casual, naturalised, plus an "uncertain" bucket — and those labels end up in the county atlas, which planning officers pull records out of when they're weighing up a development application. So it isn't harmless clerical work. If a rare native gets stamped "casual" by someone in a hurry, a site can quietly lose the protection it should have had. I have eleven volunteers, most of them retired like me, one weekend of training between them, roughly ninety seconds per record, and I sign off after spot-checking a random ten percent. We're doing about two hundred records a week and it goes up in the spring.

What's eating me is that the same record, given to two different volunteers, comes back with two different labels, and I've stopped being able to say which one is wrong — because the difference tracks what else was in the pile that morning. Concretely: one flowering plant of a garden escapee on a roadside verge, right beside a house, no fruit, no previous record for that vice-county. Taken on its own the guidelines say casual, obviously. But the volunteer who got it had forty other records of the same species from the same parish over three years in the same box, and she labelled the lot naturalised, and I'm not at all sure she was wrong; a population spread over that many years and that many sites doesn't look casual to anybody. Meanwhile the volunteer who works strictly one card at a time, which is what the handbook tells everyone to do, labels each of those forty-one cards casual, and individually he's following the rules to the letter. The handbook we were handed assumes each record exists on its own and has one true label sitting on it waiting to be read off. I don't believe that any more, and I don't know how to write a handbook that doesn't. Part of why I'm asking you and not the county ecologist is that I've been going to a philosophy reading group at the library — my wife finds the whole thing hilarious — and through it I fell into that old Hofstadter and Melanie Mitchell material on a program called Copycat, the abc-becomes-abd, now do the same to xyz puzzle. What grabbed me wasn't the puzzle so much as the surrounding claims: that the trouble with xyz is that the rule you wanted to apply is blocked, so one of the ideas inside it has to bend and slide over to a neighbouring one; that which idea bends isn't fixed by a definition but by the whole configuration you're looking at; that there's a network of related concepts — a slipnet, I think the word is — and that the interesting cases are ones where a concept gets stretched rather than where it gets violated. And that these models are built deliberately for a tiny little "microdomain" where you can see everything. A county plant-status vocabulary with five words in it is exactly one of those microdomains.

So here's what I want, and please make it something I could photocopy and hand to a volunteer who has never heard of any of this: a decision procedure for labelling one record, with the questions in the order they should be asked, what counts as a sufficient answer to each one, where the procedure is allowed to stop at "uncertain", and where it stops entirely and hands the record to me because what's left isn't a factual question. It has to cope with the cards that simply don't contain the information the rule wants — no fruit, no date, a ten-kilometre grid square instead of a precise location, a 1920s collector's handwriting. It needs an explicit rule for when a label is permitted to slip to a neighbouring category under pressure and when that slippage is just an error, which I suspect is the hardest thing in here and the thing the current handbook pretends doesn't exist. And it needs to take a position on the batch problem: whether a judgement made about forty-one records together is legitimate evidence about each of them, or whether record-level and batch-level labelling have to be kept in separate passes that can't contaminate each other — if you say two passes, tell me what the second pass is allowed to change and what it isn't. Then tell me plainly whether two of my volunteers disagreeing on a genuine borderline case is a training failure I should be correcting or a property of the concepts themselves that I should be designing around, because if it's the second one I need to know how to write that into a guideline without the guideline collapsing into "label it however you feel". Run the procedure on three cases: the verge escapee with its forty sisters in the box; a 1950s sheet where the old recorder wrote a category we no longer use, with what was clearly a broader concept than ours, and where I have to decide whether to import his judgement or re-run our rule over his material; and one where it overrules me, because there's a population on a disused airfield I've been insisting for six years is naturalised and I'd genuinely rather the procedure tell me I'm the one doing the slipping than flatter me. Finish with what the procedure cannot do, and which of its steps are judgement calls wearing a procedural costume. One last thing, and it matters to me: please separate out what's actually settled among people who work on concepts and categorisation from what's still live and argued over, and if the fluidity-of-categories picture I've been leaning on is a contested position rather than an agreed result, say so and tell me which parts of your procedure rest on it. I spent a career distinguishing instrument error from real variation and I'd like the same courtesy here.
