I am a third-year undergraduate in a joint statistics-and-public-policy program, and this term I am assisting with the weekly workshop section of a second-year course on measurement and evidence for policy. The students have completed one term of probability: they are comfortable with conditional probability, Bayes' rule, and reading a contingency table, and most have seen a receiver operating characteristic curve drawn once, but none has taken decision theory or machine learning. In four weeks I have fifty minutes alone with about twenty of them, a whiteboard, and no slides, and my assigned topic is the incompatibility between the group-wise statistical fairness criteria — specifically, between requiring that a risk score mean the same thing within each group and requiring that the decisions cut from that score produce the same false positive and false negative rates in each group, in the ordinary case where the groups' underlying base rates differ. I can produce the incompatibility myself on a sheet of paper with a small numerical example, but I have twice tried to walk students through it during office hours and both times lost them: they follow every line and then conclude that the theorem must be a quirk of how the criteria were defined. What I want from you is not an explanation of the result but a plan for how I should explain it.

Please lay out the order in which the pieces have to be introduced, and say explicitly what each piece depends on. I suspect the students cannot hear calibration as anything other than a slogan until they already hold the idea of a score as an estimate of a probability, and cannot hear equal error rates until they see that a threshold is what converts a score into a decision, but I want that dependency structure argued rather than merely asserted, including which steps can be compressed and which cannot be skipped without the conclusion sounding arbitrary. Then give me the single running example I should build the whole session on, and tell me what that example must contain and what it must lack for the tension to arise honestly rather than by sleight of hand. My own candidate is the university's early-alert system, which ranks first-year students by estimated probability of not returning in the autumn and sends the top slice to mandatory advising appointments, where the two groups are students who are the first in their families to attend university and everyone else; the advising capacity is genuinely limited, the base rates do differ, and nobody in the room will be tempted to say the group label is standing in for something the university should not be measuring at all. Tell me whether that example will bear the weight or whether it quietly begs a question — in particular, whether the fact that the difference in base rates is partly a product of the institution's own past admissions and funding practices will tip the session into a debate about rectification that I have no time to hold — and if it is unsuitable, propose and justify a replacement at the same level of concreteness.

Finally, map the misconceptions I should expect and tell me the precise point in the sequence at which to head each one off, rather than collecting them at the end as a set of caveats. The ones I anticipate: that bias in a model means the model was handed the group label, so deleting the column settles everything; that the incompatibility is an artefact of crude definitions and would evaporate for a better-specified criterion; that because no criterion can be satisfied at once, the choice among them is merely political and the numbers cease to matter; and — the one that worries me most — that since the criteria conflict, the right response is to abandon the score and adjudicate case by case, which they will take to be self-evidently fairer. Say plainly which of these I should refuse to engage with at this level and why, what I ought to declare out of scope in my opening two minutes (I assume the proof itself, the individual-fairness literature, and the question of what to do about historically produced base-rate differences), and give me one closing question I can pose in the final five minutes whose answers would tell me whether the students have grasped the structure of the trade-off rather than merely the fact that some trade-off exists.
