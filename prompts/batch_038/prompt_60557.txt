I am assembling the sessions for an upper-division undergraduate seminar — 26 students, one 80-minute meeting, a room with movable tables but no computers for the students — that sits within a unit on the limits of formal reasoning in practical deliberation. Roughly half the class has had a term of introductory game theory including equilibrium concepts; the other half has had none. I want an in-class activity built around the centipede game, but not the demonstration I usually see.

The standard demonstration shows students that backward induction requires the first player to take immediately, then shows them that in laboratory play almost nobody does, and the students find this mildly amusing and go home thinking that game theory is unrealistic about human nature. That is not my target. What I want them to feel is narrower and harder, and I want the activity engineered so that they run into it rather than being told about it: the moment at which the argument, if they accept it, gives them no usable guidance about what to do — because it has already assured them that the situation they are actually in cannot arise. I want them to experience the reasoning as withdrawing its own support at precisely the point where they need it, not as a curiosity about other people's irrationality. They should leave unsettled about what they themselves are licensed to believe and to plan on, having discovered this in their own handwriting.

Please design the following, as a single runnable package.

1. A pairing and rotation scheme that works for 26 people and gets every student through at least two games, the second structurally different from the first in a way that is not announced beforehand.

2. A payoff table for a short centipede — I prefer six or seven nodes to Rosenthal's hundred — denominated in something other than money. The department permits a small, capped number of extra-credit points, but I will not have students graded against one another, so the currency must convert in a way that is not zero-sum between the two members of a pair. Give me the actual numbers and defend the ratio between the two players' payoffs at each node, since that ratio does much of the motivational work.

3. An elicitation of predictions in advance of play, recorded so that students cannot quietly revise them afterwards, and worded so that what they commit to is a complete plan for every node rather than merely a first move. The gap between the written plan and the observed play should be theirs to find.

4. The core mechanic. A round in which the opening moves are deleted by the rules, so that a student is seated at a late node with no prior error by anyone — no tremor, no misreading of the sheet, no confusion about the stakes — and is asked to choose. I want the activity arranged so that the most confident backward inductor in the room is the one who discovers that she cannot say what she is entitled to believe about her partner at that node, and so that this registers as a loss of footing rather than as a puzzle with a clever answer waiting behind it. Tell me what to read aloud, what to withhold, when in the session to spring it, and what a student will most likely say in the first ten seconds after it.

5. A minute-by-minute timeline including the debrief, and an ordered sequence of debrief questions that maps observed behaviour in the room onto specific load-bearing elements of the argument: the induction step, the assumption each player makes about what the other will do, the claim that the reasoning survives being known by both, and the counterfactual reasoning that is supposed to govern a node the argument has declared unreachable. The naming of the paradox should come last, and nothing the students see or hear before that point should contain the name.

6. A candid account of what will predictably go wrong on the first run: where pairs will collude or play for one another's benefit, where the extra-credit currency will fail to motivate, where a pair will finish in ninety seconds and then sit idle, and where a student with prior training will announce the equilibrium aloud and spoil the discovery for everyone else. For each, a mitigation I can implement with no materials beyond paper, pens, and a timer.

7. An explicit statement of which feature of the paradox the activity cannot reproduce and which I must therefore supply by argument in the closing minutes. My own view is that no classroom can generate genuine common knowledge of rationality among people who also know each other socially, and that what my students will in fact be doing is reasoning about the dispositions of acquaintances rather than about rationality as such. Tell me whether that is the deepest limitation or whether I have overlooked a more serious one.

Please give me the activity and its materials, not a lecture script and not a survey of the literature surrounding it.
