I'm a third-year PhD student in moral epistemology and I've been asked to give a talk in a literature-and-philosophy reading group where we're doing Kazuo Ishiguro's The Remains of the Day. I want to read it through the moral testimony debate, and specifically through the pessimist intuition that there's something wrong with forming your moral beliefs on someone else's say-so even when that someone turns out to be right. Stevens is the perfect case and also, I suspect, a case the debate doesn't actually have the vocabulary for, and I want to know which.

The standard reading is that Stevens defers downstream: Darlington hands down a verdict, Stevens accepts it, and the novel's own closing pages seem to endorse the pessimist diagnosis, since Stevens admits he can't even claim his mistakes as his own because he let someone else make them for him. But I've got a distinction I want you to pressure-test for me. Downstream deference is taking an authority's verdict on what to do; what I'll call upstream deference is letting an authority determine what counts as a moral question in the first place. I think the novel is really about the second kind: it isn't that Darlington's judgment overrides Stevens' perception, it's that Stevens' professional framework has already recategorized the raw material before any perception gets a look in — his father's death, the dismissal of the two Jewish maids, his own feelings for Miss Kenton all get filed as staffing matters. Does that distinction earn its keep, or is it just the pessimist intuition wearing a hat? And run it against a counterfactual test I keep circling: a deference looks safe only if it would have tracked the truth had the authority been right. Stevens' clearly wouldn't have. But neither, arguably, would the deference the optimists happily bless — your doctor, your mechanic. Tell me whether that undercuts the pessimist's use of the novel, or shows the case was never about knowledge.

Work through particular scenes rather than summarizing: the maids, where Miss Kenton objects and Stevens tells her it's simply not their business, which is awkward for my upstream story because he does seem to see something; the evening in the village where the locals tell him dignity belongs to ordinary people who think for themselves and he can't manage a reply; the bus shelter meeting with Mrs. Benn, where she says the road not taken might have been better and in the same breath insists her actual life is good; and the pier at the end, with the resolution to practice bantering for the new American employer. Is that last bit repentance, relapse, or something the pessimist/optimist frame just can't name? I also want you to say whether the fact that we get all of this from Stevens' own mouth — a narrator visibly failing to report his own life accurately — makes us pessimists about his testimony in a way that's a real point and not a cute one. Be honest if it's cute.

Ground rules: don't quote the novel verbatim, neither of us has the text in front of us and a garbled line will annoy me; paraphrase the scenes and I'll know which ones you mean. No bibliography and no lit review — Hills on understanding not being transmissible, Driver's line on the virtues of ignorance, and the authenticity-based replies are fine to invoke by name where a position needs an owner, but that's the ceiling. Take a side: does the novel vindicate moral testimony pessimism, resist it, or expose a question the debate misdescribes? Commit to that and give me your reasons, don't lay out the options and hand me the choice — I've got forty minutes and I need a thesis. Write the whole thing as continuous prose, the way you'd write an email or a letter, no headings, bullets, bold, or tables. And close with a short paragraph flagging where your reading leans on a contested interpretation of the book or stretches the philosophy to fit it; I'd rather know that before the Q&A than be caught out in it.
