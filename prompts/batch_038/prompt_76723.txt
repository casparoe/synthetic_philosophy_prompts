My school's philosophy club meets Thursdays in the back of the library, and last week we got into the usual fight about whether the far future should swamp everything else. Half the club is dead serious about the "there could be a gigantic number of future people, so whatever we do now is a rounding error" line, and I tried to say that the whole simulation thing deflates it. Then I went home and actually wrote my reasoning out, and what came out looks like it proves way more than I wanted, and now I can't tell whether I have a cool argument or a broken one. Here it is:

(1) How much an action matters depends on how much it changes the amount of good in the future. That's the far-future people's own principle, so I'm not smuggling anything in.
(2) If we're in a simulation, everything inside it, including our whole future history, runs on equipment controlled by beings outside.
(3) Those beings can pause it, edit it, re-run it from an earlier point, or shut it down, and whether they do any of that depends on facts in their world, not ours.
(4) So if we're in a simulation, how long our future is and what's in it is settled by outside facts, whatever we decide.
(5) So if we're in a simulation, our choices can't change how many future people there are or how good their lives go.
(6) So if we're in a simulation, (1) implies our choices don't matter for the far future, and the huge-number argument for working on it loses its force.
(7) We can't rule out that we're in one, and the standard trilemma-style argument suggests that if civilizations mostly survive and if survivors would run ancestor simulations, then simulated people hugely outnumber real ones, so we're probably simulated.
(8) So probably, obsessing over the far future is pointless.

The problem is that (4) through (6) don't seem to care about when the effects show up. If the simulators' decisions fix the far future, they fix next Tuesday too — my choice to study or not, whether I eat the second slice of pizza, all of it. And the same move works on the simulators: they're probably running inside somebody else's simulation, and so on up, forever. So my argument seems to show that nobody anywhere has ever made a choice that changed anything, which is exactly the kind of conclusion that makes you suspect you proved too much. My friend Maya says that's fatal, but when I asked her why it's fatal for my argument *specifically* — since I'm only making a claim about the far future, not about pizza — she just said "you know it's fatal" and changed the subject, which isn't help.

So: which step do I reject? I'd genuinely rather keep (1), since it's the other side's principle and beating them with it is the whole fun, and (2) and (3) feel like they're just spelling out what "in a simulation" means, so I don't want to lose those either. Tell me which step is bad, why it looked completely fine to me when I wrote it down, and what the person who rejects it has to pay — and if there's more than one candidate step to blame, rank them for me. Then the bigger question: once the bad step is fixed, does the simulation hypothesis actually leave far-future fanaticism alone, or does it still dent it? I keep having the opposite thought too, that if simulated futures are cheap to run then there are way *more* future people than the longtermists think, which would make (1) push harder instead of softer, and I can't tell if that's a real consideration or just me flailing. Also, is there a version of the worry that survives the repair — like if the simulators can re-run a simulation where we got the right answer, does that make getting it right worth less, or more likely?
