Our forecasting committee is deciding between two mechanisms for extracting internal estimates — an iterated anonymous survey in which each round's average is fed back to the panel, and a small internal prediction market in which employees buy and sell contracts, out of a real budget line, on things like whether a product ships by a given date or whether demand lands in a specified band. Two colleagues, Marta and Dev, argued about this for the better part of an hour after the meeting, with the rest of us listening. I could not tell who was winning, and I have no horse in the race, so I want an outside adjudication.

1. Marta opened by saying the market's advantage is epistemic: when people have money on the line they are more careful and less self-serving, so the price aggregates dispersed private information — a warehouse lead's sense that a supplier is slipping, a sales manager's sense that a region has already peaked — that an unpenalized survey simply leaves on the table. Cheap talk, she said, is not a belief report, because nothing goes wrong for the speaker if it is false.

2. Dev replied that the theory she is borrowing says the opposite. Under the no-trade result, if agents share a common prior, reason correctly, and start from an allocation that is already optimal with respect to that prior, there is no speculative trade they can all agree to: private information alone cannot generate a mutually acceptable exchange. So a market only moves when at least one party fails an assumption — is not reasoning correctly, does not in fact share the prior, or wants the contract for some non-informational reason, such as hedging a bonus or cultivating a reputation for boldness. An internal market is the closest thing to that theorem's ideal setting, since everyone starts from the same company data, which is exactly why he thinks it bites hardest here: the price would be tracking those failures rather than information.

3. Marta said that is an over-reading. The theorem excludes trade whose only source is differential information among common-prior agents; it does not exclude trade driven by differing risk attitudes, differing exposure to the outcome, or a need to shift inventory. The launch team genuinely wants protection against a schedule slip, and that motive produces volume without any claim about the date. In the standard models, informed traders profit precisely because they trade alongside uninformed ones, and the price then discloses their information partially. The theorem describes a limiting case that never obtains.

4. Dev said she had conceded what he needed. If some fraction of trades are hedging or noise, then we, reading the price from outside, cannot decompose a price move into its informational and non-informational parts; and the impossibility argument about informationally efficient markets says a price that fully revealed what the informed traders knew would leave them nothing to gain by becoming informed, so the price must stay partly opaque. Markets are informative only to the degree that they are not informative. Her slogan is therefore not licensed by the literature: at best it licenses the claim that prices carry some unknown quantity of information mixed with an unknown quantity of everything else.

5. Marta said he was now refuting a position she had never held; she needs only that the market beats the alternative, and the same literature cuts against the survey. With a common prior, agents who truthfully announce their posteriors cannot go on disagreeing — iterated announcement drives them together — so a Delphi round should deliver agreement without any trade at all, which means Dev cannot claim the survey dominates on grounds of pooling. But that result assumes the announcements are truthful, and the survey supplies no reason for them to be; a market announcement costs the announcer something, and it is that cost, not the trading as such, that gives the resulting number its weight. Convergence in the survey may be convergence on whoever spoke last, or on the house narrative.

6. Dev said that if the dispute had come down to which mechanism is more reliable in practice, then it is empirical, and proposed running both for a year and comparing calibration and resolution on the same questions.

7. Marta's closing objection was that the comparison would corrupt what it measures: an internal contract on an event the traders can affect gives the holder of the paying side an incentive to bring the event about, so the resulting price would reflect, in part, willingness to influence outcomes rather than belief about them.

Please work through the exchange move by move. For each of the seven, assign a rating — clearly mistaken, partly right but overstated, roughly right, clearly right — and justify it briefly. Where a move states or applies a formal result, say whether it states it correctly, and if not, whether the error changes the force of the argument or is incidental to it. Then say who is more right overall about the committee's actual choice, and identify at least two considerations that bear on that choice and that neither of them raised, explaining why each of them matters here.
