so I've been asked to write up where I stand for a little social ontology workshop I'm in — mixed group, two philosophers, a demographer, and one extremely skeptical historian who thinks my whole topic is astrology — and I want to sanity-check the package before I circulate it. The question is whether generations (Boomers, Gen X, Millennials, Gen Z) are real kinds or just journalistic bookkeeping. Here's roughly what I want to sign up for:

(1) I'm a realist, sort of. I think there are generations in something like the sense that there are alumni of a particular school year — the cohort isn't a mere label, it picks out a genuine unit of analysis, and claims about it are true or false independently of what anyone stipulates.
(2) But I don't think it's a similarity kind. What makes you a member isn't that you resemble the other members, it's that you were exposed to the same stretch of history at the same point in your biography. The line I keep using on people: a generation is more like "everyone who was living in Lisbon during the revolutionary years" than like "all redheads." Real, but real in a kind of indexical, boring way.
(3) Boundaries are objective-ish. There's a fact of the matter, discoverable rather than decreed, about where one generation stops and the next begins; that the labels we actually use got frozen by market researchers (Pew's 1996 cutoff or whatever it was) is an accident, not what makes the boundary what it is.
(4) I hold the impressionable-years view: political and cultural dispositions largely crystallize somewhere between the mid-teens and mid-twenties and then get carried forward. So the principled way to draw a boundary is to ask when a historically significant stretch of events landed relative to somebody's impressonable window.
(5) I take the age-period-cohort identification problem seriously — arguably too seriously, my demographer friend says. Age, period and cohort are linearly dependent, so whatever "cohort effect" you extract depends on a constraint you imposed from outside the data. When people do the hard work, genuine cohort differences are usually small, and a lot of what gets called generational is life-cycle or period effects wearing a costume.
(6) Which is why I also think it's just bad epistemic practice to predict an individual's attitudes from their generation, and I want to say that firmly, not as a caveat.
(7) But I want to resist the reduction: "the Boomers did X" isn't always shorthand for a giant conjunction of facts about individual boomers — the cohort as such shows up in explanations in a way the conjunction doesn't.
(8) smaller one: I want to keep the old sociological idea (Mannheim, I think?) that a generation only really becomes a generation when the cohort collectively does something with its shared situation — potential versus actualized generations — while also being a methodological individualist who doesn't believe in collective agents over and above individuals.

Two case verdicts I'm attached to. Someone born in late '96 who emigrated at seven and grew up somewhere that missed most of the relevant events: I want to say they're a Millennial by birthdate and a Zed by formation, and I do *not* want to say either of those is a mistake or that one is merely "technical." And for diaspora kids generally I think the honest answer is that they belong to a different generation than their birth year suggests, which I realize sounds weird out loud.

Here's where I'm stuck: I can't tell which of these are actually pulling against each other and which only look like they are. My gut says (2) and (7) might be fighting, and seperately that (3) plus (4) can't survive contact with (5) — but I suspect I'm wrong about at least one of those hunches and that there's a tension I'm completely blind to. Can you go through it as an audit rather than a lecture: which pairs genuinely conflict, ranked by severity rather than dumped on me all equally bad, and for each, what the cheapest revision is and what that revision costs me? For the ones that only look like conflicts, say explicitly why they dissolve, because if I can write "this one's fine, here's the reason" it saves me a paragraph of defensive hedging. And separately — do those two case verdicts follow from (2) through (4), or do they need an extra principle I haven't listed? Be blunt, I'd much rather find the problem here than in the room with the demographer.
