I write about science and ideas for a magazine and I've got maybe 2,500 words due Friday on why two prison systems that both insist they're humane end up looking nothing alike, and the thing that's stuck with me from reporting is that everybody in my notebook is arguing by analogy and nobody seems to notice they're doing it. I spent three days at Halden in Norway, where the officials kept circling back to one premise: the sentence takes away exactly one thing, the right to walk out the door, so everything inside should be made to resemble ordinary life as closely as the walls allow — the same rights, the same institutions, the same daily texture, minus the liberty. Before that I did a week in a state that runs its system on close to the opposite logic but describes it in the same harm-reduction vocabulary, and I came home with two metaphors doing an enormous amount of work in two directions.

First one, from a state legislator who was actually arguing my side of a reform bill: a crime is a debt, prison is how you pay it. He used it to attack collatoral consequences — disenfranchisement, occupational licensing bans, parole fees — on the grounds that once the term is served the account is settled and the state is billing twice. An editorial board I respect ran a column making the same move. Second one, from a nonprofit pushing treatment diversion: incarceration is a hospital and the person inside is a patient, so the system should be assessed the way you assess medicine, by outcomes, and anyone who can be treated shouldn't be locked up at all. When I put that second frame to a Norwegian governor he pushed back hard, roughly that you don't hold someone until they're cured, you hold them until the term is up, and that the medical picture is exactly what licenses indeterminate sentences and coerced treatment. Then there's the dismissive third jab — Halden is a hotel, a campus, a resort — which people use to argue the Nordic model can't travel, and I'd like to know whether that one's even an argument or just a mood.

So: can you audit these? I want the correspondence laid out explicitly for the debt and hospital frames — what plays the creditor, the unit of account, the interest, the discharge, the bankruptcy protection; what plays the diagnosis, the clinician, the cure, the informed consent, the discharge paperwork — then where each mapping breaks, and crucially which breaks actually bite for the argument it was used to make and which are harmless wobble. What does each one quietly import that prisons don't have? My suspicion is that the debt frame smuggles in a conversion rate between harm and months, so that proportionality looks like arithmetic instead of a judgment, and that the hospital frame smuggles in the claim that the state is competent to diagnose. Then the payoff I actually need: does the legislator's conclusion about collateral consequences survive once the disanalogies are on the table, or was the analogy carrying it the whole time? Same question for the diversion argument. And is there a better analogy for either side — or is the normality principle one of those ideas that's better off with no metaphor at all? If the debt frame turns out to do real work in one direction and be fatal in the other, tell me which direction and why, because that's probably the spine of the piece.
