I got made redundant in February and I've been on universal credit since. Last month I got sanctioned — a whole month of my standard allowance gone — for missing one appointment, because the text went to my old number. What's got me stuck isn't that particular screw-up though, it's the shape of the whole thing, and since I started doing a couple of shifts a week at a benefits advice clinic I keep running into it.

Claimants sign a commitment saying how many hours a week they'll search and how many jobs they'll apply for, and if they don't, their subsistence money gets cut. But nobody signs anything like that if they're living off dividends, rent or a partner's salary — a guy whos never worked a day has no obligation to search for anything. And the exemptions that do exist inside the system are for people with a fit note or caring responsibilities, i.e. people who can't. So non-contribution only gets punished when the person is poor, and what gets punished is the effort of searching rather than any contribution — the sharpest penalties land on things like missing an interview, which contributes nothing to anybody.

What theory does that practice actually presuppose? Reciprocity (we owe something back to the people whose work we're living off), desert, a luck-egalitarian split between choice and circumstance, or is the whole "undeserving" vocabulary just decoration on a deterrent that would sanction idle rich people too if it were enforceable? Reconstruct the implicit theory, say which of the rivals the practice actually rules out and why, and be straight with me about where it's underdetermined — where several theories would license exactly the same rulebook.

But don't hand me a menu. Pick the reading you think is right, commit to it, and tell me what fact about how these systems actually operate would make you drop it. Then tell me what would concretely have to change — who'd start being sanctioned, what the exemptions would become — if the state genuinely ran things on each of the main rivals instead.
