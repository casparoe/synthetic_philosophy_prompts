I am an epistemologist, and next month I am to deliver a commentary on a colleague's manuscript concerning the norms of assertion. She describes her own position as a refined version of the knowledge norm. My difficulty is that I am no longer confident this is what her considered verdicts actually commit her to, and I should not care to suggest in print that she has abandoned a view she still believes she holds unless I am sure of it. I should therefore value a reconstruction that is independent of my own hunch.

Below are the cases on which she has stated her verdicts explicitly, whether in print or in discussion I chaired. In each I have tried to fix the features that seem to me to matter: whether what is asserted is true, whether the speaker believes it, the character and strength of her evidence, whether she possesses any reason to doubt it, whether further evidence was within easy reach and what it would have shown, and the practical setting of the assertion.

1. *The fabricated notice.* A laboratory head tells her research group that their renewal has come through, on the strength of an email indistinguishable from the funder's usual correspondence. It was composed by a graduate student as a prank; but the panel had approved the renewal that same morning, so what she says is true. Before speaking she had twice been reminded by the department's grants administrator that award notices issue only through the grants office, and she had noticed that the message came from an address she did not recognise. A telephone call, which she did not make and which would have confirmed the news, was two minutes away. — Verdict: improper.

2. *The safety finding.* An epidemiologist tells a regulatory committee that a compound is cardiac-safe, having conducted a systematic review of everything then available and exhausted the avenues of inquiry open to her. The compound is not safe: the harm runs through a mechanism nobody at the time had reason to suspect and which became detectable only a decade later. — Verdict: proper; and she insists, in a published exchange, that the assertion satisfied the norm and was not merely excused or blameless.

3. *The unwilling teacher.* A secondary-school biology teacher, a convinced creationist who does not believe a word of the modern synthesis, nevertheless teaches it, asserting its claims to her pupils and defending them under questioning, on the ground that her office is to convey what the evidence supports. She takes the evidence for those claims to be overwhelming and has no grounds for thinking it misleading; what she lacks is belief. — Verdict: proper.

4. *The open forum.* A climate scientist asserts, at a public meeting where the audience has been encouraged to weigh all contributions equally and where a later speaker asserts the opposite to equal applause, a true and impeccably well-evidenced claim about regional rainfall. She has checked everything that could be checked, and nothing further was within her reach. My colleague's remark, which I record because it seems to me to do work, was that the audience's inability to discriminate this testimony from the crank's is no defect in the assertion. — Verdict: proper.

5. *The lottery ticket.* A man buys a single ticket in a large and fair lottery. The draw has not yet taken place. He says to a friend that he will not win. — Verdict: improper. Her gloss was roughly that the astronomical odds are beside the point, the evidence being of the wrong sort to license a flat claim; a gloss which, I confess, I find more a label than a criterion.

6. *The surgical report.* An attending surgeon tells a resident, minutes before an operation, that the imaging places the mass on the left. She has read the radiologist's report and no more; the films are in the next room and she has not looked at them. The report is correct, nothing in her possession gives her reason to doubt it, and had she looked, the films would have confirmed it. I asked whether the gravity of the decision made any difference, and she said it did not. — Verdict: proper.

At the symposium I intend to put a seventh case to her, and I should like to know in advance what to expect.

7. *The seminar.* A historian asserts in a research seminar, on the basis of a memoir she has read closely and a secondary literature that repeats it without dissent, that a certain meeting took place on a particular date. What she says is true. She possesses no reason to doubt the memoir. The relevant archive is twenty minutes away, open to her, and she has never entered it; she did not judge a visit unnecessary, she simply never thought of it. Had she gone, she would have found a single ambiguous docket entry appearing to place the meeting a week earlier — an entry which a colleague who did go found, laboured over for a season without resolving, and which the profession has since come to regard as a copyist's error, though establishing that required work nobody had done at the time of her seminar.

What I should like from you is the following.

(i) State the general rules that are jointly consistent with all six verdicts — stated as rules I could apply to a seventh case, not as labels drawn from the literature — and say, for each, what it delivers on case 7.

(ii) Predict her verdict on case 7, with your degree of confidence and the reasoning behind it. If the surviving rules diverge there, say which you favour and why; if they converge, say so and identify instead a case on which they would come apart.

(iii) Tell me which of the six is the closest analogue to case 7 and exactly where the analogy breaks. I have in mind the surgical report, but I should like to be told if that is a mistake.

(iv) Show me how to separate a requirement that one's evidential position would withstand the inquiry one is in a position to make from the weaker requirement that one's failure to inquire be blameless. I suspect these come apart somewhere in the vicinity of case 7, and I should like the case that drives the wedge.

(v) Identify which verdicts are load-bearing: were she to withdraw one of the six, which candidate rules would be eliminated, and which would be revived?

(vi) Assess her self-description. What would knowledge have to come to mean for the safety finding and the unwilling teacher to be proper assertions, and would anything recognisable as the knowledge norm survive that redefinition? My suspicion is that she has migrated, without noticing, from a norm keyed to knowledge to one keyed to evidence and to inquiry, and I want to know whether that suspicion is fair or an artefact of my own impatience.

(vii) If you judge the six verdicts jointly inconsistent, say so and locate the inconsistency rather than straining to fit them. Note also whether the appeal to the right sort of evidence in case 5 can be made principled, or whether it is an expedient for blocking a threshold reading of her view.

For what it is worth, my own expectation is that she will say improper, on the ground that a historian asserting a precise date in a seminar ought to have been to the archive; but I recognise that this may be my own intuition about professional diligence rather than anything her verdicts entail, and I should like to be told plainly if it is.

I am not asking for references, a bibliography, or a survey of who defends what. Where my descriptions are silent on a feature you think turns the verdict — the speaker's intentions, the conventions of the setting, the cost of inquiry, what the audience already believed — flag the gap and tell me how the answer would depend on filling it one way rather than the other.
