Three weeks ago I was made chair of the patient-safety review committee at the hospital where I have worked as a clinical pharmacist for eleven years, and in that role I have to sign off on a rewritten version of our just-culture policy before it goes to the board in the spring. I would like to understand what the policy actually assumes about blame and responsibility, because I can no longer tell whether its parts hang together, and I am not willing to approve a document whose own logic I cannot state.

The practice, concretely. Any clinician who reports their own error within twenty-four hours is guaranteed freedom from disciplinary action, provided the report is complete and the act was neither deliberate nor performed while impaired; the report is stripped of identifiers and enters a database used solely for redesigning the system. The stated rationale is that we cannot fix what we never hear about, and that people only tell us the whole truth if telling does not cost them their licence. The same document carves out what it calls at-risk behavior — a shortcut taken knowingly, a protocol bypassed for no reason, a repetition of an error for which the person has already been coached — which remains fully disciplinable, and the committee decides case by case which side of the line an act falls on, using a question borrowed from the standard just-culture toolkits: would another reasonably competent clinician with the same training, on the same shift, under the same staffing, have made the same choice? Where the answer is yes we treat the person as a symptom of a broken system; where it is no we treat the person as the problem. Separately, we run a disclosure-and-early-offer program in which the clinician involved is expected to sit with the patient's family, describe what happened, and express remorse; the program's materials insist that its purpose is not to assign fault, yet families in our own surveys say over and over that what they wanted was to know that the person responsible felt bad about it. And two years ago, after a wrong-drug infusion killed a patient here, the state prosecutor charged the nurse involved; our policy had nothing to say about that possibility, and the committee's advice at the time was that she should decline to speak to anyone.

A colleague on the committee says the whole thing turns on whether resentment is an instrument we may deploy or withhold, or a response that fits or fails to fit what the person did. I do not follow the argument, and I suspect the document does not follow it either.

So: reconstruct the theory of moral responsibility this practice presupposes. Does the immunity-for-disclosure bargain treat blame as a tool to be withheld because withholding buys information, or does it rest on a judgment about the person's state of mind — that an honest error manifests no ill will and therefore leaves nothing to resent? The two answers predict different things about the at-risk carve-out; tell me which one the document is actually committed to. What is the substitution test testing: whether the agent could have done otherwise, what the agent cared about, or what a statistically normal agent would have done? Do those come apart, and does the committee's verdict change if they do? Is the policy coherent in declaring someone blameless while requiring them to express remorse to the family — is the remorse demanded of the clinician or produced for the family, and what does that assume about whether guilt can be apt where blame has been formally disclaimed? Does the practice tacitly assume that the institution controls the resentment in question, when the prosecutor sits outside it entirely? Finally, tell me what would have to change — the twenty-four-hour window, the carve-out, the substitution question, the apology session, each specifically — if we replaced the presupposed view with one on which resentment and indignation are not quantities to be rationed but constitutive of taking persons seriously, and, going the other way, with a purely causal view on which a person is just another failure point in the process. Please distinguish what the practice strictly requires from what it merely fits comfortably with, and tell me where it is genuinely underdetermined, so that rival theories would license exactly the same document.
