So — my hometown is closing its high school at the end of this year. District consolidation, us and three other towns folding into one building forty minutes down the interstate. Because I'm the one who left and then wrote three novels that are, lets be honest, mostly about them, they've asked me to give the address at the last commencement. June 6th. Forty-one graduates, essentially the whole town, the state superintendent, and my editor, who's flying in because the new book comes out that week and is partly set there.

Here's my problem. The position I spent four years working out in that book, and that I actually believe, is that there are two claims everybody in that gym is going to run together, and I need the speech to hold them apart. One: a life bound to a place can be a good life, not a consolation prize, genuinely good. Two: the arrangement that binds people to the place is just. Those come apart constantly. Most of the people who stayed did not exactly choose it — leaving costs money and credentials and a tolerance for being nobody that not everyone has. In my notes I've been calling it elected rootedness versus stranded rootedness, and the whole difficulty is that you often can't tell them apart from outside, or from inside either.

Which means the speech can't do the two things everyone in the room is expecting. It can't tell those kids to get out and see the world — that's the superintendent's speech, and it also quietly says the ones who stay will have failed. And it can't celebrate staying either, because I don't actually know that the people who are content there are content for reasons that would survive knowing what else is on offer, and I'm not going to pretend I do. That second one is the dangerous one. If anything I say comes out sounding like your satisfaction is just adaptation, I've insulted every person in that building including my mother, and worse, I've claimed to know their good better than they do — which is exactly the move I think the professional class has no business making. Underneath all of it is the question I don't want to answer out loud but can't avoid: whether one of these two ways of valuing a life is actually more defensible than the other, or whether that's only askable from somewhere you can't stand and still be believed.

So the bind is: honor a way of life without offering anyone's endorsement of it as evidence that it's good, and without offering the lack of endorsement as evidence that it isn't.

Some concrete things. Eight minutes. No poetry. No "these are unprecedented times." One joke only locals get, one everybody gets. The school board has already voted to sell the building to a company that makes prefab sheds, and everyone knows it — the speech has to let that fact be in the room without being bitter about it and without pretending it isn't happening. My brother stayed and runs the hardware store, and he told me on the phone he doesn't want to be anybody's cautionary tale or anybody's hero. That's the sentence I keep coming back to.

Can you write it? And then, separately, in a few sentences, tell me where you think the bind genuinely forced the wording and where you faked it — I'd rather know which parts are load-bearing.
