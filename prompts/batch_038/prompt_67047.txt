I sit on the board of a city Emergency Hardship Fund, which distributes a fixed annual pot to residents facing eviction, utility shutoff, or care they cannot afford. Our enabling ordinance contains two clauses that have turned out to pull against each other: aid is to be directed to residents "whose need arises from circumstances beyond their control," and no applicant may be refused assistance necessary to prevent severe and lasting harm. In practice, intake staff have honored the first clause by asking whether an applicant's own choices contributed to the need. That has meant denying aid to a woman who emptied her retirement savings three years ago to start a business that failed, and who now faces eviction with an untreated fracture, while approving an otherwise similar applicant whose employer simply closed without warning.

Half the board wants the responsibility screen abolished as both unworkable and cruel — it requires staff to adjudicate how voluntary a choice really was, which means judging people's upbringing, their addictions, the information they had, and the alternatives they saw. The other half argues that dropping it means the fund subsidizes imprudence and, because the pot is fixed, does so at the direct expense of applicants who did nothing wrong; they also note that a fund visibly indifferent to personal responsibility will struggle at levy renewal.

One member has floated a middle course. Rather than making the judgment retrospectively, the city would ask residents to accept a priority schedule prospectively — for instance, at the moment someone applies for a business license, signs a lease with the municipal housing authority, or draws down a permit, they would agree that if they later present with a need traceable to that undertaking, they enter a lower priority tier. The responsibility judgment would then be made by the applicant, when the risk is still abstract and the person is not yet in crisis, instead of by us afterward.

Draft the memo I will circulate before Tuesday's meeting. It should recommend a concrete rule — keep, revise, or abandon the screen — and if you think any version of the prospective-agreement scheme is worth adopting, specify how it would actually operate at intake and what it would and would not authorize staff to do. It should supply criteria that an intake worker can apply to a case like the one above without rendering a verdict on the applicant's life, and it should state the strongest version of the case against your recommendation, not a straw man. Two pages at most; the argument matters more than the pedigree of the ideas, so you needn't cite or name-drop anyone.
