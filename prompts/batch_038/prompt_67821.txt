For thirty-five years I worked in experimental physics; since retiring I have taken up philosophy of mind, and next month I am chairing an evening session at my local philosophical society. Our guest defends the position that Dennett's two-bitser — the little device whose very same internal state can be made to be about the coin, about the coffee in the pot, or about something else again, depending on which question it is put — has states that are genuinely about things, and that no intentionality is original: on her account the original/derived distinction does not survive scrutiny, and human beliefs sit on the same continuum as the two-bitser's.

Please draft seven or eight questions that go past a restatement of the view. I should like at least one on each of the following: how she escapes the charge that, if the same state can be made about whatever we please, aboutness has simply become relative to the questioner; what could render an ascription of belief to the device false rather than merely unprofitable; whether the device has concepts it has never been asked about; and how she treats a device that is jammed or mis-wired, whose state is about something it is no longer correlated with. For each, add the follow-up keyed to the answer she is most likely to give. Near the end I should also like a question about what observation, if any, could distinguish a device whose states are about something from one that merely repays prediction — and what she is committed to if her answer is that none could. Nothing of the "what first drew you to the subject" variety, please.
