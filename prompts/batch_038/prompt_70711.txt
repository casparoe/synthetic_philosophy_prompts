Two regulars on a decision-theory forum I've followed for years ended up in a protracted argument over whether the St. Petersburg paradox has ever genuinely been resolved. I've compressed it as faithfully as I can, keeping each side's own reasoning:

(1) A said the paradox was dead on arrival: an infinite expected payout is beside the point, since what matters is expected utility, and Bernoulli's logarithmic utility makes the game worth a modest finite sum — on the order of ten or twenty dollars even for a very wealthy player.

(2) B said that is a patch rather than a solution, invoking Menger's objection: for any *unbounded* utility function you can write down a variant game whose payoffs grow fast enough to make expected utility diverge again. So the only real escape is to insist utility is bounded above — a substantive claim about value (that past some payoff, more is worth literally nothing) adopted for the sole purpose of saving the theory.

(3) A replied that bounded utility isn't ad hoc but independently motivated by diminishing marginal returns, and that in any case the dispute is moot: no counterparty can pay 2^100 ducats, so every game anyone could actually offer has finite expected value.

(4) B said that answers a question about casinos rather than about decision theory, and then produced what he took to be the deeper problem: a variant whose payoffs alternate between gains and losses and grow fast enough that the expected-utility series converges only conditionally. Its value, he said, isn't fixed at all — reorder the terms and you get a different number, in principle any number you like. So a theory that locates a gamble's worth in its expectation delivers verdicts that depend on how one and the same gamble happens to be described.

(5) A said the ordering isn't up for grabs: the game's canonical description is by number of tosses, which fixes the series uniquely, and conditional convergence is a mathematician's artifact rather than a decision-theoretic crisis.

(6) B said that if that's the reply, the theory either offers no verdict at all on a perfectly well-posed, playable gamble, or its verdict is hostage to which description you write down first — and both outcomes are worse than the paradox they were meant to dissolve.

Neither budged, and I honestly can't tell who did better work. I'd like a move-by-move adjudication rather than a tour of the paradox.
