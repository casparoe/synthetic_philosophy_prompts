I cover science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and I'm reporting on the wave of histories that give non-human causes — plague bacteria, the Little Ice Age, the 1815 Tambora eruption — a leading role. Almost everyone I've interviewed reaches for the same image: these were actors in the drama, not scenery. One historian used it to argue that non-human causes deserve the same explanatory standing as statesmen, since an actor does things and the plot turns on what it does. A skeptic used the identical image against the whole enterprise: an actor plays a part assigned by a playwright, so calling a bacillus an actor either credits it with intentions it lacks or smuggles back the determinism the field claims to have rejected. My editor wants the metaphor kept because it's vivid, and the piece kept free of determinism.

Audit the theatrical metaphor for me. Give the correspondence table — actor, role, script, stage, plot, audience — then separate the disanalogies that actually threaten either use from the ones that are merely decorative, and say which of my two sources the metaphor can serve, if either. If it can serve neither, tell me what figure of speech would carry the weight better and what it would still leave unexpressed.
