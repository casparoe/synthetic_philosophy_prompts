I recently read an extended essay by an author who is broadly in the ecomodernist orbit and who has spent time around the effective-altruism forums, and it leaned hard on a single comparison that I now keep encountering elsewhere: aquaculture as the Blue Revolution, with the Green Revolution as its model. The argument runs roughly as follows. High-yield crop varieties raised output per hectare and thereby spared forests and grassland that would otherwise have been ploughed under; fish farming raises output per hectare of water and thereby spares wild stocks that would otherwise be fished to collapse. In both cases the technology is doing conservation work that regulation and consumer restraint demonstrably failed to do, and in both cases the objections of the squeamish were about the aesthetics of the production system — monocultures, crowded pens, the loss of a picturesque landscape — rather than about anything that actually matters morally, since neither wheat plants nor the ocean have interests of the sort that could be harmed. On that basis the essay concludes that welfare objections to aquaculture, whatever their intrinsic merit, should not be allowed to slow the sector down, and that the morally urgent business is to make it bigger and cheaper faster. A friend of mine, who works on invertebrate welfare, claims the analogy is not merely loose but inverted: in the terrestrial case the extra yield displaced wild ecosystems and paid for itself out of the biosphere, whereas in the aquatic case the extra yield is *subsidized by* the wild — the forage fish ground into feed, the juveniles scooped out of the ocean to fatten in ranching operations — so that the farm draws the ocean down rather than sparing it, and the escapees, sea lice and pathogens flowing out of the pens make it a net externality imposed on the thing it supposedly protects. My own impression, which may be badly out of date, is that the truth is messier than either of them: the share of aquaculture that depends on marine inputs has been falling for years, while a large fraction of farmed tonnage never gets fed at all because it is carp or bivalves or seaweed.

What I would like is an audit of the analogy rather than a verdict on aquaculture. Please lay out the correspondence explicitly — a table is welcome — saying what in the Blue Revolution plays the role of the dwarfing genes and the high-yield varieties, what plays the role of the spared hectare, what plays the role of the population that would otherwise have gone hungry, and what plays the role of the sentimental opponent. Then identify the features of the source case that the comparison quietly imports into the target: I suspect at least four are doing unearned work, including the assumption that the units of production have no morally relevant interests, the assumption that raising yield per unit area translates into reduced pressure on the wild rather than merely cheaper supply and therefore more demand, the assumption that the relevant denominator is area rather than number of individuals, and the assumption that the opposition in the historical case was in fact motivated by aesthetics. For each disanalogy you find, please say whether it matters for the conclusion the essay actually draws — that welfare concerns should not be permitted to slow the sector — or whether it is an objection to a weaker straw version of the claim. Some candidate places to look: the enormous asymmetry in individuals per kilogram between a farmed salmon and a farmed shrimp or a sardine-sized feed fish, which means the same tonnage figure conceals wildly different moral arithmetic; the fact that the wild population and the farmed population share one medium rather than being separated by a fence, so that substitution and contamination cannot be cleanly distinguished; the possibility that welfare improvements raise survival and growth and thereby make expansion more profitable than it would otherwise have been; and the difference between a technology adopted under famine conditions by governments with land-reform leverage and an industry adopted under market conditions by firms optimizing for unit cost.

Finally, tell me whether my friend's inversion is right, overstated, or right only for a subset of the sector — and if it is right only for a subset, say which subset, and whether the correct move is to abandon the revolution framing altogether in favor of something that tracks the relevant variable, which I take to be net effect on wild animals per unit of protein produced rather than yield per hectare. If you think a better analogy exists, offer one and say what it would license and what it would smuggle in on its own account, because I assume no comparison of this kind arrives without a payload. And if your honest assessment is that both analogies are being used mainly to end an argument rather than to structure one, say so and say what would have to be measured for the underlying dispute to become decidable at all.
