I am a junior, and for the past two years I have served on my school's Student Activities Board, which divides a single annual fund among the forty or so clubs that request money. Last month the board spent an entire meeting on a dispute between the debate team and the community garden club, both of which had applied for the last $1,800 remaining in the fund, and when the argument got stuck our faculty adviser cut it off by saying, "Let's just split the difference — half each, and then nobody can complain about fifty-fifty." Several of us objected that the situation was not symmetric at all: the debate team had already secured an outside sponsor covering most of its travel costs, so losing the entire $1,800 would barely have affected it, whereas the garden club's whole operating budget came from this fund and its planting season depended on it. The adviser repeated the slogan and the vote was taken. I have since been reading about bargaining theory on my own — I know there is a Nash bargaining solution and something called the Kalai–Smorodinsky solution, and that the two can give different answers in problems that look superficially like this one — and I have come to suspect that "split the difference" was doing a great deal of quiet work in that room, but I cannot yet say exactly what.

Could you lay out, as a menu rather than as a single preferred interpretation, the different precise claims that "split the difference" could be making when someone offers it as the resolution of a two-party division problem? I am interested both in readings that differ about what is being split — dollars, utilities, gains measured relative to each party's fallback position, equal fractions of each party's best attainable outcome, concessions made from some aspiration point each side started out demanding — and in readings that differ about what is being assumed regarding whether those quantities are comparable between the two parties at all. For each reading, please say whether it is a platitude no reasonable person could dispute, whether it is substantive but defensible, whether it is flatly false, or whether it only looks like a claim and is really a gesture in the direction of fairness; and wherever a reading depends on a technical notion from bargaining theory, explain that notion plainly enough that I could use it in a meeting without having read the original papers.

Then I would like you to bring the menu back to my adviser's argument. Which of the readings, if any, would actually support the conclusion he wanted — that the division was thereby settled, and that our objection concerning the debate team's outside sponsor was beside the point? Which readings would instead make our objection decisive? Please also address explicitly how the answer shifts depending on whether the fallback position is treated as zero for both clubs or as the value of each club's next-best alternative, since I suspect that is where the entire disagreement lives, and say whether any reading of "split the difference" survives the further worry that utilities may be merely ordinal and therefore not comparable across the two clubs.
