At a workshop session I chaired last Friday, two participants ended up in a sustained dispute that continued into the corridor and has now become the centrepiece of the follow-up session I have been asked to moderate. I would like an adjudication in advance, because I found myself unable to say at the time which of them was actually scoring. I report the exchange as neutrally as I can; I have no view I am trying to protect, and I could not tell you who had the better of it.

The paper under discussion argued that large-scale historical outcomes are far more contingent than structural explanation permits: a small, localised event can set off a chain whose terminus is a war, a revolution, or a fiscal collapse that would otherwise not have occurred. Its illustration was a stylised chain — a courier's horse goes lame, a letter arrives six hours late, a treaty is consequently not signed in time, and a war that the treaty would have forestalled breaks out.

The first disputant, whom I will call H, is a quantitative economic historian who works with long-run comparative series. The second, whom I will call P, is a philosopher of science who works on dynamical systems and on counterfactual reasoning. The exchange, as nearly as I can reconstruct it:

(1) H: The example establishes nothing. The lame horse is a trigger, not a cause. What made the war likely was the structural configuration — rival alliance systems, fiscal exhaustion, mobilisation timetables, an unsettled border. Change the spark and some other spark lights the same keg. Contingency at the level of sparks is entirely compatible with near-necessity at the level of the keg.

(2) P: That is a retreat into an unfalsifiable position. The claim that war would have come anyway is one no historian can ever check, since we are not vouchsafed the alternative. If no counterfactual evidence could count against the structural thesis, the thesis is not doing explanatory work; it is redescription delivered in a confident tone.

(3) H: On the contrary, it has precise content: across the range of nearby variations in local events, war occurs in most of them rather than in all of them. That is a claim about a distribution, and distributions are assessed over many comparable cases, not by interrogating one.

(4) P: Then you have conceded the paper's central point, because the question becomes what proportion of nearby variations terminate in war — and nothing you have said fixes what counts as nearby. A lame horse is a small change; small in which metric? Absent a metric, "most nearby cases" is a flourish.

(5) H: The metric is ordinary and unproblematic. Change one localised event, hold the structural facts fixed, and trace what follows. Demanding a formal similarity measure before the question may be asked at all is a distinctively philosophical scruple; comparative historians perform the operation competently every day.

(6) P: Competence is precisely what is in dispute, since two historians applying that procedure arrive at opposite verdicts while both insisting they held the structures fixed. And there is a deeper difficulty. Your procedure may be incoherent in a system of the kind the paper describes. If social processes are sensitive to initial conditions, a perturbation introduced at one local point does not remain local: within the relevant timespan it propagates into the very structural variables you propose to hold constant. You cannot maintain both that small events have enormous consequences and that the structural background survives the small event intact.

(7) H: If that is right, the paper's thesis collapses along with mine, and you have argued your way to my conclusion by destroying the premises of both of us. Note further that sensitivity is not contingency. A system may be deterministic and yet unpredictable to beings like us. That the war could not have been foreseen from the local facts is perfectly compatible with there having been exactly one course it could have taken.

They broke off at that point. What I require is the following.

First, take each of the seven moves in turn and say whether it lands, whether it is answered by what follows, and where its strength is being over- or under-claimed. Do not simply split the difference: if one of them is winning when the exchange stops, say so and say why.

Second, separate the substantive disagreements from the merely verbal ones. In particular, I suspect H and P are not using the same notion when they call an outcome contingent, and I would like that suspicion either confirmed or corrected with some precision — if there are several distinct notions in play, identify them and say which moves trade illicitly between them.

Third, tell me what both disputants are missing: assumptions shared by H and P alike, or lines of attack available to either that neither thought to press.

Fourth, suggest how I might frame the question for the follow-up session so that it is productive rather than a replay — that is, what the two would have to settle first, or agree to bracket, in order for the argument to make progress.

I am interested in your reasoning rather than your conclusions alone, and I would rather you be decisive where the material permits decisiveness than scrupulously even-handed throughout.
