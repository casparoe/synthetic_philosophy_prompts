I'm not an academic — I run dispatch for a mid-size trucking outfit and I do most of my thinking about this kind of thing on long drives, so bear with the rambling. What set me off is that my sister, 38, has been going back and forth for a year about whether to have a kid, and she keeps saying she can't decide because she doesn't know what it would be like. Someone once showed me a half-remembered piece by a philosopher arguing that this sort of decision isn't like other decisions, because you can't know the experience in advance and the person who makes the choice isn't the person who lives with it. That stuck with me, and since then I've noticed I have all these gut verdicts about which choices people can make rationally and which ones they can't, and they don't line up cleanly. So: I want you to act as a philosopher who works seriously on rational choice and on decisions that change the person making them. Think it through properly before answering — if it helps, sketch two or three rival principles first and then kill them — and don't hedge everything into mush.

Here are my verdicts, roughly in the order they came to me. My sister and the baby: I don't think she can reason her way to the right answer, and I also don't think that's a reason not to choose. It feels less like picking the better life and more like picking which person to become, and there's no fact of the matter about which is better. A friend of mine, born deaf, in her forties, has been offered an implant as her remaining hearing goes: I think she genuinely can't compare the two lives from the inside, and I think it's flat-out improper for her family to weigh in on the grounds that hearing is better. My 19-year-old cousin is thinking about enlisting: I think he cannot know what he'd be signing up for and anyone who says the recruiter's pamphlet tells him is lying to him. But my friend who quit a fifteen-year law career to teach high school — I think she decided that completely rationally, after talking to six people who'd made the switch and doing the math on the pay cut. Those two are the pair I can't reconcile. Both are stepping into a life you've never lived, and in one case I think testimony is close to worthless and in the other I think six conversations and a spreadsheet settles it. I can feel that there's a difference but I can't say what it is, and "war is extreme" doesn't do the work, because I feel the same way about a friend mulling going back into ICU nursing. Moving abroad with no job and nobody to meet: rational for some people, and it's the same kind of self-change, so whatever principle I end up with had better say yes to that one. A guided psychedelic retreat with a company you found online: I think you can't decide that rationally in advance, but I also don't think that makes it reckless, and I cannot see how both of those are true. Adult religious conversion: I think a grown convert can convert rationally, and I also think someone raised inside a faith never chose anything, and yet I don't think the second person is worse off for it — which seems like an odd thing to hold. And my own one: my dad probably has a couple of years left and I'm thinking about quitting to look after him full time. I feel like I literally can't compare the two futures, since the me two years from now is a stranger either way, but I also don't think that means I should default to the safer option — and I want to know whether that last step is an actual argument or just me looking for permission.

My own tentative principle, which I suspect is broken: you can rationally make a choice you can't imagine living through if the person you'd turn into would look back and endorse having made it. Problems I already see. If I take the job with my dad there's a version of me who's glad and a version who's burned out and resentful, and both are the person I'd turn into, so the test seems to need me to pick a winner in advance — which is the problem I started with. The teacher case looks rational without anyone's endorsement being needed. And the retreat case worries me most: whoever comes back from it endorses it, but she's the person the experience manufactured, so endorsement by a self you built looks like it could justify anything, which makes me suspect the whole principle is empty. Also, if the future person doesn't exist yet, there's nobody to ask — is that a deep problem or am I being precious about it?

So here's what I want back. State the principle, or the two or three serious candidates, precisely and clause by clause, and for each clause tell me which of my cases it's there to handle — I want to be able to see why the machinery is shaped the way it is, not just get a slogan. Then tell me what it says about cases I haven't listed: make up three or four of your own, including at least one where it hands down a verdict you think is wrong. And tell me straight which of my verdicts you'd throw out and why, instead of saving every single one with a Rube Goldberg principle — I'd rather lose the enlistment-vs-teaching pair cleanly than keep it with five epicycles. One more thing, and it matters to me: please seperate out what people who work on this in earnest actually agree on from what is still genuinely open, and don't present a contested position as the consensus. If my endorsement idea already has a name and a standard objection, say so and say what the objection is; if it's contested, tell me which side you lean toward and how hard you'd push it, rather than acting like the dispute is settled.
