I ring bells, or rather I'm one of the volunteers who looks after the tower here, Saturdays mostly, and over about fifteen years I've been in on three separate bell jobs: a rehang, a retune, and a full recast after our old tenor cracked in a hard frost. I've no philosophy training at all, never read anything about identity or whatever they call it, but the foundry man and I got into a long argument about it last month and then my sister had a go at me over it too, and the upshot is I've realised I can't actually say what rule I'm applying when I say the things I say. So here are my verdicts, as plainly as I can put them. The recast: our tenor cracked right through, went off to the foundry, got melted down and poured again, the invoice said something like four fifths of her was the old metal and the rest new, they copied the old inscription on the waist letter for letter including the date and the founder's name, she came back to the same tower on the same beam, the whole town calls her by the same name, and we rang her in with a little service. My verdict is that's a new bell. It's like if you melted down your grandmother's rings and poured a new one out of the metal and then told everyone it was your grandmother's ring: the gold is hers, the ring isn't. Meanwhile there's a parish ten miles up the road whose bell cracked and they never raised the money to recast it, so its been sitting in the porch for thirty years, silent, unringable, and that one is definitely still the bell, no hesitation, a bell you can't ring is still a bell. On wear: our third had a groove worn into her at the strike point by eighty years of the same clapper hitting the same spot, and the foundry man turned her a quarter of the way round so the clapper now meets fresh metal, and separately they've shaved metal off the inside on a lathe over the decades to bring the note right, so she's maybe lost a tenth of her weight. Same bell, obviously. On gear: a bell whose headstock, wheel, bearings, clapper and rope have all been replaced while the bronze was never touched, same bell, that's just maintenance. On theft: a bell taken out of a tower and turned up twenty years later in somebody's garden and got rehung, same bell, even though it hadn't been a working bell in a tower for two decades. On copies: a foundry took a mould off an old bell and cast a copy in new metal, the original sits in a museum and the copy hangs in the tower and gets rung every Sunday and the villagers call it the bell, and I say that's not the same bell, however good the copy is it's a copy. A bell sold from one parish to another and rehanged is the same bell, just moved house. And two cracked bells melted down together and poured as one new bell is a third bell, not either of them, and I'd say the same about one big bell poured as two small ones.

If you squint, what I seem to be tracking is the pouring. My idea, and it may be complete rubbish, is that a bell just is whatever came out of one casting, so it's over the moment it goes back in the furnace, no matter what comes out again or how much of the metal is the same or how exactly they copied the shape and the lettering and the name. I think that gets me most of what's above. Where it comes apart is the one I keep going back to: there was a job where a bell cracked and they recast her but kept the crown, the top part with the old canons and the founder's mark on it, and worked that into the new casting, so the very top of the bell now hanging in that tower is the original bronze and the rest of her is a fresh pour. At the dedication I said she was the same old girl and I meant it at the time, and now I'm not sure I should have. So what I want to know is what the principle behind all of this actually is, stated properly and clause by clause, and which of my verdicts each clause is there to deliver. Does one-pouring survive contact with the crown case, or is there something better, and if there's something better, which of the things I've said does it force me to give up, because I'd rather be told straight than go on being inconsistent without knowing it. I'd also like to know what the principle implies about three cases I haven't ruled on at all: the crown case itself; a recast where literally all of the metal is the old metal and nothing new goes in whatsoever; and a crack repaired by welding or brazing with the bell never going near a furnace. And my mate insists the same rule can't possibly work for a car, since a car that's had a new engine and a new gearbox and a new clutch is still the same car, so if you can say why bells and cars might legitimately come out differently, or why they shouldn't, I'd be grateful for that too.

Please write back to me the way you'd write an email: ordinary paragraphs, no headings, no bullet points, no bold, no tables, just continuous prose from start to finish, because I'll be reading it on my phone up in the tower between touches and anything fancy gets mangled on the screen.
