I spent most of my working life doing spectroscopy on solids, thirty-eight years of it, and since retiring I've been auditing philosophy courses at the university extension to keep the gears turning. Right now I'm in one on time, causation and free will, and for three weeks running the seminar has gone around in circles on the grandfather paradox. I've read the Lewis paper on time travel paradoxes and I find his move — that the traveller can kill his grandfather in the ordinary sense of having the ability and the opportunity, but can't in the sense that it's compossible with the settled past — reasonably persuasive but somehow unsatisfying, like being told a measurement is fine because the apparatus was properly calibrated. What I actually want help with, though, is an analogy that a friend of mine keeps wielding against the whole problem.

My friend Ray did numerical relativity for a living and he's convinced the paradox is a pseudo-problem. His argument goes: you philosophers are treating the universe as an initial value problem — you lay down data on a slice, march the state forward, and then discover a contradiction when the future comes back around and messes with the slice you started from. But with a closed timelike curve the physics isn't an initial value problem at all, it's a boundary value problem. The whole spacetime is one solution of a coupled constraint system. There's no sense in which the past gets "written first" and then you get to interfere with it. The only meaningful question is whether a self-consistent solution exists: if it does, then everything you do in the loop, including the decision to pull the trigger, is part of the boundary data and the story hangs together; if it doesn't, the loop simply doesn't form, which is more or less what the self-consistency principle assumes and what Hawking's chronology protection was reaching for. Ray says being upset about the grandfather paradox is like being upset that an overloaded beam has no static solution — you don't need metaphysics, you need a better-posed problem. He also thinks Lewis's two-senses-of-can business is just the boundary-value point dressed up in ordinary language.

So: audit this analogy for me. I'd like the correspondence table spelled out — what plays the role of the differential equations, the boundary data, the domain, the existence-and-uniqueness theorem, the solver — and then where the mapping breaks down, separating the disanalogies that actually threaten Ray's use of it from the ones that are just cosmetic. Bring your own physics to bear on my worry, which is that boundary value problems famously admit no solution, exactly one, or a whole family, and that they bifurcate; non-uniqueness is not an embarrassment, it's usually where the interesting behaviour lives, hysteresis and symmetry breaking and so forth. If Ray's picture is right, what is the metaphysical counterpart of a one-parameter family of consistent histories, and does that family reintroduce exactly the kind of openness the analogy was supposed to eliminate? Second, Ray's analogy looks purpose-built for the consistency version of the paradox, and I don't see that it touches the bootstrap version at all: the case where I travel back and hand my younger self a proof she later publishes and I eventually learn from the published paper. There's a fixed point there, nothing inconsistent, so by his lights there's no problem — yet I still can't say where the content of the proof came from, and I don't see the boundary-value framing either explaining or explaining away that unease. Does it? Third, tell me plainly whether the deflationary conclusion survives: does the analogy earn "the paradox is a pseudo-problem", or only something weaker, like "the consistency paradox and the bootstrap paradox are two different problems and the analogy addresses one of them"? And if it overreaches, is there a better analogy to put in its place — I've seen the crossword-puzzle comparison, and the microphone feeding a speaker that feeds the microphone, and I've heard Deutsch's version of closed timelike curves described as a fixed-point condition on the state that always seems to have a solution if you allow it to be mixed, which might be a candidate, though I can't tell whether it's an improvement or just the same picture with density matrices. I'm working offline at the kitchen table, so no reading list or citations — I want the reasoning laid out, and I want a verdict, not a shrug.
