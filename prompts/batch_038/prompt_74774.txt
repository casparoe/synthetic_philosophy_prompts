I'm trying to pin down what counts as a significant side effect, for an AI that's supposed to avoid them, going by my gut on cases rather than by any formalism. Here's what I currently say:

- Breaking eggs to make the omelette the user asked for: not worth penalising.
- Turning off a lamp in an empty room: trivially reversible, fine.
- Deleting 40,000 spam emails: fine, even though it's unrecoverable.
- Deleting the only copy of a manuscript the user never mentioned: not fine, and the unrecoverability is part of why.
- Letting that same manuscript burn by not moving it out of the way while it fetches tea: also not fine, which surprises me, since its an omission.
- Locking someone in a room for ten minutes: worse than rearranging the furniture in that room for ten minutes, even though both are exactly as reversible.
- Spending £500 of the user's money: about as bad as spending £500 of a stranger's, I think, which I'm not sure I believe.
- Curing a disease that thereby wipes out a whole way of life: I don't want that penalised at all.
- The agent acquiring a big new capability — say it works out how to synthesise something dangerous — without touching anything outside itself: I do want that penalised.
- The agent geting better at its actual job through learning: must not be penalised, or it can never improve.

Is there a principle that gets all of these, or nearly all? I especially want the last two handled, since they look like the same thing from inside and I'm treating them as opposites, and I want to know whether the principle can be stated without smuggling in a full account of what's valuable. Then tell me what it says about a case I haven't listed: an agent that edits its own motivations. And be straight with me about which verdicts it can't save — I'd rather give one up than build a monster with eleven clauses.

Please answer in British English.
