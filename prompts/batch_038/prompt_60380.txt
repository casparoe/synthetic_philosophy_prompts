A friend and I spent three hours last night on self-deception and got nowhere. He holds the partitioning view: for a case to count as genuine self-deception, the agent must in some sense retain the true belief while also holding the false one, and the only way to make that coherent is to divide the mind into semi-autonomous subsystems. I want to deny that any such division is needed, and I want the denial argued rather than merely asserted.

Make the case for this claim: self-deception is a real, literal phenomenon that requires no pair of contradictory beliefs, no partitioned cognitive architecture, and no intention to get oneself to believe what one knows to be false. On the account I want defended, self-deception consists in the agent's own biased conduct of inquiry — what evidence is sought, what is treated as sufficient, how readily contrary testimony is discounted — with the result that the true belief is never formed at all.

Two points in particular need pressing. First, his sharpest case is one in which the agent's information-gathering is impeccably thorough: she has canvassed everything available to her and suppressed nothing. Argue that the bias can reside wholly in the standard of proof she applies, so that no unacknowledged knowledge is required to explain her state. Second, he charges that this account abandons the deception in self-deception and leaves only ordinary wishful thinking under a grander name. Argue that the deception attribution is nevertheless literal — that the functional role of a deceiver is genuinely occupied by the agent's own regulating activity, even absent any intention to produce a belief known to be false.

Then be candid about where the view is most exposed: what must be true about motivation for it to work at all, given that no awareness of the truth is available to supply the motive?
