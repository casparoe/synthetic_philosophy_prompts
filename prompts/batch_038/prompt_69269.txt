I'm TA-ing a week-long interdisciplinary summer school on minds and machines — 18 people, roughly half philosophy grad students, half third-year CS undergrads, and the two halves are wierdly good at each other's material, which makes discussion harder to run than you'd think. Thursday I've got 80 minutes and it's meant to be on whether neural networks actually have representations at all, or whether saying the model represents gender or represents the concept of a door is just a convenient way of talking about what a probe can decode out of the activations. I've now tried just discussing this twice and both times it collapsed in exactly the same way: the CS students say obviously there are directions in activation space that line up with things, heres the plot, and the philosophers say but that's your probe finding the structure you built it to look for, and then everyone nods politely and nobody's position moves an inch. I want them to run into the problem rather than hear me describe it.

So: design me an activity that needs no computers. The room has one whiteboard, a couple packs of index cards, markers, and tables people can stand around. I want some of the students to be a very crude hand-built network — people who take an input card, apply some fixed little rule I've written on their card, and pass the result along — and the rest to be interpretability researchers who can only interrogate it in restricted ways: they submit inputs, they get back the output card, and maybe they get to see one intermediate layer, but they can't ask anyone what they're thinking. The thing I actually need the activity to produce, and this is the part I don't know how to engineer, is two failures at once. One: a feature the researchers can decode beautifully and reliably but that the network demonstrably doesn't use for anything downstream — a phantom that predicts the output perfectly and matters causally not at all. Two: a feature the network genuinely does use but that no probe the rules permit them to build could pick out. If both of those happen in the same room in the same hour, then the it's-decodable-so-it's-real people and the it's-all-just-our-projection people both come away with a bruise, and that double bruise is the feeling I'm after.

Give the researchers a payoff that rewards the cleanest, simplest story about what the network represents, judged by the students playing the network who know the ground truth — I suspect the pressure to confabulate a tidy interpretation is half the phenomenon and I want them to catch themselves doing it in real time. Then I need a debrief that maps moments of the game onto the pieces of the real debate: which stretch is the decodability-versus-use worry, which stretch is the superposition point, which stretch is the instrumentalist move that a hundred equally good interpretations fit the data. Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it — my guess is the network people get bored and start freelancing, which would sink the whole thing — and how to patch it mid-session without admitting the patch. And be straight with me about the one feature of real interpretability work that eighteen people with index cards structurally cannot reproduce, the part I'm going to have to just stand up and say afterwards. Please write the whole thing as continuous prose, like an email — no headings, no bullet points, no bold, no tables. I know a lesson plan normally wants bullets, but I'll be reading it on my phone on the bus Thursday morning and anything with a table in it wraps into garbage.
