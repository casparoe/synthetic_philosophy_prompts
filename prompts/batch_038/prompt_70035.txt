Here's the setup as I have it, so you can check my math:

Driver's going home. Two exits on his route, they look identical, and he's so absent-minded he can't tell them apart or remember whether he's already passed one. Exit at the first: payoff 0. Exit at the second: 4. Blow past both: 1. With perfect recall he'd just take the second and get 4. Without it, his only lever is a probability p of continuing at whatever intersection he finds himself at. Payoff is p(1-p)·4 + p²·1 = 4p − 3p², maximized at p = 2/3, worth 4/3.

Now here's the essay.

**What I think the driver paradox actually is**

Everyone presents this as a fight between two standpoints — the planner before the drive and the driver at the intersection — and then argues about which one gets to decide. I think that framing creates the puzzle instead of solving it, and that once you drop it there's no clash left to explain.

Step one. At an intersection the driver's only evidence is "I'm at an intersection." Given the plan p, the chance this is the first one is α = 1/(1+p) (he always hits the first, hits the second only with probability p), so the chance it's the second is p/(1+p).

Step two. Value of continuing at the node, holding the rest of the plan fixed: if it's the first, continuing is worth p·1 + (1−p)·4 = 4−3p; if it's the second, continuing is worth 1. So continue = α(4−3p) + (1−α)(1) = (4−2p)/(1+p). Exit = α·0 + (1−α)·4 = 4p/(1+p). The difference is (4−6p)/(1+p).

Step three, which is the bit I like. The derivative of the planning objective 4p − 3p² is 4 − 6p. So the local "should I continue?" comparison is *exactly* the global marginal test, divided by a positive number. At p = 2/3 the driver is indifferent at the node (both options worth 8/5) and the plan is at its maximum. Below 2/3 he wants to continue and continuing raises his payoff; above 2/3 he wants to exit and exiting raises it. Local reasoning isn't in conflict with planning, it's a gradient signal pointing at the same place.

So where does the feeling of paradox come from? I think from one illicit move: treating the decision at the intersection as if it were a *seperate* variable from the plan. It isn't. Because the driver can't tell the intersections apart, whatever he settles on at "the node" he settles on at both nodes. His local indifference at p = 2/3 is not permission to do whatever he likes there — doing whatever he likes there means p = 0 or p = 1, which is worth 0 or 1, not 4/3. Indifference at the node is a knife edge in policy space, not slack. There is only ever one decision variable, and the guy at the intersection and the guy on the couch are both pulling it.

The version of this I find most satisfying is a team reading: think of it as two decision makers sharing one payoff function who can't talk, each with his own information partition. In team problems it's routine that the team-optimal strategy isn't something any member would arrive at from his own information alone, and nobody calls that dynamic inconsistency. Here the catch is that "unilateral deviation" isn't even definable — the member can't deviate at one node only — which is just another way of saying the local and global questions are the same question.

Where I don't trust myself: (a) the team reading has two members who are the same person, which maybe makes it a metaphor rather than a model; (b) it sounds like I'm dissolving the problem by description, and I can't tell whether that's a real objection or the whole point; (c) I'm treating the driver's belief α as computed from the plan, but if he's considering changing the plan, shouldn't the belief be computed from the plan he's considering?
