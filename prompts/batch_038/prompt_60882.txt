Ok so this isn't really my fight and I've already regretted agreeing to referee it, but here goes. I'm a postdoc in a cell-bio lab. We have exactly one usable confocal microscope, and only three people in the group are trained on it: me, Marta, and Elias. Back in September, in the kitchen, nobody else around, nothing written down, Elias told Marta she'd have first claim on the scope for all of January, because her grant deadline was mid-February and she needed the imaging done beforehand. Then in late November a new postdoc, Jo, joined — also trained, deadline January 20th — and our PI, who had no idea about the September arrangement, handed out January slots by lottery. Elias drew good ones. Marta said he has to give them to her. He said no. And then the thing metastacized into a three-hour argument about why promises bind in the first place, which is not what anyone had signed up for. Roughly the exchange, as best I can reconstruct it:

1. **Marta:** You chose to say those words, and that's the entire point of the device. Promising exists precisely so that you can't change your mind once your preferences change. If you could wiggle out every time the balance of reasons shifted, I'd have no use for your promise — I could just estimate your reasons myself.

2. **Elias:** That's the fallacy right there. Promising isn't a magic trick that lets me bind myself regardless of cost. Keeping it is a real reason but not an infinite one. Jo's position is worse than mine, the PI's lottery is a legitimate procedure I had no hand in, and a kitchen conversation doesn't become a trump card against all of that. Promises bind because they're useful — they generate reliance and let people coordinate — and here coordination has been taken over by a fairer mechanism.

3. **Marta:** Fine, but notice what you've just said. On your account all I ever got from you in September was evidence about what you'd probably do, plus a reason to rely. That's prediction and expectation-management. So on your view there was no promise at all, and you can't now claim you're keeping the *promise* — there's nothing to keep.

4. **Elias:** Not quite nothing. What changed is that the practice which gives your kitchen words their meaning wasn't in play. There's an institutional allocation procedure now and there wasn't in September. Promises don't work by sheer willpower; they're moves inside a social game with rules, and when the rules change the moves change meaning. What you extracted from me in September was closer to "please intend to help me," and nothing in the game converts that into a claim-right you can enforce against a third party's schedule.

5. **Marta:** Then you've conceded my point in disguise. If the force of a promise comes from conventions that could easily have been otherwise, then whether promises bind is a fact about our society, not a moral fact — and we both think breaking a promise would be wrong even among people with no such convention. Also your game story predicts I *couldn't* release you, since the rules aren't mine to change. Obviously I can. And release only makes sense if the obligation is owed *to me* and is mine to control, rather than a contribution to the general good.

6. **Elias:** Release cuts my way. The fact that it's *your* call shows the obligation exists to protect your interest in controlling what happens to you — which is my welfare-and-coordination story in the only non-spooky version available.

Neither budged, and I got sent home to look things up, which is how I'm bothering you. Can you go through this move by move and tell me who's actually right at each step — rate each of the six, from clearly mistaken to clearly right, with a sentence or two of justification? Where a move is leaning on a general family of views about promissory obligation (reliance-based, practice/convention-based, second-personal or authority-based, will/autonomy-based, straightforwardly consequentialist) you can name it, but I care way more about whether the move works than about labels, and please don't turn this into a survey of the literature.
