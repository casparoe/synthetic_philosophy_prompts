I am a second-year doctoral student in philosophy working on moral ignorance, and for reasons that have nothing to do with my dissertation I have spent the last few weeks collecting uses of a single phrase: "teleological suspension of the ethical." I have now heard it made to do three quite different jobs, and I cannot tell whether any of them is the job the text actually assigns it — or rather, the job Johannes de silentio assigns it, since the reading group I audit keeps insisting, with some heat, that the difference between the pseudonym and Kierkegaard is not a technicality.

The use that started this for me was at a cross-departmental workshop on AI governance, where a visiting researcher from a frontier lab explained, with no evident embarrassment, that he had copied a set of model weights he had been given access to under a non-disclosure agreement and published them, on the grounds that their concentration in a single firm would be worse for the world than his broken promise. When someone in the audience objected that this was theft and promise-breaking by any ordinary description, he answered: "This is a teleological suspension of the ethical. Kierkegaard would recognise exactly what I did — Abraham violated every obligation he was under, for a higher telos, and you cannot judge him by ordinary morality." He then added, apparently to close the matter, that the difference between him and a common criminal is that he acted for everyone rather than for himself. Afterwards, in the corridor, he told me he had read *Fear and Trembling* once in college and chiefly remembered the passage where the knight of faith turns out to look like an unremarkable tax collector.

The other two uses are secondhand but common enough. In my reading group, a classmate glosses the phrase as the thesis that moral norms are always defeasible by a person's sincerely held convictions — existentialist relativism with a biblical illustration attached. On a podcast clip that keeps circulating among my friends, the host invokes it as an elevated way of saying that the ends justify the means provided one is sincere about the ends.

What I want is a disambiguation, not an interpretation. Treat the phrase as a piece of language recruited into an argument and lay out the distinct precise theses it could be expressing, from the merely trivial to the genuinely radical. For each candidate reading I would like to know three things: which of the book's three problems it actually belongs to (I know the phrase comes from the first, and that the second concerns an absolute duty to God and the third Abraham's concealment from Sarah, Eliezer and Isaac, and my suspicion is that the researcher was borrowing authority from all three simultaneously); what the word "suspension" commits him to — whether the ethical is cancelled, overridden by a competing consideration, or left fully in force but set aside, since the reading group spent an entire session on that and got nowhere; and whether the claim is de silentio's own, part of the Hegelian position he is arguing against, or an accretion that arrived later. Please also engage explicitly with two features of the text that seem to me to bear hard on the workshop exchange: the way the tragic heroes — Agamemnon, Jephthah, Brutus — are distinguished from Abraham precisely by sacrificing for a larger communal end, and de silentio's own admission that anyone claiming the paradox owes us criteria for telling the paradox apart from a mere temptation. If you think those features do the work I suspect, say why; if they cut the other way, say that instead.

Format. Please organise the answer under these headings, in this order: (1) **The three jobs the phrase was asked to do** — roughly 150 words separating what each of the three uses above actually commits its user to; (2) **Candidate readings** — roughly 800 words, numbered R1, R2, and so on, each stated as a precise thesis with the three tags I asked for; (3) **Verdicts** — roughly 400 words, sorting the readings into trivially true, substantive and defensible, false, and "not a candidate reading of this text at all"; (4) **What the researcher's defence requires** — roughly 300 words, identifying which reading or readings would have to be true for his appeal to carry any weight, whether they are, and whether his own closing remark about acting for everyone lands him in the tragic-hero category rather than Abraham's, and if so whether that helps or damages him; (5) **Where you are least sure** — roughly 150 words. No bibliography, no page numbers, no further reading: I want the analysis itself, not pointers to it, and I cannot check anything tonight.

Two requests about how committed the answer should be. First, where you are reconstructing a reading rather than reporting one you find in the book, say so — I would rather have a flagged reconstruction than a confident paraphrase. Second, if you think the task is mis-set, tell me plainly at the top instead of complying politely: it is possible that a term of art inside a dialectical pseudonymous work does not admit of a menu of loose "readings" the way a proverb does, or that my characterisation of the researcher already loads the conclusion I want from you, or that asking which reading "Kierkegaard" held is exactly the question the pseudonymity forbids. Any of those would be more useful to me than a tidy list.
