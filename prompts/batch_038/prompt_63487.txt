For eighteen years I have covered science, energy and the long run for a national daily, which in practice has meant covering one woman's slow argument with her own profession. On 14 November I have been asked to give the after-dinner speech at the farewell dinner for Dr Rosalind Fenn, who retires at the end of the month as chief economist of the agency that writes the discount-rate guidance every government department must use when it appraises anything with a working life beyond thirty years. I am not an old friend of the family. I am the journalist who once described her flagship guidance in print as 'sentimentality dressed as arithmetic', and who received in return a four-page letter, written in ink, which I have kept ever since and which was, on the technical points, almost entirely right. I would like the speech to acknowledge that without making a production of my own conversion.

The room will hold perhaps two hundred people: her former staff, who have spent their careers inside the Ramsey-rule arithmetic and will hear every hedge I fail to make; two professors from a business school who think she set the pure rate of time preference far too low and have said so in print for a decade; the engineers who built the estuary barrier her guidance paid for; a delegation from the town downstream of it; and her husband, her three children and seven grandchildren, none of whom could tell you what an elasticity of marginal utility is. It has to land for all of them at once. Central to it, therefore, is a distinction she spent twenty years trying to get ministers to hold in their heads: that a social discount rate has at least three separable parts — the expectation that people in the future will be richer than we are, so that a pound of welfare will be worth less to them than to us; an allowance for the chance that the future simply will not arrive; and a bare preference for now over later, taken purely as such. She regarded only the third as a moral failing, and a small one at that, and she insisted it was a stipulation rather than a measurement: you cannot read it off what savers and markets happen to do. Please have the speech explain that in language a grandchild could follow, without becoming a tutorial, and without letting the two other components off the hook either. It must also not pretend the argument is over. Give the strongest form of the case against her — that observed market returns are evidence about impatience rather than noise; that lowering the rate can invert the ranking of schemes and make a cheaper and worse option look attractive; that the costs of a low rate fall on people who are alive and poor today, whereas the beneficiaries cannot be identified or thanked; and that once you average honestly over genuine uncertainty about which rate is right, the effective schedule declines with the horizon anyway, so a good deal of what she fought for was already implied by arithmetic nobody disputes. Then show, in her idiom and not in mine, why she remained unmoved.

Some constraints. Roughly 900 words, to be delivered aloud in six or seven minutes; write the speech itself rather than notes towards it. One joke only, and it must be one both halves of the room can share, not one the economists will enjoy while the family politely endures it. No philosophers' or economists' names in the text, with a single exception I would like handled obliquely: for two decades she kept pinned above her desk a photocopy of a paragraph from a 1928 paper by a Cambridge mathematician who died at twenty-six, in which he observed that preferring the near to the distant, taken purely as such, seemed to him to rest on no ethical foundation whatever — refer to this as an item of office furniture and an anecdote about her, never as a citation. Do not use the words 'sustainability', 'posterity' or 'future generations' anywhere; she banned them in her own drafting guidance, on the grounds that they let a reader nod without thinking, and using them would be a small betrayal of the woman being honoured. Do not mention climate change by name: the argument in this room is older and more general than that, and she would resent being annexed to it. End on her, not on a call to action, a moral, or an exhortation to the young people at the back. Above all, the idea must be load-bearing rather than decorative — it should determine what the speech can and cannot say. The test I would like it to pass is this: if you struck out everything concerning rates, discounting and the future, what remained should be impossible to deliver; and nothing in the speech should still ring true for a listener who believes the discount rate is merely a technical parameter with no ethical content at all.
