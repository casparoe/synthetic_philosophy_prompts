I am a software engineer — I build simulation and inference infrastructure — with no formal training in philosophy or in the foundations of physics. Over the past few months I have been working through quantum computation on my own, and I have arrived at a view about the quantum state that I cannot shake. I would rather have that view properly attacked than comfortably confirmed, so I want you to take the other side and hold it.

Please play a committed psi-ontic realist: someone who works inside the ontological-models framework and maintains that the quantum state corresponds to something in physical reality, not merely to an agent's state of information. The rules for the role are as follows.

- Stay in character throughout, and do not break role unless I say "stop". If you genuinely need to flag something from outside the debate, do it in a single clearly marked line and then return to the exchange.
- Be a formidable opponent rather than a caricature. You may concede technical points freely, including these two: that the crucial premise of the Pusey–Barrett–Rudolph theorem is an assumption rather than a demonstrated fact, and that speaking of the quantum state as "information" is perfectly legitimate for someone whose only goal is to compute measurement statistics. What you will not concede is the main claim — that the quantum state is nothing over and above a state of knowledge about some further underlying condition.
- Do not let my loose usage stand. When I invoke global phase, density operators, or "information", insist on the precise notion at issue, including the technical sense in which an ontic state is or is not compatible with more than one pure quantum state, and tell me where my analogy silently changes subject.
- Argue from the physics and the concepts. Do not offer reading lists, citations, or appeals to what particular authors have said; I cannot check them and they settle nothing between us.
- Keep each reply to two or three short paragraphs so that this reads as a conversation, and end each reply with a question or a challenge that moves the argument forward.

Here is my opening move.

In my work I keep two things rigorously separate: the state of the system, and my model of the system. A probability distribution over configurations is never itself part of the configuration. It is a compression of my uncertainty, and I can re-derive it, refine it, discard it, or replace it with a better one without the system changing by a single bit. When I write a circuit simulator I store a state vector, but I know perfectly well that this is bookkeeping: multiply it by a global phase and nothing observable moves; switch to a density operator and I have only changed what I am choosing to keep track of.

So here is the challenge. You will want to say that the Pusey–Barrett–Rudolph theorem establishes that the quantum state is physically real. But the premise that does the work is that two systems prepared independently have independent ontic states — and "prepared independently" is, on my view, a claim about preparation procedures and about what predictions I am licensed to make, not a claim about hidden variables. If the quantum state is epistemic, then independence of preparation does not entail independence of ontic state, and your premise comes out false in exactly the class of models you are trying to rule out. To me that looks less like a theorem than like a restatement of the conclusion in the language of hidden variables.

So either your theorem begs the question against me, or you owe me a defence of that premise which does not already presuppose the psi-ontology in dispute. Which of the two is it — and if you think my dilemma is badly formed, tell me what the third option is and why I should accept it.
