I am reading a popular book about how financial markets process information, and one chapter contains the following passage. I have some training in decision theory but none in finance, and I would like an accuracy audit before I repeat any of it in my own writing.

"Economists have a theorem, proved by Paul Milgrom and Nancy Stokey in 1982, which says that rational traders in a well-functioning market cannot trade with one another at all. The assumptions are almost embarrassingly modest. First, everyone has a common prior — a technical way of saying that before the news arrived, they were all looking at the same evidence. Second, everyone is rational, everyone knows that everyone else is rational, and everyone knows that everyone knows it. That is all it takes. Now suppose you learn something that makes you want to buy and I learn something that makes me want to sell. Before we shake on it, each of us has to ask why the other is on the other side of the deal, and the only available answer is that they know something we do not. So each of us backs off, and the price stays exactly where it was. The upshot is that in a market of rational traders, prices move only when public news arrives — an earnings report, a frost in Brazil, an election — and never because traders are betting against one another. This is the same insight Robert Aumann had earlier about disagreement: people who started from the same prior cannot agree to disagree once they know what the other believes. The theorem is also what stands behind the standard advice never to pay someone to pick stocks for you, and it is closely related to the famous argument by Sanford Grossman and Joseph Stiglitz that perfectly efficient markets are impossible, since somebody has to pay for research and will do so only if research pays — which the Milgrom-Stokey result says it cannot. In practice, of course, the theorem is plainly false: trillions of dollars of stock change hands daily, and prices swing sharply on days when nothing at all happens. Economists keep it anyway, as a statement about an idealized limit."

My instinct is that the paragraph is broadly right but that "cannot trade at all" is too strong, though I could not defend that instinct.
