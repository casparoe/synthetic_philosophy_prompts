Okay, this landed in my lap and I could use help thinking it through.

I'm on my university's new committee on public statements — six of us, set up last year after the president said something about a war and half the faculty lost it. Our charter has a clause everyone quoted approvingly at the time and nobody has actually had to apply until now: the university may take a public position only where the reasons for it "can be understood and assessed by members of the community regardless of their religious or philosophical commitments."

Here's the case. A student coalition wants us to endorse a city ordinance putting a year-round low-barrier shelter on a block currently zoned for a development project. Most of what they submitted is unremarkable: overdose and hypothermia deaths downtown, the cost of boarding people in the ER versus shelter beds, the fact that the campus already draws heavily on city services. But the opening section is explicitly theological. It grounds the obligation in a claim about hospitality being owed to persons as such, and then it makes a historical argument — the block was cleared in the sixties partly by taking a church's property, and the congregation's standing covenant to the neighborhood is part of what got destroyed. And here's the wrinkle: they say that historical claim is a reason *for this university specifically* only because of the covenant. So it's not separable from the rest, by their own account.

Two objectors. A faculty member says the charter plainly bars it: you can't assess a covenant obligation unless you share the faith, so the reason fails the test. A student member says the test is a racket — that translation demands fall hardest on exactly the communities whose neighborhoods got cleared, and that the clause quietly assumes the secular parts of the packet are any less commitment-dependent (they rest on views about what makes a death bad, about how to weight the worst-off, about the limits of property rights, none of which is neutral either).

Draft the memo I need to get to the other five by Monday night. It should recommend something concrete — endorse as submitted, endorse a version, decline, defer, or an option I haven't thought of — with a next step and a date attached. It should also give us a test we could run on the *next* request in ten minutes, not just a verdict on this one, and be straight about what the test rules out; I don't want a standard that turns out to permit every statement ever proposed. There's a disagreement underneath this that the memo should surface and take a side on: some of us read the charter as demanding reasons people *share*, others read it as asking only whether each member has some sufficient reason of their own to accept the position. Those are different tests and would come out differently here. Same with a second confusion — whether the charter means religious reasons can be offered so long as public reasons show up eventually, or means the public reasons have to be the ones actually carrying the argument.

Two more things. I'm a member of the congregation in question, so I've recused from the vote and this circulates as a discussion document without my authority behind it — which means it has to be genuinely even-handed, and if it reads as a defense of my own church I've made things worse. Flag anywhere you think it's tilting. And at the end, tell me how confident you are in the recommendation and which single piece of the reasoning you're least sure of. I'd rather know now which part is load-bearing on a hunch than find out Tuesday when someone pushes on it.
