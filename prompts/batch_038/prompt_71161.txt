And here's the student's answer, unedited:

> (A) is the modern dress of a very old objection to vegetarianism — the idea that abstaining from meat would make the animals it was meant to protect disappear — and it fails for several reasons.
>
> The first problem is that LDI counts deaths, and deaths are not the morally relevant currency. What matters is suffering, and the loss suffered by the particular animal that dies. A cow slaughtered at thirty months loses a great deal: she is social, she has preferences about her own future, and she is grieved by her herd. A field mouse run over by a harvester loses almost nothing, because mice are r-selected. They produce enormous litters precisely because most offspring die within weeks anyway, and adult life is short and precarious; a death that the population has already budgeted for is not a serious harm to the individual. Once you weight each death by what the animal actually stands to lose, the balance swings hard toward plant agriculture, because a vegan diet kills a great many mice but only ever kills cattle deliberately.
>
> Second, the empirical premises are not just fragile, they're mostly false. The claim that cattle occupy land too rough for crops is a description of a minority of the herd; in practice most cattle are finished on grain in feedlots, so a beef diet inherits the crop deaths of its feed as well as whatever happens on range. And the land-per-calorie comparison runs the other way: feeding grain through an animal is lossy, so plants need far less land per calorie than beef does, by roughly an order of magnitude. There's also a denominator problem the question hints at. LDI divides by edible calories, but beef and grain differ enormously in protein and fat per calorie, so a comparison that is calibrated on calories will rank a fatty food better than a comparison calibrated on protein would. (A) seems to move between denominators without saying so.
>
> Third, on the counterfactual: if demand for beef collapsed, the pasture would not just sit there. It would either be converted to cropland, which is worse on LDI's own terms, or it would revert to native habitat. Rewilding is where this connects up with the wild animal suffering debate. Habitat means predation, parasite load, starvation in winter — the standard picture of a wild life as net-negative. If that picture is right, converting land out of habitat and into fields reduces the total amount of wild suffering, and the rewilding that (A) ignores actually cuts against (A). Singer presses exactly this point when he argues that the sheer scale of suffering in nature gives us a standing duty to intervene in ecosystems to reduce it. On that reading the LDI framework is a bit of a distraction, because the real question is whether we should be managing wild populations at all, and the crop-death objection is just that question wearing farm clothes.
>
> Finally, even granting (A)'s arithmetic, it wouldn't show that eating beef is permissible. The deaths in crop production are foreseen side effects of an activity whose point is feeding people, whereas the cow's death is the point: the animal is brought into existence in order to be killed. That is a straightforward double-effect case, and double effect disposes of it — we may foresee the field deaths without willing them, and the good of feeding people is proportionate. Since LDI is a comparative metric anyway, and the comparison comes out in favour of plants once you correct the land-use numbers, the framework has done the work it was built to do.
