I am the director of governance for a national professional association — roughly 12,400 members — that publishes safety standards for pressure equipment (boilers, unfired storage vessels, industrial piping) and certifies the inspectors who enforce them. Because most states incorporate our standards by reference into their boiler law, a clause we adopt becomes a legal requirement, and a clause we get wrong can contribute to a vessel failure somewhere down the line. Our standards council has 41 seats drawn from three chambers: credentialed engineers and inspectors, manufacturers and owner-operators, and a small public-interest block (workers' representatives, municipal safety officials, and a handful of lay members seated after a fatal incident a decade ago). Ballots are one member, one vote, standards pass by simple majority, and charter amendments need two-thirds plus ratification by the membership. Two or three times a year, a proposal arrives to depart from that equal weighting — votes weighted by certification tier, a standing technical panel with a veto over clauses it deems unsound, sortition-selected lay jurors, restricted suffrage on technical annexes, and, most often, supermajority requirements that quietly function as minority vetoes. We have no framework for any of it. The last such fight cost us a board seat and nearly cost us the manufacturer chamber, which represents about forty percent of dues and had begun talking about publishing a rival standard.

What I want is a decision procedure my governance committee can actually run on any such proposal. The committee is seven people — two practicing engineers, a lawyer, three elected council members, and me — and nobody on it has graduate training in political theory; one member is a retired plant manager who will not read anything longer than four pages. We can afford roughly a half day per proposal, twice a year, plus an hour of preparation, and our output is a written recommendation to the full council, which votes; the council, not we, decides. That output has to be readable by the members who lose, in the sense that a defeated faction can see their objection was engaged with rather than outvoted by a body claiming superior judgment. So please give me an ordered sequence of questions with explicit sufficiency conditions at each step: what answer lets us move on, what answer stops the proposal outright, what answer converts it into a narrower proposal we should send back, and which questions are empirical — where we could in principle settle them with data we already hold, such as twenty years of ballot records, member surveys, certification demographics, and incident reports — as opposed to questions of value that we must hand to the council rather than resolve ourselves. A colleague who reads more philosophy than I do pointed me at the Condorcet jury theorem and then immediately at three deflationary remarks: that deliberation destroys the independence the theorem needs, that a body selected for credentials will be demographically skewed in ways that give no reason to expect better decisions, and that whoever writes the qualifying examination is doing the real deciding while everyone else argues about the ballot. Use whatever in that neighborhood is genuinely usable, name the distinctions you are relying on in plain language rather than by jargon, and tell me which ones you had to bend or drop to make the thing runnable by non-specialists — and flag the steps that are really judgment calls wearing a procedural costume.

Then run the procedure on three live proposals. First, tier-weighted voting: full fellows cast two votes and associate members one, on technical standards ballots only. Second, a standing Technical Review Panel of seven senior fellows holding a once-per-standard veto over any clause it judges technically unsound, exercisable only with a published written reason — I drafted this one myself and expect to be embarrassed by your verdict, so please do not soften it. Third, a proposal from the public-interest chamber to reserve a third of council seats for a sortition-selected panel of plant workers and nearby residents, voting equally on all matters including technical annexes. For each, give the verdict, the specific step where it turns, and what a modified version that survives would look like if one exists. Also say in a sentence or two how the procedure handles the recurring supermajority requests, since those are the ones most likely to arrive next quarter dressed as caution rather than as expertise. Finally, be explicit about the limits: what this procedure cannot settle at all, whether it silently assumes that legitimacy is a matter of getting correct outcomes rather than of treating members as equals, and what will predictably go wrong the first time we use it — including the awkward question of what authority our own unelected committee has to be filtering the membership's votes, and whether the procedure can be turned on itself.
