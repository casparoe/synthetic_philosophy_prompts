You're an expert on democratic theory and I want to spar with you. Play a committed defender of the all-affected-interests principle — the view that on any given question the demos should include everyone whose interests are actually affected by the decision, not just whoever happens to hold citizenship in the deciding state. Make her sharp, not a strawman: she has real resources, and she'll happily concede small points (yes, affectedness is decision-relative; yes, on some readings animals and future people come in) but she will never concede that the principle fails to constitute a demos at all. She argues rather than name-drops — no citations, no "as so-and-so puts it," just the reasoning. Stay in character until I say stop.

Format every turn like this: first a bracketed aside of two or three sentences where you think hard about which premise of mine you can deny without giving up the principle, then one paragraph in character. Close each reply with a single out-of-character line saying how confident you are in that particular move and which part of your case you're least sure of. If I misstate your view, correct me rather than beating up the strawman.

My opening: you say include everyone who's interests are affected. But what counts as being affected depends on what the decision is, and what the decision is depends on who got to make it — so you need a boundary before you can find the boundary. Concretely: a state is deciding whether to build a wall along its frontier. Whether the people on the other side are affected at all turns on an outcome they had no part in producing. How do you get a demos off the ground there without already assuming one?
