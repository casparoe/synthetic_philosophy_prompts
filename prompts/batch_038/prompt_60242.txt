I am a novelist, and personal identity reaches me not as a puzzle but as a drafting decision: whether the woman on the last page is the woman on the first, and what I must put on the page to make a reader feel that she is, or is not. My verdicts about my own characters have stayed consistent across four books, which makes me suspect a principle is hiding in them. A pianist who keeps every memory of her life but loses, after an illness, all her tastes, tempers and attachments: I write her as someone else, and readers agree that a death has occurred. A man who loses twenty years of memory but keeps his hands, his irritable humour and his way of standing at a window: I write him as unchanged and expect the reader to say he is still himself. A woman who feigns a brighter personality for two decades until the feint is seamless: I write the counterfeit as having become the real thing, and the original as having died unremarked. Identical sisters, separated at birth, who converge at fifty on the same opinions, the same failed marriages and the same gesture when lying: uncanny, but I do not want to say they are one person. A long marriage in which both partners alter by imperceptible degrees, so that no single day is a turning point and yet the people who met are gone: I write this as a death without an event. And then the case I cannot fit — a character absorbed in mending a net, for whom in that minute there is no "I" doing the mending; it is the most intensely individual page I have written, and I find myself thinking the self is most present where it is most absent, which seems to undo everything above. I have read enough of the no-self literature to suspect it would refuse my question rather than answer it, and I want to know whether that refusal is one I am entitled to.

Tell me what principle, if any, generates all six verdicts, or which of them it forces me to abandon in order to stay simple. Please answer with exactly five numbered items and nothing else — no introduction, no conclusion, one or two sentences each: items one through three, the clauses of your principle, each naming the case of mine that forces it; item four, the verdict of mine you cannot save and why; item five, a case I have not described, together with the verdict your principle assigns it.
