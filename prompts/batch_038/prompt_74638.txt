I'm TAing a graduate modality seminar and I have to run section next week before the instructor has posted her key for problem set four, so I want to predict her verdict on one question in it. Her answers from the earlier sets — she gives the verdict and usually almost nothing else:

- "Humphrey might have won in '68" — true. A student asked whether that's really about Humphrey or about somebody merely like him, and she said the question misses the point: if the sentence is true it's true of Humphrey, and any account on which it comes out true of someone else instead has changed the subject. Half the class took that one way, half the other.
- "This table might have been made of a slightly different hunk of wood" — true.
- "This table might have been made of a completely different hunk, by a different cabinet maker, two centuries earlier" — false.
- Someone proposed a slide: one percent of the molecules different, true; twenty percent, true; sixty percent, false. She said that's about right and she wasn't handing out a number for where it turns.
- "I might have had different parents" — false. "I might have been a poached egg" — false, and not a hard case: a joke, not a possibility.
- On Goliath and the lump: "Goliath might have survived being squashed" — false as the name is being used, though the same words would have come out true if the name had been introduced for the clay rather than the shape. She was firm this isn't one possibility described two ways; there's a way things might have been and the sentence either manages to pick it out or doesn't.
- Asked whether there are two exactly similar tables in some other world and if so which is ours, she said there's no further fact and there doesn't need to be.
- Asked whether she holds "the view where nothing exists in more than one world," she said that's a slogan, not a view, and told the student to bring her sentences he wants to come out true or false. Asked flat out whether, on her view, anything in another world is literally identical to something here, she said she doesn't answer questions about what's literally in another world, only about what's true.

Question 3 on the new set: its already established from set three that this table could not have been made of a completely different hunk. The question asks whether it could nevertheless have been the case that it could have been made of a completely different hunk — i.e. whether the doubly modal claim can be true when the single one is false.

What's she going to say? I want the candidate accounts that fit all eight of her earlier answers, what each of them predicts for question 3, your best guess at her verdict and why you'd bet on it, and — if question 3 doesn't fully pin down which account she's running — one further case I could put in front of her in section that would. Also tell me which of the eight items is actually load-bearing, because I suspect the students are leaning way too hard on two or three of them.
