Write a dialogue between two epistemologists about hinge commitments in the sense of Wittgenstein's *On Certainty*. The first maintains that hinge propositions are genuinely believed and carry a kind of non-evidential warrant — an entitlement — so that accepting or rejecting one is a rationally assessable matter. The second maintains that hinge commitments are arational: not beliefs at all, but ways of acting that stand outside the space of reasons, and therefore neither justified nor unjustified. Give them a case to argue over: a colleague, previously unremarkable for scepticism, who has quietly stopped relying on her own memory after a season of slips — notes she does not remember writing, a friend's firm recollection contradicting hers — and who now treats every apparent memory as a hypothesis to be corroborated before it may be believed. Each must say what, on their own account, she has done: forfeited an entitlement, migrated to an alternative framework, or simply drawn a well-supported empirical conclusion about her own cognitive apparatus — and must face the possibility that this third description shows the case was never a hinge case to begin with. Both should argue at full strength, with no strawmen and no concessions extracted by caricature, and at least one exchange must turn squarely on whether the two accounts issue in different verdicts about her or merely redescribe one situation in rival vocabularies. End without a tidy resolution, and include no citations, references, or reading lists.
