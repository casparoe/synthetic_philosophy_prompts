I am trying to work out what I actually mean when I say a historical outcome was locked in, and I have been going case by case rather than from theory. My verdicts:

1. **QWERTY.** A typewriter-era arrangement that persists despite better layouts. Locked in — and I take its very persistence as evidence that it is inferior, since an efficient standard would not need lock-in to survive.
2. **Standard railway gauge.** Arbitrary origin, now immovable. Locked in, for the obvious reason that the track is in the ground and the rolling stock is built to it.
3. **Sweden, 1967.** An entire country reversed a nationwide traffic convention in one night. Not locked in at any deep level; the convention was only ever a coordination equilibrium that a determined state could move.
4. **The metric system.** Locked in across continental Europe, where it was imposed; the same system, backed by comparable authority, failed to displace everyday customary units in Britain and the United States. So locked-in seems to be a property of places rather than of standards.
5. **The French Revolutionary ten-day week.** Abolished within about a dozen years, and the seven-day week — which has no astronomical basis whatever — returned. Locked in.
6. **Betamax losing to VHS.** Contingent at the level of the format, but home video was coming regardless. I do not think the world would look much different if the token had been otherwise.
7. **Gasoline beating electric cars around 1900.** Equally contingent at the token level, and yet here I do think the world would look very different — cities, oil, climate. Same shape of case as 6, opposite verdict on whether the contingency mattered.
8. **Base-60 minutes and seconds.** I want to say both locked in and inevitable, since any literate civilization needs a fine-grained time standard, though the particular base is an inheritance.

My working rule is that a convention is locked in just when reversing it would require scrapping complementary capital that no single agent owns. That plainly fails as stated: 3 and 5 look like the same kind of thing — a state decree changing a nationwide convention, one of them by a government that also controlled the calendar and the weights and measures — and I gave opposite verdicts. And 6 and 7 are both "the type was inevitable, the token was not," yet I think the token mattered in only one of them.

Could you give me a principle, or a small set of candidate principles, that accounts for as much of this as possible? For each, I would like every clause tied to the case that forces it, the principle's verdicts on two or three cases I have not listed, and an explicit statement of which of my eight verdicts it cannot save. I would rather have one clean principle that overturns a verdict than an epicycled one that preserves them all — but tell me if you think it runs the other way here, and say which single verdict you would abandon first.
