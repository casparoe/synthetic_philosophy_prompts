I'm a policy analyst and we're drafting a two-page brief for a mid-sized country's finance ministry that's flirting with adopting a wellbeing budget — a think tank has been lobbying them to commit to cracking the global top 20 by 2035. My colleague, a survey methodologist who is very smart and likes to win, handed me an argument for scratching the whole idea, and I've been unable to break it for three days now. I don't want the argument answered, I want it diagnosed. Roughly in his order:

(1) The world happiness ranking orders countries by one thing only: the national average of the Cantril ladder, where each respondent puts their life as a whole on a 0-to-10 scale, pooled over three years of Gallup interviews. The six familiar variables — GDP per capita, social support, healthy life expectancy, freedom, generosity, corruption — aren't in the ranking at all; they get regressed onto it afterwards to explain it.

(2) Which number a person gives depends on the reference group they compare themselves to and on the scale habits their language and culture taught them; what counts as a 7 in Helsinki needn't be what counts as a 7 in Lagos or Bogota.

(3) So equal wellbeing can produce unequal averages, and some part of the gap between the top and the bottom of the table is artifact rather than lives.

(4) And there's no way to catch that from inside the data. A population that has uniformly shifted its use of the scale looks exactly like a population that's genuinely better off — same spread, same shape, different level, and the level is the entire measurement.

(5) Separately, the thing is redundant. The top ten is nearly the same list of rich countries year after year, and the report's own decomposition shows the ladder score tracking income, social support and life expectancy, so the survey adds nothing over statistics we already collect properly.

(6) So the ladder is doubly useless: it doesn't cleanly measure happiness — it asks for a considered judgement about how your life is going, which isn't a report of how it feels, so it can't distinguish a life that feels good from one that merely looks respectable when you step back and describe it — and whatever it does measure is contaminated by artifacts you can't detect.

(7) Therefore no government should make it a target. Once a ladder average is the goal, you optimize the survey answers, and what you end up with is a country that's good at describing itself as fine.

(8) Recommendation: pull the ranking out of the brief and advise against adopting any national happiness index.

I want to keep (2) and I want to keep the judgement-versus-feeling half of (6) — both seem right to me and giving them up would feel like a real loss. I'm least attached to (8). What I can't do is find the step where the reasoning actually breaks, and I've started to suspect it doesn't break anywhere and I should just concede. So: which premise or step do you reject, why did it look fine going in, and what does rejecting it cost? Two things I need handled rather than waved at. First, if your answer is "treat it as a noisy indicator like everything else in policy," deal with (4) head on, because I don't see why noise whose size you can't estimate is any different from uselessness. Second, (4) and (5) both feel true to me and I have a vague sense they're pulling in opposite directions, but I can't turn that into an objection he'd respect, and I can't decide which one to let go of.
