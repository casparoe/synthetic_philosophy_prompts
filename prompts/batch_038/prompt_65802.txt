For thirty-six years I worked in analytical chemistry, the last fifteen at a national measurement institute, where my job was less about running instruments than about specifying what an instrument was supposed to be measuring and how much confidence its number deserved: defining the measurand, constructing an uncertainty budget, organizing round-robin intercomparisons so that different laboratories using different methods would nonetheless report the same quantity, and worrying about the ugly cases in which the bias of a method happens to correlate with the composition of the sample — where the error is not random noise but tracks precisely the thing you are trying to compare. I retired two years ago and have taken up political philosophy, largely self-taught.

I have been reading the debate over what it is that equality is equality *of* — welfare, resources, capabilities, primary goods, access to advantage — and I keep having the uneasy sense that the participants are arguing about a measurement specification that nobody has written down. Each camp produces cases the others handle badly, but no one states in advance what a candidate currency would have to do in order to be admissible at all. I should like that specification.

Fix the purpose as follows: a currency to be used in designing and evaluating actual tax, transfer, and social policy in a large modern state with a competent statistical system, applied over decades, under conditions of persistent reasonable disagreement about what makes a life go well. Adequacy is relative to that purpose; I am not asking after the accounting unit of an ideal theory.

Write the desiderata. For each one, give the phenomenon, case, or consideration that makes it a requirement rather than a preference; state whether it is non-negotiable or is in truth the proprietary prejudice of a single camp; and say what a candidate gives up by satisfying it. Then:

- Which desiderata are in genuine tension, and is any subset jointly unsatisfiable? My suspicion is that requiring the currency to be publicly ascertainable without intrusive inquiry into people's inner states, requiring it to be sensitive to how differently people convert identical holdings into a life, and requiring it to hold people responsible for their own choices cannot all be had together. If that is right, say so plainly — the impossibility interests me more than the list does.
- Which single requirement does the most exclusionary work, in the sense that most candidates die on it?
- Should the list be indexed to the institutional setting — a national tax-and-transfer system, a cross-country comparison, a small community dividing a common fund — or is there one specification that ought to serve all of them? Commit to an answer.

I also have candidate requirements of my own, imported from my old trade, and I want them judged rather than indulged: ascertainability without intrusive access; invariance to who does the ascertaining, which is my round-robin condition; a measurement error that does not correlate with the level of the quantity measured, so that the worst off are not systematically under-recorded by an instrument calibrated on typical cases; stability of the metric across time, so that a score in 1995 and a score in 2045 mean the same thing even as technology alters what people can convert holdings into; and robustness to the behaviour of those who know they are being measured. Tell me which of these survive translation into political philosophy and which are artefacts of a discipline that measures things far simpler than lives.

I want a committed response: take a position on the list, rank what matters, and if you judge that one of the familiar camps cannot satisfy the specification, name it and say why. Do not present me with a survey of options and leave the choosing to me, and do not append a bibliography or reading list — I have the time and the library card. A numbered list or a table is acceptable, provided each item carries its reasoning with it.
