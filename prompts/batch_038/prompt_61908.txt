I cover science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and for the better part of a year I have been reporting on the small machinery of ordinary life: the unwritten rules that govern tipping, queuing, and crossing the street, and what becomes of those rules when people travel between places that run on different ones. The assignment that started it was a state legislature voting to decriminalize jaywalking, and what struck me in the hearing room was how little of the argument was about safety. Almost all of it was about who the rule belongs to, what a violation expresses, and who gets to be excused from it. Since then I have been keeping a private list of my own judgments about when a visitor is bound by a local convention. I would like help finding out whether they hang together.

Here they are, roughly in the order I arrived at them.

1. An American visitor in a Midwestern diner who thinks tipping is odious on principle should still tip. The server has organized their expectations and their household budget around the practice, and the visitor has accepted a meal on terms that included it.
2. That same American leaving a tip at a restaurant in Tokyo has done nothing wrong. It is a blunder, an irritation, possibly a small embarrassment to the staff, but I would resist anyone who called it a moral failing.
3. A visitor at a red light on an empty street in a northern European city at two in the morning, alone and with nothing coming in either direction, may cross. I feel no obligation to the absent.
4. That same visitor at the same empty crossing, holding the hand of a seven-year-old, may not. This verdict is far firmer than the one before it, and I cannot ground the difference in harm, since nobody is endangered in either case.
5. Crossing against the light in Manhattan when a crowd is already crossing is unremarkable and permissible. Standing alone at that curb waiting for the green when everyone else walks feels like a small defection, though I would not go so far as to call it wrong.
6. Joining a loose cluster of people at a street-food stall where nobody could say who arrived next is not queue-jumping. There is no order to violate.
7. Leaving a bag to hold a place in a genuinely ordered queue while I use the restroom, and returning within a few minutes, is fine.
8. Leaving a bag to hold a place in that same queue so that a friend can join twenty minutes later is not fine. I want to stress how alike these two look to me on every dimension I can articulate: same queue, same bag, no complaint from anyone, the same small cost to the people behind, and in both cases the people behind understand exactly what is happening and accept it without protest. My verdicts nonetheless differ, and they differ firmly.
9. Removing one's shoes at a host's door in Japan is obligatory, and the obligation feels weightier to me than the tipping one, even though the cost of refusing is trivial and falls on a single person who has invited me in rather than on a class of strangers who depend on the rule for their income.
10. A visitor driving in a country that keeps to the left is obviously bound by the local rule, but I suspect this case belongs somewhere else entirely: law and safety, not convention in the sense I mean.

Out of these I have been nursing a principle for the column, and I want it attacked rather than flattered. Call it the reliance test: a local convention binds a visitor only when the visitor can reasonably be expected to know the rule or to be told it, and when identifiable people have shaped their plans around general compliance, so that the visitor's departure costs those people something they were counting on. The first clause is there to let the uninformed tourist off the hook. The second is there to explain why tipping in Tokyo is a faux pas while withholding a tip in Ohio is a wrong, and why an empty crossing at two in the morning carries no obligation at all.

The difficulty is that the reliance test looks to fail on at least three of my judgments: the child at the crossing, the shoes at the door, and the friend joining the queue. In none of them has anyone been cost anything they were counting on, and yet I will not surrender the verdict. My own suspicion is that I am running two ideas together. One is reliance. The other is something I have started calling underwriting: the thought that breaking a rule publicly does not merely fail to help sustain it but lends my weight to its erosion, which can be a wrong to the people who need the rule even when my single act changes nothing measurable for any of them. Underwriting as I have stated it, however, threatens to prove far too much. It would seem to make the lone two-in-the-morning crossing wrong as well, and I am confident it is not.

Three requests, then. First, tell me what principle or principles actually generate these ten verdicts. I would rather have two or three precisely formulated candidates, each clause tied to the case that forced it, than one elegant formula that quietly ignores a judgment or two. Second, state plainly which of my verdicts each candidate cannot accommodate, and where a candidate overturns one of them, say whether the principle should be revised or the verdict simply abandoned; I expect judgment 8 to be the casualty, and I want to know whether I ought to mourn it. Third, run the surviving candidates on cases I have not listed and on which I have no settled view: a visitor who speaks none of the language and is told nothing; a woman returning to her hometown after twenty years abroad to find that the local conventions have changed in her absence; a visitor who complies outwardly while making plain to his companions that he considers the practice absurd; a long-haul driver who lives by the conventions of his trade and passes through a dozen jurisdictions in a week; and a community whose convention is itself regarded by outsiders as unjust, for instance a queuing norm that assigns a subordinate position to one group as a matter of settled practice. Finally, tell me whether reliance and underwriting are genuinely two distinct grounds of obligation or whether the second collapses into the first at one remove, and, if they are distinct, which of my verdicts each one is doing the work on.
