ok so I'm TAing a masters seminar on intergenerational justice — half philosophy students, half public-policy people who've never read anything with a footnote in it — and for the last session I want to run a silly-but-serious exercise. Setup: a mock "Court of Future Generations" convening in 2125. The defendant in the dock is us, collectively: everyone who was alive and voting and buying stuff in the 2020s. The charge is grossly underpricing carbon while knowing what that meant downstream. Each defendant gets one closing brief, and the seminar's job is to work out which briefs are real defenses and which are just rhetoric wearing a wig.

Here are the six briefs I've drafted. They're courtroom costumes for arguments people actually make, so please take the philosophy seriously rather than laughing at the framing:

1. **"We Didn't Know"** — emissions are cumulative and the science only gradually firmed up, so blame can only attach from the moment knowledge was genuinely in hand, and most of the tonnage went up before then.
2. **"You'll Be Richer"** — projected growth means people in 2125 are much better off per capita even after the damage, so demanding transfers from the poorer past to the richer future gets the direction of justice backwards.
3. **"No Complainant"** — the non-identity move: the same choices that warmed the planet also determined exactly which people came to exist, so nobody sitting in the gallery is worse off than they'd otherwise have been, and there's no one with standing to sue.
4. **"Time Is Money"** — a positive pure rate of time preference isn't a prejudice against the unborn, it's just the honest price of preferring sooner to later; you can't call arithmetic a moral failing.
5. **"We Paid For The Exit Ramp"** — our emissions bought the wealth and the technology that made the transition affordable at all, so the future's options were financed by the very century it's prosecuting.
6. **"Nobody Elects Us Either"** — the emissions are locked into supply chains, treaties, housing stock and grid infrastructure that no individual voter, firm or legislature controlled, so the wrong exists but has no agent who could have owned it.

What I want from you: rank all six by how well they'd hold up under cross-examination from a very sharp philosopher-judge. Not how persuasive they'd be to a jury, and not how comforting they are — how much genuine exculpation is left once every objection has landed. I want a strict ranking, 1 through 6, no ties, and I want you to actually commit to an ordering even where two are close. Be willing to rate one of these much weaker than people usually assume it is, if that's your honest read.

Please give me just a single JSON object, nothing outside it, with this exact shape:

```
{
  "ranking": [
    {
      "rank": <integer 1-6>,
      "brief": <string, the name exactly as I listed it>,
      "score": <number 0-10, how much real exculpation survives everything>,
      "key_move": <string, ≤20 words: the single philosophical move this brief actually depends on>,
      "worst_question": <string, ≤30 words: the one cross-examination question that does it the most damage>,
      "survives": <boolean: does it still have any force left after that question>
    }
    // ... one object per brief, six total, in rank order
  ],
  "hidden_overlap": [
    {
      "briefs": [<string>, <string>],
      "assumption": <string, ≤25 words: the premise both of them quietly lean on>
    }
    // list any pairs that turn out to rest on the same thing; empty array if none
  ],
  "notes": <string, ≤80 words: which single empirical or normative assumption, if you flipped it, would most reorder your ranking, and why>
}
```

No citations, no bibliography, no "for further reading" — I want your own reasoning only, and the policy students need to be able to read `key_move` and `worst_question` cold without googling anything.
