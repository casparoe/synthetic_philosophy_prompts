Write a dialogue between two people who disagree about what a very large modern firm proves about the socialist calculation debate. One is an operations researcher at a global retailer — several thousand stores, its own warehouses, trucking fleet, and private-label factories — who has just deployed a nightly optimizer that sets shadow prices for warehouse space, truck slots, shelf facings, and promotional inventory and then allocates accordingly; she argues this is exactly the trial-and-error tâtonnement the market-socialist side described, that a planning authority can discover clearing prices without any haggling, and that the knowledge objection was an artifact of an era without telemetry and machine learning. The other is an economist in the Mises–Hayek line who argues the same case proves the opposite: the optimizer's inputs are all bought on real outside markets, and the shadow prices it emits do something importantly different from what market prices do. Both must be at full strength. Give the operations researcher genuine answers to the charge that store-level knowledge — what customers substitute during a stockout, which loading-dock routine actually works, which demand forecast is quietly padded — never reaches the model, and let her press the point that market prices are themselves nothing but a distributed tâtonnement run on worse data. Make the economist explain mechanically why a solved-for shadow price is not a discovery procedure, rather than simply insisting that rational calculation requires prices. Have them argue about how the thing works rather than about who said what, let each concede real ground, and end without a verdict — but leave the reader able to say exactly which claim in the debate the firm's existence supports and which it does not.
