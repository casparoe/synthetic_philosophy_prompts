Our reading group has spent the term on the dogmatism paradox, and the member who convenes it has handed down considered verdicts on a succession of cases we constructed. We have written them all up, and next week she has promised to rule on one further case, which I will describe at the end. What I want from you is a prediction of that ruling together with an account of the principle it presupposes.

Her verdicts so far are these. First: an oncologist knows from a biopsy, run by a laboratory she has excellent reason to trust, that a patient's growth is benign; at a dinner party a guest says he has been reading about the case and is sure the growth is malignant. She ruled that the oncologist is under no obligation to reopen the question, and that nothing epistemically objectionable happens if she lets the remark pass. Second: a departmental coordinator knows a seminar is on Tuesday and receives an email saying it has been moved to Thursday; she already knows that the address it came from was compromised that morning and that anything arriving from it is false. She ruled that the coordinator may delete the message unread, on the ground that the coordinator settled the question of the message's misleadingness by other means before it arrived. Third: a researcher knows a sample is pure, having run it on an instrument she calibrated that morning and knows to be in order, and a colleague insists on a repeat run before anything is written up. She ruled that the demand is unreasonable in principle, since one need not redo what one knows to have been done correctly, while adding that if the repeat costs almost nothing, refusing it is petty and uncollegial rather than epistemically wrong. Fourth: an instructor knows the final examination is on the twelfth and hears that it has been moved. She ruled that if the rumour can be traced to a source she has independent reason to distrust, she may dismiss it, but that if its origin is unknown and it might have come from the dean's office, she must find out before dismissing anything. Fifth, on the general shape of the paradox itself: she ruled that the argument she would reject is the slide from a restricted claim to an unrestricted one, and that a solver may consistently grant that knowledge permits dismissing some particular piece of contrary evidence while refusing the licence to dismiss anything whatever that might turn up against it. Sixth: a manuscript's authenticity rests entirely on the word of a single trusted scholar and a thin provenance; she ruled that its owner may not dismiss an allegation of forgery without examining it, because her grounds are of a kind that the examination itself could destroy. Note that throughout the term she has treated permission to dismiss a piece of evidence and permission to refuse to look at it as one and the same thing, and has never distinguished them.

The case she has promised to rule on is this. A curator knows a ledger in her keeping is genuine: she has handled it under controlled conditions, matched its paper and ink to other dated documents, and traced an unbroken chain of custody back to the counting house that produced it. A journal is about to print a high-resolution scan of one of its pages, supplied by an estate two removes from her own institution. She has not seen the scan, but she knows with certainty that the estate's agent has retouched it to restore a figure that was inked over in the nineteenth century, that the restoration is conjectural and wrong, and that the printed page will therefore misrepresent the ledger. What she cannot do, having been refused access to the file, is say which figure was altered, or in which direction, or by what method. Checking the published image against the original would take her an afternoon, and she has been asked to do so by two colleagues; the journal's editors have been told nothing.

Predict her verdict on whether the curator may dismiss the scan without inspecting it, and set out the candidate principles that account equally well for the six rulings already on the record. At least two of them should diverge on the new case, so say explicitly what each predicts, commit to the prediction you think likeliest, and give your reasons for preferring it over the alternatives rather than simply listing them. Then say what she would have to give up to remain consistent with herself if she ruled the other way, which of the earlier rulings would come under strain first, and whether the new case is one on which she would keep equating refusal to look with dismissal or finally prize them apart. Finally, propose the single further case we should put to her next week that would most cleanly separate the principles you have identified, and say what each of them implies about it.

Please write the whole thing as continuous prose, in the manner of a letter or a considered email: no headings, no bullet points, no bold or italic emphasis, and no tables. I would rather have six or seven well-argued paragraphs than a survey in which nothing is staked.
