Six weeks ago my employer switched on an insider-risk pipeline that I helped build the plumbing for: it ingests badge swipes, VPN session times, commit metadata, expense reports, and the sender-and-receiver graphs of internal chat, and produces a weekly risk score per employee that goes to a small security team. Nobody on my group objected at design review, and when an engineer from another team raised the question at the all-hands, our director of security answered, in effect, that if you have nothing to hide you have nothing to fear, and that the score is only ever looked at when something appears anomalous. I have been chewing on that sentence ever since, partly because I keep meeting it in unrelated arguments — a relative of mine deployed it about cameras on a city high street, and someone in a group chat used it to defend the social credit experiments in China — and it never seems to be asserting the same thing twice. I have no formal training in philosophy, so what I am after is not a verdict on whether our monitoring system is acceptable but a careful dissection of the sentence itself. Could you lay out, as a menu, the distinct precise claims that slogan could be making, ordered roughly from the unobjectionable to the radical, and say for each what it is really asserting and whether it is true or at least defensible? What I care about most are the hidden parameters: whose judgment fixes what counts as something to hide, mine at the time of the act or the observer's model of me assembled later; whether hiding means active concealment or merely non-disclosure, and whether the claim is about a single fact or about an aggregate of individually innocuous facts that becomes diagnostic only in combination; whether fear is standing in for every harm that matters or only for the harm of being sanctioned for wrongdoing; and whether the sentence is a claim about individuals or about populations. As an engineer, the version I cannot make fit is the base-rate problem: a scorer that is 99 percent precise about behaviour occurring in 0.1 percent of a workforce still generates overwhelmingly false positives, so on most readings the slogan looks as though it is quietly assuming the observer is a perfect classifier. Please tell me which readings make that assumption openly, and which survive its removal.

Two further things. First, at the all-hands the sentence was doing something more aggressive than stating a conditional: it seemed to shift the burden onto whoever was worried, implying that concern is itself evidence of guilt. Say whether any reading supports that use, and whether that reading is something one can legitimately say to employees who did not consent to the pipeline and cannot opt out of it. Second, a friend doing a law degree in Berlin tells me that the German constitutional court, in striking down parts of a census law in the early 1980s, grounded a right to something like informational self-determination partly on the thought that people who cannot be sure what the state knows about them will begin limiting their own behaviour even when they have done nothing wrong. If that is broadly right it looks like a direct counterexample to the slogan, and I would like to know which reading of it the argument targets, and whether the German approach, the more harm-based and sectoral American approach, and a scoring regime of the social credit type each presuppose the same version of the sentence or different ones — I suspect the slogan is a straw man in at least one of the three and would rather know which. Please write all of this as continuous prose, in the manner of an email or a letter: no headings, no bullet points or numbered lists, no bold text, and no tables. When you are working through several readings in sequence, introduce them within running sentences rather than setting them apart typographically, so that the whole thing still reads as one piece of writing.
