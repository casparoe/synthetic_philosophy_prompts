I teach a graduate methodology seminar, and every year a student reconstructs the following argument out of Milton Friedman's 1953 essay on the methodology of positive economics. The premises read like careful restatements of what the essay actually claims; the conclusion reads like a proof that empirical economics is impossible. I have never been able to give a diagnosis that satisfies the room. The argument is this.

(1) Positive economics is an objective science whose theories are instruments for generating predictions. A theory is to be judged by the accuracy of the predictions it yields over the class of phenomena it is meant to cover, not by whether its assumptions are descriptively true statements about the world.

(2) Assumptions do their work by abstraction: an assumption picks out a small set of factors as relevant and omits the surrounding welter of circumstances.

(3) The more an assumption omits of what is descriptively present in the situation, the more complete its abstraction; and the more complete the abstraction, the less descriptively accurate the assumption is.

(4) Therefore, among theories that predict equally well, the one with the less descriptively accurate assumptions is the more significant, and a theory's assumptions being descriptively accurate is, other things equal, evidence that it is an inferior theory. This is the claim Friedman is famous for, the one Samuelson christened the F-twist.

(5) Now take any theory T that has just been refuted by some phenomenon it got wrong. Construct T-prime by inserting a new term into T's assumptions — a friction, an expectation parameter, a taste shifter, a wedge — whose value is chosen so that T-prime fits the phenomenon. T-prime's assumptions are descriptively less accurate than T's: they postulate an entity T did not postulate, they say nothing about where that entity comes from or how it behaves independently, and they remain silent about everything T was silent about.

(6) By (3) and (4), T-prime is therefore more significant than T. By (1), the unreality of its new term is no objection, since predictive performance is the only test; and T-prime's predictive record now contains everything T's did, plus the phenomenon that defeated T. So on Friedman's own criteria T-prime is strictly preferable to T.

(7) Hence empirical failure never counts against a theory. Every refutation is an opportunity to trade realism for significance, and the more a theory is falsified, the more significant, by these criteria, it becomes.

(8) A methodology on which no conceivable observation can count against any hypothesis does not deliver an objective, empirically tested science, which is precisely what (1) says positive economics is.

(9) Therefore (1) is false, and assumptions must after all be tested for descriptive truth.

What I want from you is a diagnosis, and I want it delivered in the following order.

First: where does the argument break, if it breaks? Several of the steps look as though they carry a term from one line to the next with its sense quietly altered — accuracy, abstraction, unrealistic, significance, predictive record, the class of phenomena a theory is meant to cover — and I cannot say which of these slides, if any, is doing the real work. Name the line at which the argument first stops being sound, explain why that line looked unobjectionable when you first read it, and show what has to be true for the inference from (4) and (5) to (6) to fail.

Second: take seriously the alternative that the argument is valid and the trouble is that one of its premises is something Friedman should never have asserted, or should have hedged into near-harmlessness. For each of (2), (3), (4), (5), and (6), say in a sentence what it costs the instrumentalist to give that premise up, and identify which of the five is cheapest to sacrifice.

Third: adjudicate whether the argument's Friedmanian premises are fairly attributed. Use the essay's own style of illustration — the expert billiard player who behaves as though he had solved the elaborate equations governing the shot, the foliage positioned as though each tree were trying to equalize the sunlight reaching its leaves while in fact knowing nothing about sunlight — and say whether these examples commit Friedman to (3) and to the unrestricted form of (4), or whether they commit him only to something weaker that (6) cannot exploit. Be explicit about what is doing the limiting work in his cases that is missing from the student's T-prime.

Fourth: state, as precisely as you can manage, the condition on assumption-choice that a surviving version of (4) would have to carry — the discriminating criterion that separates an abstraction which omits what is irrelevant to the predictions at hand from a merely false assumption which omits what matters — and then demonstrate that your criterion actually blocks step (6) rather than restating the objection in more technical vocabulary. If your criterion cannot be applied without first knowing which predictions are true, say so plainly, since that would concede the point of (9).

One further constraint on your answer. If you reject (4), explain why the instrumentalist should not simply fold: if descriptive accuracy of assumptions turns out to be a genuine criterion of theory choice after all, what is left of the essay's central polemic against the demand for realistic assumptions, and is there any distinction between testing assumptions directly and testing them through their implications that survives your diagnosis?
