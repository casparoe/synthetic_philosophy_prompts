For three years I have been writing a novel whose money is made out of the dead. In June I am to lead a ninety-minute session for sixteen working novelists at a residential workshop — peers rather than students, several of them published and impatient, none of them trained in philosophy, all of them mid-book on societies, invented or historical, that sort persons into categories. What I have to get across is that a kind — a currency, a race term, a legal status — can be socially constructed and still be wholly real, coercive, and resistant: that construction is not a matter of anyone's deciding, agreeing, or believing, and that a constructed kind does not become optional the moment its subjects notice that it is one. I am not asking you to explain this to me. I am asking you to plan how I should explain it.

The case I intend to build the session on comes out of my own manuscript, and I want you to tell me whether it can carry the weight or whether it will do the wrong work. In the book, currency consists of fired-clay tokens called days. Each is impressed at a funeral with the name of the dead and the number of days of labor their household stood owing the community; the tokens are handed to the creditors at the graveside and thereafter pass hand to hand as money, nominally redeemable against the named household's descendants for actual days of work. Three centuries on, most of the named households have died out, been absorbed, or fled, and the redemption right is honored in practice for a handful of great houses and nobody else. Everyone still spends the days, because everyone expects the next person to take one. The state has twice mass-struck tokens for plague-dead who never existed. And there are the unfuneraled — those who die with nobody to conduct the rite — whose deaths generate no tokens, who therefore cannot be owed and cannot be creditors, and of whom the local idiom says that they are not money.

Two worries about this example I would rather you address than soothe. The funeral rite gives my tokens an author, a ceremony, and an institution, which may be precisely the feature that teaches the wrong lesson. And the case is so conspicuously about value and redemption that the room may spend ninety minutes discussing money and never arrive at persons. Tell me whether to lead with the fiction and reach the real world afterward, or the reverse, and what is lost either way.

What I need from you is the order in which the pieces must go, with the dependencies argued rather than listed — and an account of which single component, taught one step too early or one step too late, produces which specific failure. Say what my days must have and what they must lack in order to do the job, and whether I should keep, cut, or relocate the mass-struck plague-dead, which is my favorite part and therefore suspect. Map the misconceptions the room will bring or form — I can already predict "constructed means arbitrary," "constructed means not real," and "once the characters know it is a fiction it stops binding them" — and place each at the exact point in the sequence where it must be headed off, together with what I should say at that point. Give me one question for the final ten minutes that a successful novelist could not answer correctly merely by being agreeable, and that separates having grasped the idea from having enjoyed the hour. Finally, tell me what I should decline to explain at this level and announce that I am declining: I would rather be openly partial than vaguely comprehensive.

Do not send me to the literature, and do not give me names and titles to invoke from a whiteboard; I cannot check them, the room cannot check them, and authority borrowed that way purchases compliance rather than understanding. Everything you give me should stand on the reasoning and on the cases. Please write in American English.
