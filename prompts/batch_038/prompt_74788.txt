My friend and I argued for two hours last night about pain asymbolia, and I came away unconvinced but without a case of my own to press. His position is that what makes a pain bad for the person who has it is its aversive grip — its being felt as something one wants gone — and he takes asymbolic patients, who describe their pain yet neither mind it nor try to escape it, as decisive proof. Mine is that the badness lies in the felt quality itself, so that a sensation can be awful for someone even when it has no motivational purchase on her whatsoever.

Please construct the case I could not: one paragraph, in which a subject's pain is genuinely bad for her on my account and not bad for her at all on his, the dissociation running in the opposite direction from asymbolia. Constraints: the gap must arise from something that could actually happen to a patient — a lesion, a drug, a psychiatric condition — not from a stipulated neural switch or a zombie twin; she must be lucid, articulate, and a reliable observer of her own experience; and the scenario must block his three standard replies by construction, so there is no competing desire or belief that makes endurance preferable, no second-order wish that the feeling stop, and no covert aversion betrayed by flinching, autonomic arousal, or avoidance.

Then show your work. State what each account says about her and why it says it, identify the single feature of the case that does the work, say what both verdicts become if that feature is removed, and give his best attack on the case — including whether the case can be repaired or must be abandoned.
