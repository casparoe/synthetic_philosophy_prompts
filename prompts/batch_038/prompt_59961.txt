I am assembling a problem set for a graduate seminar on the foundations of decision theory, and one of my own constructions has turned against me. The argument below was meant only to dramatize how little room expected-utility theory leaves for strategic ignorance. It now appears to show that refusing to know is never rational, which I cannot accept. I would like to know precisely where it fails.

The argument begins from the result usually known as Good's theorem, after I. J. Good.

(1) Suppose an agent faces a fixed decision problem: a set of acts, a partition of states, a utility function assigning a value to each act–state pair, and a prior credence function over the states. Suppose further that some observation, obtainable at no cost, would reveal which cell of a partition of possible observations obtains; that which cell obtains makes no difference to the value of performing any act in any state; and that on learning a cell the agent will conditionalize on it and then choose an act maximizing expected utility relative to her resulting credences. Then the expected utility of the policy of observing first and choosing optimally afterwards — an expectation computed with the agent's own prior — is at least as great as the expected utility of the best act she can choose now, without observing. The inequality is strict unless the act she would choose is the same in every cell of the partition.

(2) So, in any decision problem meeting those conditions, arranging to receive costless evidence cannot be expected to lower the agent's utility, and it raises her expected utility whenever the evidence would alter her choice.

(3) Rationality requires choosing so as to maximize expected utility. By (2), observing is never worse than declining to observe, and is strictly better whenever the observation would have made a difference to what the agent does.

(4) Hence declining costless evidence is never strictly rational. If the evidence would not have changed the agent's act, observing and declining are exactly tied; if it would have, observing is strictly better. There is no third possibility.

(5) Hence any apparently rational refusal to know must dissolve in one of three ways: the evidence was not really costless; or the agent is mistaken about what she would do with it; or she has a preference for ignorance that is itself irrational.

(6) Yet each of the following looks like a rational refusal that dissolves in none of those ways.

(a) A member of a hiring panel requests the applications with names and institutions redacted. Redaction costs nothing, and she holds no false belief about herself: she knows that reading an applicant's pedigree moves her rating in a direction she has already judged irrelevant, and she expects that once she has seen the name she will go on weighting it, whatever she says afterwards. Blinding is the only means available to her of arriving at the evaluation she has most reason to want.

(b) A woman is offered a test that is free, reliable, and conclusive for a late-onset condition that is untreatable, unpreventable, and non-hereditary; nothing she could do with the result would change any outcome for her or for anyone else. She declines it. By (1) this is exactly the tied case, since no act's consequences differ across the cells — and yet she is plainly strictly better off declining, with twenty further years of ordinary expectation rather than twenty years of waiting.

(c) A candidate for a post can learn the employer's salary band at no cost, but only by asking the recruiter, and the offer eventually made to him depends in part on whether he asked. The evidence is free; the situation he would occupy after receiving it is not the situation he occupies now.

(d) A physician with a full clinical load walks home past a street where an elderly man has fallen, out of sight of anyone, and cannot get up. If she learns of him she will not be able to leave him there for the hour it takes an ambulance to arrive; if she does not learn of him, a neighbour will find him within minutes. Nothing distorts her reasoning, and the knowledge itself costs her nothing; what costs her is what the knowledge obliges her to do.

(7) Therefore Good's theorem is false, or expected-utility theory cannot after all accommodate the rationality of refusing to know.

I want to resist (7) while keeping (1), which looks to me like sound mathematics about the maximum of an average and the average of the maxima, and while also keeping my judgment that the agents in (a) and (b) behave rationally. So:

1. Which of (2) through (6) do you reject, and does the failure take the form of a suppressed premise or of a scope shift between steps? Explain why the step you reject looked innocent — several of them read as nothing more than restatements of (1).

2. Three replies are open to the defender of the theorem: that the agents in (6) do not satisfy its antecedent, because their future selves will not in fact maximize on the evidence they receive; that they do not satisfy it, because their utility functions take the observation itself as an argument; and that in cases like (c) there is no single fixed decision problem for the theorem to range over. Assess each, and tell me what is left of the theorem once they are granted — whether it retains any application to a decision an actual agent might face. My worry is that the hypotheses have been made to do all the work, leaving the result as a truth about the empty set.

3. Is the trichotomy in (5) exhaustive? If it is not, identify the omitted category and say whether a framework on which utility is a function of acts and states can admit that category without ceasing to be such a framework.

4. Do (a), (b), and (d) come apart, or is one of them reducible to another? I suspect (d) is doing something different from both, and I cannot say what.

5. Finally, price the repair you recommend. If utility may depend on what an agent believes, does every preference over one's own beliefs license declining evidence — and what then becomes of the distinction between a rational and an irrational refusal to know?
