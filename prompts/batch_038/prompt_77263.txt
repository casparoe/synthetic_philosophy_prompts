I am a junior double-majoring in history and philosophy, currently enrolled in a seminar on Nietzsche's second Untimely Meditation, and I also work twelve hours a week at a county historical society. We make three kinds of decision constantly and have no framework for any of them: whether to mount an exhibit that celebrates a prominent local figure, whether to accept a large donation of ordinary domestic material we lack the staff to catalog, and whether to publish material that reflects badly on the founders whose names are on the building. Our director defaults to honoring everything, and, under pressure, to saying nothing at all.

Please act as a philosopher who knows the monumental, antiquarian, and critical modes of history thoroughly and who also understands the constraints of a small, volunteer-run museum, and think the problem through carefully before answering. Begin with a short brainstorming pass: what each of the three modes would look like if it were written as an institutional decision rule, and what goes wrong when any one of them runs unchecked. Then convert the distinction into a procedure my director could run on any incoming proposal — the questions in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, which answers terminate the procedure with a no, and where the procedure must stop and hand the matter to a board vote because the question is one of values rather than fact.

Build in Nietzsche's notion of a culture's capacity to absorb and metabolize its past as a genuine constraint rather than ornament: tell me what exceeding that capacity would look like observably in an institution like ours, and give me a threshold rule I could defend to a board already suspicious of theory.

Finally, run the procedure on two cases: a proposed exhibit on the town's most successful industrialist, whose fortune was built partly by breaking a strike with hired gunmen, and a four-thousand-piece donation of one farm family's receipts, photographs, and tools. Point out explicitly the step at which the procedure produces an answer the donor or the board will resent, and flag honestly which steps are judgment calls dressed up as procedure.
