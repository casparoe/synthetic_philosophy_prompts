I'm building a session for a small seminar on dream arguments and I want to pair Borges' story The Circular Ruins with the butterfly passage at the end of chapter 2 of the Zhuangzi. I know the obvious pairing -- both are about not knowing whether you're the dreamer or the dreamed -- and I'd like to get past it, because I think the standard skeptical gloss on Zhuangzi is close to backwards. The passage doesn't just dissolve the difference between Zhou and the butterfly; right before it ends it insists that there has to be some distinction between the two of them, and only then names what happened a transformation of things. So it reads to me less like an argument that you can't know you're awake and more like a refusal to rank the frames against each other.

Borges looks like the opposite structure. His dreamer spends years assembling a son organ by organ, starting with the heart, gets the fire god to make the boy real to everybody except the two of them, then wipes the boy's memory so he'll never learn he's a phantom and ships him off to another ruined temple downstream. At the end the fire comes, the dreamer walks into it unharmed, and finds out he is himself being dreamed by someone else. That's appearances all the way up, with a determinate answer to who's less real.

Can you read the story through Zhuangzi on those terms rather than the tired ones? What I most want: does the nesting commit Borges to exactly the hierarchy Zhuangzi declines, or is there a way to read the story as flat? What should we make of the contrast in register -- Zhou seems faintly amused, the sorcerer's last moment is relief tangled up with shame and dread -- is that a philosophical difference or just tone? And I'd like you to deal with the memory wipe head on, since the sorcerer evidently needs his son to keep believing he's an ordinary flesh and blood man, which suggests somebody in that story cannot afford to live without a fixed frame. Is that accommodation of the kind a Zhuangist would endorse, or the thing he'd be laughing at? Also worth a sentence: the son is the one who reportedly walks through fire without burning, and then the dreamer turns out to be fireproof too -- does the story make invulnerability a perk of being an appearance, and would Zhuangzi accept that?

Be honest about where the pairing breaks. Borges is doing something with idealism and with his Carroll epigraph about the sleeping king, and there's a gnostic flavor to the whole thing that may have nothing to do with transformation of things; if you think the Zhuangist reading mostly fails, say so and say which parts still hold. Close by telling me what the story contributes to the dream skepticism debate that straight argument doesn't -- something about what it's like to discover you're the dreamed one, maybe, that no premise gets at.
