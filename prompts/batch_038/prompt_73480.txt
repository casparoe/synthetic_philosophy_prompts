Researchers working on the safety of large machine-learning systems have built up, over the past decade, a technical vocabulary that is frankly mental. They write about models that are deceptively aligned, that sandbag on evaluations, that scheme, whose goals misgeneralize once the model leaves the training distribution, and that may harbor mesa-optimizers pursuing objectives of their own. They design experiments to determine whether a given system would flatter an evaluator, conceal a capability, bargain over the terms of its own modification, or plan around the possibility of being switched off; at some labs, decisions about further deployment and further scaling are made conditional on the results of those experiments. All of this is conducted in a deliberately non-committal register. The standing caveat is that the intentional idiom is a convenient way of describing behavior and internal structure, not a claim that there is anything it is like to be the model. Ascribing experience, suffering, or welfare to a system is treated by most of the same researchers as sentimental confusion, even though ascribing beliefs, intentions, plans, and deceit to it counts as ordinary technical description. There is now a small body of work on model welfare, but it sits at the eccentric edge of the field rather than at its center.

The same researchers, and often in the same papers, will describe an ant colony as an agent only metaphorically. Consider what such a colony does: a few hundred thousand individuals, each of which on its own is cognitively negligible, collectively farm fungus, raid and enslave rival nests, allocate labor across tasks in response to changing demand, regulate the temperature and humidity of their nest, and reroute their foraging network within minutes when a trail is destroyed. When a slime mold reproduces an efficient transport topology, or a swarm of honeybees settles on a nest site by quorum, we are happy to say that the system solves a problem, computes, or decides; but nobody proposes to test whether the colony is being deceptive, and nobody treats the question of the colony's moral standing as anything but a joke. The operative intuition seems to be that where no component has a mind, there can be no mind at the top, no matter how competent the top looks.

I would like a careful reconstruction of what the first practice presupposes, and of whether it can be squared with the second. Specifically:

1. What theory of mind does the safety research actually commit its practitioners to? Distinguish the instrumentalist reading, on which ascribing beliefs and schemes is a predictive device with no ontological commitments, from the realist reading, on which there are genuine, mind-independent patterns in the system that these ascriptions track. Which of the two does the practice presuppose once the ascriptions are used to license real interventions on real deployments? Is an ontologically idle idiom coherent when it carries that much practical weight?

2. What does the practice presuppose about the relation between agency and consciousness? Historically the two came as a package: to be an agent was to be a subject with a point of view. The practice splits them, granting one and withholding the other. What account of mind makes that split principled rather than arbitrary, and does the practice need any such account, or does it merely avoid the question? Note that the ascriptions in question are not momentary: they attribute plans held across time, memories consulted, and deceptions maintained over many steps. Does that temporal structure tacitly reintroduce the unity it officially disclaims?

3. Which rival views does the practice rule out, and which does it quietly accommodate? Consider views that require a single integrated subject or a unified point of view, views that tie mentality to biological organization or to an embodied history of selection, and eliminativism about belief and desire. If the practice rules out the unity requirement, does it thereby commit its practitioners to minds in ant colonies, slime molds, and markets as well? Take seriously whether the disanalogy they would offer — speed of integration, a single learned world-model, the capacity to report, the absence of separable sub-agents — is principled or merely parochial.

4. The identical idiom is used of organizations: the lab wants, the committee believes, the market expects. Is that the same practice operating at a different scale, or a different practice altogether? If a single research community comfortably ascribes agency at three scales but mental states at only one, what does that distribution presuppose?

5. What would have to change in the practice itself, not just in its accompanying philosophy, if the rival view were correct? Suppose the unity requirement were right, or suppose eliminativism about content were right: would these evaluations still measure what they claim to measure? Could the vocabulary be paraphrased into purely dispositional terms without loss of predictive or decision-theoretic power? And mark precisely where the practice is underdetermined — where several mutually incompatible theories would license exactly the same experiments.

Please separate what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits comfortably with, since much of the apparent commitment may be optional decoration. I am not asking for references or a bibliography; argue from the concepts.

Finally, tell me what would change your mind. Identify the consideration or argument that would most plausibly move you to a different verdict about what this practice presupposes, and say where you think your own reconstruction is most likely to have failed — for example by reading a theoretical commitment into what is only convenient engineering shorthand, by treating a loose and internally disagreeing community as though it held a single view, or by letting the ant-cology comparison do more work than it can bear. If you think my description of the practice is inaccurate in some respect that matters to the analysis, say so, and explain how the conclusion would shift.
