A major home-improvement review site is adding a proprietary "Kindness Score" to its bug-control roundups this year. Write the flagship entry, 600–700 words, for the new category "the most humane way to kill a house fly," in the site's usual voice and structure: how we tested, top pick, budget pick, upgrade pick, also tested, and a short closing section on the cup-and-index-card release.

The humor should come entirely from the site taking the humane question seriously while having no means of answering it. The Kindness Score is computed only from what a phone camera can capture — seconds to immobility, visible convulsions, noise, ease of cleanup — and the entry must at some point concede, in the flat register of a spec sheet, that these measures cannot distinguish a rapid death from a slow one, or a reflex from an experience. Let ordinary product-testing vocabulary (ergonomics, throughput, a satisfying finish, value per use) run alongside the real substance, handled correctly: knockdown is not death; immobility is not absence of sensation; whether insects feel anything rather than merely react is genuinely unresolved; freezing and carbon dioxide are contested; and any per-fly verdict is multiplied by an enormous number of flies.

Keep it deadpan throughout — no winking, no stated moral in the last line, no named real people, brands, or studies. It should read as competent consumer advice to a homeowner, and as satire to an entomologist and a moral philosopher, without either of them feeling lectured.
