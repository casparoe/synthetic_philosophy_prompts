I work in compliance at a mid-sized asset manager, and I've been asked to build the firm's annual training deck on information barriers. Trying to explain the rules to portfolio managers has left me with a puzzle I can't state cleanly, and I'd like help reconstructing what the practice is actually committed to. Two things sit side by side. On one side, an officer, director, or anyone tipped by the company is forbidden to trade on material nonpublic information; the standard formulation is that such a person must either disclose the information or abstain from trading, and the wrong is usually described as running to the shareholders on the other side of the trade. On the other side, my firm's analysts are actively encouraged to do the reverse: visit the factories, buy satellite imagery of the loading docks and the retail parking lots, interview suppliers and former employees, run channel checks, and trade hard on whatever they assemble, provided none of it crosses into material nonpublic information coming from the issuer or its agents. The mosaic idea says that a conclusion built out of individually innocuous public pieces plus real legwork belongs to the analyst, even when the conclusion she reaches is the very conclusion the insider holds. So a manager who hears from a supplier's finance director that orders have collapsed must stand aside until the news is public, while a manager who infers the same fact from truck counts may buy this morning. Identical belief, identical trade, opposite status; the difference seems to lie entirely in provenance and obligation, not in the epistemic quality of the belief or in the position of the uninformed counterparty.

That sits badly with the justification the rules are usually given, which appeals to a level playing field, to investor confidence, and to the integrity of prices. If informational equality among traders were really the goal, the mosaic exemption would be the scandal rather than the safe harbor: the diligent analyst is as well informed as the tipped executive, and the retail investor on the other side of her order is just as badly placed. So the practice appears to be enforcing something else while describing itself in egalitarian terms. Meanwhile I've been reading the theoretical literature and keep running into a result I cannot reconcile with any of it: if traders are Bayesian, share a common prior, and are commonly known to be rational, speculative trade on private information should be impossible, since the willingness of someone to take the other side of my order is itself news about what she knows, and I should withdraw. On that picture, profiting from what you privately know is not something rational agents can do, and a legal edifice organized around who knew what, and how they came to know it, would be policing a phenomenon that could never arise. The deck has to gloss all of this in about forty slides, and I don't want to hand the portfolio managers a story I can't defend.

So: what epistemology of markets does this body of practice presuppose — the disclose-or-abstain rule, the mosaic exemption, and the level-playing-field rhetoric offered to justify both together? Reconstruct the implicit theory as precisely as you can, spelling out what it must assume about information (whether it can be owned or misappropriated, whether where a belief comes from bears on its epistemic standing, whether prices are the sort of thing that can be more or less informative) and about traders (whether sustained disagreement among rational people is possible at all, and whether trade is fundamentally an exchange of information or something else entirely, such as the transfer of risk or the meeting of differing needs). Please separate what the practice strictly requires from what it merely happens to fit well: I suspect several rival pictures would license the very same doctrine, and I'd like to know which ones, how they differ, and what fact about markets or about the law would settle which is operative. Tell me whether the mosaic exemption and the level-playing-field rationale can both be true descriptions of one coherent commitment, or whether one of them is doing covert work and should be dropped from the deck. Say whether the no-trade result is a genuine threat to the whole apparatus or is compatible with it; if it is compatible, identify exactly which of the theorem's assumptions the practice denies, and explain why that denial is stable rather than ad hoc. Finally, suppose we replaced the implicit theory with the one the theorem suggests — a market whose volume is hedging and liquidity provision rather than disagreement. Tell me concretely what would have to change in disclosure regulation, in insider-trading enforcement, and in the account courts give of who has been wronged when an insider trades, and whether anything recognizable of the current regime would survive the substitution.
