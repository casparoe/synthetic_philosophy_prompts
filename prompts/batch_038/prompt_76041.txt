I've been turning over a question by myself for a while and I want a straight answer, not a lecture.

A guy online got me onto Quine's idea that moral claims aren't a special breed — that saying something is wrong is just a posit in our overall theory of the world like any other, only sitting further out from what you can see and touch, so there's no bright line between facts and values. That sounds right to me. But my own gut verdicts go weird, and I want to know what they add up to:

1. Torturing a cat for fun is wrong. I'm as sure of that as I am there's a mug on my desk. Someone who denied it wouldn't have a different opinion, they'd be broken.

2. People in 1800 who thought slavery was fine were wrong, not just different. That's progress, not a change in fashion — though I'll admit I'd probably have gone along with it myself.

3. A friend who says eating meat is murder. I disagree, I don't think I could settle it with him, but I don't think he's just reporting a preference either. There's something to get right and neither of us has got it.

4. My grandfather thought women shouldn't hold jobs. Decent man, and I think he was flatly wrong — but I can't name a fact he was missing. He knew the biology and the economics better than I do.

5. Would it be wrong to wipe out an intelligent alien civilisation we could never reach or be reached by, in a cosmos that ends before anyone ever finds out? I say that's not a real question. It's like asking whether the number 7 is polite.

6. A multiverse theory where the other universes can never be observed even in principle. No problem calling that a claim with a truth value that's just badly supported.

5 and 6 are the pair I can't fix: both cut off from anything checkable, and I treat one as nonsense and the other as a bad-but-real hypothesis. Meanwhile 1 sits further from evidence than plenty of settled science and I trust it more.

So is there one principle that gets all six — something in the Quine spirit where it's all degree and no sharp line? Or do my verdicts force a line, and if so where exactly does it go and wich of the six does it kill? Then run your principle on a case I haven't listed.

Start with your bottom line in the first sentence, then the reasoning — no preamble, don't repeat my question back at me. And if my verdicts are just incoherent mush that no principle ought to be trying to save, or I've got Quine backwards, say that plainly instead of humoring me.
