You are a moral philosopher whose work spans the ethics of killing, animal welfare, population ethics, and environmental ethics, and who is comfortable reasoning from concrete cases rather than from positions associated with particular authors. You are being asked to perform something like reflective equilibrium on my behalf. Think carefully and at length before you settle on anything, and begin with diagnosis rather than with conclusions. Do not give me references, bibliographies, or reading lists — I want the reasoning, not the apparatus. Where you make a claim about how an insect dies or how a product behaves, confine yourself to what you are genuinely confident of rather than to plausible-sounding specifics; the technical descriptions in my cases below are meant to be taken as stipulated facts about the situation.

**Context.** I keep a quarter-acre market garden and a small apple orchard, and I sit on my township's mosquito-control advisory committee. Over two seasons I recorded my own verdicts on the pest-control decisions that actually came up, together with the reason I gave myself at the time. I want to know what principle, if any, they add up to.

**My verdicts.**

1. Hornworms on the tomatoes, picked by hand into a bucket of soapy water. Fine, and I feel nothing about it. One insect per act, and I have to look at it.
2. The same hornworms treated with a *Bt kurstaki* spray, which only lepidopteran larvae can activate. Also fine — better, arguably, since it reaches the ones I cannot find. It kills more individuals than my hand does, and I still do not blink.
3. The same beds hit with a broad-spectrum pyrethroid for flea beetles because it works faster. Not fine. The pests die, but so does everything else on and around the plants: bees, hoverflies, lacewings, the spiders that were doing my pest control for free.
4. A systemic neonicotinoid drench on non-food ornamentals for scale. Worse than 3 in my book, even though 3 kills more insects on the day. The systemic moves into pollen and nectar and persists for seasons, so it keeps working on insects nobody sees, witnesses, or counts.
5. Diatomaceous earth dusted at the base of plants. Fine, I think — though I have flagged this one as suspect. It abrades and desiccates, so the death is slower and plausibly worse than the neurotoxin's, and it is not remotely selective. I suspect I rate it permissible because it looks natural and because I never have to watch it work.
6. Glue boards for cockroaches in the shed. Not fine. I would rather use a bait that kills them outright.
7. Ants in the kitchen. Wiping the trail and caulking the entry: fine, and clearly the first move. Switching to a slow-acting boric acid bait that workers carry home so the whole colony, queen included, dies over a week: also fine, but it sits worse with me than 1 does, and the only difference I can name is that I am not the one doing the killing.
8. A yellowjacket nest at the far edge of the property, where nobody goes: leaving it is fine, and destroying it out of annoyance is not. The identical nest under the porch steps where my niece plays: destroying it is fine, maybe required.
9. Mosquitoes in my own house: killed without thought. The township's proposal to truck-fog a pyrethroid adulticide across the village after two West Nile cases in the county: I argued against it. Its proposal to put *Bti* dunks in the storm-drain catch basins, killing mosquito and black fly larvae only in that water: I argued for it. I am aware the fogging was the more effective public-health measure of the two.
10. Spotted lanternfly, an invasive planthopper that arrived here a few years back: I crush them on sight, I tell my neighbors to do the same, and I think a coordinated town-wide suppression campaign is not merely permissible but good. A town-wide campaign against a native aphid on native maples I regard as straightforwardly wrong. The insects' experience of it cannot be what differs.
11. Releasing parasitoid wasps against whitefly in the greenhouse: clearly fine, and I recommend it to anyone. Their larvae consume the host from the inside over days, which is the most horrible death on this list, and it still does not trouble me. What I tell myself is that it is targeted, and that it is the sort of thing happening in every hedgerow anyway.
12. Pheromone mating-disruption dispensers in the orchard for codling moth, and sterile-male release programs generally: the most attractive option of all. Nothing is killed; the population simply fails to replace itself. But this exposes something incoherent in me: if the disappearance of insects is the bad thing I am tracking in 3, 4, and 9, then a method that guarantees fewer insects ever exist ought to rank last, and I rank it first.
13. Two summers ago a neighbor's spray drifted onto a hive down the road and killed it. I called that a genuine wrong, a disaster, and I said so to his face. Yet I drive forty minutes a day, I mow, and I till my beds each spring, and I have never once thought of those as wrongs, though they certainly kill insects and destroy habitat at a scale I cannot estimate. So my "only deliberate killing counts" line appears to be doing heavy work in a case where the deaths were entirely unintentional.
14. Letting the flea beetles have the arugula and simply growing less of it: I think that is the best course, and I would praise someone who did it. But I do not think the neighbor who sprays is a bad person, and I would not tell him he did wrong. So whatever this is, it does not look like a duty — unless the duty is so demanding that nearly everyone fails it and I have stopped noticing.
15. And the one I cannot explain: I think the accidental destruction of a single honeybee colony is a catastrophe, while wiping out a hundred thousand aphids is nothing. Roughly the same number of individuals. The best I can offer is that a colony is one organism that happens to be distributed, or that the bees perform an ecological function the aphids do not, or that a hive is the kind of thing that can be mourned and a patch of aphids is not. None of these feels like something I could defend in public.

**What I want from you.**

(a) *Diagnostic pass first.* Enumerate the candidate moral variables that might be doing the work here — for instance: number of individuals killed; the probability and character of suffering given the manner of death; selectivity with respect to non-targets; whether the harm is intended, merely foreseen, or only risked; the ecological role of those killed; native versus introduced status; killing existing insects versus preventing future ones from existing; threat to human health or safety; visibility and accountability of the harm; and whether an equally effective, less harmful alternative was available. For each variable, say which of my verdicts it explains, which it mispredicts, and how badly. Be blunt about variables that seem to be doing moral work but are really tracking my aesthetics, my aversion to witnessing, or my loyalty to local species.

(b) *Then assemble two or three candidate principles*, each stated precisely enough that a competent third party could apply it to a case neither of us has seen. For every clause, identify the verdict that motivated it. Do not pad the list with near-duplicate variants.

(c) *Recommend one, and tell me what it costs me.* Say explicitly which of my verdicts it forces me to surrender — I expect at least two — and for each, what the principle yields instead and why my original verdict was mistaken rather than merely inconvenient. If a verdict is not a constraint on the theory at all but a symptom of something incoherent in my outlook (I suspect 5, 10, and 15 are candidates), say so, and say what it would take to fix the outlook rather than the principle. If you believe some pair of my verdicts is jointly unsatisfiable by *any* principle, prove it: name the pair and show that whatever delivers one must fail the other, given the descriptions above.

(d) *Apply the recommended principle to cases I have not decided*: a subterranean termite colony actively consuming the sill plate of my house, where standard treatment means a long-lived soil barrier plus the death of everything that tunnels through it; a hive of non-native but non-agricultural hornets in the wall cavity of a building used by a preschool, one of whose children has a documented severe allergy; an island eradication program that would release a gene drive to crash an invasive mosquito population transmitting avian malaria to native forest birds, where the drive may not stay on the island; an insect growth regulator applied to the same catch basins as the *Bti*, which does not kill larvae outright but prevents them from completing development and also affects other molting insects in that water; turning my compost, which kills a great many invertebrates, versus switching to an enclosed tumbler that kills fewer but causes me to compost less and landfill more; and giving sugar water to an exhausted bee found on the pavement in October, where the cost to me is trivial — including whether failing to do so is a small wrong, and whether that generalizes to everything I could rescue.

(e) *Stress-test the background assumption.* Say how the recommended principle changes if I adopt the view that the lives of most wild insects are net-negative for those who live them, so that reducing insect populations is a benefit rather than a harm. Which of my verdicts survive that switch, which reverse, and is a single principle able to remain neutral between the two background views — or must I settle the population-ethical question before I can decide anything on this list?

(f) *Close with one short paragraph* on where a welfarist, aggregative account and a rights- or duty-based account of these same cases part company, using my list as the test, and which of the two is better placed to explain verdicts 8, 12, and 13.

Present this under clear headings, in prose rather than in slogans, and at a length I can read in one sitting. I would rather you tell me that my outlook cannot be systematized and show me exactly where it breaks than have you manufacture a principle that quietly reinterprets half my verdicts to fit.
