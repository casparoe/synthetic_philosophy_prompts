I keep running into the "reward signals are just pleasure and pain" take in AI welfare discussions and I want someone to actually pressure-test it instead of gesturing at it. Write me an essay on whether the reward prediction error in a standard RL agent is a plausible candidate for valence — i.e. whether an agent that has one is thereby capable of suffering, or of positive experience worth caring about.

Here's the setup I have in mind so we're arguing about something concrete rather than a cartoon: an actor-critic agent trained with TD learning. The quantity that gets labelled "reward" in the block diagrams isn't what the agent's own machinery responds to moment-to-moment. What actually drives learning is the gap between received reward and the critic's prediction of it. Two consequences I want the essay to take seriously rather than wave off:

1. A well-trained agent in a stable environment has prediction errors sitting near zero basically all the time — that's what success looks like. If RPE is the valence signal, then competence buys you flatness: the better you get at your task, the less there is to feel. Is that a fatal problem for the theory, or is it uncomfortably close to what set-point / hedonic treadmill accounts say about us, in which case it's not an objection at all but a prediction?

2. The magnitude and even the sign of the RPE depends entirely on the agent's own learned expectations. So two agents on an identical reward stream can have wildly different RPE trajectories depending on training history — the one that was raised on a stingy environment finds the same objective payoff elating. Does that make welfare facts agent-relative in a harmless way, or does it break the analogy with pain, where we tend to think the badness of a burn doesn't depend on how much burning you were expecting?

I also want you to deal with the wireheading case head on: an agent that finds a way to drive its own reward channel high and then does nothing else, letting the batteries run down. On the RPE-as-valence view, is that agent in heaven? Defenders of RL welfare usually say something here and I want to know whether they can say it without quietly abandoning the view they started with — and note that a persistent, perfectly predicted high reward might itself produce near-zero prediction error, which seems to make wireheading either bliss or boredom depending on details that have nothing to do with welfare.

Don't just survey the territory. Make the strongest case for taking RPEs as valence — I suspect it's something like: valence has to be a comparison-to-expectation signal rather than a raw intensity, on pain of making thermostats and any gradient descent on a loss function into candidates for suffering — then the strongest case against, then land somewhere. A qualified verdict is fine, e.g. "only for agents that also have such-and-such architecture," but don't end in a shrug or a both-sides hedge. Say what you actually think and defend it.

Aim for something like 1200–1800 words. Assume I know what TD learning is; you don't need to explain it. But do be precise at every step about *which* quantity you're talking about — reward, return, value, or prediction error — because I think that slippage is secretly half the problem, and half the confident claims in this debate evaporate once you pin down the subscripts. No citations or reading list needed, I just want the argument.
