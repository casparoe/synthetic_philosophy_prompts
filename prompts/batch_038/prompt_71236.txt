Last night I lost three hours to an argument I still cannot settle, and what I want now is a careful audit rather than a summary of the two positions.

My friend, who is a physician, insists that the autobiographical chapter of *Ecce Homo* in which Nietzsche takes up nutrition, climate, and daily routine is best read as a self-experiment — in effect a one-subject trial. The analogy is carrying an enormous amount of weight in his argument. It is what allows him to say that Nietzsche's dietary pronouncements are evidence rather than symptom; that the recurring claim that a philosophy is a kind of confession about its author's body is an inference drawn from longitudinal self-data rather than an aphoristic flourish; and that those of us who read the whole performance as hypochondria have simply failed to notice the protocol.

Could you audit it for me?

- Give me the correspondence, ideally as a table. What in Nietzsche's practice plays the role of the intervention, the baseline or control period, the washout, the outcome measure, the pre-specified hypothesis, the blinded assessor, and the investigator who is prepared to accept a negative result?
- Separate the disanalogies that are fatal to my friend's particular use from those that would afflict any self-experiment and therefore prove nothing against him. Blinding is missing from most one-subject work too; I want to know which failures are specifically load-bearing here.
- Tell me what features of the laboratory the analogy quietly imports — a calibrated instrument, an observer separable from the observed, a fixed endpoint, a willingness to record a null result, a variable that can be held steady — and which of those the text actually exhibits rather than presupposes.
- Weigh the circumstances of composition: the discussion is retrospective, written in a deliberately boastful register at the end of an extraordinarily productive year and only weeks before the collapse in Turin; his reported endpoints are as much evaluative (did I write well, could I affirm) as symptomatic; and his conception of a health that incorporates illness seems to make failure unscoreable. How much does each of these cost the trial reading, and do they cost it the same amount?
- My friend adds that later work on alcohol, meal timing, and the interaction between digestion and mood vindicates the hunches, which he takes as proof that the method was sound. Is a second analogy operating there — prescience as validation of procedure — and does it survive scrutiny?
- Finally, if the trial reading fails, is there a better source domain that still carries what he wants to say: a regimen or rule of life, a craftsperson tuning an instrument, a convalescent's diary, a registering device rather than a manipulating one? Say which of these you would propose, what each licenses, and whether his deeper claim — that Nietzsche's talk of the body should be treated as physiology rather than metaphor — survives the loss of the laboratory framing, or whether the analogy was supplying it the whole time.
