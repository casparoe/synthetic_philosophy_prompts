A former student of mine, now writing a chapter for a trade anthology on the history of ideas, has sent me his gloss on the Meno for comment. It reads: "Plato's Meno contains two puzzles about knowledge. The first, which Socrates raises early in the dialogue, is that inquiry is impossible: you cannot search for what you already know, since there is nothing to look for, and you cannot search for what you do not know, since you would not recognize it even if you stumbled upon it. Plato's answer is that we knew it all along; learning is recollection, which Socrates demonstrates by drawing a geometrical result out of an uneducated slave who has never been taught anything. The second puzzle comes later and matters more: why knowledge should be worth more than mere true belief, given that true belief gets you to Larissa just as reliably as knowledge does. Socrates's answer is that true beliefs are like the statues of Daedalus, which run off unless they are tied down — tethering a belief that happens to be true is what we now call justification, and it is what converts the belief into knowledge, so knowledge is the more valuable state because it is stable, and stability is what makes believing worth anything. Contemporary epistemologists who say that adding justification to a true belief adds no value to it, under the name of the swamping problem, are simply reopening this old question, and their objection comes to nothing, since the whole point of a tether is that it makes the belief more likely to stay true, and reliability is valuable for exactly that reason."

My own field is the philosophy of science; I teach the Meno in my introductory course every year, but I have not kept up with the literature on the value of knowledge, and I am reluctant to mark up his draft on the basis of hunch. My hunch, such as it is, is that the tethering remark is not about justification in the contemporary sense at all, and that his final sentence does something to the swamping argument that the argument will not tolerate. Please work through the passage and tell me what a scholar of the dialogue would accept as a fair simplification for a general audience, what is a genuine distortion, and what turns on the difference — attending in particular to whose puzzle the first one is, to the status of recollection as a solution rather than a substitute for one, to the equation of tethering with justification, and to whether his dismissal of the swamping problem survives once the passage is corrected. I would like to know, at the end, what I can safely tell him to revise and what he may leave standing.
