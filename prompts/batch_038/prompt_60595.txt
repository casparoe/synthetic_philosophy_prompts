I teach a third-year seminar on the history of economic thought — 24 students, one 80-minute session — and I want an in-class simulation that makes the socialist calculation debate felt rather than recited. Split the class into a central planning board and several production units, and arrange the design so that they run into two distinct failures in sequence: first the absence of any common denominator for comparing heterogeneous inputs and long-lived capital equipment against one another, and then, once the board is permitted to set and revise shadow prices by trial and error, the failure of local and tacit knowledge to reach it. The second phase should also make them feel why the trial-and-error reply weakens once the underlying data keep shifting between rounds.

Please give me the roles, the materials (assume paper slips and a whiteboard only), a scoring scheme that requires no real money, a minute-by-minute timeline, and a debrief that maps particular moments in play onto the arguments on both sides, naming the positions only afterwards. Include one phase in which calculation in kind genuinely succeeds, so that the exercise is not rigged. Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how to recover mid-session, and be frank about which feature of the debate the simulation cannot reproduce.

Finally, say which of your design choices you are least confident about: what outcome in play would suggest the activity is demonstrating something other than a calculation problem — mere communication cost, say, or an ill-chosen incentive scheme — and what you would alter in response.
