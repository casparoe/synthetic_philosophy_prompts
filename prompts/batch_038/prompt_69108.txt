I have spent about fifteen years reading rationalist blogs and their comment threads, and one sentence has gradually become a universal conversation-ender there: Schopenhauer's dictum, usually given in something like the form "a man can do what he wills, but he cannot will what he wills" (translations vary; that is simply the version I keep meeting). Einstein, as I understand it, kept the line close and folded it into the same package as his denial of a personal, rewarding-and-punishing God and his talk of a cosmic religious feeling, and it now travels with his authority attached.

It came up last week in a thread about whether a person's behaviour is explained by their character or by their circumstances. One participant invoked it to argue that praise and blame are conceptually confused: if you cannot choose your wants, then resentment and desert have nothing to attach to, and the honest response to bad behaviour is engineering — incentives, institutions, selection. A second participant quoted the identical sentence to the opposite effect: precisely because wanting is something that happens to you under the right causes, you should set about changing what you want — therapy, chosen company, deliberate self-design — and treat your own desires as raw material. Both insisted they were merely saying what the sentence says.

I suspect that at most one of them is entitled to it, and that the sentence has been made to carry several distinct theses, some trivial, some substantive, some simply false. Could you lay out, as a menu I can bring back to that thread, the precise claims it might be making? For each reading I would like: which sense of "will" is in play (a first-order desire, a choice or decision, or a second-order want about wants); which sense of "cannot" is in play (causal impossibility, absence of voluntary control over one's own willing, empirical difficulty, or mere lack of opportunity); whose willing and at what time; whether the reading presupposes determinism or would survive an indeterministic world; and a verdict — platitude nobody disputes, substantive and defensible, substantive and doubtful, or false.

Then please address a few specific points. First, the sentence is a hybrid: the first half reads like a Hobbesian, thoroughly compatibilist claim about freedom of action, while the second half reads incompatibilist — is that mixture a defect, or the whole point? Second, does any reading force a regress (if I cannot will what I will, can I will what I will about what I will, and where does it stop), and does stopping it require an unmoved mover of desires? Third, does any reading actually support the first participant's conclusion that praise and blame are conceptually confused, as opposed to the quite different claim that blame remains one of the motives operating through a person's character and is therefore still worth deploying? Fourth, is there a reading a libertarian about free will could happily sign, which would suggest the sentence is not an argument for determinism at all? Fifth, is there a reading that leaves room for the combination Einstein seems to have held — thoroughgoing determinism plus strenuous ethical and political effort, with no passivity — since the slogan as forum users wield it sounds like an invitation to shrug? And finally, which reading does each of my two participants need, and is there any single reading that could serve both, or must one of them be misusing the line?

Two housekeeping notes. I do not want citations, page references, or a bibliography: I cannot check them, and I would rather have the analysis. And I am not asking you to settle the free will problem — only to say what this particular sentence is doing when someone uses it to end an argument.
