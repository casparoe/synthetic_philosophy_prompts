I'm a master's student in philosophy and this year I'm the teaching assistant for our third-year philosophy of mind course, which has just finished three weeks on the philosophy of pain. The convenor has asked me to set one practice essay question for the mock paper, together with a model answer and a marking scheme, so the students can calibrate their expectations before the real exam.

I want the question to be about pain asymbolia, and I want it to be unanswerable by reciting that pain has sensory-discriminative and affective-motivational components. It should force students to take a position on what, if anything, asymbolia shows about why pains are bad: are asymbolics missing painfulness itself, or only the capacity to care about their painfulness, or is the whole dissociation argument unsafe, given that asymbolics are reported to be indifferent to visual and verbal threats as well as to noxious stimulation, and given that the presentation may not be a single syndrome at all? Please also build a practical consequence into the question — if an asymbolic's pain is not bad for them, would inducing asymbolia be as good a treatment as inducing analgesia? — since I expect that is where students will reveal whether they have kept "feels bad" and "is bad" properly apart.

Deliverables: the question itself, sized for one hour and worth 25 marks; a model answer of about 800 words; and a marking scheme listing five must-hit moves, the marks each is worth (total 25), and a one-line note on how students typically go wrong at each point, with a clear statement of what separates a first-class answer from a good one. Describe the competing positions substantively rather than by author. Begin with the exam question, followed by the model answer and then the marking scheme, with no preface, no commentary on the task, and no restatement of what I have asked for.
