I teach a third-year philosophy of mind course and I'm writing the final. I want one essay question on the reliability of introspection, worth 25 marks, answerable in 45 minutes of an exam. The trouble is the topic invites pure recitation — everybody has a rehearsed paragraph about confabulation and privileged access and can dump it without thinking. So the question is going to hang off a study I've invented, on purpose: I don't want them looking it up, and I want to control exactly what the data licenses.

The study. Participants sip five different drinks, one at a time, and after each sip write a short free-text description of how it tasted. Two minutes later, on half the trials, the experimenter silently swaps the description for one written by a different participant about a different drink and hands it back. In one block participants are then asked whether the description in front of them is the one they wrote, and how confident they are in that judgement. In a seperate block, equally delayed, they're instead asked to identify which of the five drinks they had just sipped and give a confidence rating. Results: swap-detection comes out at 61% (chance 50%) with confidence uncorrelated with accuracy, r = .03; drink identification comes out at 72% with confidence tracking accuracy, r = .51. Nobody reports finding the procedure strange.

Write the exam question itself, a model answer of about 700 words, and a marking scheme. On the question: it should include the study description, tell students outright that naming philosophers or real studies earns them nothing, and steer them onto two things — what the two blocks actually differ in, given that both are delayed, and why the confidence result rather than the accuracy result is the philosophically load-bearing one. Don't signal the answer in the wording.

On the marking scheme: five or six must-hit moves with marks apportioned across them totalling 25, a description of what a solid-but-B answer looks like and which specific move it's missing, and the two or three errors that should cost the most. I want the split between A and B to be about a claim or distinction, not about how much detail they pile on.

One more thing. A colleague thinks the whole study is a memory experiment wearing an introspection costume — that it's really testing source monitoring, so my question belongs in a philosophy of memory exam and I've mislabelled it. Tell me which of us is right, and say what would change your mind: which consideration, if I pushed it, would make you tell me to redesign the question rather than defend the one you've just written. If you think the numbers as I've described them couldn't come out that way, say so and propose the smallest change that makes the study coherent.
