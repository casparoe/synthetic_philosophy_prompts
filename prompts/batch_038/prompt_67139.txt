I am convening the closing session of a semester-long seminar on comparative philosophy of language, and the session has been ruined for two years running by the same unproductive standoff. The topic is the White Horse chapter of the Gongsun Longzi — the dialogue in which an unnamed Advocate defends the thesis that a white horse is not a horse against an unnamed Objector — and the two camps in my seminar have been arguing past one another rather than at one another. What I want from you is the document they should have produced: a joint statement that a sincere member of each camp could sign after a good-faith adversarial collaboration.

Let me fix the camps precisely, since the point is not to caricature either.

**Camp A — the doctrinal readers.** They hold that the Advocate is advancing a substantive and largely coherent semantic thesis about compound names: that 'white horse' and 'horse' differ in semantic value because the compound contributes an additional condition of application that the simple name lacks, so that inferences licensed by treating them as interchangeable — particularly inferences about what satisfies a request — systematically fail. On this reading the Advocate's remark that 'horse' designates the shape while 'white' designates the color is a claim about the contributions of simple names, his conclusion is a claim about names and their values rather than about livestock, and the chapter is a compressed but genuine contribution to a theory of how names sort stuff.

**Camp B — the eristic readers.** They hold that once the negative copula in the thesis is disambiguated — between non-identity of concepts and exclusion from a kind — every argument in the chapter is either valid and uninteresting or invalid and refuted by the Objector's own counter, and that the chapter's force depends on sliding between those readings inside a single inference, most obviously in the argument from what can and cannot be delivered in answer to a request for a horse. On this reading the text is display dialectic, characteristic of the itinerant disputers whom Han archivists retrospectively sorted into a "School of Names" that arguably never existed as a school at all, and the Objector's concessions carry no doctrinal weight.

Ground rules for the drafting. Refer to the speakers only as the Advocate and the Objector. Label the arguments you discuss W1 (the shape-and-color argument), W2 (the Objector's challenge that having a white horse is having a horse), W3 (the argument from what may be delivered in answer to a request), and W4 (the discussion of whether white and horse can be separated from white horse). Paraphrase throughout: do not render any passage into English as though the wording were settled, and do not supply reconstructed Chinese. Note at least once that the received chapter is dislocated and lacunose and that only a fraction of the chapters the Han bibliographers recorded survives, so no paraphrase should be presented as an established reading of the received word order. I want no bibliography, no list of commentators, and no named attributions to scholars living or dead; the exercise is reconstruction, and nothing you cite could be checked in the room.

The deliverable should have five parts.

1. **Agreed claims.** A numbered list of at least twelve propositions both camps can sign, including claims about the text as a text, about what each argument would have to establish if it were sound, and about what the thesis is *not* claiming on either reading. None of these may be so bland that a signatory's position does no work in producing it.

2. **Disagreements table.** Columns: the disagreement stated neutrally; the formulation of it that Camp A would accept as a statement of its own view; the formulation Camp B would accept for its own; and a verdict on whether that particular disagreement is substantive, partly verbal, or wholly verbal.

3. **Cruxes.** For each, the sub-question such that settling it would move at least one camp; a concrete case, argument, or piece of textual evidence both camps accept as bearing on it; and each camp's prediction written down in advance, together with what outcome would count against its own view. I require at least five cruxes, and at least three of them must be engaged by the following shared instrument, which I am stipulating rather than asking you to source.

   *The compound battery.* For each of the following, what does a faithful reconstruction of the Advocate's principle predict, and does that prediction embarrass the reconstruction: 'brown horse' (an intersective control); 'dead horse' and 'blind horse' (privative cases); 'wooden horse', 'seahorse', 'horse chestnut', 'guinea pig' (compounds whose extension is not the intersection of their parts); 'fake diamond', 'alleged thief', 'former senator' (non-subsective modifiers). Camp A must say whether the Advocate's principle is color-blind — if it holds for 'white horse', why not for 'brown horse', and what the cost of that generality is. Camp B must say whether the battery reveals a real theoretical distinction the Advocate was circling even if his argument for it fails, or whether the whole battery is foreign to the chapter's concerns.

   *The steward cases.* A steward is ordered to bring a horse and brings a white one; a second steward is ordered to bring a white horse and brings a yellow one; a third is ordered to bring something that is a horse and is white, and brings a white horse. Both camps must state whether their reading predicts the Advocate accepts or rejects the equivalence of the third order with the second, and what turns on it.

   *The translation crux.* Both camps must record a prediction about whether the thesis, on their own reading, survives translation into a language that marks adjective–noun agreement morphologically, or whether it is a claim about Classical Chinese compounding specifically.

4. **The verbal portion.** State explicitly which part of the dispute you judge to be about words — I suspect the rendering of the negative copula and the question whether "a thesis about names" and "a thesis about kinds" are really different theses will feature — and say why the rest is not.

5. **Annex on epistemic status.** Distinguish, item by item, what is a matter of textual and philological record from what remains genuinely contested among people who know this literature well, and do not present either camp's reading, or any hybrid of them, as the scholarly consensus; the fact that both readings have been defended for two millennia is itself part of the record. In the same annex, mark every place where the joint statement's neutrality was purchased by weakening a claim below the threshold of interest, and every place where your drafting depends on a contested rendering of the chapter's technical vocabulary — 'name', 'stuff', 'designation', the negative copula. Flag those dependencies rather than resolving them silently.

Aim for roughly fifteen hundred words, in formal prose, with the numbered section first and the disagreements table second. Where a formulation is genuinely available to both camps, give it; where you conclude that no non-slanted formulation exists, say so and explain what the absence shows about that disagreement.
