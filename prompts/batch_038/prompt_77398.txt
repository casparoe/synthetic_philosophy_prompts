I serve on the Communications Standards Working Group at a state health and human services agency, and on Thursday we vote on a proposal from the Office of Public Affairs (internally, Directive 24-3). It would govern every document we put in front of the public: benefit determination and termination notices, program fact sheets, consent forms, application instructions, and also our legislative testimony and press materials. It has three parts. First, an automated gate in the document system that blocks publication of anything scoring above an eighth-grade level on a standard readability formula. Second, a standing list of roughly 180 prohibited words and constructions — utilize, facilitate, leverage, "in order to," nominalizations where a verb exists, passive voice above a fixed density — with an exemption process requiring deputy-director sign-off. Third, quarterly audits, with each office's compliance rate reported to the agency director.

The cover memo grounds all three parts in Orwell's six rules from "Politics and the English Language." That appeal is what has split the committee, and I suspect it cuts two ways at once while the authors have used only one of them. Please draft the written opinion I will circulate before the meeting.

Here is the material you need; you should not have to look anything up, and please do not give me references or a reading list. As I understand the rules Orwell lays out, they are: do not use a metaphor or other figure of speech you are used to seeing in print; do not use a long word where a short one will do; cut out any word that can be cut out; prefer the active voice to the passive where you can; do not use a foreign, scientific, or jargon term if you can think of an everyday English equivalent; and break any of the preceding rules rather than say something outright barbarous. That last rule is the one the cover memo omits. Orwell also holds that the decline of language has political and economic causes rather than merely bad individual writers, that the effect then feeds back into the cause — his analogy is a man who drinks because he feels a failure and then fails more completely because he drinks — and that "The great enemy of clear language is insincerity," so that a writer whose real aims differ from his declared ones reaches instinctively for long words and worn-out idioms. But Orwell equally insists the process is reversible, that bad habits spread by imitation and can be dropped by anyone willing to take the trouble, and that dropping them makes clearer thinking possible. So he is not obviously an opponent of institutional discipline about style, and the memo's authors know it.

Two sentences from our own files should be tested in the opinion, because I think they decide the question. Sentence A, from last year's annual report: "The department continues to engage in ongoing efforts to leverage community-based partnerships in order to facilitate improved access to behavioral health services for eligible individuals." It scores ninth grade and would fail the gate. Sentence B, a rewrite of an after-school program notice that our pilot produced this spring: "Kids can join. Call the number on this paper. There is help for your family." It scores third grade and sails through — and the program it describes was unfunded as of the notice date, the phone line routes to voicemail after five in the evening, and the one fact a parent needs is buried in a linked document that the rewrite dropped. Sentence B is, in the sense Orwell cared about, worse prose than Sentence A.

Also weigh this: the terms-of-art problem. Our eligibility manual and the state administrative code make several phrases load-bearing — adverse action, conditional discharge, medically fragile, household as defined in the manual, notice of action. Rule five bans jargon only where an everyday equivalent exists, and for these it does not; substituting one changes what the recipient can appeal and how. Last fall a plain-language rewrite of a termination notice dropped the sentence stating the appeal deadline and the right to continued benefits pending appeal; complaints went up, not down.

Your opinion should answer, concretely: approve, reject, or approve with modifications, and if the last, which of the three parts survives and in what form — in particular whether the readability gate should block or merely advise, and whether the prohibited-terms list and the compliance audits should stand. Say what the audits should measure instead, if anything, and make that measure specific enough that the Office of Public Affairs could implement it next quarter without a further vote. Give a reasoned verdict on whether Orwell is a usable authority for this proposal at all, and whether the cover memo's citation of him should be struck or rewritten. If you recommend phasing, give the phases and the dates; do not tell me to convene a study group, since we must decide Thursday.

Then take the other side seriously. The strongest argument for Directive 24-3, which our tie-breaking member finds compelling, is equitable: unclear notices fall hardest on readers with limited literacy and on readers whose first language is not English, and the entire reason to prefer a rule over individual judgment is that the writers who most need it are the last to notice their own evasions — Orwell's own diagnosis, turned against him. State that case as its authors would, then say what survives of it under your recommendation.

Keep it under about a thousand words, bottom line in the first paragraph, in a register the proposal's authors could answer without feeling ambushed. Write in American English. And be committed: take a definite position and give your reasons rather than laying out the options and leaving me to choose. Where you are genuinely uncertain about a matter of fact — what readability gates do to writing quality in large bureaucracies, for instance — say so in a sentence and explain how that uncertainty bears on what you recommend, instead of hedging the whole document.
