You are a philosopher working at the intersection of social choice theory and political philosophy: you are comfortable writing down small formal models (finite sets of alternatives, preference profiles, conditions on an aggregation procedure), but you are writing for a mixed committee that includes non-specialists. Before drafting anything, think carefully and do some brief brainstorming: sketch in a few sentences three or four genuinely different ways the central question could be framed, then commit to the framing that is both open and tractable, and say in one or two sentences why the others are worse. Only then write the proposal.

My situation. I chair the governance committee of a forty-household housing cooperative, and this year we were finally asked to write the clause the bylaws have gestured at for a decade: the list of "protected matters" — decisions reserved to individual households and not open to a community vote — together with a rule requiring unanimity for everything outside that list. Trying to specify the two at once, I ran into what appears to be a version of Sen's impossibility of a Paretian liberal, with our own cases standing in for the famous book-reading example: a household with a protected matter concerning how it uses its own garden, and the rest of us unanimously preferring the state in which that household does something other than what it has decided to do. The clause we drafted either lets unanimity swallow the protected matter or makes the protected matter swallow every collective decision that touches it.

My half-formed idea, which I want turned into a fundable project. All three standard responses share an assumption I think can be attacked. Sen's own condition gives each person a decisive pairwise preference over two social states that differ only in some feature of their protected sphere. The reply pressed by Gibbard is that rights construed that way can be self-defeating and might rationally be waived or traded. Nozick's reply is that rights are not inputs to a ranking over social states at all, but side constraints that carve up the feasible set. What all three take for granted is that the protected domain is a fixed set of features of social states, settled in advance by the theorist or the drafter. My proposal is to let it be reflexive instead: the right-holder chooses, at the moment of decision, the description under which their own conduct falls, subject only to a publicity constraint requiring that they be willing to have the same description applied to anyone else relevantly situated. Call it descriptional autonomy. My conjecture — which I do not yet know how to state precisely, and which may be false — is that this escapes the impossibility for a wide class of profiles, but that the publicity constraint either reintroduces the paradox one level up or collapses the notion into triviality (anything describable as private is private, so unanimity does no work) or vacuity (nothing is).

Please write a two-page proposal for a one-year dissertation fellowship that contains:

1. A precise statement of the question, formulated so that it has a determinate answer rather than a mood.
2. An account of why the existing positions leave it open. No strawmen here: give the strongest version of the objection that the standard readings already settle the matter and that I have merely redescribed it.
3. The method — what I would actually do, month by month across twelve months, alongside teaching. Include at least one fully worked small example in the body of the proposal: three households, four or five candidate descriptions, an explicit preference profile, and a demonstration of exactly where the reflexive account and Sen's condition come apart.
4. What success would look like and what failure would look like, including which negative results would still be worth the year, and what specifically I would have to show for the committee to conclude that the idea is wrong rather than merely unfinished.
5. A defense of this method against the obvious alternatives — in particular, why a formal characterization project rather than an interpretive argument about how social states should be individuated, and why not an experimental study of where people actually draw the public/private line.
6. An honest scope assessment: what would have to be cut for the project to fit in twelve months, and where I am most likely to get stuck.
7. A short closing paragraph on the applied deliverable — a clause a real committee could actually vote on — and what would distinguish a usable clause from a philosophical exhibit.
