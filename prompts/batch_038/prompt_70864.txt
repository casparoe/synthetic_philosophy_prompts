I write novels, and my fourth contains a poem that I built to a set of constraints I devised after reading Hofstadter's Le Ton beau de Marot — the book that works its way through many English renderings of one slight Renaissance French poem by Marot in order to ask what survives a translation and what does not. My poem is sixteen lines of four-beat couplets, but it is permitted only two rhyme-sounds in total, alternating throughout; its sixteen rhyme-words, read in order, are the names of sixteen trees; and those same sixteen trees, in that same order, are the chapter headings of the novel, so that the poem doubles as a table of contents. Its last line repeats its first verbatim.

My Italian translator met the predictable collision. The sixteen tree-names in Italian will not yield a two-sound rhyme scheme, so she could preserve the index or the rhyme, but not both. She preserved the index and let the rhymes go, with the result that in Italian the poem reads as loose verse while still matching the chapter headings, which she rendered literally.

At a festival panel last spring, an academic on the platform was asked whether she had made the right call. He answered with traduttore, traditore, and then went considerably further: he said that because betrayal is constitutive of translation rather than an accident of it, there is no fact of the matter as to which loss is the worse, and the question put to her was therefore malformed. I have been turning that exchange over ever since. I am fairly confident something in it is wrong, but I cannot say what.

So: what precise claims could that proverb be making? Please lay them out as a menu rather than selecting one and expounding it — beginning with whatever is close to analytic (that every translation leaves some feature of the original behind), moving through claims about the translator's interpretive choices displacing ones that belonged to the author, about fidelity and aesthetic success trading off systematically, about gains elsewhere failing to compensate a particular loss, and on up to the radical claim that there is no determinate fact about whether a translation is correct at all. For each reading, tell me whether it is trivial, substantive and defensible, or simply false, and give me one line on what would falsify it. Then tell me which reading, if any, would actually license the move he made — the inference from "betrayal is unavoidable" to "no available betrayal is better or worse than another" — and name whatever further premise that inference requires. I would also like to know which reading people most often intend when they deploy the proverb dismissively, as he did, as against when they deploy it consolingly, to a nervous translator.

One further wrinkle, which I suspect is not incidental. The proverb is itself a formally constrained utterance: its bite depends on a rhyme that English cannot reproduce, so anyone rendering it must either lose the rhyme or invent a different pairing. Does that self-application support any of the readings on your list, undercut any of them, or sit beside the point? In particular: if the proverb's own Englishing sacrifices form for the sake of sense — precisely the trade my translator made — can it consistently be invoked to deny that such trades admit of better and worse?

Please write this up as something I could hand to my translator across a dinner table: numbered readings, each carrying its verdict, and your judgment on the panelist's inference at the end.
