Our film club is doing a "philosophy of science at the movies" night next month and I got stuck with the causation slot. I picked Rear Window (the 1954 one with Jimmy Stewart and Grace Kelly) partly because everyone's already seen it, and partly because of something a friend of mine said after we rewatched it — she does philosophy of science and she claimed it's "the only good movie about the difference between Lewis and Woodward," and half the room didn't know what she meant, so I have to be able to explain it before the night or pick a different film. My memory of the plot, correct me if I've got any of it wrong: Jeff has his leg in a cast from a shooting accident, he's stuck in a wheelchair by the window in a heat wave, he has his telephoto lens and his binoculars trained on the courtyard, and over the course of the movie he builds up a case that Thorwald across the way killed his bedridden wife — the night trips, the saw, the trunk, the flower bed dug up and then re-dug, the wife vanishing, the dog strangled down by the fire escape. Lisa and the nurse Stella and the detective Doyle all think he's cooked it up out of boredom until it turns out he was right.

Here's the setup I want to use, and the thing I actually need help with. There are two big accounts of what causation is. On Lewis's counterfactual theory, to say the wife's death was caused by Thorwald is to say something about patterns of counterfactual dependence — roughly, which ways the world would have to differ for the outcome to differ — and the theorist says nothing essential about anyone being able to wiggle the world. On Woodward's interventionist account, causal claims are about what would stay stable under interventions, where an intervention is an imagined change to a variable that comes in from outside the system and switches off every other route by which that variable was being set, so that anything that changes downstream changes only because of it. Two extra bits of machinery I want to keep in play because I think the movie turns on them: modularity (the idea that the equations linking your variables each stay true and separate when you intervene on one of them, which can fail in systems where the parts are entangled), and the distinction between a generalization that survives a wide range of interventions and one that only holds while nobody's touching anything. Jeff, obviously, cannot intervene on anything. He never manipulates a variable once. He just watches. And on Woodward's picture he is therefore not running experiments at all — and yet he ends up with a true causal belief. Is that a case where observation alone really does deliver causal knowledge, or is he just lucky and the film cheats?

So what I want is a real reading of the movie through that debate, not a plot summary with the word "causation" sprinkled on it. In particular:

- Is Jeff's evidence counterfactual in Lewis's sense, or does he only ever have correlations that happen to line up with the truth? Be specific about which stretch of the movie is which, and about what the dog's death does to his evidence, since that's the moment I think everything turns.
- Take the flashbulb climax seriously as an intervention and tell me what it actually is: Jeff's one genuine handle on the world across the courtyard is firing a camera flash at Thorwald in the dark. Is that a Woodward-style intervention on anything at all, or a signal, or something else — and does the fact that he buys information at the cost of Lisa's safety matter for how we score it?
- Lisa, Stella and Doyle function as his hands. Does a borrowed intervention count as your own when you can't perform it, and what does the film seem to think about the answer, given who ends up in danger and who ends up in the courtyard at the end with both legs in casts?
- Tell me where the movie resists all this. Jeff's stuckness is obviously there for other reasons too — voyeurism, marriage, the ethics of looking — and I don't want to flatten the film into an illustration. Say how much of the Lewis/Woodward reading it can bear, where I'd be overreading, and whether Rear Window adds anything to the causation debate that the philosophy papers miss.

I know the club, so end with the bottom line spelled out for them: a one-line verdict saying whether you think the film sides with Lewis, sides with Woodward, or leaves the standoff unresolved, and it has to be the very last line in the form "Verdict: sides with Lewis" / "Verdict: sides with Woodward" / "Verdict: unresolved", nothing after it.
