I cover science and ideas for a weekly magazine, and I am reporting a piece on whether machines can form a concept from a handful of examples — the territory that Bongard problems were built to probe. Rather than lean on the published hundred problems, which every researcher I interviewed has already seen and which several have memorised, I had a designer and I invent six new tasks of the same kind, so that whatever a system does with them it cannot have been trained on. My editors want the sidebar to carry numbers rather than adjectives, so I need structured judgment from you rather than an essay.

Please take the role of a researcher working at the intersection of the philosophy of mind, the cognitive science of concept formation, and machine perception — someone who has thought hard about what these puzzles actually demand of a solver, and who is equally at home with the classical literature on fluid concepts and with contemporary vision systems. Think carefully, and work in the order set out below.

Each task follows the standard format: six pictures that satisfy a hidden rule, six that violate it, with the same vocabulary of shapes and the same general look on both sides, so that no low-level cue gives the game away. Described in words, they are:

1. **Nesting.** Every positive contains two or more closed outlines, at least one of which lies wholly inside another; the outlines vary wildly in shape, size and line weight, and stray dots and dashes appear on both sides. In no negative is any closed outline contained within another, though outlines may cross or touch, and interiors may hold dots.

2. **Crossing.** In every positive, two straight segments cross at a point strictly interior to both. In every negative, two straight segments stop short of one another, leaving a small gap of consistent size — roughly a tenth of their length. Both sides are drawn freehand, with endpoint wobble of markedly smaller magnitude than the gap in the negatives.

3. **A stack of simple shapes.** In every positive, the drawing can be read as two or three complete familiar figures — circles, squares, triangles — layered one over another, with the nearer figure interrupting the outline of the one behind it wherever they overlap; numbers, sizes and placements vary. In every negative, no such reading exists: at least one visible contour closes into no familiar figure unless an ad hoc shape is posited — a closed outline of three straight edges and one inward-bowing arc, a square with a spur reaching into its own interior, two arcs meeting at a point where no occluder could account for the interruption.

4. **Twice the same relation.** Each positive contains two disjoint clusters of small figures, and within each cluster the same arrangement holds — one small figure inside a larger one — although the figures filling those roles differ between the clusters and from picture to picture. Each negative likewise contains two clusters of comparable size and variety, but the arrangement inside one cluster does not match the arrangement inside the other.

5. **The shape of nothing.** Each positive positions between four and seven small marks so that the empty region they bound is a square — some marks at corners, some along edges. Each negative uses a comparable number of comparable marks to bound an empty region that is a non-square rectangle, a rhombus that is not a square, or an irregular quadrilateral. No outline is ever drawn; the concept is a property of the background alone.

6. **Wobbly circles.** Each positive contains a recognisably circular outline drawn freehand, its deviation irregular and in several cases including a shallow dent or flat spot. Each negative contains either a clearly elongated ellipse, whose deviation is smooth and systematic, or an outline with a deep bite taken out of it.

I chose these to pull in different directions. Here are five candidate inductive biases — call them the principles — that my sources invoke, each stated as a claim about what a solver brings to the problem:

A. **Topological invariance.** The hypothesis space consists of properties that survive continuous deformation: enclosure, connectedness, inside/outside, number of holes. Metric detail is noise by construction.

B. **Generative parsing.** Hypotheses are stated over reconstructions of how a scene might have been assembled from simple parts, some possibly occluding others; the preferred hypothesis is the one with the shortest assembly story.

C. **Relational transfer.** Hypotheses may quantify over roles rather than over objects, so that a pattern of relations is recognised regardless of what fills the roles.

D. **Calibrated tolerance.** Small metric deviations are assimilated to the exact configuration they approximate, and accidental coincidences are discounted; the size of "small" is inferred from the whole set rather than read off any single picture.

E. **Fluid re-description.** The solver holds a library of mid-level descriptions and is licensed to trade one for another mid-search, at a cost; difficulty scales with the number of trades needed to reach the description in which the rule is actually stated.

Three deliverables, in this order:

1. Begin with a short, explicitly labelled brainstorming pass — no more than a paragraph — noting for each principle what it would have to explain in order to earn credit, and where you expect it to run out.

2. A task-by-task table. For each of the six tasks, name the single principle doing most of the explanatory work and say in one sentence why. Where two principles give conflicting verdicts about the same picture, say which wins and what the loser would have said; where two must compose rather than compete, say that too. I built at least two of these conflicts in deliberately, and I would much rather you caught them than that I pointed at them.

3. An allocation of explanatory credit across A through E for human success on this set: five numbers summing to 1.00, given to two decimal places, each with a sentence of justification. This is what my art department will turn into a bar chart, so commit to figures instead of hedging — and resist spreading the credit evenly, since a near-uniform split would tell me nothing.

Two housekeeping requests. My descriptions are verbal substitutes for pictures, which loses something: if any is too underspecified for you to place, say so, name the principle you would need to see to settle it, and tell me what visual detail to add. And no reading list and no citations — I want your own analysis, and I will chase down the literature separately.

Finally, for the kicker line of the sidebar: rank the six tasks from easiest to hardest for a high-capacity vision model that has been trained on a very large labelled dataset but has never seen these tasks, and tell me in one sentence what the ordering shows.
