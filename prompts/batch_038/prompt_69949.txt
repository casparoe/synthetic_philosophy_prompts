In November, voters in my city of roughly 210,000 approved a charter amendment establishing a standing Citizens' Budget Panel with power to redirect up to $4 million of discretionary capital spending each fiscal year. The amendment is remarkably thin. It provides only that: the panel consists of twenty-one members seated in three cohorts of seven; each cohort serves a single term of eighteen months, with a new cohort seated every six months; members are drawn "by lot from the residents of the city"; no person who currently holds or has held within the previous five years an elected office, a paid city position, or a state lobbying registration may serve; no member may ever be reselected, under any circumstances; and the panel's adopted recommendations take effect unless the council rejects them within ninety days by a two-thirds vote.

Everything the amendment left blank now falls to me. I direct the office of civic engagement, which will run two selection cycles a year in perpetuity. The council's rules committee will consider my operating regulations in January, after which the city attorney must certify any provision touching eligibility or exclusion. My resources are $60,000 per cycle, one part-time analyst who can run descriptive statistics but has no training in survey sampling, eight weeks from the first mailing to the first session, and no power to compel anyone to serve. Quorum is a majority of seated members.

Please write the selection-and-composition procedure my office can adopt as a regulation and run every cycle. I want it as an ordered sequence of steps, each stating its inputs, the decision it makes, and what would count as a sufficient resolution rather than a deferral. Where the procedure should halt and refuse to seat, say so and say why. Where the question is normative rather than empirical and must therefore go to a person, name the person and specify exactly what that person is being asked to decide, so that the choice is visible rather than buried in my office's discretion.

The procedure should confront three structural problems head-on rather than assuming them away. First, the panel's legitimacy rests on its resemblance to the city, but resemblance is purchased from a pool of people who volunteer, and the two come apart; I need a defensible rule for how much divergence from citywide targets my office tolerates, applied against what, and on what basis. Second, the one-term-only rule caps every member's tenure at eighteen months, which means the only people who accumulate knowledge of how this process works across cycles are the unelected staff and any consultants we hire — the rule meant to prevent entrenchment guarantees that the entrenchment, such as it is, will be unelected. State plainly who carries continuity, by what mechanism, and what that costs. Third, because the panel's recommendations survive unless two-thirds of the council votes them down, opponents who cannot reliably command that vote have every incentive to shape the selection procedure itself instead. I therefore want the procedure to specify what must be fixed, published, and unalterable before the city knows which items are on the coming cycle's agenda, and what may legitimately be adjusted afterward — treating the design as something that must be pre-committed to be credible.

Then run the procedure on three cases:

1. A cycle whose agenda includes capital items for school facilities, announced in the same month the district proposes closing an elementary school. Response volume triples, and the respondent pool is roughly two-thirds drawn from two neighborhoods, heavily weighted toward people who have already organized publicly on the closure.
2. A council member requests that a retired city engineer, who is ineligible for elective office and has no lobbying history, be seated on the coming cohort, on the ground that the panel would otherwise make a technical error about drainage.
3. The stratum for residents aged 18 to 29 in rental households returns four respondents against a target of nine, and one of the four served on the first cohort and asks to serve again.

At least one of these should produce an outcome I will find unwelcome, and I would rather be told so directly than handed a compromise that conceals the cost. I also want to know which of my office's likely practices would require an amendment rather than a regulation — for instance whether I may exclude close relatives of current council members, whether I may reserve seats, and whether a former member could be seated in a non-voting procedural capacity without violating the clause against reselection. Where the answer depends on the city attorney, tell me what to ask him.

Finally, include a short closing section on the limits of what you have given me: which steps will misfire, which look mechanical but are in fact value choices wearing a number, and what the procedure cannot settle at all. Do not give me a reading list, citations, or names of studies; put the reasoning inline, and write for a reader who understands the general case for and against selection by lot, and the general case for and against amateur legislatures, but has never had to design one under a budget and a deadline.
