Background: I'm in a reading group with two friends, Ines and Marcus. After last week's session they got into a long argument about grounding, and I honestly couldn't follow the shape of it. Here's roughly how it went, in order.

1. Marcus opened by saying "grounding" doesn't name a relation at all. Look at the examples people give: {a} grounds a; the ball's being scarlet grounds its being red; the arrangement and activity of the particles grounds there being a table; the true disjunct grounds the disjunction; the mental is grounded in the physical. Those are about as unlike each other as jadeite and nephrite are — and "jade" picks out a shopping list, not a mineral kind. So we've taken a useful bit of English, "in virtue of," and talked ourselves into thinking it labels something out there in the world.

2. Ines said fine, maybe it isn't one relation, but a disunified grouping isn't therefore an empty one, and in any case the examples obviously share a job: each is a non-causal explanation on which one thing holds because another does, and what runs between them is irreflexive and asymmetric. So even if grounding is multiply realizable, there's a single *role*, and the role is what does the philosophical work. Fundamentality is just the flip side: "fundamental" means "not grounded by anything else," which lets you say what's basic without listing every basic thing.

3. Marcus said that's exactly his point — if all the work is being done by the role ("in virtue of," asymmetric, non-causal), then the word is redundant. You can state each claim using the specific relation actually at issue — set formation, realization, functional definition, the determinable/determinate tie — and just note that the statement is explanatory. Adding a generic label on top doesn't buy you any new distinctions or any new ways of being wrong. "Thing" doesn't become a theoretical posit just because everything is a thing.

4. Ines said it does buy generality, and that's not nothing. "Cause" ranges over shoves, poisonings, and collisions, and it earns its keep because of that; "grounds" ranges over all these non-causal dependencies. Without the generic term you couldn't state structural principles once and for all — nothing grounds itself, if x grounds y then y doesn't ground x, grounding is well-founded — you'd have to restate each of them separately for every specific relation, and you couldn't even formulate the thesis that reality bottoms out. And she said the disanalogy cuts her way: causation is uncontroversially a productive abstraction even though everyone agrees it's multiply realizable.

5. Marcus said the causation comparison is where it falls apart. Causal claims get cashed out in mechanisms, they support counterfactuals, they show up in experiments. Grounding claims get cashed out in more grounding claims. When he asked what makes it the case that the redness grounds the colour rather than the other way round, she said it's in the nature of the determinable — which is an essence claim, not a further fact about a relation. And he said he can't tell what would count as evidence that a purported grounding claim is *false* rather than merely mis-described, because every candidate counterexample gets re-sorted: "oh, then that wasn't really grounding." Unfalsifiable bookkeeping, he called it.

6. Ines said it isn't unfalsifiable at all, because grounding claims can go wrong in their structure and people disagree in ways that look substantive: which direction the dependence runs between an object and its singleton, between a disjunct and its disjunction, between a determinate and its determinable, and whether a whole is grounded in its parts or the parts in the whole. Real arguments get given on both sides of those. And she said his re-sorting charge applies to causation too — preemption, overdetermination, and omissions all get handled by refining the account, and nobody concludes that causation is unfalsifiable.

7. Marcus's closer: grant her that. But then notice what she's conceded — if the interesting disagreements are always about which *specific* relation runs which way, then "grounding" is at most an umbrella term, and the real metaphysics is about essence, realization, and set formation. And worse, the supposed common core isn't even agreed: people fight about whether grounding is transitive and about whether it has to be well-founded at all, i.e. about whether there are infinite descending chains of grounds. So the role she's appealing to doesn't have a settled profile either, and the word is a convenience at best, never a posit.
