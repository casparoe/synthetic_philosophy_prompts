I'm three years into a political theory PhD and my dissertation is basically about the politics of working time — why shorter-hours campaigns keep winning the argument and loseing the fight. Last month I was at a small workshop and got seated at dinner between two people who'd been circling each other since the morning panel: Marta, who does evaluation of working-time policy, and Devin, who works on the cultural sociology of work. The restaurant was loud, my food went cold, and the two of them went about six rounds without either one noticing anyone else at the table. I've been chewing on it for a month and I genuinely still can't tell who had the better of it.

Here's how it went, roughly. **(1)** Marta opened with: legal working time is the only lever that reliably changes what a life contains, and she had two cases. France cut the statutory week from 39 to 35 hours in two laws at the end of the '90s, mandatory first for bigger firms and a couple years later for smaller ones, and people got hours. Japan put in the first nationwide, legally binding overtime caps, from 2019 for large employers and 2020 for smaller — 45 hours a month and 360 a year as the principle, with special-circumstance ceilings of 720 a year, under 100 in any single month and 80 averaged across a run of months. Talk about changing how work is *valued*, she said, has no mechanism and no version of it that ever tells you what you'd observe if it were wrong. **(2)** Devin said: then look at what was observed. In France a good chunk of the reduction was handed straight back through annualising hours and speeding up the pace, so the same output in fewer hours, and the people who actually gained were disproportionately the already-secure salaried workers whose jobs could be reorganised around the new limit. In Japan the ceiling landed essentially on top of the monthly overtime figure the ministry itself uses when certifying that a death was work-related — the law's worst-case allowance starts where the official health risk begins — and firms absorbed it by getting hours off the books. Both, he said, are what you get when the prestige attached to long hours is left untouched: the rule gets reshaped around it. **(3)** Marta answered that annualisation was a bargaining choice, not a law of nature, so that's an implementation failure; that off-the-books hours is an enforcement problem and Japan attached criminal penalties, so he was dressing up weak inspectors as a crisis of the soul; and that his account fits any outcome at all — reform fails, culture absorbed it, reform works, culture finally moved — which is not a theory.

**(4)** Devin said turn it around: when hours fall and people end up better off you book it as evidence that hours are what matter, and when hours fall and nothing changes you say the rule was badly drawn or badly policed, so yours can't lose either. But he offered a version that could: if the valuation of work is the binding constraint, a uniform statutory cut that leaves the reward structure alone — the overtime premium, promotion keyed to visible presence, the expectation of immediate responsiveness — should produce intensification inside the new cap rather than relief outside it, and should produce it unevenly, falling hardest on work that can't be spread across more people. That's a prediction about the distribution, not about vibes. **(5)** Marta said notice what he'd just done: premium rates, promotion criteria and response norms are all rules somebody can write down, so the cultural story has collapsed into institutional design, and now they're disagreeing about which rules rather than about meaning. And she said the history cuts against him anyway — the eight-hour-day people did not wait for workers to stop treating labour as a calling; they took the hours and the attitudes moved afterwards. **(6)** Devin said that sequence is the concession, not the refutation: if dispositions move after the rules, then a reform that only does the first half will look like a failure precisely while it's starting to work, and that's how you loose the coalition that would have defended it — which is why he keeps insisting the real fight is over what the shorter week is *for*, relief or a different answer to what a person is measured by.

Can you referee this move by move — rate (1) through (6), say who's right at each point and where a move is a dodge even when its conclusion happens to be right? Then three specific things I care about. Does Marta's unfalsifiability charge actually land on Devin, or is it symmetric, and what would a genuinely risk-taking formulation of his position look like — something that could be shown wrong by a case where hours fell and nothing else did? When Devin's list in (4) gets folded into "rules someone can write down" in (5), is Marta scoring a real point or changing the subject — is there any residue left that's cultural in the sense he needs it to be, or has he quietly become an institutionalist? And what are they both missing: I'd guess something about how the hours get measured in the first place (self-reported versus time-use data, and what that does to a claim about hidden overtime), the gap between average and distributional effects, and whether the time that's handed back gets re-colonised rather than becoming leisure — but tell me if I'm wrong about that. Finally, flag which of the empirical claims either of them makes are ones I shouldn't repeat as settled in a chapter introduction; I don't need sources, I'll hunt those down myself, I just want to know where the ground is soft.
