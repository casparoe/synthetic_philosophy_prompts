I teach a second-year philosophy module on the limits of cooperation — 24 students, one 75-minute session, a room with no computers, and nothing but paper slips, a whiteboard, and a hat. Design a live, hand-run evolutionary tournament in which each student embodies a written strategy card that they must follow mechanically, rather than a person exercising judgement, and in which the population is reshuffled between generations in proportion to score.

What I want them to arrive at, without my saying so, is that two quite different questions get conflated: whether a way of behaving can be dislodged once it is common, and whether it can get any foothold at all while it is rare. So build three phases. In the first, the well-mixed population is seeded generously and a reciprocal strategy sweeps the field. In the second, reset the room to near-total defection with one or two reciprocal seats only, and let them watch it die out despite having just seen it win. In the third, keep the same meagre seed but restrict pairing to physical neighbours on a fixed seating grid, and let the cluster spread. The reveal should be that what changed between phase two and phase three was not the strategy and not the payoffs, but the rule for who meets whom.

Use T=5, R=3, P=1, S=0, eight rounds per encounter, three partners per student per generation, reproduction proportional to total score, with vacated seats refilled by copies of the scorers. Tell me where the seeding threshold falls on those numbers, how many of the 24 seats I must give a reciprocal strategy for one run to land just below it and the next just above it, and how much that allocation moves if I shorten the encounter to five rounds. Then add a mutation rule: with probability *p*, a refilled seat receives a slightly altered copy of the strategy that earned it rather than an exact one. Tell me whether mutation genuinely solves the bootstrapping problem or merely conceals it, and what it does to the other half of the result — the stability of the cooperative population once achieved.

One practical constraint overrides elegance: the per-generation bookkeeping must be something the students can complete themselves in under four minutes with pen and paper. If the engine needs more arithmetic than that, simplify it and tell me plainly what you sacrificed.

Please structure your answer in these sections, in this order:

- **Materials and room layout** (~120 words)
- **The engine** (~350 words): strategy cards, pairing procedure, scoring, reproduction
- **Run sheet** (~250 words), timed to the minute across the 75
- **The seeding arithmetic** (~250 words)
- **The mutation question** (~150 words)
- **Debrief** (~300 words): mapping named moments from each of the three phases onto the underlying distinction, with the sequence of questions that gets the class to state it themselves rather than receive it from me
- **What will go wrong** (~200 words): the predictable failure modes the first time this is run, and what to do in the room about each
- **What the activity cannot reproduce** (~150 words), including whatever cost the neighbour-pairing phase quietly imposes on the cooperators that I will have to supply by hand for the point to be honest

No reading list, no citations, no named theorems or tournaments — I know the literature and my students have read none of it. Write in British English.
