I chair a seven-person funding panel and I've spent the last year designing our voting protocol. Next month I'm presenting the design at a small workshop on collective epistemology in Manchester, and there's going to be at least one person in the room who enjoys tearing things apart, so I'd rather get torn apart by you first. Here's the package, roughly as I'd state it publicly:

(a) We decide premise-first. A proposal is fundable iff it clears four questions — is the question significant, can the proposed design actually answer it, can this team execute that design, does it fit the budget. Each question goes to a seperate vote and the verdict is read off the conjunction. I want the group's view built out of the reasons, not out of a headcount on the bottom line.

(b) Not everyone votes on everything. On "can the design answer it", only our two methodologists vote. On "does it fit the budget", only the panel economist votes. Everyone votes on significance and on team capacity. I describe this as weighting by competence rather than by headcount. When the two methodologists split, I've been breaking the tie with a second vote as chair; I havent written that down anywhere, which is slightly embarassing.

(c) The justification I give is basically Condorcet: if members are each better than a coin flip, vote on what they believe rather than what they want, and their errors aren't correlated, then majority voting is likely to be right and gets more likely as the group gets bigger.

(d) No path dependence. What we decide shouldn't turn on the order the questions get put to the room. This is one of my main selling points for premise-first.

(e) Safety valve: if the derived verdict strikes anyone as obviously wrong, any single member can call for a fresh vote on the verdict alone, and that vote stands. I added it after a bad quarter.

(f) Non-dictatorship, and I mean it strongly: no one member should be able to settle any of the four questions on their own, whatever the rest of the panel thinks.

(g) Verdict-first aggregation is unprincipled. It lets a panel be right for no reason, and it throws away the only thing that makes convening a panel worth the trouble.

(h) Last spring the premises came out fundable but five of the seven told me afterwards they'd have voted no on the bottom line. I think the protocol was right and the five were wrong, and I'm willing to say so in the paper.

Is this a coherent package? Tell me which of these genuinely pull against each other and how badly, which pairs only look like they do, and for each real one what has to give and what giving it up costs me — including whether there's some commitment I'm quietly relying on that isn't in the list at all. Rank the conflicts by severity; I specifically do not want a report where everything gets flagged as mildly problematic. And don't take any hint from me about which of these I'd sacrifice, because I honestly don't know yet.

You don't need to cite anything, I know the literature, I just want the audit. Please give it to me as a single JSON object with exactly this shape:

{
  "overall_verdict": one or two sentences on whether the package hangs together,
  "tensions": [
    {
      "items": the letters of the commitments in conflict,
      "kind": "genuine" or "apparent",
      "severity": "high", "medium" or "low" for genuine ones, "none" for apparent ones,
      "why": what exactly does the work in the conflict, or why it dissolves on inspection,
      "what_has_to_give": the minimal revision,
      "cost": what that revision costs me
    }
  ],
  "unstated_commitment": anything I'm relying on that isn't in (a)-(h),
  "cheapest_single_repair": the one change that cleans up the most
}

Order the entries under "tensions" from most severe to least, putting the apparent ones wherever they belong in that ordering rather than lumping them at the end. Write every string value in British English, spellings and idiom both, since the Manchester crowd will be reading it — ignore the fact that I've written this bit in American style.
