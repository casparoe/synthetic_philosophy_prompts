A philosophy department and a robotics lab where I work run a joint seminar. After last Thursday's session, two of the regulars argued for the better part of an hour about Buridan's ass: H., who works on decision-making for autonomous vehicles, and M., who works in the philosophy of action. I audited the exchange without taking a side, and I'm not confident I understood who was ahead at any given point, so I've tried to reconstruct it faithfully below. H. had opened with a modernized version of the case: a search-and-rescue drone receives two simultaneous distress signals from swimmers at equal distance in identical conditions, and has fuel for exactly one recovery.

(1) H.: The drone shows the ass is a fiction. Any physical deliberator has asymmetries on hand — one signal was timestamped microseconds earlier, the wind differs, sensor noise differs — so exact indifference never actually obtains. What gets advertised as a paradox about rationality is really an impossible physics problem, and the example ought to be retired rather than solved.

(2) M.: That answers a question nobody was asking. The ass is a normative thought experiment, not a prediction about donkeys or drones. The issue is what rationality requires when reasons genuinely run out, and observing that reasons rarely run out in fact quietly assumes that when they do, there is nothing to be said — which is precisely the claim in dispute.

(3) H.: Not assumed, but argued. Idealizations earn their place when they are limits of real cases: the frictionless plane works because you can approach zero friction and the results converge. The indifference case behaves differently. As the difference between two options shrinks toward zero, a planner built to require a strict ranking doesn't converge smoothly on taking the marginally better one; its deliberation time blows up. Exact indifference is therefore not the limit of a well-behaved family of cases but a singularity, and a singularity is exactly where a model announces that it has stopped applying.

(4) M.: Grant the singularity, but locate it correctly — it is in the model, not in the choice. A planner whose deliberation time diverges is one that has been built to demand a reason for every selection. Supply a tie-breaking rule — a coin flip, a parity check on the timestamps, a standing instruction to favor the port side — and the divergence vanishes. So the paralysis was never forced by indifference itself; it was forced by an internal requirement that selection be reason-determined. Drop that requirement and the ass eats.

(5) H.: But a coin flip is not choice without preference; it is preference displaced one level up. What the drone prefers is that someone be rescued, either one, and that preference together with a randomizer fully accounts for the behavior. There is always a preference somewhere in the picture — the preference for acting rather than dithering — and the ass's actual failure is to treat a tie among the sub-options as though it defeated an overwhelming preference for acting at all. Read properly, the story never exhibited choice without preference; it exhibited mismanagement of preferences.

(6) M.: That wins the words and concedes the substance. If the top-level preference is rescue-someone-either-one, it does not select between the swimmers; the selection is made by noise. The choice that actually matters — this swimmer rather than that one — is therefore exactly as undetermined by reasons as the paradox claimed. The principle that every choice has a reason survives only in the weakened form that every act has a reason, and that weaker principle is perfectly compatible with arbitrary selection among equally supported options, which was the whole claim.

At that point each insisted on having won. H. said M. had smuggled in the premise that only token-level reasons count as reasons at all; M. said H. had relabeled a randomizer as a preference in order to keep a principle safe.

Please adjudicate the exchange move by move: for each of the six numbered steps, say whether it succeeds against the step before it, and if it does not, what would have to be added to make it succeed. Then say who is right about what, and whether the standoff at (5) and (6) is a substantive disagreement or a verbal one. Finally, identify anything both of them are missing — for instance, whether the "indifference" in the case is indifference between outcomes or between descriptions of outcomes, whether the drone is a fair stand-in for the ass, and whether either party has any business claiming the thought experiment's verdict.
