I'm the note-taker for a monthly strategy meeting at the small policy shop where I work (about fifteen of us, we write memos for foundations and the occasional agency), and last month two colleagues got into an argument that ate the whole hour and never got settled. Our boss had asked for an actual number: what's the probability the post-1945 international order is still recognizably in place in 2055.

Dev went first. He said don't theorize, count. He listed the hegemonic runs since 1500 — Portugal, the Dutch, Britain twice, the US — and got roughly a century at the top on average, with a huge spread. Since we're maybe 80 years into this one, he figured we're near the tail of a typical lifespan and wrote down 25%. He said this is the only honest way to do it, because everyone who starts reasoning about the specifics ends up telling a story in which the thing they're rooting for survives.

Mara said the number is meaningless because the reference class is arbitrary. Draw the boundary at great powers and you get one average, at hegemons holding a reserve currency another, at industrial-era orders a third, and the spread across those choices is bigger then the spread within any of them. She said Dev picked the cut that made his case, and since there's no principled way to choose, 25% isn't a finding, it's a costume for a hunch.

Dev said that proves too much. Almost nothing worth forecasting is a large-n repeatable case. If you can kill a base rate by pointing out the class could have been drawn differently, no outside view is ever available and you're thrown back on the inside view, which is the thing that's broken. And anyway, if several defensible classes give several answers, the right move is to average them or widen the interval — not to go home.

Mara said the deeper problem is that the class is built out of the outcome. We call those states hegemons because they dominated; the ones that didn't aren't on the list, so the denominator was chosen using the very thing being predicted. And every case in the sample has already ended — you're estimating the lifespan of a running order from a pile of corpses, which she claimed systematically understates how long the long-lived ones last. She also said the units aren't exchangeable: one nuclear-armed economy with satellite comms and a global reserve currency isn't a draw from the same process as a 17th-century maritime trading state, and frequency talk only means something when there's a repeatable generator behind it.

Dev said then name what she's using instead, since earlier she'd said orders like this rarely survive losing their anchor currency, which is a base rate too, just with a fuzzier class. His charge: she isn't against base rates, she's against the ones that disagree with her. And if the rule is "the sample is too small to mean anything," she has to say how small, or it collapses into ignore the outside view whenever it's inconvenient.

Mara said size was never her objection, construction was. Her version: a class is usable if its membership criteria were fixed by things independent of the outcome and the members plausibly share a mechanism. Tiny but pre-specified and homogeneous beats big but assembled after the fact. She wouldn't give a number at all.

They asked me to break the tie and I genuinely couldn't. Can you go through it move by move and say who's right at each one, and separately tell me whether the pile-of-corpses point is a real statistical problem or a rhetorical flourish? I'd also like to know whether their last exchange actually settles anything or if they just moved the fight somewhere else.
