I am a second-year student in a cross-listed law-and-history seminar on the origins of international criminal justice, and in the final week each of us gives a fifteen-minute précis of one chapter of our thesis and then sits through an open question period with no slides and no handout. My chapter argues that the retroactivity objection to the first count of the Nuremberg indictment — crimes against peace — is not met by the two answers the literature usually offers. The first points to the Pact of Paris of August 1928 and the interwar material built on it; my claim is that those instruments established the wrongfulness of aggressive war as between states but never supplied the machinery of personal liability — no definition, no penalty, no forum — so what the defendants lacked was not notice of wrongdoing but notice that the wrongdoing would be prosecuted as a crime in their own persons. The second answer is the Tribunal's own, which treats the *nulla poena* maxim less as a limit on sovereign lawmaking than as a principle of justice; I argue that this concedes the legislative character of the Charter and shifts the question from whether the law already existed to whether the victors were entitled to make it, which is a question they are structurally the wrong people to answer about themselves. I add what I think is the more interesting piece: the count that failed to travel is the retroactive one, since aggression went undefined for decades afterwards and only became prosecutable at the ICC in the last decade, whereas crimes against humanity — equally novel in 1945 — was absorbed into Control Council Law No. 10, into national statutes, and eventually into the Rome Statute.

The room is neither hostile nor homogeneous, and I want the prep sheet to reflect that rather than a generic list of things people say about victors' justice. Our convenor is an international lawyer of the codification school who thinks the Charter largely declared existing law, and he will press me on whether I am simply applying a domestic pedigree test to a legal order that never met one. Two of the students work in Hart-influenced legal philosophy; one will want to know whether my complaint is really about the rule of recognition of the post-war order, and whether a system can legislate for itself at all. There is a diplomatic historian who works on the wartime planning process — the London negotiations, the American argument over summary execution versus trial — and he will ask whether I have read the negotiating record rather than the judgment, and whether the retroactivity point does any explanatory work that political necessity does not already do. There is a transitional-justice scholar who will want to know what my position implies for the current court and for the expressive value of the whole enterprise, and who will hear my chapter as tarring a legacy rather than quarrelling with one count. And there is a Tokyo specialist who thinks I am re-fighting the Nuremberg debate while ignoring that the Tokyo tribunal rested on a different jurisdictional foundation — a supreme commander's proclamation rather than an inter-allied treaty — and that a sitting judge dissented there precisely on the ex post facto ground.

Please draft the question-period prep sheet. Give me exactly five numbered items and nothing else: no introduction, no closing summary, no section headers, no reading list or citations, since I know the literature and am asking only about the room. The first four items should be the questions I will actually get, ordered from most damaging to least, each identified by who raises it and phrased in that person's own vocabulary — the historian will talk about the archive and the contingency of the moment, the Hartian about secondary rules and pedigree, the transitional-justice scholar about legitimacy costs and expressive function. For each of those four, give the concession I should make in my first sentence, a reply I could deliver in about a minute, and one sentence naming the place in that reply where I am least secure and should not bluff; keep each item to six sentences at most. The fifth item should be the question I am hoping nobody asks: I suspect it concerns Article 8 of the Charter, since an argument against legislating new crimes after the fact seems to leave me with no resources for defending the removal of the superior-orders defence, but if you can see a worse one, give me that instead, together with a sentence on why I cannot presently answer it and what I would have to give up in order to.
