I chair the programme committee of a mid-sized family foundation. Twice a year, and with increasing frequency, somebody proposes that we widen our remit to take in a class of moral patients we currently ignore — wild animals, people who will not be born for centuries, non-human apes, vanishing languages, and most recently, artificial systems that might one day have interests. These proposals arrive as claims about moral progress: the circle is expanding, and to refuse is to be on the wrong side of it. Refusing does feel like a moral failure, yet accepting feels like letting each season's fashion rewrite our mandate, and every acceptance quietly reduces what we can do for the claimants already inside. The board must ratify anything we recommend, and a remit change redirects roughly a tenth of our annual spending.

Please write a decision procedure my committee can run on one such proposal within a single hour, no member holding any philosophical training. Ground it in what the philosophical literature on moral progress and the expanding circle actually licenses, rather than in its slogans, and give me the questions in the order they should be asked, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, the points at which the procedure returns a no, and the points at which it must stop and hand the matter to a person because the question is one of values rather than fact.

It has to keep apart four changes that campaigners and their critics alike run together: a genuine widening of the class of beings whose interests count; a change in how heavily we weigh beings already inside; a change in vocabulary that leaves our behaviour untouched; and a widening for one group paid for by a narrowing for another. Tell me plainly which of the four a committee can detect from outside and which it cannot. Include a test for whether a proposal is driven by moral argument or by prestige dynamics within a small, tightly networked community whose members all read one another — and say honestly how good that test is, given that the same dynamics produced several expansions we are now glad of. Handle the objection that the claimants likeliest to be excluded are precisely those unable to complain, so that "no one affected objects" cannot serve as a filter. Mark every step that is a judgement call dressed as procedure.

Then run it on three proposals: funding research into the welfare of wild invertebrates; adding the very long-term future as a standing beneficiary category; and adding artificial moral patients. I expect at least one verdict to be one I shall dislike. Finish by telling me which category we already fund would fail the procedure today.
