Act as a senior AI safety researcher who has sat on a hundred program committees and has a reputation for asking the one question that ends talks. Assume you know the technical literature on impact measures and side-effect penalties — relative reach, attainable utility preservation, conservative agency, the gridworld benchmarks, that whole family — but don't cite any of it back at me, I've read it.

Situation: I'm giving a 20 minute talk at an agent-foundations workshop at a big ML venue in about six weeks, and I want to be ready for the question period.

My thesis is the contrarian one. The standard objection to low-impact AI is that you can't penalize side effects without already knowing what's valuable, so 'avoid side effects' just collapses back into full value specification by another route. I think that objection is aimed at the wrong target. My line: an impact measure doesn't need values, it needs a measuring rod for restorability. I define the impact of an action as the degree to which it shrinks the set of world-states that are still cheaply reachable afterwards, where 'cheaply' means the physical and computational work required to get back there. AUP-style measures already gesture at this by using random auxiliary goals as the rod; I argue that rod isn't a smuggled-in utility function, it's a claim about the modal structure of the environment, and that's precisely the sort of thing you'd want. The agent avoids smashing the vase not because vases are good but because reassembling porcelain is expensive; it eats the sandwich without compunction.

The audience: mostly RL people who work on reward hacking and model-based planning, who've never seen the safety-specific literature and will treat my penalty as just another regularizer with a weird name; two or three people who have actually built impact measures and know every toy-environment failure mode cold; and a couple of philosophy-of-science types who turned up becuase the abstract mentioned value specification.
