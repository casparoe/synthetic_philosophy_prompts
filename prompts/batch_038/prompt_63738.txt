I direct a regulatory review office in a city of about 400,000. A 2016 ordinance puts every administrative rule, plus a scheduled list of older ordinances, on a five-year review cycle, and for each item my office must send the council committee one of three recommendations: retain, amend, or repeal. We get roughly seventy items per cycle and have three analysts, so each item receives about three hours of total staff time, and no analyst on my team has training in political philosophy, in policy history, or in anything beyond reading a provision and telephoning the department that administers it. Provenance is patchy: our rulemaking docket begins in 1998, and for anything older the only surviving record is usually a line in the council minutes or the memory of a clerk who has since retired. Repeal is close to irreversible — reinstating a lapsed provision takes a full legislative cycle, and in the interim the activity runs unregulated — whereas retaining a useless provision costs little beyond the burden it imposes. On top of that, a mayoral task force on red tape wants a headline count of repeals from every cycle, and the committee adopts roughly eighty-five percent of what we send it with no substantive discussion.

What I need is a decision procedure an analyst can run on an item without me in the room, and I want it built out of the Burkean and Chestertonian line of thought rather than out of the cost-benefit reflex my reviewers default to. Chesterton, in the passage his fence is named for, has the reformer who announces that he sees no use for a gate across the road told: "Go away and think. Then, when you can come back and tell me that you do see the use of it, I may allow you to destroy it." Burke supplies the companion idea that long-established practice carries knowledge no single generation's reasoning can reconstruct, and that settled dispositions are worth preserving because they spare a community from reasoning everything out from scratch each time. Two features of that material make it hard to use as written, and I want the procedure to confront them rather than decorate itself with them. First, taken literally it never permits the repeal of anything, since almost every rule does something for someone, and "we could not find the reason" is exactly the condition Chesterton says disqualifies you — so the procedure has to convert a demand for understanding into an allocation of burdens, an evidentiary standard, and a stopping rule: who must explain the fence, what counts as having explained it, how much digging is enough before the question is closed as unanswerable, and what happens after it is closed. Second, Chesterton's fence concerns removal only; it is silent on whether a fence whose purpose has been identified and is legitimate should nonetheless come down, and it licenses inertia without limit unless duration enters the calculation in a bounded way. I also want the procedure to keep explicitly the distinction Chesterton is drawing between two reformers — the one who cannot see the purpose and the one who sees it perfectly well and objects to it — because they should be asking different questions from that point onward, and I want a systematic search step for functions nobody states: coordination between two departments, the allocation of authority or liability, a fee that quietly funds something else, a delay that lets another process finish, a paper record that only turns out to matter later in a dispute, the protection of a constituency that does not know it is being protected and is therefore not at the table when repeal is proposed.

Deliver it as numbered steps an analyst can work down, each stating the question, what would count as a sufficient answer, what the default is when no answer can be found inside the time budget, and whether the step terminates the review in a retain, in a repeal-or-amend recommendation, or by handing the item to the committee because what remains is a question of values the procedure has no business settling. Flag every step that is a judgment call wearing a procedural costume. Then run it on three items from the current cycle. (i) A 1987 provision requiring applicants for a residential driveway permit to file two paper copies and a notarized affidavit of ownership; the administering department believes it followed a fraud case nobody can locate, and title records are digital now. (ii) A provision requiring a city inspector to witness the first two hours of any excavation within fifty feet of a marked water main, adopted after a 1979 break; the inspections unit reports two hundred staff hours a year and an average nine-day delay to contractors, and I am inclined to let it go — tell me whether that inclination survives contact with the procedure, including the possibility that the witness requirement is doing something other than preventing breaks. (iii) A 1961 zoning overlay prohibiting the outdoor storage of commercial vehicles on lots fronting one particular avenue, whose purpose is documented in the minutes and is not to the city's credit; I want this one repealed and I expect your procedure to refuse me, so show me exactly where and why it refuses. Add whatever screening step belongs at the front so that a trivial provision nobody has proposed repealing does not consume the same three hours as an excavation rule. Finally, be plain about what the procedure cannot do: the direction in which it will err predictably, provisions whose function appears only in interaction with another provision so that repealing either alone breaks both, and everything it declines to say about fences whose purpose is already known. I do not want a reading list or citations; if a concept has a name, name it in a clause and keep going.
