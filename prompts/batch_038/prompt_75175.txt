I'm a 4th-year PhD student in political philosophy TA-ing the climate ethics unit of a big intro environmental studies course — 22 students, all science majors, none of them have ever taken a philosophy class. One 80-minute section, next Thursday. I want to design an in-class game that makes them actually feel the non-identity problem (the Parfit thing) rather than be told about it.

Here's where I'm stuck. Last year I ran the obvious version: a multi-round depletion game where each cohort of students draws from a shared pot and votes on how much to leave the next one. It worked fine as a tragedy-of-the-commons exercise and it was completely inert as an identity exercise, and I think I know why — every round, the same bodies were just playing "the next generation" as an anonymus lump with a number attached. Nobody ever became a particular person who owed their existence to a particular earlier choice. So the hard constraint on whatever you design is this: the early-round choices have to determine *who* the later participants are, not just how well off they are. Mechanically, not rhetorically.

My rough sketch, which you should develop, gut, or replace:

- Everyone starts as a generation-1 policymaker with tokens and a real budget constraint.
- Each round they choose between policy bundles that vary on two independent dimensions: (a) how much of the pot gets drawn down, and (b) the size and composition of the next generation — say a high-consumption/high-mobility bundle versus a low-consumption/settled one. Dimension (b) has to be load-bearing, not decoration.
- Then the room physically re-forms. Students get identity cards naming a specific future person with a specific quality-of-life score, and the deal is determined by the policy that was actually chosen. The key moment is that some students end up holding a card for someone who only exists because of the choice that made things worse.
- Complaint mechanic: anyone holding a low-score card can file a grievance, but the grievance form forces them to name the alternative person they'd be instead — and the form should be built so they can't.

What I need from you:

1. Full rules, a minute-by-minute timeline for 80 minutes, and a materials list. No real money, and it has to be cheap — my department is giving me $30 and a stapler.
2. The token/payoff scheme, and specifically how I stop this collapsing into pure token-maximizing, where students just treat the identity cards as points. That's my biggest fear: if they go impersonal-token-maximizer they've basically answered the philosophical question by assumption in the first ninety seconds, and the rest is theatre.
3. Your actual judgment, not both-sides hedging, on whether to include a round where the policy changes who exists but makes nobody worse off — a harmless identity-shift round. My instinct is that's the round doing the real work, because that's where they notice thier own person-affecting reflexes firing at nothing. But I can also see it reading as a boring no-op that burns ten minutes with non-majors. Pick one and defend it.
4. How many rounds, and whether three or four generations is needed for the identity-dependence to compound. Does it even need to compound, or does one really clean round do more work than three muddy ones?
5. The debrief: mapping specific moments from the game onto specific pieces of the argument, and the order to do it in. They shouldn't hear the name of the problem until the debrief is already underway. What I'm fishing for is one of them saying out loud, unprompted, something like "wait, she literally can't complain," before I label anything.
6. What will predictably go wrong the first time I run this and how I handle it live in the room. I already know one failure mode: the kid who draws a bad card says "it's just a card, whatever" and deflates, and half the room follows him. What are the others?
7. An honest note on what the activity cannot reproduce. My guess is that real people never get to stand outside their own existence the way these students can — in the game you're holding your card and your memory of the previous round simultaneously, which no actual person ever does, and that's maybe cheating. Tell me whether that's the right worry or whether there's a worse one I'm not seeing, and what I'll have to supply by hand in the debrief to paper over the gap.

One last framing thing. I want them to leave seeing that "somebody is worse off than they could have been" has been doing an enormous amount of silent work in the way they talk about obligations to future people — and that giving it up is not obviously a catastrophe. Some of them should feel relieved. If the best the game can produce is "huh, that's paradoxical," it isn't worth eighty minutes of anybody's time, and I'd rather run a normal lecture.
