Three weeks from now I am giving my first paper outside my own department, at a philosophy-of-biology workshop where the thirty-odd attendees are mainly analytic philosophers with real training in population-genetic modelling, one senior figure who has spent four decades defending the gene's-eye view and knows the replicator/interactor distinction inside out, two working biologists (an experimental evolutionist who runs microbial selection lines, and a behavioural ecologist who builds inclusive-fitness models and regards purely conceptual work with polite impatience), and a scattering of graduate students who will be looking for a reason to disagree with the senior figure. I came to this debate from oncology rather than from philosophy, which is why the position I am defending starts from somatic evolution, and which is also why I expect to be told that I have dressed a clinical intuition in technical language. My supervisor is broadly sympathetic to the view, so nobody in my own building has stress-tested it properly. Please write the whole answer in British English.

The thesis is that the gene/individual/group dispute is not a substantive disagreement about the world so much as an artefact of posing the unit question without fixing a resolution, and that once the resolution is fixed the question dissolves in an instructive way. Concretely: a candidate level — gene, cell, organism, colony, group, species — counts as a unit of selection relative to some trait only if (a) the ensemble at that level shows heritable variation in reproductive success with respect to that trait, and (b) the processes that would dismantle that ensemble at a finer resolution operate more slowly than the timescale over which (a) is being measured. Clause (b) is the part I care about. An organism is a unit not because it enjoys any metaphysical privilege but because a separation of timescales ordinarily holds between within-body cell competition and between-body reproduction, and a tumour is what that separation looks like when it fails locally. I intend to claim three payoffs: the account explains why the group-selection controversy was so intractable, since the two camps were implicitly fixing different resolutions; it gives a non-arbitrary answer for holobionts, because the rate of vertical transmission sets the relevant resolution for microbiome-encoded traits; and it demotes the gene from being the unit of selection simpliciter to being the unit only for traits whose relevant resolution is generational and recombination-slow.

What I want from you is a preparation sheet for the question period, not a defence of the position. Give me the objections this particular room will actually raise, in roughly the order they will arrive and with a candid ranking of how much damage each could do. I expect to be pressed from the formal side on whether clause (b) can be cashed out in anything other than metaphor borrowed from physics; on whether the standard intertranslatability of group-selection and kin-selection models already makes the choice between them pragmatic, so that I have solved a pseudo-problem by declaring it a pseudo-problem; and on whether I have merely redescribed a question about additive genetic variance. From the biologists I expect the experimentalist to ask what measurement in a microbial evolution experiment would come out differently on my account, and the behavioural ecologist to ask why I should not simply say that everything I need is already contained in a well-parameterised inclusive-fitness model. Please phrase each objection in the vocabulary the person raising it would use, supply a reply that fits into about two minutes of speaking time, and mark clearly where that reply is soft or where it quietly concedes more than I would like. Separate the objections I should meet head-on from the ones I should neutralise by conceding something early and cheaply, and suggest what I ought to put in the opening five minutes so that the concessions cost me least. Finally, tell me what you take to be the most damaging line of attack available in that room — the one I would most prefer not to hear, which I suspect concerns the circularity of clause (b), since I seem to identify the relevant timescale by looking for the level at which selection is already measurable, and the senior figure in particular is exactly the person to notice that.
