I am trying to pin down the rule I actually follow when I revise my credences on learning a conditional, and I want to know whether such a rule exists. All of the verdicts below are mine; the numbers are ones I worked out myself, and I am confident in the arithmetic even where I am not confident in the verdict. Conventions throughout: a standard 52-card deck, "picture card" meaning jack, queen or king, one card dealt face down which I will eventually be shown. I gather that (3) below is a well-known puzzle, but I am reporting my own reactions to it, not anyone else's.

**Priors, before anyone says anything.**

(1) Asked how credible "if the card is red, then it is a picture card" is, I answer 3/13: I suppose the card is red and ask how things then stand, and 6 of the 26 red cards are picture cards. Asked instead how credible "either the card is not red, or it is a picture card" is, I answer 8/13, since 32 of the 52 cards satisfy that. I am perfectly happy holding both numbers at once; the second sentence is not what the first one says.

(2) Same pattern with a die. "If it lands even, it lands 2 or 6" gets 2/3 from me; "either it lands odd, or it lands 2 or 6" gets 5/6.

**Testimony.** In each of the following a friend who has seen the card, whom I trust completely, and who is stipulated not to be evasive or ironic, tells me something, and I take what she says as certain.

(3) She says: "If the card is red, then it is a picture card." My credence that the card is red should fall from 1/2 to 3/16. I get that by deleting the 20 red non-picture cards and noting that 6 of the 32 survivors are red. I have stared at this and it still looks right to me: what she said fails only where the card is a red non-picture card, and that region was a substantial chunk of my prior, so my confidence that the card is red should take a substantial hit.

(4) Suppose instead that she says: "Either the card is black, or it is a picture card." Same update exactly, 3/16, and I see no room for a difference: on my own account of what she said in (3), this is what she said.

(5) Suppose she says: "If the card is a picture card, then it is red." Now my credence that the card is red should rise, but only to 13/23: six black picture cards are deleted and 26 reds survive out of 46.

(6) Different setup. A die I know to be loaded, so that the even faces carry total probability 1/100, split evenly between 2, 4 and 6. Someone who has thrown this die for years and whom I trust completely says: "If it lands even, it lands 6." Doing to this case what I did in (3) — deleting even-and-not-6, which is 2/300 of my prior, and renormalising — takes my credence in an even outcome from 1/100 to 1/298, about a third of what it was. I reject that verdict. Her remark concerned how things stand if the die lands even; it did not concern whether the die will land even, and my credence in even should stay at 1/100, or move by an amount I would not bother to write down.

(7) Control case. I have just read on a scoreboard that tomorrow's match has been called off. A friend says: "If it rains tomorrow, the match will be called off." No change to my credence in rain and no change anywhere else. Every account I know of agrees here, which is why I include it.

(8) The one I keep reversing on. A fair coin is about to be tossed and my utterly reliable friend says: "If it lands heads, it lands tails." By the procedure I used in (1) and (2), that conditional deserves credence 0 from me: I suppose heads, and tails does not follow. But its material paraphrase, "either it does not land heads or it lands tails," is an ordinary contingent claim to which I should give 1/2 — and conditionalising on that would leave me certain of tails. Here, then, the two readings do not merely assign different numbers; they point in different practical directions. One says the sentence is unacceptable coming from anybody. The other says to become certain how the coin will land. My instinct is that I should downgrade my trust in my friend rather than become certain of tails, but I cannot see how any rule that treats her utterance purely as content to be conditionalised on could deliver that.

**What I want from you.**

(a) State the rule, or the small set of competing rules, that generates these verdicts. State it precisely enough that I could apply it unaided: what its input is, what it does to the probability of the antecedent, and what it does to the rest of the distribution. For each clause or parameter, identify which of (1) through (8) forced it in.

(b) If no single rule generates all of them, say which of my verdicts have to go and why. I would much rather be told that (3) or (6) is indefensible than be handed a rule whose extra parameter I cannot interpret independently of the cases it was tuned to fit. Wherever you overturn one of my verdicts, give me the number the surviving rule puts in its place.

(c) I take it that (1) and (2) jointly mean that no element of my algebra has, across all choices of antecedent and consequent, the probability I assign to conditionals, so that learning a conditional cannot in general be learning some proposition. Say explicitly whether (3) and (4) already commit me to treating it that way, what that inconsistency costs me, and whether the cost can be localised to a subclass of cases rather than paid globally.

(d) Give me your rule's verdicts on cases I have not listed, in particular these two. (i) My friend tells me both "if the card is red then it is a picture card" and "if the card is a picture card then it is a queen": what should my credence that the card is red be, and does the order in which she says them matter? (ii) She is merely 90% reliable, in the sense that what she tells me turns out true nine times in ten, and she says "if the card is red, then it is a picture card": what happens to my credence in red, and does the direction of the shift depend on whether her unreliability concerns the antecedent or the connection between antecedent and consequent?

(e) Finally, say what your rule implies in case (3) for my credence in the converse conditional, "if it is a picture card then it is red," and check that the result is consistent with the 13/23 I want in (5).

If the rule you settle on is a recognised one, its name will suffice; I am not asking for sources or a survey, only for the rule itself and an honest accounting of which of my verdicts it cannot save.
