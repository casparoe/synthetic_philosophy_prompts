A colleague in my political theory group has constructed an argument he thinks shows that democratic legitimacy is impossible, and I have been asked to respond to it before our next meeting. I cannot make it fail. The case he builds it on is invented but, he insists, does no work beyond illustration.

Forty researchers occupy a platform in international waters. A funding consortium allocated the forty places by lottery, on terms all forty accepted: those drawn would constitute a self-governing community, competent to set its own admission and franchise rules. Two years in, the assembly votes to restrict the franchise to residents of at least two years' standing, disenfranchising nine people who remain fully subject to the rules the assembly makes. Is that vote legitimate? His argument:

(1) A decision is democratically legitimate only if it is made by the body that it binds, i.e., only if those entitled to a say in it coincide with those subject to it.
(2) Whether some plurality is the body entitled to a say is a question about the boundaries of the demos, and it arises before every decision, including the franchise vote.
(3) A question about the boundaries of the demos can be settled only by a decision: either the demos decides it, or someone else does.
(4) If the demos decides it, that decision presupposes a demos already constituted, so it either assumes what it was to establish or appeals to a prior demos, whose boundaries likewise require a decision, and so on without end.
(5) If someone else decides it, the boundaries are fixed by fiat, and a plurality whose boundaries are fixed by fiat does not govern itself.
(6) So the boundaries of every demos rest on circularity, infinite regress, or fiat; in no case does a demos settle its own boundaries.
(7) Therefore, by (1), no decision of any demos is democratically legitimate.
(8) Nor is the damage confined to democracy. The same reasoning shows that no normative power has a settled scope: a court cannot determine its own jurisdiction without presupposing a rule that already determines it, a club cannot admit a member, an assembly cannot found itself, a parent cannot bind a child who never consented to be bound.
(9) But (8) is false. Courts routinely settle their own jurisdiction without anyone thinking the settlement void; and at the platform, all forty, including the nine, accepted the lottery, and the resulting community strikes everyone as competent to govern itself.

He calls this the boundary problem, though I do not think the label carries any weight in the reasoning. My own instinct is that (1) and (9) are the claims worth preserving and that (3) is the one to sacrifice, but I am not sure that combination is stable: if no decision settles the boundaries, it looks as though nothing does, and a question with no answer cannot constrain the franchise vote either.

What I want from you:

- Identify the step or steps you would reject, and say whether the inference to (7) is valid once those steps are removed, or whether further repair is needed.
- Explain why the rejected step looked compelling. What intuition does it capture, and can that intuition survive in a weakened form that the argument cannot exploit?
- Price the rejection. If the boundaries of the demos are settled by a moral criterion rather than a decision — an all-affected or all-subjected principle, say — what new difficulties does that create, and what becomes of the claim that democracy has non-instrumental value?
- Say whether your diagnosis applies uniformly to the franchise vote and to the original allocation of the forty places, or whether the two require different premises, and if so which.
