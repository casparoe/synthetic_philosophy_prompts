so I'm auditing a philosophy-of-science seminar this term and we spent two whole sessions on the interpretations of probability thing, and honestly I think I've been the only one who noticed how the argument is supposed to shake out. Two people in the group pushed back on me and I'm not convinced they actually followed it, so maybe you can sanity check my reasoning.

here's the line I was making. De Finetti's representation theorem says that if your degrees of belief over an infinite sequence of coin flips are exchangeable, then they can be written as a mixture over some bias parameter — i.e. you're implicitly carrying around a prior over a theta in [0,1] and treating the flips as independent given theta, whether or not you ever thought of theta as a real thing. And then as more flips come in, your posterior over theta tightens up and your predicted relative frequency settles down. So the theorem seems to prove that a coherent Bayesian is mathematically compelled to converge on the objective long-run frequency of the process generating the data. Which means the frequentist's limiting relative frequency isn't some extra metaphysical ingredient you have to add to the world — it's just what a sufficiently disciplined subjectivist ends up believing anyway. So the interpretation debate gets settled in the subjectivist's favour, and frequentism turns out to be a limiting special case of it rather than a rival?

Related: my flatmate, who's a stats PhD student and keeps insisting this whole debate is just semantics, said the Dutch book argument shows that subjective probabilities are automatically well calibrated, which I thought supported me, since calibration is supposed to be exactly the property the frequentists say they care about. If coherence buys you calibration for free, then the subjectivist has got the frequentist's own success criterion built in.

Am I overreaching? I'd specifically like to know whether the exchangeability assumption is doing something sneaky here, because I have a feeling a frequentist or a propensity person would say I've helped myself to a claim about the world that they weren't allowed to make, and I can't tell if that's a real objection or just them being sore about losing. Also does it matter that the theorem wants an infinite sequence when no actual experiment ever has one?
