I sit on the board of a small charitable trust in north Warwickshire, in the country George Eliot knew; I came to the board rather sideways, since I am doing a part-time master's and writing on her treatment of dissenting religion, which is why I have been asked to speak at the consultation. Our parish church closed two years ago, and two others nearby are following. In three weeks we present a proposal the diocese has half-endorsed and the local paper has already called Methodism without the Methodists. The scheme is this: the three buildings pass to the trust and become houses of common life, open on Sunday mornings for an hour's gathering — choral music, a twenty-minute address on how to live, a shared silence, a meal afterwards — with a board in the porch on which that week's caring duties are written down and signed for: lifts to the hospital, shopping for the housebound, a rota for sitting with the dying. There is no creed, no prayer, no blessing. The hymn tunes are kept and the words rewritten so that they assert nothing theological; the conveners are to be drawn from the former clergy and lay readers, retrained. Separately, we have been asked to help establish a non-religious pastoral care service in the district hospital, using the same vocabulary of presence and accompaniment without any doctrine. Half the board thinks Eliot is the patron saint of the whole enterprise: they cite her translations of Strauss and Feuerbach, her refusal in 1842 to go to church with her father, and the end of her 1867 poem 'The Choir Invisible', where she asks to join that company whose music is the gladness of the world. The other half, our vicar among them, thinks she would find it a sentimental counterfeit — that she preserved religious forms precisely because she took the feelings to owe their grip to being commanded by something outside the self, so that a Sunday assembly with the commands edited out is reverence performed by people who have ceased to reverence.

Tell me what she would actually have said, and give me the reasoning rather than the atmosphere. I want to know which half of the board is nearer to her and where each of them is merely projecting. Build the prediction out of her settled commitments: what she took the Strauss and Feuerbach translations to have established; what she thought religious sentiment was, and why she supposed it capable of outliving the doctrines it grew up inside; what she made of dissenting and evangelical communities as social facts — Amos Barton, Mr Tryan, Dinah Morris, Silas Marner's Lantern Yard, Farebrother and Bulstrode, Mordecai — and how far the affection she extends to her clergy can be treated as her own conviction rather than her craft. Then take the questions we shall actually be asked on the night: whether keeping the stones, the tunes and the calendar shape while excising the words carries the feeling over intact or produces a museum exhibit; whether an assembly without a creed can generate obligation as opposed to uplift, and what she thought made obligation bind at all; whether staffing the venture with former clergy is an asset or a contradiction; and whether she would judge such schemes to slow the erosion of common moral life or merely to decorate its ruins.

Two things besides. First, the poem is the crux of our internal argument: the humanists on the board quote 'Divinely human, raising worship so / To higher reverence more mix'd with love' as proof that she wanted worship reformed rather than abandoned, and the vicar replies that the piece is a meditation on the dead, addressed by a woman to herself about her own influence, and not a programme for a Sunday morning, so that it has been pressed into a service it will not bear. Say which of them reads it properly and show your working on the passage itself, not on her reputation. Second, commit. I am not looking for a balanced account of her complexity; if your honest conclusion is that she would have supported one part of the scheme and despised another, name the part and give the reason, and tell me what further evidence about her would falsify your prediction. Where the record is genuinely silent — on hospitals, on the disposal of church buildings, on anything of this sort — say that it is silent and argue from what she did hold, without inventing letters, opinions or lines she never wrote.
