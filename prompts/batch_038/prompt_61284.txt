I'm helping put together an interactive "where do you actually land?" quiz for a sentencing-reform newsletter. Our readers think of themselves as anti-cruelty progressives, but their gut reactions to concrete cases keep turning out pretty harsh, and I want an instrument that exposes that gap without lecturing anybody. So: eight to ten questions, each one a concrete case with two or three options, and no question is allowed to use the words free will, deserve, desert, blame, retribution, or responsibility — the whole point is that people answer with thier gut about what to do, and only afterwards get told which position they've been holding all along. Then a scoring key mapping answer patterns onto named views. I need at least these leaves:

- the compatibilist who thinks desert is real and does actual work in setting how much someone gets (this is roughly Dennett's side of the exchange with Caruso — but put him wherever he actually belongs, even if that turns out not to be a tidy leaf, since I don't think he's a straight retributivist)
- the free-will skeptic who still wants the state to be able to restrain dangerous people, but on a quarantine / public-health logic instead of a desert logic (Caruso and Pereboom)
- negative retributivism: desert sets a ceiling and maybe a floor, expected consequences pick the spot inside it
- punishment as a price on behavior — pure deterrence/incentive, no message attached
- the expressivist/censure view where the amount has to track the seriousness of the wrong because the sentence is the state saying something
- flat abolition: no state-imposed suffering at all, civil compensation and physical separation only, nothing more

Every question has to be a case where at least two of those come apart, and I want two worked in specifically: (a) a man doing ten years for armed robbery who is now demonstrably no longer a danger to anyone — let him out, or make him serve the rest? and (b) someone whose violence is fully explained by a growing brain tumor but who is clearly still dangerous to be around. Those two together should split the desert views from the quarantine views fast.

Two more things that should keep this from being a rehash of a textbook chart. First, a node that separates Caruso's version of the quarantine idea from Pereboom's — something about whether the state owes the person a fixed background (housing, healthcare, the social conditions that produced the crime) before it may lock him up at all. If your questions genuinely can't pull those two apart, say so and say what question would. Second, run the same instrument in the other direction: three or four questions about praise and reward — the surgeon who saved the kid, the founder who got rich, the kid from a wrecked neighborhood who made it out — and then give me the cross-tab of blame-side answers against praise-side answers. Which cells are crowded and which are empty? I'd bet "harsh on criminals, no such thing as a self-made success" is a big cluster and "lenient on criminals, big believer in merit" is nearly empty; tell me whether that's right, and whether either one is even coherent or just an unstable resting place.

For each leaf, one sentence on its most awkward commitment — the thing its defenders would least like a journalist to ask them about in public. And flag every pair of leaves your questions can't really separate rather than pretending otherwise, plus any cell in the space nobody occupies, with a guess at why: incoherent, unattractive, or just unexplored.
