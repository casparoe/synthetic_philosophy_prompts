I've been reading rationalist blogs and forums since roughly the Overcoming Bias days, and one piece of the received catechism has never quite sat right with me: the flat injunction to ignore sunk costs. I can recite the reasons — a cost already paid cannot be recovered, so it cannot bear on any decision about the future — and I know the standard demonstrations, including Arkes and Blumer's season-ticket study, where the theatergoers who had paid full price turned up to more performances than the ones who had been handed a discount, and the aircraft vignette in which an executive who has already poured most of a development budget into a plane is supposed to walk away the moment a competitor markets something faster and cheaper. What bothers me now is that in every one of those demonstrations the money already spent is causally inert with respect to everything that comes next. It bought no evidence about the state of the world, no one is watching how you behave and adjusting their future terms accordingly, and the value of the project is exactly the sum of the values of its remaining stages. That is a highly specific structure, and my suspicion is that a great deal of real-world escalation of commitment is rational precisely because the structure is usually different.

I run a monthly rationality meetup that I've facilitated for about four years. Expect sixteen to twenty adults around tables, mixed backgrounds: a couple of economics graduate students, several software engineers, a teacher, a nurse, and a few people with no formal training but a lot of enthusiasm. Ninety minutes, community-room setting, whiteboard available, no laptops.

I want you to design a game that gets them to discover this themselves, before anyone says the words "sunk cost" or "escalation of commitment." The core thing I want built in is that sunkness is a structural property of a payoff scheme rather than a temporal property of money. Design three arms of the same surface-level game — a staged project, four teams of four, each round the team decides whether to pour in more tokens or abandon — where the hidden structure differs. In one arm, each round of spending yields private signal cards about the project's underlying quality, so the teams that have spent the most are also the best informed, and continuing is updating rather than stubbornness. In a second arm, a funder or partner role observes who finishes and who quits and offers measurably better terms in a later round to teams with a record of finishing, so completing a losing project can pay off through the next one. In a third arm, abandoning forfeits the returns from earlier stages, which means the earlier spending was never actually sunk at all — it was contingent. In at least one arm I want a moment where two teams have spent identical amounts and make opposite choices and both turn out to be right. Crucially, teams should not be told which arm they are in until the debrief, and the inference in the informational arm has to be felt through cards and tokens, not computed; nobody should have to write down a likelihood ratio.

Give me the rules and a materials list, the token-and-payoff scheme, and a minute-by-minute timeline. The stakes should be real but not monetary — propose something appropriate, keeping in mind I can supply a small prize and the group directs a modest annual donation. Start the session with a two-minute warm-up in which everyone writes down their answer to a classic inert-money vignette plus a confidence rating, so the orthodoxy is on the record before it gets punished. Then the debrief: map specific moments of play onto each of the three channels and onto the inert-money condition, and be explicit about what the game licenses them to conclude and what it doesn't. Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it — a team that reverse-engineers the arm structure early, a room where everyone abandons immediately and the informational channel never shows up, arguments during the debrief that the informational arm is cheating — and how to handle each. Tell me what changes if only eight people show. And give me one harder variant for a numerate group.

End with a frank note on which feature of the phenomenon the game cannot reproduce and I will have to supply by hand; my own guess is the self-justification and identity component, and the fact that in real life nobody ever tells you whether your spending was buying information.

Please don't include references, a reading list, or a bibliography — I want design, not scholarship. And write the whole thing as continuous prose, the way you would write a letter or a long email: no headings, no bullet points, no numbered lists, no bold, no tables. If you need to give me a timeline or a payoff scheme, put it in sentences.
