I'm writing the final for a senior seminar in the history of political economy — it's half econ majors, half philosophy students, two hours, one essay question, 30 marks. The unit we're finishing is Hume's monetary and commercial essays: "Of Money," "Of Interest," "Of the Balance of Trade," "Of the Jealousy of Trade." I need a question they cannot get through by reciting the standard summary. And you know exactly what the standard summary is: money is a veil in the long run, Hume anticipated the price-specie flow mechanism, there's short-run non-neutrality because prices adjust gradually, therefore mercantilism is a confusion between money and wealth. Half the class will write that and think they're done.

Here's the tension I want the question built around, and I'd like you to sharpen it rather than soften it. Hume's self-correcting equilibrium argument is an argument about money and prices: lose four-fifths of your specie and your labour and commodities become so cheap abroad that the money comes back; multiply it five-fold and the reverse happens, money drains away until the level is restored. But in the same essays he also seems to concede that real productive capacity does *not* behave like that — once a nation has let a manufacture go, the skills and the habits and the foreign workmen who have the start don't come back with the money, which is why the jealousy of trade isn't purely the blind passion he spends most of the essay calling it. So the equilibrium mechanism apparently can't carry the weight Hume's policy remarks want it to carry. The question should make students decide whether that's a genuine inconsistency, or whether the two claims are about different variables (monetary flows vs. accumulated know-how and human capital) and can be reconciled — and then make them say what the reconciliation costs, because I think it does cost something: if the stock of money self-corrects only via prices while the stock of capability doesn't self-correct at all, then Hume gets to keep the protectionist-friendly conclusion and has to give up the "nothing is really lost" cheerfulness, and the mechanism is doing less work than his rhetoric implies.

What I want back: the exam question itself, worded so a student who only knows the labels has nowhere to hide; a model answer of about 900 words; and a marking scheme out of 30 that names the specific moves — separating the veil claim from the flow-mechanism claim, noticing that the mechanism needs prices to be responsive while the "intermediate stage" story about quickened industry needs them to lag, not collapsing money-as-neutral into money-as-merely-a-fiction, getting the hysteresis point, and costing out the reconciliation — plus which mistakes lose marks. Flag the two I see every year as explicit mark-losers: treating the specie-flow mechanism as if it were an argument about the balance of trade in goods rather than in money, and reading Hume's short-run non-neutrality as a Keynesian demand effect rather than as something about the order and speed in which different people's incomes adjust.

Two more things. Don't put anything in quotation marks from Hume unless you're confident it's verbatim — I'll be checking against the text, and I'd much rather have careful paraphrase than a plausible-looking quote. And at the end tell me where you think my reading is wrong: what argument or consideration would lead you to a different verdict about whether the tension is real, which item in the marking scheme is most likely to be too harsh on a good answer, and anything you've asserted about Hume that you're not actually sure of.
