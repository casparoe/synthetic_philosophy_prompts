Last night a friend and I argued for three hours about whether an artificial agent's "side effects" can be quantified without specifying a full theory of value, and I have been unable to dislodge his argument. I would like a careful diagnosis rather than a survey.

The setting, stated as neutrally as I can manage. An agent operates in an environment with a set of states S and a transition relation. A baseline state b is the state that would obtain absent the agent's intervention. An impact measure is a function I(b, s) ≥ 0 meant to represent how much disruption the agent causes in moving from b to s, and it enters the agent's objective as a penalty: the agent maximizes its task reward minus λ·I. The philosophical hope is that I can be read off the structure of the environment alone — for example, by comparing which states remain attainable from s against which were attainable from b — with no appeal to a utility function over states, so that an agent can be discouraged from upheaval without our having to tell it what is worth preserving.

My friend's argument that this hope is incoherent runs as follows.

(1) Value neutrality requires that I depend only on non-evaluative facts about the environment: the states, the transition relation, and the baseline. If it depended on anything more, it would encode somebody's view about what matters.

(2) If I(b, s) ≠ I(b, t), then I is sensitive to some feature F that distinguishes s from t.

(3) For every feature F of an environment, there is a coherent value function wholly indifferent to F.

(4) Hence, for every F, whether F obtains is not an evaluatively significant fact about the world; a measure sensitive to F thereby imports an assumption that some perfectly coherent agent rejects.

(5) Therefore I must be insensitive to every feature F.

(6) A measure insensitive to every feature is constant across all pairs (b, s).

(7) A constant penalty is inert, since it subtracts the same amount from every course of action and so cannot change what the agent does; and since inaction leaves the state at the baseline and must count as zero disruption, the constant is zero.

(8) Therefore no nontrivial value-neutral impact measure exists. Any penalty on side effects is either empty or a utility function in disguise, and avoiding side effects cannot be defined without a full specification of value.

He does not stop there. He observes that (3) through (5) is a template: substitute risk, or irreversibility, or evidential support, for impact, and the same conclusion follows for each, so on his account there is no value-neutral way of characterizing anything normatively suggestive about an agent's situation. He thinks this is not self-undermining, because the argument shows only that structural notions cannot bear normative weight, and he is content to go on using the transition relation as a purely descriptive notion in his own reasoning.

I find (8) unacceptable and his extension to risk and evidence obviously unacceptable, yet I cannot locate the step that licenses either. My instinct is that (1) and (3) are both true, which appears to commit me to (8), so presumably I am not entitled to one of them and cannot see which.

Which step or steps should I reject? For each candidate, tell me why it looked compelling in the heat of an argument like ours, and price the rejection: specifically, whether giving it up leaves any room for the structural approach sketched above, or whether it forces me to concede that every such measure is a disguised utility function. I would also like a separate verdict on (7), which seems to be doing two distinct jobs at once — establishing that a constant penalty cannot affect behaviour, and fixing the constant at zero — and on whether his claim that the template generalizes to risk and evidence can be resisted without resisting the original argument. Finally, if you think the argument is sound and (8) is simply a conclusion I have to learn to live with, say so and tell me what the least damaging way of accepting it looks like.
