I teach an upper-level undergraduate philosophy of religion seminar — about thirty students, all of whom have already taken a metaphysics course and so know what libertarian free will is and can state the standard foreknowledge puzzle. Our final is three hours, and students answer two of four essays, forty-five minutes each. Last year I set a Molinism question asking whether divine middle knowledge reconciles providence with creaturely freedom, and the results were disappointing in a very particular way: nearly every answer reproduced the taxonomy of natural, middle, and free knowledge, mentioned that counterfactuals of creaturely freedom are supposed to be true prior to any divine decree, named the grounding objection, and stopped there. Marks clustered in the B range, and no answer engaged with why the view is vulnerable in the first place. This year I want a question whose difficulty is structural rather than recall-based, and I want the exam paper itself to supply every piece of technical machinery a candidate needs, since I am not interested in rewarding memorization of the secondary literature.

Here is the material I want the question built on; please have it printed on the paper as a stipulated argument, with the terms defined on the page. Let K be the maximal set of true counterfactuals of creaturely freedom — propositions stating what such-and-such a creature would freely do in such-and-such circumstances — and suppose, as Molinism does, that K is complete, that its truth depends on no divine decision, and that God knows K prior to his decree. Given K together with God's choice of which initial circumstances to create, every subsequent free action in that world is already settled. The argument I want candidates to work through has two horns. First horn (call it the selection argument): for every free action A of a creature S, the truth of the proposition that S does A is fixed before creation by facts — the contents of K plus God's choice of circumstances — over which S has no control; and if the truth of a proposition about what S does is fixed by facts over which S has no control, then S is not free with respect to that action; hence Molinism is inconsistent with libertarian freedom. I want the marking scheme to catch candidates who simply re-run the familiar foreknowledge argument here, because the fixing facts in this version are pre-decretal and God's knowledge of them is not the mechanism doing the work. Second horn (the luck horn): a Molinist who rejects the transfer step by insisting that a true counterfactual of freedom is a brute fact authored by neither God nor creature does not thereby escape, since K is then brute luck from God's side as well, and his providence amounts to choosing among worlds whose free histories nobody wrote — which shrinks Molinism's advertised advantage over open theism considerably. I would like part (b) to ask what the Molinist must concede on that second horn, and whether grounding K in the creature's essence or settled dispositions preserves its independence from God's will without reintroducing determination.

Please write: (1) the exam question itself, with the stipulated argument and any needed definitions printed as the candidate would see them, timed for forty-five minutes and worth twenty marks, split into a twelve-mark part (a) and an eight-mark part (b); (2) a model answer of roughly 800 words that a strong student could realistically have produced in the time allowed — it must take a position on the dilemma rather than survey it, and it must say explicitly whether the transfer step is being rejected, restricted, or granted, and what the cost of that choice is; (3) a marking scheme broken into five or six must-hit moves with the marks each carries and, for each, a one-line note on the standard way candidates botch it — I already expect several, such as answering that God does not cause the action and treating that as a complete reply, invoking compatibilism without noticing that Molinism requires the libertarian reading of freedom, and conflating the grounding objection with the selection argument; and (4) a short calibration paragraph explaining what separates a first-class answer from a solid B, where the difference should turn on whether the candidate can explain why the two horns amount to a genuine dilemma rather than two independent complaints. Candidates should not be required to cite or even name any philosopher, and the model answer should not depend on knowledge of the literature: everything needed should be on the paper or in the course's core material on providence and free will.
