Here's the position I want translated. There's a long-running argument in the philosophy of biology about whether DNA really carries information in anything more than a loose, metaphorical sense. One side — I think this is roughly Maynard Smith and Szathmáry, but don't take my word for it — says the genetic code is a genuine code because the information in DNA is semantic, not just Shannon-type correlation: the codons don't merely co-vary with amino acids, they specify them, and they do so in a way that's arbitrary-but-conventional, like a symbol system, where the mapping between the 64 codons and the amino acids could have been otherwise and is held fixed across almost all of life. They ground that arbitrariness in a teleosemantic story: the content of a gene comes from what it was selected to do, so function is what turns causal influence into meaning. Related to this is Crick's frozen accident idea — the code got locked in early and can't be revised, because any change to the mapping garbles every message at once. And there's a claim floating around that the code is near-optimal at minimizing the phenotypic damage from point mutations and mistranslation. The other side says all of this is metaphor inflation: genes are just one causal factor among many, the same word information gets used for correlation with the past, for instruction, and for function, and nobody's bothered to check whether these are the same relation; a deflationary critic (Sarkar, I believe) argued that what genes have is at most differential causal specificity, and that calling it semantic information smuggles in a sender, a receiver, and a purpose that molecular biology doesn't actually contain. There's also the recipe-versus-blueprint point — DNA is supposedly not a description of the organism but a procedure for building one, and the organism is not recoverable from the genome the way a frame is recoverable from a bitstream.

My naive prior, which I want you to attack rather than flatter: I suspect the whole debate collapses under codec vocabulary. A fixed 64-entry lookup table that never updates is just a bad codebook; degeneracy isn't error correction, it's redundancy, and people are confusing the two; frozen accident is just a codebook you can't re-train because the decoder is distributed across the whole biosphere and every message depends on it; near-optimality claims are suspect because you need a distortion measure to call anything optimal, and in a codec the distortion measure is chosen by the designer, not discovered; and recipe-vs-blueprint is the old procedural-versus-declarative distinction with a biology costume on. On this view the semantic information talk is just people getting spooked by their own metaphor.
