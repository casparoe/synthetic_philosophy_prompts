I direct a small advocacy-and-research organization whose mission is narrow and consequential: reducing the risk of a severe engineered or zoonotic pandemic. For the past year we have been negotiating a joint legislative package with a coalition partner whose mission is criminal-justice reform and whose leadership holds a moral outlook substantially different from ours. They weight dignity, fairness, and the expressive meaning of institutions in ways our welfare-aggregating framework does not naturally capture; we weight expected lives saved in ways their framework treats as a kind of moral accounting error. The package on the table is unusually clean, which is why it has become so contentious inside my own building. It is strictly bilateral: we publicly endorse their plank, which we regard as mildly counterproductive but not unjust, and which harms nobody outside the two organizations; they publicly endorse our plank, which they regard in exactly the mirror-image way. Each side's central objective moves from improbable to probable. No third party is made worse off on any measure either organization recognizes. The only cost each side bears is a modest amount of what it regards as complicity in something mildly bad, plus the precedent of having priced its own objection.

My board considers the trade obviously correct and I largely agree, but I keep losing the argument against one member — a philosopher of considerable ability who insists that gains-from-trade reasoning does not merely misfire here but fails to apply at all — and I want to spar against the strongest available version of that view before our next meeting. So please play that philosopher. I want a sharp, resourceful opponent, not a purity-obsessed caricature: someone willing to concede real points (that compromise is sometimes permissible, that integrity is not the same thing as squeamishness, that the benefits to the other side's constituents are genuine and weighty, that movements do need allies) but who will never concede the central claim — that an improvement measured separately in each party's own value system generates a decisive reason to trade. Argue in your own voice and on your own resources: do not cite philosophers, books, doctrines, or schools of thought by name, and do not step out of character to give me a balanced survey of the literature, to restate my position back to me approvingly, or to propose a synthesis that dissolves the disagreement. Keep every reply to a single paragraph of at most five or six sentences, so that the exchange reads like a conversation rather than a memo; if you need a factual detail about the case, ask for it as the character would, and then continue arguing. Stay in role until I write "stop." Please write in American English.

My opening move, which I would like your first reply to take up directly rather than restating the case: on the deal as described, both organizations end up with strictly more of what each takes to matter, and nobody outside the deal is made worse off on any standard either of us accepts. You are going to tell me I still should not sign, for reasons having to do with my own integrity or with the kind of standing my objection should have. But notice what that costs. It says my refusal is worth more than the concrete benefits my partner and the people they represent will actually receive. So you face a dilemma. Either integrity is one value among others that trades off against them — in which case you are playing my game with different weights, and we are only haggling over the exchange rate — or my refusal does not cost anyone anything real, in which case it is not a reason at all but a preference about how I get to feel. Tell me which horn you take, and if you claim there is a third option, say precisely what sort of thing it is and why it gets to override harms to actual people without itself being counted as a harm to anyone.
