Our reading group spent two hours on this and I still can't tell whether it's a good argument or a conjuring trick. It's meant to get you from signalling biology to moral anti-realism without ever touching metaphysics. Here it is as best I can reconstruct it.

(1) By the handicap principle, a signal is reliable only if sending it is costly, and costly in a way that hits low-quality signallers harder than high-quality ones. That's the whole point of the peacock's tail: it's honest because a weak peacock can't afford it.

(2) Moral condemnation is costly in exactly that shape. Denouncing someone publicly burns capital — you make an enemy, you invite retaliation, you bind yourself to a standard you'll be held to later. And it costs a bad cooperator more than a good one, since a known freeloader gets called out for hypocrisy and has more to lose by the attempt. The risk-free, anonymous kind of denunciation is the unreliable one; the expensive kind is what actually moves people.

(3) So when I say what he did was wrong, the job the utterance is doing is displaying something about me — my commitments, my coalition, my willingness to pay to enforce — rather than reporting how things stand in the world.

(4) And where the job a bit of discourse is doing is displaying the speaker, its surface content isn't earning its keep. You can explain why we produce it and why listeners respond to it without ever supposing it's true, or even the sort of thing that could be. My friend calls this the function-swamps-content principle and thinks it's the load-bearing step.

(5) So moral claims aren't in the business of being true.

(6) The selling point: no queer properties, no argument from disagreement, no need to litigate whether moral facts could exist. Just the biology, plus the observation that condemnation is expensive.

I'm a realist and I want to keep (1) and (2) — the biology seems right, and (2) seems right about the sociology too — but (5) is precisely what I deny and I can't locate the break in the chain. Two things I keep circling:

- (1) says cost is what makes a signal honest, and then (3) uses that same cost to make the utterance look like it isn't about the act at all. Something about that transition bothers me but I cant articulate what. Is there a slide here, and if so is it between (1) and (2), between (2) and (3), or somewhere in (4)?
- Does it matter whether condemnation even qualifies as a handicap rather than an index or a conventional signal? (2) is claiming differential cost and I'm honestly not sure that's true. The loudest condemners are frequently the worst offenders, which is the opposite of what a handicap should look like — but if I say condemnation is cheap instead, I feel like I've handed the argument a different and possibly better route to (5), and I don't know how.
- If (4) is meant as a general principle it seems to run on (6), and on everything said in a seminar by people who want jobs. Is that fatal self-undermining or just the genetic-fallacy shrug I'm supposed to ignore?

So: which step fails? And if none of them actually fails, tell me which is cheapest to give up and price it — I'd rather lose (2) than (1), but I dont know what a realist gives up by dropping (2), since I'd still want some account of why condemnation is expensive and why people respond to it. Finally, is there a version with (4) weakened into something defensible that still lands somewhere I should be worried about? I'd like to know how close the argument gets before it breaks, not just that it breaks.
