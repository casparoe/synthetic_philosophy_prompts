For eleven years I have kept the minutes for a small volunteer-run hardship fund in my town. We raise roughly forty thousand dollars a year, we can help about one applicant in four, and since this spring we publish a one-paragraph written reason for every decision we make, which is why an argument two of our trustees had last month matters more than it otherwise would. I have no academic training in philosophy. What I have is eleven years of sitting at a table while people work out, out loud, who deserves what. Two of the trustees, Nora and Emmett, went several rounds on this, and I wrote the exchange down as it happened. Neither of them has any more training than I do. I would like you to referee it.

The case that started it involved two applicants, both now unable to work, both asking for the same sum. The first is a woman in her late fifties with a degenerative joint condition she was born with a predisposition to; she worked as a school secretary for thirty years, saved steadily, and watched those savings go to surgeries and unpaid time off. The second is a man of about the same age with comparable damage to his hands and back, worsened by twenty years of loading freight — work he took because it paid better than anything else within thirty miles — who had also, on two separate occasions, put what he had saved into restaurant ventures that failed. On paper their needs are identical.

Emmett's first move: the fund should not treat them alike, and the reason has nothing to do with need but with how each arrived. The woman's condition was dealt to her; the man spent his buffer on bets he chose. If we pay him the same amount, we are asking the careful to finance the incautious. He added that a fund which never asks how a person got here will eventually be resented by the people who donate to it, and that this resentment is not snobbery but a legitimate sense of unfairness.

Nora's reply: that cannot be operated as a rule, because almost no misfortune arrives without a choice somewhere in its causal history. The man's hands were wrecked by freight work he never enjoyed; he took it because it was the best-paying job in the county, which was itself a consequence of the mill closing, which he had no part in. If the mere presence of a choice somewhere in the history is enough to make a person responsible for the outcome, then everyone is responsible, and Emmett's principle collapses into the claim that the needy brought it on themselves.

Emmett's counter: she was flattening his view. The question is not whether a choice appears in the history but whether the person had a reasonable alternative and could have foreseen the risk. On that standard the freight work is beside the point; the two restaurant gambles are the point, since anyone can see that a second venture of that kind, launched after the first one failed, is close to a coin flip. He then pressed her: if she genuinely cannot draw any line here, she has no grounds to refuse an applicant who deliberately drained the fund either — and she does refuse such applicants — so she is drawing a line whether or not she will acknowledge it.

Nora's answer: she draws one, but at deception and at deliberate taking, cases where a person harmed the fund rather than merely lived badly. Emmett's reasonable-alternative test sounds administrable until one tries to apply it, because it converts a Tuesday-night meeting into an assessment of whether a stranger's life choices were the correct ones. Once we start crediting some gambles and not others, we will credit the ones that resemble our own. And a fund that grades applicants' judgment is doing something worse than being arbitrary: it is being humiliating while announcing that it is being fair.

Emmett's response: she had abandoned the argument for the mood. There is a difference, he said, between assessing a choice and assessing a person, and we already do the first constantly — we check whether an applicant owns a car, whether family live nearby, whether a landlord is the reason they moved twice in a year. He then named a cost of her rule that she had not advertised: it means a lifetime of care buys a person nothing at the moment help is needed, so the careful pay in twice, once through their own sacrifices and once through their donations. As for our judging by our own lights, he said that is an argument for writing our reasons down, not an argument for refusing to have any.

Nora's last move: then he had quietly given up the position he began from. The claim that the careful pay in twice is not a claim about how the applicants arrived at need; it is a claim about what the careful are owed. It would apply just as forcefully to two people whose misfortunes were equally a matter of bad luck — the secretary who saved and the man whose hands were ruined by freight, neither of whom bet on anything. If the careful are owed something for their carefulness, we owe it to them whether or not the other applicant ever made a bad choice, and the distinction between dealt misfortune and chosen misfortune is not carrying the weight he has been putting on it.

Emmett did not answer this one. We adjourned, split the difference between the two applicants as we usually do, and the fund has been uneasy about it since.

What I would like from you:

1. Go through the six moves in order. For each, say whether it holds as stated, holds only if it is repaired (and if so, supply the repair), or fails — and in each case say what it actually establishes, which is often less than the speaker took it to establish.

2. Say whether Nora's final move is the knockout that Emmett's silence suggests, or whether a reply was available to him that he failed to find. If a reply exists, put it in the strongest form you can manage and then say whether it survives her earlier objection about grading strangers' judgment.

3. There is a distinction of my own that I have been turning over for some time and that neither of them articulated. I suspect that some of what happens to us we steer into and some of it merely arrives, and that the difference between steering and arriving may not be a difference in the world at all but a difference in how much of ourselves we can recognize in the outcome — which would mean the line Emmett wants is real but sits in the applicant rather than in the event. Tell me whether this does any work here or whether it only restates the difficulty in prettier language.

4. Say what neither of them said that should have been said at that table.

5. Give me two verdicts and keep them apart: who is more right about how this particular fund should have handled those two applicants, and who is more right about the underlying principle. I suspect these may not come out the same way, and if they do not, I want to understand what makes them come apart.

Please do not send me a reading list, a set of names, or an account of what has been written on the subject; I could not chase any of it down, and it is not what I am asking for. Argue from the exchange itself. I would much rather receive a definite verdict I can push back against than an even-handed summary of both sides.
