I TA'd a small seminar on incompleteness last term and one session was the MU puzzle — you start from MI, and you've got the four rewrite moves (tack a U onto the end of anything ending in I; double whatever comes after the M; trade any III for a U; delete any UU), and the ask is to produce MU. Half the class spent an hour grinding out derivations and getting nowhere, which is kind of the point, and afterwards I started writing down which of the things people did I'd count as actually jumping out of the system and which I wouldn't. The list has a kink in it I can't get straight, and my friend who's way more into logic than me just said I was being inconsistent, so now I want to check it properly.

Here's where I land, case by case. (1) The student who stopped pushing symbols, noticed the count of I's can never land on a multiple of three, and concluded MU is out of reach because MU has zero I's — textbook jump, obviously, they left the string game and started talking about the strings. (2) The guy who wrote a breadth-first search over a few million derivations, found nothing, and said "so it's probably impossible" — I don't call that a jump at all, it's just the same grinding, only faster and by proxy, even though his script did more work in a minute than any of us did all session. (3) A classmate who took the exact same mod-3 argument and formalized it — coded the strings as numbers, proved the invariant by induction inside Peano arithmetic, done. I want to say that's not a jump either, because now it's just another system mechanically churning out a result. But the content is literally identical to (1), which I called the paradigm jump. That's the kink. (4) A student who proposed adding a fifth rule, "you may delete a single I," after which MU falls out in two steps — not a jump, that's rigging the game so you win it. (5) But then when we got to the Gödel sentence of a system that can talk about its own proofs, and someone said "fine, just move up to a stronger system that can prove it," I said yes, that's a real jump out. Which looks structurally identical to (4): you changed the rules until the thing you wanted became derivable. (6) Someone who just said "it can't be done, trust me," with no reason, and happened to be right — not a jump, just a lucky guess. (7) Someone who said "MU is underivable therefore the system must be inconsistent" — not a jump, just wrong. (8) The Lucas-Penrose move, where a person is handed the Gödel sentence of some formalization of their own reasoning and says "well I can see it's true anyway" — I don't count that as a jump, mostly because I suspect the formalization isn't actually of them. But I'm not sure that's principled, since it seems to boil down to "I only credit jumps I already agree with."

So: is there a principle, statable without smuggling in words like real insight or genuine understanding, that gets all or most of these? I'd like it laid out clause by clause with which case motivates each clause, and I'd like to know what it says about ones I haven't thought of — like a student who finds the invariant by staring at a histogram of the search program's output rather than by thinking, an LLM that produces the mod-3 argument cold because it's seen the puzzle a thousand times, someone who jumps out and lands somewhere false, and someone who alters the rules not to make MU derivable but to make the impossibility result provable. And be honest about which of my eight verdicts the principle has to break: I suspect it's either (3) or the (4)/(5) pair, but I don't know which one to sacrifice, and I don't know whether it even makes sense to call jumping-out a property of a move in the first place, rather than a property of a move relative to some particular box you're standing in when you make it.
