I'm a fourth-year PhD student in political theory, and I need help auditing an analogy that has quietly become load-bearing in my dissertation chapter on the counter-majoritarian difficulty.

My supervisor's draft defends strong-form judicial review — final, judicially supplied answers on contested questions of rights that legislatures cannot revisit by ordinary majority vote — by way of the Homeric precommitment story: Ulysses has himself lashed to the mast so that he can hear the sirens without steering toward them, and a constitution with judicial review is a democratic people lashing itself to the mast so that it can survive its own predictable episodes of panic, prejudice, and short-termism. On this picture, unelected judges overruling legislatures are not antagonists of self-government but its instruments: the rope is the people's own earlier, calmer hand. When I pressed him on the vagueness of constitutional text, he retreated to a second analogy — judges as umpires who call the balls and strikes but never pitch or bat — and he treats the two as mutually supporting. I am not convinced either does the work he needs, and I want the audit done carefully before our department workshop.

Please give me the following, in this order:

1. An explicit correspondence table for the mast analogy: what plays Ulysses, the crew, the mast and rope, the sirens, the moment of clarity on shore, the anticipated distortion, and the instruction not to untie him. Be concrete about which political actors and institutions each slot is filled by, since the mapping is doing the argumentative work.

2. A list of the disanalogies, sorted into those that are merely picturesque and those that are fatal to the specific use my supervisor makes of it. I want particular attention to: whether Ulysses binds his limbs or his judgment; whether the binder and the bound are the same agent; whether the anticipated distortion is episodic and externally triggered or a standing condition of reasonable disagreement; who supplies the standard by which a later self is judged distorted rather than merely changed; and whether the rope's instructions are self-executing or must be interpreted by the very body they constrain.

3. An account of what the analogy smuggles in — the empirical and normative commitments a listener must already accept for the story to feel like an argument, including anything about the asymmetry of judicial versus legislative error.

4. Your verdict on whether the umpire analogy rescues the position or inherits the same problems, and whether it is consistent with the mast analogy or in tension with it.

5. A design question I want answered on the merits rather than by appeal to authority: does any version of the precommitment analogy support strong-form review, or does it at best support entrenchment coupled with a genuinely cheap amendment path or an override provision of the Canadian kind? Consider my own proposal — a judicial ruling on a contested rights question that expires after a fixed term unless reconfirmed by the court, with the legislature free to respond in the interval — and tell me whether the mast analogy endorses it, condemns it, or is simply silent about it.

6. If you think a better analogy exists for whatever survives the audit, propose one and state precisely what it can and cannot carry.

I would rather have a clear-headed negative result than a defense of my supervisor. Please do not pad this with a reading list; I want the reasoning itself.
