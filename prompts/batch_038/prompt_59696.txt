The question we set was:

"Explain why, on Dworkin's account, an advance directive can still bind a patient with advanced dementia who now seems content and offers no resistance to treatment. What is the strongest objection to this, and does it succeed? (800 words maximum.)"

The entry runs to about 500 words and reads:

"Suppose Margaret signed a directive twenty years ago refusing antibiotics for pneumonia, and refusing tube feeding, once she could no longer recognise her family; and suppose that now, in advanced dementia, she smiles at her carers, enjoys music and makes no objection to being treated. Should the directive be followed?

Dworkin's answer is that it should, and it turns on a distinction between two kinds of interest. Experiential interests are interests in how things feel: pleasure, comfort, the absence of pain. Critical interests are interests in how ones life goes as a whole, in its being the sort of life one can endorse — a life that is not, on one's own view, wasted or degrading. Advanced dementia damages the first kind of interest very little; Margaret may still be having a pleasant enough time. But it wrecks the second, because the life she is now living is the life she judged to be no life at all. Dworkin's claim is that critical interests carry a special authority, and that this authority is not cancelled by the fact that the person in the bed can no longer form critical interests of her own. To disregard the directive would be to make Margaret's life worse as a whole in the way that matters most to her. These critical interests are essentially Frankfurt's second-order desires — the reflective wants that are entitled to overrule the immediate ones — so the directive binds because it records what Margaret most reflectively wanted.

The obvious objection is that this sacrifices the person who is actually there. This is Dresser's point, developed around a real patient known as Margo, who had advanced dementia, painted, listened to music and was reported by those who knew her to be happy. Dresser's objection is that by that stage Margo was no longer the same person, so the directive does not reach her at all: there is nobody left for Margaret's earlier decision to be a decision about. Parfit's reductionist account of personal identity supports this. On Parfit a person just is a chain of psychological connections, and when those connections are broken the later person is literally someone else. Since Margaret shares almost no psychological continuity with the woman in the bed, Dworkin's talk of a life going badly as a whole is empty — that life is not being lived by anyone who is Margaret.

Jaworska pushes back. On her account Margo retains the capacity to value things, and that is enough for full moral standing, so the present patient cannot simply be written out of the decision. Buchanan and Brock make a related point from the other direction: the authority of a directive need not rest on identity at all, because what it records are the patient's own values, and respecting those values is a way of respecting her even if strict identity is in doubt.

Where does that leave us? If identity in these cases is genuinely uncertain — and on Parfit's own view it may be — then we cannot know whose directive it is. So the directive should be treated as advisory only: strong evidence of what the patient valued, but not something that overrides the interests of the person actually in front of us."
