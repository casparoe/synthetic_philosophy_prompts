I help program a 60-seat rep house and in March we're doing a Kaufman double bill — Being John Malkovich paired with Synecdoche, New York — and I'm doing the ten-minute talk before the first one. The angle I keep landing on is Douglas Hofstadter's account of the self in I Am a Strange Loop: that an "I" isn't a substance but a pattern, a strange loop the brain spins up and that then, weirdly, pushes back down on the neurons doing the spinning, so the self isn't nailed to any particular lump of matter and could in principle be realized somewhere else. The part I actually want to build the talk around is the consequence he draws from that — since a self is a pattern with no volume, it doesn't have to stop at the skull wall. On his picture, people who know you well are running a working model of you inside their own heads, and to that degree a piece of your loop is genuinely instantiated there, which means selves are always somewhat smeared across more than one brain, and being-the-same-person comes in degrees instead of on/off. He tends to make this sound like the most consoling fact in the world, something about love. Im not sure the movie agrees with him, and I'd rather find that out from you than from the audience.

So read the film through that picture and tell me where it holds and where it snaps. Quick recap of the beats I'm leaning on, in case your memory of it is as blurry as mine: Craig Schwartz, out-of-work puppeteer, takes a filing job on the floor squeezed between the 7th and 8th of the Mertin-Flemmer building, finds a little door behind a cabinet, crawls through and gets fifteen minutes riding behind John Malkovich's eyes before being dumped on the New Jersey Turnpike; he and Maxine start selling the ride; his wife Lotte goes through and falls for Maxine, who only wants her while she's inside Malkovich; Craig turns out to have real control of the body, moves in for eight months, makes Malkovich a famous puppeteer, marries Maxine; Dr. Lester is really Captain Mertin, hopping bodies since the 1800s, and a vessel goes ripe at midnight on its 44th birthday, miss it and you're sealed into a newborn; so on the birthday Lester and his friends pour in, and Craig, too late for Malkovich and too early to steer the kid, ends up trapped inside little Emily watching Lotte and Maxine be happy. Plus the bit where Malkovich goes through his own door into a world where every face is his face and the only word anybody says is his name. What I want from you: does the portal metaphysics — a self that can be *in* a location, that fits through a doorway, that occupies a vessel someone else has to vacate — even make sense on a view where the self is a pattern with no volume? Every transfer in this film is an eviction, one occupant per body, which is zero-sum real estate, and Hofstadter's interpenetration is exactly the opposite, non-zero-sum, nobody gets kicked out when somebody else takes up residence in you. Is the film just uninterested in that distinction, or is it quietly arguing against it? Treat Craig's puppetry seriously too, since the puppets are the one place in the movie where a person's agency really is spread outside their body and nobody calls it possession, and it's the thing that gets him the girl and the career. Somebody should tell me whether the all-Malkovich world is a picture of what a strange loop looks like when there's nothing outside it to differentiate against. And the ending: Craig inside Emily with no purchase on her at all, no way to close a loop and push back down — is he still a self on Hofstadter's account, or has he stopped being one and become a memory that happens to be looking out of a child's eyes? Meanwhile Lester's crew, several minds steering one aging body, might be the only real distributed "I" in the film, which would mean the movie answers Hofstadter's question yes, but grotesquely, and for money. Push back on any of that if you think I'm forcing it; a talk that's honest about where the analogy fails is worth more to me than a slick one.
