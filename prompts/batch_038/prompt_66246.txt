Last night I spent roughly four hours arguing with a friend — she is a third-year philosophy graduate student, I am a programming-languages researcher who works mostly on denotational semantics and effect systems — about the Frege–Geach problem for expressivism, and I came away persuaded that we were talking past each other because I do not actually possess her vocabulary. She was less defending expressivism than trying to explain why the standard objection to it is not fatal. I kept insisting that what she was describing was simply a failure to give a compositional semantics for a fragment of a language: say what an expression denotes, treat assertion as a separate matter — an evaluation context, or a top-level command, or whatever — and the puzzle about moral sentences turning up as the antecedents of conditionals dissolves into bookkeeping. Her reply was that my reformulation already assumes the thing in dispute, since on her account a moral sentence does not denote anything at all, and the hard question is what could possibly play the role of a denotation when the sentence occurs embedded rather than asserted. She also said her side has proposed repairs involving attitudes toward combinations of attitudes, and something about accepting systems of norms, and I confess I followed neither, partly because at that hour I could no longer tell whether she was describing a semantics or a psychology.

Here is what I would like from you. Restate the dispute between expressivism and the Frege–Geach objection — the objection itself, the expressivist's reasons for resisting a truth-conditional treatment of moral sentences, and at least two of the repair strategies available on the expressivist side — in the vocabulary I actually use: expressions and values, denotations, typing judgments, evaluation contexts, compositionality as a property one proves about a semantics rather than a hope one has about a language, substitution and referential transparency, suspended computations and the difference between a thunk and its forcing, side effects and the annotations that track them, and the distinction between what a program means and what happens when it runs. Work claim by claim rather than in summary. Above all, do not smuggle: if the only way to render a given philosophical claim in my terms is to hand moral sentences denotations and layer assertion on top as force, say so plainly and mark it as a distortion rather than presenting it as neutral translation. I specifically want to know where my "it is just a compositionality failure" intuition was doing unearned work, and where an effect-style or evaluation-context framing secretly imports the very content/force separation she denies me.

Then give me the residue report. Which of her claims survive intact? Which survive only after being bent out of shape, and what exactly is lost in the bending — inferential validity as truth-preservation, the higher-order attitudes, the normativity of the whole enterprise? Which have no expression at all in my vocabulary; I suspect the claims about what it is to be competent with moral language, and about the role an utterance plays in a life rather than in a calculus, are candidates, but tell me if I am wrong about that. For each item that fails to translate, I want your judgment on which side the defect sits: is my framework simply missing a dimension that a semantics for natural language has to model — commitments, say, or the fact that utterances are acts rather than computations — or does the failure show that the philosophers' disagreement is less substantive than it sounded at two in the morning? And if the residue is genuine, tell me whether some technical notion I already have (effects and handlers, continuations, polarity, the assertion boundary in a sequent presentation) could be extended to carry it, or whether what I need is not a better formalism but a different question.
