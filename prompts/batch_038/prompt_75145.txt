I got an ADHD diagnosis at 33 and that's how I ended up in a group chat plus a once-a-month meetup of about forty people, nearly all diagnosed as adults, a few self-diagnosed, a handful of partners who tag along. We have the same argument every couple of months and it never ends. It goes: someone says they didn't have half the traits on the list until they read the list and now they have all eleven; someone else says that's just finally noticing what was always there; someone else says no, the label really did make them more like that, which is why they're careful who they tell; someone else says the label didn't change them, it changed how people treat them, and those are two completely different claims. Nobody ever wins because we're all saying the same sentences and meaning different things.

I want to build a one-page decision tree to hand out at the meetup — basically "where do you actually stand on whether the diagnosis made you." Not a quiz with a right answer, a sorting thing. I've got no academic background at all, I've thought about this on my own, and I heard someone on a podcast once describe the idea that classifying people changes the people being classified, which is roughly what I'm circling. Don't send me a reading list or names of books, I won't read them.

Here's what I need.

Seven to nine nodes. Each node is a concrete situation with two or three ways to answer, worded so someone can pick without knowing which position their pick leads to. No node should be an abstract question like "do you think diagnoses are social constructs" — if it is, half my group will answer with their team instead of their brain.

Every leaf gets a plain-English name and one line saying what it claims about the person who got diagnosed. If there's a standard label for that position among people who study this, put it in brackets, but the tree has to work fine without the brackets.

Please use some of my cases, since these are the ones that actually come up:

1. The checklist one. Someone swears they had maybe two of the traits before they looked them up and eleven now. Is that noticing, becoming, or both — and does the answer shift if the eleven are things they'd have described differently before, versus things other people also noticed as new?
2. The 1998 kid. A kid in 1998 gets called hyperactive and handled as a problem; the same kid, same brain, in 2024 gets called ADHD and handled as someone whose brain runs differently. Where does the difference live — in the kid, in the people around the kid, or in what counts as a symptom?
3. The dropped-label one. When the manual got rid of Asperger's as its own diagnosis in 2013, some people who had that label lost it without changing one bit. Did they lose a fact about themselves, or just a word?
4. The scan one. If there were a perfect brain scan that told you the same thing the questionnaire does, with no community and no paperwork attached, would the label still change people? I want a node on this because half my group says the loop needs other people in it and half says it only needs you talking to yourself.
5. Two people, one score. Two people score identically; one is told, one isn't. Six months later, do they differ in kind, in degree, or only in paperwork?
6. The story one. I want a node that seperates two things my group always mushes together: the label changed what you do, versus the label changed what you can say about what you already did. I think a lot of us rewrote our own childhoods afterwards and nothing else changed, and I can't work out whether that counts as the label changing you.

Leaves that have to appear somewhere, in whatever words you like: the traits were there and the label found them and it doesn't respond to being found; the label changed the person and not just the paperwork — and I want that one split in two, a version where the change is only in how the trait gets expressed and a version where there's genuinely no fact about what you'd have been like unlabeled; the label changed your situation (school, boss, family, how you get treated) but not you; the kind is real but couldn't have existed before there was a word for it, which isn't the same as calling it made up; the loop runs the other way too, where the people who get the label push the criteria around so the manual is downstream of us — I think the dropped-label case and the checklist case belong here; and nothing loops at all, labels are shorthand, every report of change is memory getting tidied up afterwards.

Then three things at the end:

- Which pairs of leaves your tree can't really seperate, and what extra question would split them, or why no question could.
- Which combinations of answers nobody actually gives — the ones your tree makes logically available but where no leaf sits. For each empty spot, say why it's empty: is the combination incoherent, is it just unappealing, or has nobody thought of it yet?
- For each node, mark whether the disagreement there is still live among people who know this material properly, or whether it's basically settled and the real fighting is somewhere else on the tree. I specifically don't want the tree built so that your own view is the default path down the middle and everybody else's is a weird side branch. If a node is contested, say contested and leave it contested rather than smoothing it into a consensus that isn't there.

Whole thing needs to print on one sheet, front and back.
