I keep meaning to read up on this and I keep putting it off, so let me get the spec first instead. The question: what makes every 'a' an 'a'? Not what makes this particular mark pretty — what it is for two marks to be tokens of the same letter.

Before I look at any candidate answers I want the requirements list: what an account of letterform identity has to deliver. For each requirement give me the case or consideration behind it, and say whether its non-negotiable or just something one camp insists on. Here's the raw material ive been collecting — use what's useful, drop what isn't, add your own:

- a double-storey a (Times, most serif faces) and a single-storey a (Futura, Comic Sans, children's primers, most people's handwriting) share essentially no outline features, and yet nobody hesitates for a second about calling them the same letter.
- mirror a b horizontally and you get a d — a different letter. Mirror an a and most people, shown it, say "that's a backwards a," not "that's some other letter." Why does reflection change the identity in one case and merely damage it in the other? whatever the answer is, it should fall out of the theory rather than being bolted on afterward.
- rotate an n 180 degrees and you get a u; rotate a W and you get an M; rotate a b and you get a q. Rigid transforms sometimes preserve the letter, sometimes swap it for a neighbor, sometimes produce garbage.
- the Latin C and the Cyrillic Es are the same shape in most fonts. Same letter?
- Morse a is dot-dash, braille a is one raised dot, ASCII a is 0x61. If those are all the letter a, then shape and sound have both dropped out of the account and I want to know what's left that gets carried over. If they aren't, say what the relation between them and the printed a actually is.
- a in cat, in father, in cake. Three different sounds, one letter. And c in cat vs city. So the sound-based account dies fast — but any account still owes me a reason why sound feels like it ought to matter.
- in plenty of fonts lowercase l, uppercase I and the digit 1 are the same mark. Is the mark a letter, a letter, or a number?
- A and a: one letter or two? They sort together, they're different code points, and a typeface can contain one without the other. Whatever relation holds between A and a must not also hold between A and B, and that's a constraint on the theory, not a slogan.
- æ is a ligature in English and a full letter of the alphabet in Danish. ß vs ss. The long s, which about half of us now read as an f.
- a manuscript nobody has been able to read in five hundred years still contains letters, presumably. But a four-year-old who produces a mark that looks exactly like an a while telling you she's drawing a cat has not written one.
- and the one I care about most: my own handwriting. My a and my g and my x look like they came from the same hand even though they don't share any feature I can name. Any account that only ever asks about one mark at a time is going to miss that, and I want to know whether it's a requirement on letter identity or a separate problem that gets kicked next door.

Then tell me which requirement is doing the most work killing candidates — my guess is the Morse/braille one but I could easily be wrong. And flag any pair you think can't be jointly satisfied; I have a feeling something like "shape can't be what matters" and "shape obviously matters" is in there, but I can't make it precise and I'd like you to. When the list is done, briefly — two or three sentences each, no more — say how a pure-shape account, a pure-system-role account, and a "there's no essence here, it's family resemblance all the way down" account do against it.

Purpose, in case it changes the answer: I want a theory good enough that a program could use it to decide whether some glyph it's never seen before is an a. So nothing in the requirements can lean on facts the program couldn't in principle check.
