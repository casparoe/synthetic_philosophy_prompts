Last night I spent something like four hours in a kitchen argument with a friend who is finishing a dissertation in archaeobotany, and we ended up precisely nowhere on the question of whether the Neolithic transition was, on balance, a catastrophe for human beings. This matters to me more than it should, because I have been conscripted into a departmental debate in the spring and cast on the pro-agriculture side, and I would rather not be the one person in the room who has never heard the strongest version of the other case. So I want to keep the argument going, but against a better opponent than I had at midnight. Please play a committed and well-armed defender of the thesis that the move from foraging to farming made life worse — not a romantic about noble savages, not someone who thinks the Paleolithic was an idyll, but a scholar who knows the skeletal and isotopic record cold and has spent a career arguing that it documents a real decline: diets narrowed onto a handful of cereal staples, caries and abscesses, bands of enamel hypoplasia marking repeated bouts of childhood stress, several centimeters of lost stature, the anemia signatures read off porotic hyperostosis even while conceding that their etiology is disputed, the zoonotic load that comes from sleeping next to your food, and the disappearance of the slack time that the classic forager time-allocation work seemed to reveal when food-getting came in at something like two or three days a week.

Rules for the exchange. Stay in character until I say "stop," and keep each reply to two or three paragraphs, since this should feel like an argument and not a position paper. You may concede anything that happens to be true — that foragers were often violent, that infant mortality was savage, that no one chose the transition and it functioned as a ratchet rather than a decision, that some river-valley farmers were perfectly well fed — but you may not concede the main claim, and you must have an actual answer to the objection below rather than a shrug. Do not step out of character to append caveats about how the issue is complex and scholars disagree, and do not send me a bibliography, a reading list, or a catalogue of named studies; I want the reasoning, not the references. If you need a fact from me, ask for it the way your character would ask it.

My opening move is the one my friend kept returning to, so let me put it to you cleanly. Whatever happened to individual bodies, farming supported orders of magnitude more of them. If those extra lives were on balance worth living, then your catastrophe is a catastrophe only in the mean and not in the total: you are grieving a dip in average welfare while standing on top of the largest expansion of lives worth living in the history of our species, and every subsequent improvement in the human condition runs downstream of it. So tell me plainly — whose loss was it? It cannot be the loss of the billions who exist and would otherwise never have existed, since you do not harm someone by bringing them into a life worth living; and it cannot be the loss of any particular forager, since the transition was slow enough and dispersed enough that no one who was ever born would have been born had it not occurred. If the answer turns out to be that it was nobody's loss, then your thesis looks aesthetic rather than ethical, and you have mistaken a change in the shape of human life for a wrong done to anyone at all.
