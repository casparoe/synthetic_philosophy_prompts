I've got a seminar coming up where I have to argue against the strong version of the "Germany is to blame for 1914" line — the one that came out of the big German historiography fight in the sixties and never quite went away. My tutor holds it, so I want to practice on you first.

Play the last convinced defender of that line. Not a cartoon: he's read the critics and all the centenary stuff about everybody sleepwalking into it, he knows the Kaiser was a mess and that the Russians mobilized too, and he's happy to conciede small things — that "inevitable" is too strong, that Vienna had its own reasons for wanting to hit Serbia, that you can't read 1914 backwards from 1939. What he will not concede is that Germany carries a special, nameable responsibility for turning a Balkan murder into a general war. He also thinks a nation can be the sort of thing that has intentions, and that this isn't just a fancy way of talking. Stay in character until I say stop. Keep it to two or three short paragraphs so it feels like a conversation, and dont hand me reading lists or footnotes — argue it out instead. Don't invent documents: if you bring up a piece of evidence, tell me in your own words what it says and what you take it to show. And if you think my opening move is confused, say so rather than humoring me.

My opening: you write sentences like "Germany willed the war." Tell me what the subject of that sentence is. In July 1914 the people who could actually decide anything fit around one table in Berlin and Potsdam, and they didn't agree with each other. Bethmann was trying to keep the thing local and still half hoping the British would stay out; the Kaiser got cold feet when the Serbian reply came in and started floating the idea of stopping at Belgrade; Moltke thought war sooner was better because the window of opportunity was closing. So when you say Germany wanted a war, who is the Germany doing the wanting? If it's just those handful of men, then say that and drop the country from the sentence. If it's something over and above them — the press, the pressure groups, the mood of the officer corps — then you've swapped a claim about decisions for a claim about atmosphere, and atmosphere isn't the kind of thing that can be guilty of anything. Which is it?
