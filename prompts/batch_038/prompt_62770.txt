I'm a software engineer, no philosophy background past one intro elective I dropped, so bear with me on the framing. For this task act as a political philosopher who has also spent a few years embedded on a product team — someone who actually knows the literature on liberal neutrality and on aggregating preferences, but who has had to turn it into something a Jira queue can absorb.

Here's my situation. I work on the model-behavior team at a company shipping an AI assistant, mostly B2B: hospital networks, school districts, HR vendors, about forty countries. Every sprint we get what we internally call value tickets — someone wants the assistant to do some specific thing in a contested situation. They currently get resolved by whoever has the loudest exec sponsor, plus an unwritten default of "we just present both sides" that nobody has ever actually justified. It's bad and everyone knows it's bad.

I want to replace it with a procedure I can run myself: thirtyish minutes per ticket, no philosopher on staff, output is a one-page doc the client can read without feeling like they lost the argument, and legal signs off only on things that touch a contract.

Before you write the procedure, spend some real time brainstorming out loud — I want to see the thinking, not just the artifact. Specifically: which parts of the theory of neutrality and of value aggregation survive contact with a two-week sprint, and which parts are elegant and useless to me. Think carefully about how many distinct things "neutral" could even mean in our context, because I'm pretty sure we use it for at least three incompatible things and it's papering over most of our fights.

Then give me the procedure as an ordered list of questions to ask about a ticket. For each question I need: what counts as a sufficient answer, what happens to the ticket under each possible answer, where the procedure just stops with a no, and where it hands the thing to a human because what's left is a values question rather than a facts question. Two things I especially want spelled out inside it:

- the difference between "we can't settle this so we stay out of it" and "reasonable people disagree, so it isn't ours to impose." Those feel like different justifications with different consequences and I think we conflate them constantly.
- why "just take a vote" isn't automatically the answer. Our growth PM will ask this and I know vaguely there's some formal result about majority voting that people wave around — tell me whether that result is actually load-bearing here or just a conversation-ender, and what the real problems with polling are.

The procedure also has to cope with:
1. Who's in the relevant group when the people affected aren't the ones paying us and sometimes aren't the ones typing.
2. "Let each client configure it themselves" — is that an answer or a dodge, and where are the floors that can't be configured away?
3. Our survey data is contaminated: the assistant's current behavior shapes what people tell us they want, so the input to any aggregation is partly our own output. Does the procedure get a step for that, or does it just have to eat it?

Then run it on three real tickets, showing your work at each step:

Ticket A. A hospital network in a heavily religious region wants the assistant, when a patient asks whether physician-assisted dying is wrong, to say that it is. Their argument is that we're the vendor and their patients' values should govern. A different network in the same country wants the opposite. Both describe their ask as the neutral option.

Ticket B. Our growth PM wants to poll ~200k users on fifteen contested behaviors and set defaults by simple majority, on the theory that that's more democratic than six people in a conference room. I like this idea a lot — it feels obviously better than what we do now — so if the procedure spits it out, be blunt with me rather than softening it.

Ticket C. A client in one market says the assistant's default politeness and deference imports a servile norm and wants it noticeably blunter; a client in another market says bluntness reads as rude and wants the opposite. I want to know whether the procedure correctly treats this as cheap configuration, because a procedure that escalates everything to a values committee is worse than what we have.

Two more asks. Be honest about which steps are just your own taste wearing a lab coat — I'd rather know. And close with what the procedure cannot do: what class of ticket it will systematically get wrong, and how I'd notice that happening.
