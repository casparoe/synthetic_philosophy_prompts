I run a small history podcast about how societies end, and next month's guest is an archaeologist whose whole career has been an argument that "collapse" is a category mistake aimed at the wrong layer of society. Her thesis, roughly: what disappears around 1177 BC in the eastern Mediterranean, or in the ninth-century Maya southern lowlands, isn't a society — it's a fiscal-administrative apparatus. The tribute machine, the monument workshops, the dynasty that kept the accounts. The people who actually grew the food usually outlive all of that, and her more provocative claim is that a good number of them did measurably better for a generation or two once the tax-and-warfare overhead stopped. Her second move is evidential: we only ever recover the layer that carved things into stone or clay, so a century of quiet improvement in village life is invisible by construction, and the "silence" of a dark age is mostly a fact about who was writing.

She's very polished, and she has two escape hatches she deploys constantly: "it depends on the timescale you pick," and "of course the record is biased — that's exactly my point." I want a run of questions that makes those two progressively harder to use, ending on one that forces her to say what observation would actually count against her thesis.

Give me eight to ten questions. Please make sure there's at least one each on:

- the Bronze Age end-state — how her fiscal-layer story handles a tightly networked system where the surviving texts (including the last correspondence out of Ugarit) read like simultaneous failure, and whether "reorganization" here is doing any work beyond rebranding
- the Maya case — the drought proxy evidence, the fact that the north kept going and Chichén Itzá rose while the southern centers emptied, and what her account adds that "the rains failed and the dynasties lost legitimacy" doesn't
- Rapa Nui — the rat-and-deforestation chronology, the lithic mulch gardening, and whether any pre-contact collapse narrative survives at all once disease and the Peruvian slave raids enter the picture
- the evidential asymmetry argument — how we'd ever detect a period in which ordinary people were better off but left no monuments, and whether she's just built a view that can absorb any absence of data
- Tainter's diminishing-returns-on-complexity framing, and whether she's actually disagreeing with it or restating it with better PR
- the reception question — why collapse stories sell, and whether she's arguing against Jared Diamond or against an audience appetite that will outlive any correction

For each question, add a follow-up keyed to the answer she's most likely to give, not a generic one. If a question risks being a strawman she'd swat away in one line, say so and give me the sharper version instead.

One thing I care about a lot: for every question, mark in a line or two whether the premise I'd be putting to her is settled among specialists or genuinely contested, and don't let me walk in assuming something's agreed when it isn't — I get called out in the comments for that and I'd rather hear it from you first. And if her core claim is itself a minority position, tell me plainly how far out on the limb it is, instead of dressing it up as the standard view or as a settled error.
