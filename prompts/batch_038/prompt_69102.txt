Act as a professional historian of the Holocaust who has taught the Browning–Goldhagen dispute repeatedly and who also designs undergraduate historical-methods curricula. Think carefully about the inferential structure of the disagreement before you draft anything, and give me a plan rather than a performance of the explanation itself.

I convene a hundred-minute methods seminar for second-year history undergraduates. They have read Browning's opening account of the first massacre at Józefów and the passage near the end of *Ordinary Men* where he enumerates the factors he takes to have been at work — orders and authority, routinization, the deflection of responsibility, conformity to the group, careerism, and the gradual dehumanization of the victims — together with a substantial extract from *Hitler's Willing Executioners* and a summary of the 1996 controversy. What I want to teach them is not the debate's verdict but the thing the debate is actually a debate about: what a historian is entitled to generalize from a single, non-randomly composed unit, and how two accounts can accept every documented fact and still disagree about what explains it.

Their starting position is a liability I need you to design around. They have just come from a social-psychology unit on Milgram and Zimbardo; they were told there that the Stanford study has been widely discredited as demand-characteristic-driven and partly staged; and they have concluded that "the situation explains it" is settled science that my seminar is going to confirm. Two of them have told me they expect Browning to collapse along with Zimbardo. They also arrive believing the dispute is factual (were the men antisemites or not) and moral (were they evil or merely ordinary), and they will press me for a verdict on who won.

Some material I can put in front of them, which you should treat as available and decide how to use: Reserve Police Battalion 101 was formed in Hamburg and its rank and file were largely middle-aged, working-class reservists found ineligible for regular military service; on one occasion the commander, Wilhelm Trapp, offered the men the chance to step out if they found the task too hard, and fewer than a dozen of roughly five hundred took it; the battalion's operations in occupied Poland routinely ran alongside non-German auxiliary formations, the Trawniki men; and there are other cases, such as Reserve Police Battalion 133's action at Stanisławów in October 1941, carried out with the aid of security police and Ukrainian auxiliary police, which I am willing to introduce if it earns its place.

What I need from you:

1. Before the plan proper, a short triage of candidate carrying cases — the Józefów opening, Trapp's offer and the opt-out count, the auxiliary-police comparison, Milgram's paradigm — ruling all but one out and saying why. Commit to one case, and state what that case must have and must lack in order to do the work.

2. The dependency structure of the explanation: the order the pieces have to go in, with a sentence on what each step depends on. I specifically want to know where the meaning of "ordinary" in Browning's title has to be fixed — ordinary in age, class, and training, not ordinary in belief — and where the opt-out count has to be rendered ambiguous, since the same twelve-in-five-hundred is compatible with several incompatible explanations. Say where the charge of circularity against a culturally specific antisemitism belongs, and where the comparison with non-German auxiliaries in the same situation-type belongs relative to it.

3. A misconception map. Expect at least: situationism entails that people have no character; if Zimbardo falls, Browning falls with him; "ordinary men" means they were not antisemites; fewer than a dozen opting out proves the men wanted to kill; Goldhagen is refuted by exhibiting Germans who were reluctant; and the whole thing is an ethics question about blame. Add any I have missed. For each, tell me the exact point in the sequence where it should be headed off, and distinguish those that must be pre-empted from those the ordering will absorb on its own. Include what I should say when they demand to know who won.

4. One check for understanding to put to the room at the end — a fresh case or question they have not seen, whose answers would reveal whether they have grasped the inferential issue rather than merely retained the moral shock. Tell me what a passing answer looks like and what a fluent but failing answer looks like.

5. An explicit refusal list: what I should decline to cover at this level and announce that I am declining — the functionalist–intentionalist dispute over the origins of the Final Solution, the wider literature on other Order Police battalions, the reliability of postwar testimony arising from the Hamburg investigations, and the later litigation over denial. Say which of these I should name aloud to the students and which I should leave silently out, and why that differs.

No readings, no bibliography, no citations; assume I have the books open in front of me. Give me one plan, not a menu: where you make a choice, commit to it and give the reason, and tell me the condition under which the same choice would be wrong in a differently prepared room. If you think any of my stated expectations about these students is mistaken — in particular my prediction about the Zimbardo inference — say so directly and design against me rather than accommodating me.
