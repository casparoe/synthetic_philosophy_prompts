Retired about three years ago after 38 years running a mass spec lab, and I've been reading philosophy of mind most mornings since, slowly, with a pencil. I've got to the Chinese Room, and I want to run it against a real episode rather than against other thought experiments — that's how I'd have checked an instrument in the old days: you put it on a sample whose answer you already know. My daughter, who does HCI, thinks this is a waste of my pension. Humor me.

The episode is ELIZA. Here are the facts, since I'd rather supply them than have us squabble over details: Weizenbaum at MIT, published 1966; the program did keyword pattern-matching and substitution, and the famous configuration ran a script called DOCTOR that imitated a Rogerian therapist — which is to say it mostly handed your own words back to you as questions. It was a sensation. People confided in it, some at length, and his own secretary, who knew perfectly well what the thing was, asked him to leave the room so she could talk to it privately. Weizenbaum was appalled and spent the next decade arguing against the reading people gave his work; his 1976 book turns on a distinction between deciding, which he took to be computational, and choosing, which he didn't. One wrinkle I keep comming back to: four years before ELIZA he'd written a short piece on how to make a computer appear intelligent. So the illusion wasn't something that happened to him, it was close to the design goal; what surprised him was what people did with the illusion afterward. Searle's argument shows up in 1980, aimed at the strong AI claim that the appropriately programmed computer with the right inputs and outputs would thereby have a mind.

So: do the mapping first, and do it properly. Who is the man in the room, what plays the role of the Chinese, what is the rulebook, where does the script sit, who is outside the door, and what exactly is slid under it. Then tell me what the Chinese Room predicts about the ELIZA episode and what it can't say about it at all. My suspicion is that the two are talking past each other, and I want you to either develop that or show me I'm wrong rather than politely agreeing. Part of my reason: Searle's setup offers two seats where understanding might live — the man, or the system as a whole, which is the seat the Systems Reply grabs — but the ELIZA episode seems to need a third one, the person on the outside, who supplies the meaning and then reports having found it there. Is that a genuine third possibility or does it dissolve into something we already have names for: the intentional stance, ordinary pretense, puppetry and ventriloquism, the problem of other minds? Take it seriously before you dismiss it, and if you dismiss it, say what work it was doing that made it look like an option.

The timing bothers me too. The man most alarmed by ELIZA was no strong AI partisan; he'd have signed onto Searle's conclusion happily and probably thought Searle was under-selling the danger. So the arguement arrives fourteen years late and aims at a claim the artifact never made. Does that make ELIZA beside the point, or does it show what the argument was actually for — is it directed at the machines, or at the audience? And finally: what would have to be true of a chatbot in principle for it to count as evidence for or against the Chinese Room? If the honest answer is nothing, say so plainly, and tell me what sort of claim we're left holding.

I know the standard replies by name — Systems, Robot, Brain Simulator, the other-minds one — I've read enough summaries, and I dont want them recited at me. What I haven't seen is anybody point them at Weizenbaum's secretary. No bibliography or further-reading list, please; I'm not going to chase anything down and you'd be guessing anyway. Just argue it out with me.
