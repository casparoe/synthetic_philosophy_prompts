I'm writing a novel where a judge has to sentence a man who killed someone twent-two years ago, disappeared, and has now turned himself in — and by every account in the book he's a different person, a prison literacy teacher, not a danger to anybody. A friend of mine doing a PhD in philosophy of law sent me a paragraph to put in the judge's mouth, and I've basically learned it by heart:

"Dennett and Caruso agree nobody is an uncaused cause — neither of them thinks we pick our own starting point. Where they split is punishment. Dennett keeps just deserts: your character is really yours, built out of your own choices, so you can genuinely deserve blame and a sentence proportioned to it. Caruso says that's empty — your character came from genes and luck and upbringing, so there's no self left to deserve anything — and wants to swap punishment for quarantine: hold people only while they're dangerous and only as much as they're dangerous, then let them out. So under Caruso this judge would have to walk the man out of the courtroom today; under Dennett he can serve out the debt owed by someone he no longer is. The whole argument is about whether desert needs an uncaused causer or just a competent one."

Is that fair? Go at it sentence by sentence — what would each of them actually sign off on, and where has she quietly moved them. My suspicion is she's handed Dennett a premise he'd never accept, and that the "no self left" bit isn't Caruso's argument at all but something else wearing his coat. Also whether quarantine really comes out as pure risk-management with no proportionality in it, since that feels too clean.

And then the part I actually need: does the judge's problem — desert owed by a person who has genuinely changed — belong inside this debate, or has she bolted a different puzzle onto it (identity over time, who the offender even is now) that neither of them is addressing? If the paragraph's off, what would the judge have to say for the two positions to come out properly opposed on the page?
