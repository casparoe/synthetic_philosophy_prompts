I'm setting the take-home final for a masters seminar on paternalism and public policy — 48 hours, one essay, 25 marks — and I want a question that can't be passed by reciting what a nudge is. Here's the thing I keep coming back to and want the question to turn on: nearly every nudge in the literature gets pitched as helping the person being nudged. The cafeteria layout, the retirement opt-out, the smiley face on the electricity bill. And most of the defenses lean on exactly that — the architecture just tracks what you'd pick yourself if you weren't tired or rushed, so nobody's deliberation is really being bypassed, Thaler and Sunstein's whole point is that the nudgee would endorse it. But a lot of real choice architecture runs the other way: it steers A, using A's own biases and defaults, so that B or the public gets the benefit, and A sometimes just eats the cost. Organ donation opt-out is the obvious case, but so are defaults on a hospital intake form set to whatever helps the trust's quality metrics, or auto-enrolment that quietly shifts contribution risk onto the employee while cleaning up the employer's balance sheet.

I want the question to make students work out what happens to the autonomy objection in that situation. Does it get weaker, because it was never really about the nudgee's own good in the first place and the self-regarding framing was doing all the justificatory work? Or does it get sharper, because now the person being steered is being used as an instrument for somebody else's ends — and if so, what should actually be said about it, given that the beneficiaries of these designs are often genuinely worse off people who can't steer themselves? And I'd like the question to close off the obvious student escape route of writing well, then just tell them about it, because some of these designs only work while they go unnoticed, and I want them to have to explain why disclosure might not repair anything.

So: write the question itself, built around one concrete policy — invented is fine, and better, since I don't want them recognising it — with enough operational detail that they have to reason about the details rather than around them. Then a model answer of about 700 words. Then the marking scheme out of 25, giving me the specific moves a first-class answer has to make and what each is worth, plus a line or two on how answers typically lose marks. Two of those failure modes I want named explicitly: treating manipulation as if it were one thing with one criterion rather than a family of distinct complaints, and assuming that other-regarding automatically means more objectionable than self-regarding, when sometimes it's less.

Also tell me honestly whether 700 words is enough to do both the autonomy question and the transparency point properly, or whether I should cut one of them.
