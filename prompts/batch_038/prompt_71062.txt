Two years of volunteering in the back room of our county records office and I've finally got an interesting request, if you're up for it. I want a short story — call it 1,200 to 1,800 words — about the philosophy of the archive: who gets recorded, in what form, and what the form itself does to the person inside it. But not the usual version where the tragedy is a burned library or a lost diary and the moral is that the past is gone forever. What I've actually run into, sorting other people's boxes, is stranger: the record is often *there*, complete, well preserved, indexed, and it still tells you almost nothing about the human being in it, because every surviving mention of them is a mention of a transaction. A payroll line. A gate pass issued and returned. A ration allocation. A disciplinary form for something trivial. A signature on a form that had already been filled out above it. So the silence isn't a hole in the collection, its a property of the paperwork, and that's what I want the story to be about.

Here's the conceit, and I'd like you to commit to it rather than soften it. Setting: somewhere invented — please make up the country and the port town instead of using a real one, I don't want the whole thing resting on facts you might get wrong. Some decades after a colonial administration pulled out, a local records office inherits the files the departing officers didn't bother to ship home. The story concerns one person who shows up in those files — night watchman, cook, typist, boatman, your choice — and the hard constraint is that this person is available *only* through the genres of the paperwork. So tell it largely in archival forms: a finding aid, a box-and-folder list with scope-and-content notes, an accession memo, a researcher's access request and the letter denying it, a note about a document that got re-filed into the wrong series and was never seen again. The documents shouldn't narrate anybody's inner life; they should just be documents, doing what documents do. If the reader ends up understanding what this person's life was like, it has to be because the shapes of the forms make it legible — a two-month gap in the payroll run, a name that gets spelled three different ways across one year, a field on a form with no possible value for them, a separate index of "natives' complaints" that exists and contains one entry. Then the second strand, which is the one I actually care about: the archivist processing the accession (I'm picturing a woman in her late fifties who's held this job through two governments and one flood) has to decide whether to add corrective metadata — a catalogue note asserting who this person was, what the people who knew them called them, why they vanish from the run of records. Every instinct in the profession says fill the silence. But she knows no source supports the assertion, that her note will sit there looking exactly as authoritative as the payroll line beside it, that once it's in the finding aid it becomes the thing everyone cites and the thing nobody checks. The repair is an act of the same power that produced the silence. Don't make her heroic in either direction — have her do something definite and specific, show it inside the documents, and let the form carry the argument.

After the story, give me a craft note of maybe 250 words, and I want it to take a side rather than survey the readings. Which position does the story you just wrote actually land on: is a life like this one recoverable by reading the paperwork against its own grain, or does any account assembled that way just re-inscribe the categories the administration imposed in the first place? Pick one, say why, and then tell me where the story takes liberties — where it leans on a contested idea, where it lets the archivist off easier than the argument deserves, where a professional historian of archives would say I've romanticized something. And if any of the formal choices were driven by what makes good fiction rather than what's true of records work, flag that too, because I'd rather know.
