In experimental cognitive psychology — and in the usability research that borrowed from it — there is a standard method for studying problem solving: the concurrent verbal protocol. A subject works on a task while instructed to keep talking, saying whatever comes to mind, and to do so without explaining or justifying anything; when they fall silent, the experimenter interjects a neutral prompt to get them going again. The transcript is then segmented and coded, and the resulting sequence of utterances is treated as a trace of the sequence of information-processing steps that produced the performance. Three features of the method strike me as philosophically loaded, and I would like them separated out rather than collapsed together.

First, the method's own taxonomy of acceptable verbalization distinguishes (a) voicing content that is already in words, as in reading aloud or rehearsing a number; (b) recoding content that is not in words into words; and (c) explaining or justifying. Categories (a) and (b) are admitted; (c) is banned on the ground that it inserts new processing into the trace. Second, the method is routinely advertised as not being introspection in the discredited sense. Third, the standard validation requirement is non-reactivity: if verbalizing changes accuracy or latency, the protocol is suspect.

Meanwhile the same literature, and the wider psychology of judgment, treats subjects' reports of the grounds of their judgments as close to worthless. People shown two faces, allowed to choose one, and then handed back the other by sleight of hand will confabulate a detailed justification for a choice they did not make; people who reliably prefer the last of a series of otherwise identical items deny that position had anything to do with it. And the protocol method itself incidentally generates cases its assumptions sit badly with: subjects who report knowing what they were about to say but being unable to put it in words, or who recover the first sound and syllable count of a target before the target itself.

What I want is a reconstruction of the implicit theory of thought the practice rests on. What view of the format and architecture of thinking does the concurrent protocol presuppose? Does it commit one to a language of thought — proprietary, compositional, Mentalese — or to something weaker and quite different, namely that at least conscious thought is carried in natural-language vehicles such as inner speech, so that verbalization is a readout rather than a translation? Is category (b), the recoding of non-linguistic content into words, distinguishable in principle from the interpretive act that generates the confabulations in the second batch of findings, or does the clean line the method draws between reporting content and reporting grounds collapse under pressure? And what about the inference from the order of utterances to the order of computations: does that presuppose more than language-likeness — namely that reasoning proceeds in discrete, serial, individually expressible steps, quietly ruling out architectures in which candidate contents are held in superposition or in which the work is done in non-propositional imagery?

Please distinguish what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely sits comfortably with. Is it licensed by a hybrid view on which unconscious computation runs in a language of thought and only a thin conscious surface is in natural language? If so, what evidential work is that thin surface doing, and how does it account for partial access to properties of an unexpressed content? Where is the practice underdetermined — which of these theories would the same coding schemes and the same validation standards equally well serve?

Finally, tell me what would have to change if the presupposed view were replaced. Suppose self-reports are the output of an interpretive mechanism operating on subpersonal states, with no privileged access and the same fallibility as an observer's inference from behavior: does protocol data survive as evidence about the process, or only as evidence about the subject's model of themselves? And conversely, suppose conscious thought is literally in natural language and the recoding category is empty: what would the method then be entitled to claim that it now claims on weaker grounds? In each case, identify which specific methodological commitments — the ban on explanation, the non-reactivity requirement, the inference from utterance order to processing order, the insistence that this is not introspection — would have to be abandoned.
