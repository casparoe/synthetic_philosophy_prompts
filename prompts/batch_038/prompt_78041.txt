I help to run a neighbourhood time bank in a mid-sized English town. It is not a business but a members' association of about four hundred people, mostly retired or on low incomes, and I wrote most of the current rulebook myself, so I can state its rules exactly rather than vaguely:

1. One hour of your time earns one credit, and one hour of somebody else's time costs one credit. What the hour consists of is irrelevant. An hour spent by a retired accountant completing a neighbour's self-assessment return is worth precisely the same as an hour spent walking that neighbour's dog or sitting with her mother so that she can go to the shops. At induction evenings we put this as a motto: an hour is an hour.
2. Credits cannot be bought, sold, converted into money, lent outside the scheme, or spent with anybody who is not a member. They record hours owed to you by the membership as a whole.
3. Credits lapse eighteen months after they are earned, whether or not they have been used. This was adopted deliberately: a handful of early members had built up balances of two or three hundred hours and were not spending them, and the committee decided that sitting on credits was not what the scheme was for.
4. A member may go into deficit down to a floor of minus thirty hours. Those credits come into existence at the moment the service is first received; nobody had saved them up beforehand.
5. Every transaction is reduced by a twentieth of an hour on each side, and the fractions accumulate in an organisational balance which pays for the coordinator's part-time salary, the telephone line and the newsletter.

Some years ago a member who had read philosophy at university told me that Aristotle discusses this sort of thing in the books on justice in his Ethics, and I worked through that chapter in translation. I have no training and may well have misread it, but I took from it that goods must be made comparable before they can be exchanged at all; that the builder and the shoemaker are unlike and unequal in every respect, yet exchange somehow requires them to be equalised; that money was introduced by agreement to do this measuring; that what really holds exchange together is people's need or want for things, and money stands in for that only by custom, which is why it is not money by nature and can be altered or made worthless by decision; and, elsewhere in his writings, that making money out of money itself is the most unnatural way of getting wealth, since money was invented for exchange and not for increase.

What I would like is an account of what our five rules commit us to, set out in a way I could put to the committee. Specifically:

- What theory of money and exchange does the scheme presuppose? Is the credit a token of labour time, a claim on the future labour of the membership, a conventional measure standing in for need, or something else again? Please distinguish what the rules strictly require from what they merely happen to suit comfortably, and tell me where the practice is underdetermined, so that several quite different theories would license exactly these rules and no others.
- Which rival views of money does the scheme quietly rule out, and where does it merely decline to implement a view without contradicting it?
- Is the first rule consistent with the second? If an hour of an accountant's work and an hour of dog-walking are genuinely equal measures of one and the same thing, what is the measure measuring — labour, need, contribution, or the bare fact of giving time? And if it is a measure, why can it not be measured against pounds?
- Does the third rule show that our token was never functioning as money at all, given that it refuses to store value, or is it an example of the point that money is conventional and can be made worthless by decision?
- Would the fourth rule look like money breeding money, or is it the opposite, since nothing is charged for the advance and the credits are claims on hours rather than on wealth?
- Is the fifth rule a fee, and therefore a return to pricing in money, or is it of a piece with the rest?
- If we replaced our implicit theory with a different one — say, one on which the credit is simply a claim on future labour, or one on which it is a measure of subjective need — what would concretely have to change in the rulebook? Which of the five rules would survive, and which would have to go?

I am not asking for references, further reading or a bibliography, which I should not be able to chase up; I am asking for the reasoning itself, argued through rather than listed. Please write your answer in British English.
