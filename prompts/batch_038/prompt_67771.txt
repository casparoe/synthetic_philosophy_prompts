ok so context: i've been on my school's policy debate team for three years and last weekend my cousin (CS major, second year) sent me something about "AI safety via debate" — the idea that you get two AIs to argue both sides of a question and a regular human picks the winner, and that this supposedly lets you oversee systems way smarter than you without being smart enough to check their answers yourself. His take was basically: that's literally what you do every weekend, and it works, right? The truth is easier to defend than a lie, so the honest side wins, and the judge doesn't have to know the topic.

I want to know how far that analogy actually stretches, because I have feelings. Can you give me the correspondance — what in my world is playing the judge, the two debaters, the resolution, cross-x, the flow, speaker points, the two weeks of research before a tournament — and then where each of those breaks down?

My counterexamples, which he waved off: last month I won a round defending a position I think is flatly wrong, mostly because I spread (read way more arguments way faster than the other team could cover) and they failed to answer two of my evidence cards, and the judge voted on the drops. Also half the judges at our invitational are parents who've never debated and mostly vote on who sounded confident. So if winning doesn't equal being right in my world, why would it in his?

But I can feel where he's got something, and I don't want to just dunk: in the rounds that actually decide things, the good judges flow carefully and force you to answer the specific link, and I have never once gotten away with a made-up statistic in front of a judge who knew the topic cold. So maybe the analogy holds for good judges and collapses for parent judges, and the real question is which kind the AI version gets — and whether "good judge" is even possible when the judge can't understand the subject.

Specific things I want an answer on: is the truth-is-easier-to-defend part doing the same job as anything that happens in real debate, or is it a completely different kind of claim that's about computation rather than about people? What does his analogy quietly import from my world that the AI setup doesn't have — like, I can get caught and my reputation is on the line, my opponent and I at least agree on what the words mean, and there's a real world of evidence a judge can send us back to? And is there a better analogy for what they're actually building, or is debate just a good marketing word?

Also please seperate out what's genuinely settled here from what people who work on this still fight about — I've seen both "they ran experiments where two models debated and the human judges got more accurate" and "those experiments don't show anything that generalizes," and I don't want you to tell me one of those is the consensus if it isn't.
