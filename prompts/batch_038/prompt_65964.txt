I'm working on a chapter about Henry Sidgwick and I keep running into the fact that he's basically two different people in the literature — the guy who wrote The Methods of Ethics in 1874 and turned utilitarianism into a respectable academic discipline, and the guy who co-founded the Society for Psychical Research in 1882 with Frederic Myers, Edmund Gurney, Arthur Balfour and Lord Rayleigh and served as its president until he died in 1900. My supervisor insists these aren't two separate hobbies, that the psychical research was doing philosophical work inside the ethics, and told me to prove it or prove her wrong. So here are the facts I'll assume, so you don't have to hedge on them: Sidgwick and his wife Eleanor were investigating mediums from the early 1870s; the SPR took up telepathy, apparitions and mediumship; the big public case was Mrs. Piper, whom Richard Hodgson studied from the mid-1880s onward; there was a large statistical census of coincidences between apparitions and deaths; and in 1895 a Cambridge committee sat with the Italian physical medium Eusapia Palladino and concluded, more or less, that what they'd seen was trickery — after which a good chunk of the society's credibility went up in smoke and Sidgwick nonetheless carried on running it. There's also a story that he said on his deathbed that the effort of a lifetime had been wasted. I don't know whether that's reliable, so please don't build anything on it.

What I want is the analysis of the SPR episode through the dualism of practical reason. Set up the dualism properly first — the collision between rational egoism and universal benevolence, and Sidgwick's thought that the collision is only genuinely resolved if the universe is somehow morally governed so that doing your duty and being happy line up over the long run, which is a premise he couldn't establish and didn't want to just assume. Then map the psychical research onto that gap and tell me how literal the mapping is. Was survival evidence supposed to be the empirical instrument for closing the dualism — i.e., if the dead persist and their condition tracks their conduct, the moral order stops being a postulate and becomes an observation? If so, what was the hypothesis, what would have counted as confirming it, what were the actual results, and did Sidgwick in fact update? I'm especially interested in the asymmetry where telepathy seemed to him established but mediumistic survival-talk never did — is that a principled application of his own standards, or motivated reasoning in a man who'd staked his ethics on the outcome? And be honest about where the theory goes silent: the dualism is about the structure of reasons, and I'm not sure it has anything at all to say about seances. If that's right, say so rather than forcing a connection.

Second pass, running the other direction. Sidgwick also held that a utilitarian can be justified in not publishing everything he believes — that some conclusions are for the few, and that candor isn't always the morally best policy. His public posture on the SPR looks like a possible case: he never endorsed spiritualism, he defended the society against ridicule, he was scrupulous about what the reports claimed, and privately he seems to have thought telepathy real while treating survival as unproven. Is that esoteric morality actually being practiced, or is it just ordinary scientific caution about weak evidence plus a professional's sense of institutional duty? Then: what does the whole episode do to the dualism itself? Does the SPR career suggest the dualism was never really a technical puzzle about two equally rational methods, but a religious worry wearing the costume of one — that Sidgwick needed the universe to be morally ordered badly enough to spend eighteen years sitting in dark rooms with a professional cheat? Or does the episode cut the other way, showing that he held the ethical conclusion hostage to evidence and then refused to let it go when the evidence failed, which would make the dualism more like a live unresolved problem than a rhetorical flourish? Also tell me whether his evidential rule — exhaust every normal explanation before admitting an abnormal one — was the right rule for this kind of inquiry, and whether it explains why the SPR converted almost nobody, including its own founder. No references or reading list needed, I have the books — I want the argument and the places where it doesn't hold.
