I write a fortnightly column on science and ideas, and last month I spent two weeks reporting on how national health-technology agencies rank treatments by quality-adjusted life-years — the machinery by which a bureaucracy decides that extending one patient's life by two good years is worth more than extending another's by one. The philosophers I interviewed mostly defended the practice, but one conceded that the sharpest objection to it is the old claim that aggregating across lives treats society as though it were a single subject having a single experience, and he quoted Nozick's formulation of the point: to use a person for another's benefit fails to respect the fact that "he is a separate person, that his is the only life he has. He does not get some overbalancing good from his sacrifice." I built the column around that concession. Then a postdoc at the same institute, who read a draft, sent me the following argument, which he says does not merely rebut the objection but shows that anyone who presses it is committed to something far stranger than QALYs. My editor is running the piece in six weeks and I would rather not publish a column whose central premise has been demonstrated incoherent.

Here is his argument, cleaned up into steps. (1) The separateness objection amounts to a compensation principle: an act that imposes a cost on one person for the sake of a greater benefit to another is impermissible, because the person who pays the cost is left with nothing. (2) Whatever force that principle has comes from a claim about the units within which costs and benefits can be matched up: between two persons there is no one who both bears the cost and enjoys the benefit, whereas within a single life there is. (3) But the second half of (2) does not survive scrutiny. What exists at any given time is a person-stage; a life is a series of such stages bound together by memory, intention, character, and shared concern, and those bonds come in degrees rather than all-or-nothing. (4) In a great many ordinary lives, the psychological bonds between my present stage and my stage at seventy-five are weaker than the bonds between two people who have shared a household, a history, and a set of projects for forty years. (5) If the question posed by (2) is who bears the cost and who receives the benefit, then in those cases the later beneficiary is no more identical with the bearer than a long-married spouse is, and compensation fails just as completely as it does between strangers. (6) So the objection, applied consistently, condemns ordinary prudence: saving, studying, training, submitting to surgery, even enduring a wretched half-hour in a dental chair to avoid a worse year later, all impose a real cost on a present subject for the sake of a later subject who is, by the objection's own criterion, someone else. (7) Prudence is not impermissible; if anything in ethics is secure, it is that one may accept a smaller present cost to avoid a larger later one. (8) Therefore the objection is false — and since the only difference it located between intrapersonal and interpersonal trade-offs was that compensation is possible in the one case and not in the other, and that difference has evaporated, there is no principled barrier left against aggregation across lives either.

Tell me which step to reject, and then explain why it looked unobjectionable in the first place, since the postdoc's version reads as though each line follows from the last. I want the diagnosis priced out rather than merely asserted: for each of the three positions I can see occupying the room — someone who thinks the persisting person is the right unit of compensation whatever becomes of identity, a Parfit-style reductionist who is happy to say identity is not what matters, and a consequentialist who welcomes the collapse described in (8) — say what each has to give up to reject the step you name, and what each has to give up to accept it. For the record I would rather keep (7) than (1), since a column telling readers that their retirement contributions are structurally akin to theft is not a column I can defend, but I want to know exactly what keeping (7) commits me to. And once you have settled that, tell me whether the argument, if it succeeds, really delivers what he says it delivers about policy: is there work left for the separateness objection to do in the QALY case even after I have conceded every step he asks me to concede?
