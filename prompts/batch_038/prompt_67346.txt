ok so I need help turning a three hour arguement into something actually useful. I'm about a year into a geoscience PhD and my housemate does philosophy of science, and last night we got properly stuck on a case that came up in a collaborator's lab. Short version: they've recovered a naturally occurring solid from the ejecta blanket of an impact crater, chemically almost pure silica with a couple of diagnostic trace elements locked into it. Diffraction says no long-range order at all — it's a glass, flash-melted and quenched in seconds. Its bulk composition overlaps almost completely with a crystalline silica polymorph they can show it was melted *from*, and the same ejecta has the usual high-pressure shock minerals in it, so nobody disputes the shock story. The embarrassing wrinkle is that there is already a valid name for nearly-pure natural silica glass, so what really sets their sample apart is where it came from: the trace signature and a distinctive bubble texture mean you could not make the same thing in a furnace at ambient pressure. They want to propose it as a new species to the international committee that ratifies mineral names. My housemate's take is that this is bookkeeping dressed up as discovery, and his proof is that the same committee hands out valid species names to things like opal and lechatelierite — literal glasses — while every textbook definition of a mineral insists on a crystalline structure, so the requirement can't be tracking a joint in the world if the community overrides it whenever it's convenient. Add, he says, that the six named bins along the plagioclase series are cut at round numbers out of a continuous solid solution, which is about as clearly interest-relative as classification ever gets. My take is that those are two very different complaints. Same composition, no order, is exactly the difference that shows up in hardness, density, thermal stability, dissolution rate — everything you'd ever want to predict — which is just diamond vs graphite and calcite vs aragonite all over again. And I think a kind can have its origin built into its identity conditions, which is the whole point of the category here: it's a record of what happened to that rock.

Could you write the argument as a dialogue between two members of that naming committee, the evening before the vote — one a working mineralogist who thinks species are discovered and the other an outside member, a philosopher, who thinks the question they're being asked to vote on is malformed? Don't make them me and my housemate, and please don't let either one be the dumb one, I'm sick of reading dialogues where the pragmatist gets to be clever and the realist just repeats that the difference is real. I want them to land actual blows. The mineralogist has to answer the plagioclase point and the glasses-with-valid-names point without waving them away, and has to say what's different about a proposed species whose only distinguishing feature is its history. The philosopher has to answer the diamond/graphite point, and explain what it would even mean for a kind's identity conditions to be historical rather than structural — whether that's a metaphysical thesis or just a fact about what mineralogy is for — without letting it collapse into anything goes. Genuine back and forth, each responding to what the other just said rather than delivering prepared speeches, and I want at least one moment each where a point goes in and the other person has to concede something small and specific.
