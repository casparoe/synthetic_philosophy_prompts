I work as a backend engineer on data pipelines, and for the last two years I have volunteered on a small civic-tech project that aggregates state corrections and court records; part of that work involves capital cases, execution methods, and exoneration data. I have no formal philosophy background, so when our internal reading group took up Camus's essay Reflections on the Guillotine (1957) in an English translation, I followed the general shape of the argument but felt I could not tell which parts were load-bearing for him and which were local to France in the 1950s. What I would like is a reconstruction rather than a summary: given the arguments he actually makes there and elsewhere in his work, what would Camus say about developments in capital punishment that postdate his death in 1960? I am specifically interested in whether his position survives contact with them, or whether some of them expose tensions he never had to face.

Here are the six developments I want you to run his reasoning against, each of which seems to me to bear on a different commitment of his:

1. Lethal injection becoming the dominant method and being presented publicly as clinical and painless, alongside a documented record of botched procedures — failed IV access, prolonged consciousness, executioners improvising — compounded by the fact that major medical bodies prohibit physician participation, so the procedure is often run by people with no clinical qualification. If the horror for Camus lay in the condemned person's months of knowing and waiting rather than in the pain of the final minute, does a painless method matter to him at all? And would he regard the humane framing as actively worse, because it makes the practice easier for a society to tolerate? Please address the awkward point that he himself floated a self-administered injection as a half-measure improvement; is state administration of the same drug a different moral structure, and if so, why?
2. Nitrogen hypoxia, introduced as a method precisely because it is claimed to be painless and to require no medical expertise.
3. Life without parole as the standard abolitionist substitute. Camus argued for hard labor for life on the grounds that it is reversible and, in his telling, harsher than death; how would he judge modern LWOP, which in the United States frequently means decades in restrictive housing with no labor, no realistic prospect of commutation, and no functional reversibility? Is that his proposal realized, or the same disposal of the person deemed irremediable by slower means?
4. DNA-based exonerations. In his day the fallibility argument rested largely on individual miscarriages of justice; there are now documented cases of people released from death row after decades, plus quantitative estimates of error rates. Does this strengthen his case in the way he would have expected, or does it change the argument's shape from anecdote to statistic, in a way he might distrust?
5. Econometric deterrence studies. He argued that deterrence is unproven and pointed to countries that abolished without a rise in crime; today there are regression analyses on both sides, heavily contested. Would he argue with the statistics on their own terms, or reject the whole framing in which a life is traded off against aggregate crime rates?
6. Statistical risk-assessment instruments used at bail, sentencing, and parole — which is my day job, so I have a stake in this one. He objected that no one occupies the position of absolute innocence required to judge, and that the condemned is dismissed as beyond remedy. Would he treat a population-level risk score as an aggravation of that objection, since the judgment is made by averages with no individual premeditating anything, or would he consider it beside the point because his objection was always about the authority of the state rather than about who or what computes?

A few constraints on how I would like this handled. Distinguish throughout between what Camus would plausibly have said and what his reasoning implies whether or not he would have said it, and mark which of the two you are offering in each case. Where his texts genuinely underdetermine the answer, say so and explain what would settle it, rather than picking a side for rhetorical effect; where his own commitments pull against each other — severity toward collaborators in the immediate post-Liberation period versus the abolitionism of 1957, or his half-measure proposal versus his claim that the practice is intrinsically a form of premeditated murder — treat the tension as data rather than smoothing it over. Paraphrase him rather than supplying quotations you are not certain of, and if you are unsure whether a given claim is in his writing at all, flag it. I would also like a rough confidence level attached to each of the six predictions, and a closing section identifying which of these he would consider most urgent, plus at least one place where you think he would simply be wrong, or would change his mind once he understood the mechanics involved. Please avoid biographical padding and avoid generic abolitionist talking points that could be attached to anyone: I want arguments that are recognizably his.
