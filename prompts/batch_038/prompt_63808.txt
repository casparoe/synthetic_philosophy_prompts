In most parliamentary systems you get a government by having parties with genuinely opposed values sign a coalition agreement stuffed with provisions each of them thinks is wrong — the anti-war party signs a program that funds the war because it gets the housing bill, and everyone calls that mature, gains from trade, better for both than the alternative. But the same parties show up with one or two declared red lines: issues where no amount of benefit anywhere else will buy their agreement, and where a crossed line counts not as a cost but as a disqualifier, sometimes retroactively tainting the whole deal. And the final text tends to be deliberately vague on exactly the contested point, so each side can tell thier members its values weren't traded away at all.

What theory of value does that package presuppose? If values are tradeable enough that signing something you think wrong can be the right call, what makes one trade categorically different from the other fifty — is the practice committed to a lexical ordering, to a distinction between compromising on outcomes and compromising on principle, or to something about complicity and integrity? And how much of it needs no moral theory whatsoever, since a red line looks about as much like signaling to your base as like a view about non-fungible value? I want what the practice strictly requires separated from what merely fits it nicely, and then a picture of what coalition bargaining would have to become if everyone actually believed values were fully commensurable — or fully incommensurable.
