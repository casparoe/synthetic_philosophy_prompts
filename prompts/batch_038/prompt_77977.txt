I write about the history of ideas for a general-interest science magazine and I keep coming back to the Eyre affair of 1865 — Morant Bay, martial law, 400-odd killed, and George William Gordon, an elected member of the island's Assembly, hauled from Kingston and hanged after a two-day court martial. Facts I'll assume so you don't have to relitigate them: the Jamaica Committee pushing for Eyre's prosecution had Mill out front alongside Bright, Darwin, Huxley and Spencer; Carlyle set up the defence committee with Dickens, Ruskin and Tennyson on Eyre's side; the committee's own counsel, Fitzjames Stephen, thought Eyre legally guilty of murder but probably morally justified; and the prosecutions failed.

What I want is Mill's colonial thoery run through the case, in both directions. He spent decades writing despatches for the East India Company until Parliament abolished it in 1858; On Liberty carves out societies not yet capable of self-government, where he thinks despotism is legitimate so long as it aims at improvement; Representative Government has a chapter defending rule over dependencies by a free state. So does that doctrine predict Eyre, or does it predict Mill prosecuting Eyre? Which strand makes the prosecution consistent — the improvement clause, the presence of an elected assembly with Gordon in it, or some rule-of-law constraint that even a benevolent despot stays answerable? Then flip it: what does the episode expose about the doctrine? Is Stephen's split (legally guilty, morally justified) actually available to Mill, or does the case suggest the improvement clause only ever bites when the violence embarrasses the audience back home? And does it matter that Eyre defended himself by invoking the specter of another 1857 — the same rebellion that killed Mill's employer?

No references needed, just the argument.
