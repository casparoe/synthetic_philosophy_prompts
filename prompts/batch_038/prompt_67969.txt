I cover science and ideas for a magazine — mostly climate economics and the whole future-generations beat — and I've spent the last few months swimming in the discount-rate wars: pure time preference versus the growth-and-elasticity term in the Ramsey equation; Stern's near-zero 0.1 per cent rate and the fury it provoked among people who thought he'd smuggled ethics in through a parameter; Ramsey himself back in the late 1920s, in the paper that basically founded optimal savings theory, holding that discounting a future person's welfare merely because they're in the future is ethically indefensible and amounts to a failure of imagination; and on the other side the Parfit line that pure time preference is like his Future Tuesday Indifference guy, who'd sooner have agony on some future Tuesday than a mild pain on any other day — plus Sharon Street's defence of the thought that a perfectly coherent eccentric could hold a preference like that without being irrational. The culture desk has a hole in the February schedule and I've pitched them Groundhog Day as, in effect, a discounting thought experiment. I want you to write the criticism that piece would be built on. Take the part of a philosopher of economics who moonlights as a film critic — someone who takes both the maths and the movie seriously and won't fudge either — and think it through properly: start with a short brainstorm of the two or three readings the film genuinely supports before you commit to one.

Here's the pitch as I've got it. Phil Connors is stuck reliving 2 February in Punxsutawney forever, and the loop looks like an isolating device for the pure-time-preference parameter, because it strips out most of the other things that get invoked to justify discounting: he isn't going to be richer later, so there's no growth term to lean on; he has near-perfect information about the coming hours, so it isn't really risk-adjustment; and there's no scarcity of future days to trade against the present one. So the film ought to be a clean test of whether sheer futurity is a reason to care less. I want you to press on where that framing bites and where it doesn't. Obvious stations: the hedonism phase — eating, stealing, sleeping around, pure present consumption with no return stream at all; the long campaign to seduce Rita, which is an investment whose payoff resets every morning, so is that a sunk-cost trap or the one thing in the film that genuinely can't be bought at any rate; the suicide attempts, which read like an agent who's decided his discount rate is infinite; and then the piano, the ice sculptures, the French poetry, the entire town's timetable learned by heart — skills acquired with no future in which to cash them out. The bit that gets me, though, is the old homeless man: Phil keeps trying to save him and he dies anyway. Some future states aren't purchasable at any price, which feels like a claim about discounting that the economics literature can't easily make.

Now the disagreement I want you to adjudicate inside the piece, because I couldnt work out who's right. An economist I interviewed at a conference in the autumn said the loop makes pure time preference literally inoperative: with infinitely many identical days ahead, any positive discount factor gives every strategy an infinite present value, so no rate — zero, 0.1 per cent, 5 per cent — can rank Phil's options, which means the film can't be about discounting at all; though he added, a touch smugly, that the piano practice is the best investment imaginable precisely because its return stream starts tomorrow and never ends, so if you read the thing as a fable it's an argument for a zero rate. A philosopher on the same panel said pretty much the opposite: that the film tracks the collapse of instrumental rationality rather than its recalibration — once there's no future left to serve, nothing Phil does can be for the sake of anything else, and what the back half discovers is the difference between acting for a payoff and acting in a way that fills up a day, which is a separate axis from the discount rate and shouldn't be collapsed into it. Go through both claims, say which framing survives contact with the actual scenes, and be honest about where the film just resists the economic reading: its a romantic comedy with a redemption arc and a long reputation for being read as vaguely Buddhist, and I don't want to hand readers a Ramsey equation stapled onto a Bill Murray movie if that's a distortion. No reading list or citations please, I have the literature, and don't try to quote the dialogue verbatim — paraphrase the scenes. Around 1,400 words, in British English, and finish by telling me what the film contributes to the discounting debate that a journal article's argument couldn't.
