I'm a backend engineer at a small health-tech company and for the last two years I've basically owned the pain charting piece of a dashboard that a couple of hospital systems use for post-op and palliative patients. The central object in our data model is dead simple: a pain score, integer 0 to 10, typed in by the patient on a bedside tablet, with a timestamp. Everything downstream keys off that one number — the paging thresholds, the nurse's escalation flow, whether a protocol suggests another dose, the quality metrics the hospital has to report on. On a site visit I saw a laminated thing at the nurses' station saying pain is whatever the patient says it is and exists whenever they say it does, and in practice that's exactly how the software treats it: nobody overrides a self-report, there's no field for a clinician to correct it, and if someone says 8 we act like it's an 8.

Here's the wrinkle that got me thinking. We also carry a second, much less used field that a clinician asked for ages ago, something like "how much does it bother you," also 0 to 10. For almost every patient the two track each other so closely (correlation like .9) that our PM wants to delete the second one as schema bloat. But there's this one patient — stroke, damage somewhere around the insula per the chart — who has been logging 7s and 8s for intensity and 0 or 1 for bothered-ness for three weeks, is weirdly cheerful about it, doesn't guard the limb, never asks for meds. Our alerting fires an "uncontrolled pain" escalation on him every single shift and the nurses dismiss it every single shift. Meanwhile the same health system refers chronic pain patients for a neurosurgical procedure that cuts a pathway up front in the brain, and the outcome everybody describes as a success is that the patient says they still feel the pain exactly the same but it just doesn't bother them anymore. So the institution is simultaneously treating "the number goes down" as the only measure of relief and treating a case where the number stays put as a win.

I have zero philosophy training — a roommate of mine mumbled something about hedonism versus desire theories when I brought this up at dinner and I didn't follow — but I keep getting the feeling that the whole 0-to-10 practice is silently assuming one specific answer to "what makes pain bad," and that it isn't the answer I'd have guessed. Can you lay out what theory of pain's badness the practice actually presupposes? Like, is it that the raw felt intensity is the bad thing in itself, or that pain is bad because we want it to stop, or that it's bad because it's a signal carrying an instruction to change what you're doing, or something else entirely — and which of those would genuinely license what we've built versus which ones the practice rules out or makes unintelligible? I'm especially curious whether the setup is even internally consistent, since we defer absolutely to the self-report and also celebrate a procedure that leaves the sensation untouched. Then tell me where the practice is philosophically underdetermined: which bits are just engineering convenience (a scalar is cheap to store, easy to threshold, comparable across shifts and across people) rather than a commitment to any view of pain at all. And finally, if the hospital decided the thing that matters is the bothered-ness rather than the intensity, walk me through what would actually have to change in the artifact — what the scale asks, what the alert fires on, what counts as a successful dose, what the quality metric tracks — and whether treating the patient's own report as final authority even survives that switch, given that both fields are self-reports and they can disagree with each other about what the same pain is like.
