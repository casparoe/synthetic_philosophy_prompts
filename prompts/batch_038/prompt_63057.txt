I'm putting together a handout for my intro ethics-of-technology section and I want a piece of short fiction to sit at the front of it, doing work a lecture just can't do. Here's the conceptual target.

Von Neumann's 1955 Fortune essay, "Can We Survive Technology?" The thesis isn't really about any one invention. It's that the globe has become too small and too badly organized to hold what we keep producing, so the equilibrium we reach is always temporary and always thinner than the last one: you stabilize the situation, and then the next advance moves the goalposts underneath you, so the margin for error shrinks even while everybody is behaving more carefully than before. He runs the same logic twice. Once for absolute weapons, where a stalemate holds only because using it would be suicidal, which means every incremental gain in speed or accuracy quietly eats the safety that made the stalemate work. And once for automation, where machines start scheduling and improving other machines until the people in the loop stop being the relevant causal agents. Ulam, writing about von Neumann in 1958, reported a conversation that centered on "some essential singularity in the history of the race beyond which human affairs, as we know them, could not continue." I want your story to earn that sentence without ever using it.

So: write me a short story, roughly 1,400 to 1,800 words, that dramatizes this. Some constraints, because otherwise you'll drift into a genre groove and I'll recieve another Dr. Strangelove knockoff:

1. Setting should be bureaucratic and a bit dull. A standards committee, a harbor pilots' association, a regional grid dispatch office, an insurance claims unit. Not a war room, not a lab, nobody presses anything red.

2. The engine of the plot is a group of competent, mostly well-meaning people who agree on all the facts and disagree only about timing. Include one scene that is essentially a meeting about a deadline.

3. Your protagonist has to make the call that their own best reasoning supports, and it has to be locally correct, and it still shortens the fuse. No villain, no sabotage, no stupidity anywhere in the story. The instability should look like the aggregate of everyone doing the sensible thing.

4. The automation thread needs to be present but implicit: something in the story schedules, or upgrades, or replaces another thing of its own kind, and by the end the humans are approving rather than deciding. Don't point at it.

5. No catastrophe in the final scene. No blast, no blackout, no uprising. End mid-process with the equilibrium visibly holding. That's the whole point of the essay: the danger lives in the holding.

6. No narrator commentary about progress, no character delivers a speech, and von Neumann's name should not appear anywhere in the story itself. Keep the philosophy seperate from the dialogue.

7. One formal requirement: at least a third of the text should be document-shaped, minutes or a memo or a maintenance log or a form with printed fields on it. The drift should be more visible in the documents than in the prose, ideally in something as small as a column that changes meaning between two entries.

If it works I'll use it in the reader and credit you however you like. Thanks!
