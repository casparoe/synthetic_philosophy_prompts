I'm putting together a 90-minute seminar on the pragmatist fight over truth and I want to open with the case that best exposes where Peirce and James actually split, not just where they phrase things differently.

Short version of what I'm working from: Peirce thinks a claim is true if its the opinion inquiry would converge on at the ideal end of investigation — fixed by the long run and by the community of inquirers — and he's adamant this has nothing to do with what feels good to believe. James thinks truth is something that happens to an idea over the course of its being verified — an idea is true insofar as believing it pays off, carries you onward, and squares satisfactorily with the rest of your life — and he's happy for that to cover religious and moral beliefs too.

Rate each of these six twice, 0–10, where 10 means the account straightforwardly licenses calling it true and 0 means the account has no room for it being true at all. Then rank them by the size of the gap between the two scores, biggest gap first, and tell me which one goes on the board first and why.

1. A particular dinosaur species had iridescent feathers, but every fossil that could have shown it was ground to dust eons ago and nothing else can bear on it.
2. A shipping container full of counterfeit watches has been sitting on the seafloor somewhere off a particular stretch of coast since 1998.
3. Goldbach's conjecture.
4. The universe is on the whole hospitable to sustained moral effort.
5. Given what my doctors and I have found out so far, this is the right course of treatment for me.
6. There will still be beings carrying out inquiry a million years from now.

Two extra things I want: flag any case where the two accounts end up agreeing despite the surface difference, and tell me if any of the six is a dud — where the fight is really about wording.
