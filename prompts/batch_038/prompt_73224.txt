So I'm putting together a grad seminar on evidence that misbehaves — uncertain evidence, testimony from channels of unknown reliability, self-locating stuff, the lot — and my co-organizer made me do the thing where you put your own position on record in week one so the students have something to take swings at. Fine. But before I circulate it I want to know whether the package actually hangs together, because I have a nagging sense that a couple of the pieces are load-bearing in ways I never noticed, and I don't want to find out in front of fifteen people.

Here are my committments:

1. Radical probabilism, all the way down. No empirical proposition gets probability 1 from me, including the ones about how things look to me right now. 1 is reserved for the a priori.
2. So the only thing experience can ever license is kinematics: new probabilities on some partition of experience-propositions, and everything else gets carried along by the old conditional probabilities, which stay rigid. And I don't treat rigidity as an extra assumption bolted on to the rule. I think it just is what "the experience gave me exactly this much and no more" means.
3. Rigidity does fail sometimes, though — paradigmatically when what moves is my opinion about the reliability of the very channel handing me the input. For those I go to maximum relative entropy, on the grounds that it's uniquely pinned down by consistency requirements and collapses back into plain kinematics whenever the input is only new partition probabilities.
4. The posterior has to be a function of the prior plus the total evidence, and nothing else. Same priors, same total evidence, same posterior. I'm not willing to let the order in which the uncertain inputs arrived do any work, since then I can't say two such agents were both being rational.
5. The justification for all of this is dynamic coherence — the diachronic Dutch book line Skyrms pushed. Not as a pragmatic gloss on the rules; as the actual reason it's these rules and not others. I find Jaynes's complaint that kinematics is ad hoc pretty unsatisfying precisely because I think the coherence argument answers it.
6. And I think Lewis's old point defuses the whole anxiety about uncertain evidence at the foundations: if I'm certain now that later I'll conditionalize on my total evidence, then my present conditional probabilities on that evidence already match my present unconditional ones, so my shifting probabilities can always be re-described as ordinary conditionalization, at the ur-prior level, on a proposition about the character of my experience. Radical probabilism never has to stop conditionalizing. It just conditionalizes one level up.
7. One case verdict I'm keeping whatever happens. Something like van Fraassen's Judy Benjamin: the agent starts with north and south evenly likely and each half spread evenly over its sectors (say two up north, three down south), and then a source of decent but not known reliability tells her that if she's in the north she's in sector 2. Take the message as a material conditional and conditionalize on it, and P(north) gets dragged down from where it started. I insist that's wrong: the right posterior leaves P(north) exactly where it was and does all its redistributing inside the north. That's what learning only what you were told amounts to.

What I want back is an audit, not a lecture. Tell me which of these genuinely pull against each other, ordered by severity — not all seven listed as equally bad — and for each real one, what has to give: which commitment gets dropped, revised, or restricted, and what I actually lose by doing it. Please work hard at separating the real conflicts from the ones that only look like conflicts, because a couple of these pairs look awful on the surface and I'd rather you tell me which of those appearances survive inspection than hedge by flagging everything. And run (7) against the rest of the package specifically: I suspect at least one principle I've stated above can't deliver the verdict I want there, and that some other one delivers it only by accident. If that's right, say which is which, and tell me whether the cheapest repair is to weaken (6), to qualify (4), or to bite a bullet I haven't thought of.
