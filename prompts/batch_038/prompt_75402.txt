I run a small letterpress shop, and once a month I record a podcast in the back room of it. I have no university training in psychology or computer science, but for about three years now I have been drawing my own versions of Bongard problems: sheets showing twelve small figures, the six above a dividing line said to belong to some group, the six below said not to, with the solver's task being to discover the rule that separates them. I photocopy a hundred or so sheets a year and hand them out at a puzzle night in our town library, which means I have accumulated an unusual amount of evidence about how untrained people actually find rules — evidence I would like to put to work. Next month’s guest is a machine-learning researcher whose group builds systems that perform very well on these puzzles and on the newer benchmarks designed in the same spirit, and who argues in public talks that this performance shows the systems are genuinely forming concepts from a handful of examples rather than retrieving patterns they encountered during training. I want to test that claim rather than let him illustrate it.

My own sheets have produced four observations I would like the questions built around. First, when several solvers converge on a rule that differs from the one I had in mind yet fits all twelve figures, I cannot decide that they are wrong; I want a question about what settles which rule is the right one, and about whether his system represents the competing rules at all or simply emits whichever candidate it reached first. Second, my most reliable solvers say the bottom six did the real work, that they only trusted a rule once it explained every negative as well as every positive; I want a question about whether a system that can justify only the positives has acquired anything deserving the name of a concept. Third, difficulty does not track anything I know how to measure — sheets I expected to defeat everyone are solved in twenty seconds and vice versa — and I want him pressed on whether he has any account of what makes a problem hard, together with the worry that the original hundred were selected precisely because a human being could solve them, so strong performance may show only that his systems have absorbed the vocabulary of concepts humans find natural rather than that they can build new ones. Fourth, those original sheets and their intended answers have been reproduced and discussed for decades, and I want a direct question about how much of the reported performance can be separated from having seen the answers, phrased so that an appeal to held-out test sets does not end the conversation. There is also a philosophical thread I only half understand: I read a little about Goodman’s invented predicate and about the worry that some rules are projectible and others are not, and I could not get far, but I would like one question that puts the problem to him in plain terms — whether calling a rule natural smuggles in a prior commitment about which features the world comes pre-sorted into, and what his systems are being credited with if that commitment is doing the work.

Please draft nine to eleven interview questions arranged so that the sequence tightens as it goes, beginning from a description of the task he sets his systems and ending on a question about what result would persuade him that his systems are not doing what he claims. Each question needs a one-sentence plain-language setup that I can read aloud to listeners who have never seen one of these sheets, the answer I should expect him to give, and a follow-up keyed to that specific answer rather than a generic probe. Where a question is likely to be met with a deflection — that concept formation comes in degrees, or that what I am describing is a benchmark-design problem rather than a scientific one, or that the interesting comparison is to human performance under time pressure — say so and give me the counter-move to use immediately afterward. Mark the two or three questions I should cut first if we run short of time, and please leave out anything about how he came to the field, who influenced him, or what advice he would give students.
