I work in research ethics administration at a large academic hospital, and last night I spent three hours arguing with a friend — a linguist by training — about our informed-consent policy. Our disagreement, I now think, was never really about the policy's ethics; it was about whether the policy is committed to a strong form of linguistic relativity or to the denial of one, and neither of us could articulate the commitment clearly enough to win. I would like you to reconstruct it.

The policy has four operative parts. First, every consent document must be translated into each of the languages our service-area mandate specifies, and our translation vendor is contractually forbidden to paraphrase, gloss, reorder, split sentences, or add explanatory material; a translator who believes some term lacks an equivalent must escalate the term to our office rather than adapt it. Our translators have escalated one recurring problem: several of the mandated languages, they say, do not lexically mark the distinction our forms depend on between an assignment that is random and one that is merely arbitrary or unpredictable to the patient. Second, the English original must fall below a fixed grade-level threshold on a standard readability formula of the usual kind — the sort computed from mean sentence length and syllables per word — and drafts that fail are returned to the investigator regardless of content. Third, for any study involving randomized assignment, a placebo, or a numeric risk estimate, the enrolling clinician must conduct a teach-back: the patient restates the material in their own words, and the encounter is documented as incomplete until they do so correctly. Fourth, the same study's scientific protocol goes to our review board in full technical register, and the board treats protocol and consent form as two descriptions of one and the same research.

My friend's position was that Part One is relativity through the back door, since it assumes the conceptual content of the English survives intact into any target language. My position was that Parts Two and Four assume exactly the opposite — that content is invariant across register and across surface form, so that difficulty must live in the text rather than in the reader's conceptual repertoire. Part Three seemed to undercut both of us, and we could not agree on how.

Please address the following. Distinguish, part by part, what the policy strictly presupposes about the relation between language and thought from what it merely happens to sit well with, since I suspect several of these parts are philosophically underdetermined and would be licensed by rival accounts. Say whether Part One genuinely commits us to the transferability of concepts across languages, or whether it can be read as a rule about fidelity, accountability, and who bears the burden of a mistranslation — a rule carrying no semantic theory at all. Explain what Part Two's metric treats comprehension as a function of, and whether that is compatible even with the modest claim that a language obliges its speakers to encode certain distinctions while permitting them to leave others unmarked. Then diagnose whether the four parts are jointly consistent, or whether some specific pair commits us to contradictory claims about where meaning lives. Identify the weakest and most defensible version of relativity — not the strong deterministic thesis, which I take to be unavailable — that would still generate genuine tension inside this policy, and say why the weaker version is enough. Finally, describe what the policy would have to become if we redesigned it under that weaker view: what would change about the translation mandate, about the readability requirement, and about how numeric risk is expressed; and what epistemic or administrative goods we would forfeit in the redesign.
