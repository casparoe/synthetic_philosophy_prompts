Our reading group spent two sessions on the hitchhiker case from Parfit's *Reasons and Persons* and ended in a stand-off that I was asked to write up rather than referee. Below is the case as the group reconstructed it, followed by their exchange, numbered so that each move can be graded separately.

The case, as reconstructed: you are stranded on a road where nobody else will stop. A driver will take you into town only if he judges that you will pay the agreed fare on arrival. He is stipulated to be an unerring reader of character — what he detects is not what you say but what you would in fact do once delivered, when paying would do you no good at all: no reputational consequences, no chance of being found out, no effect on your self-conception, nothing. You promise, he drives you, you arrive. Paying now costs you the fare and, by stipulation, buys nothing.

The exchange:

1. **Nadia:** Paying is irrational. Once you have been delivered, the ride is a benefit you already hold and paying is a strict loss with no offsetting gain. What the case can show is that it is rational to be the sort of person who pays — a claim about dispositions, not about this act, and the two are not the same claim.

2. **Reyes:** Your transparency stipulation collapses that distinction. The driver is not selecting dispositions in the abstract; he sees through you. There is no version of events in which you get the ride and then decline to pay, because there is no such person for him to pick up. If it is rational to be someone who pays, and being that person just consists in paying in precisely these circumstances, then here the act and the disposition come apart nowhere.

3. **Nadia:** That is an equivocation, and your own opaque variant proves it. Suppose the driver cannot read character. You are still the same person with the same disposition, and now everyone agrees you should not pay. So your principle cannot be "pay because you are a payer"; it must be "pay when you are transparent." Transparency, however, is a fact about the driver's detection powers, and detection is a consequence. You have re-derived my account with the word "rational" pasted onto whichever side has consequences.

4. **Reyes:** Fine, accept the restriction: the act is rational when it is the act by which the disposition earns its payoff, when no route to the benefit exists that does not run through it. Your opaque case is no counterexample to that, since there the benefit arrives without the act. And note what your verdict costs you. You are condemning the only available path to a large benefit on the grounds that each stage of it is separately bad. A theory of practical rationality that cannot recommend any route to anything, because it appraises steps in isolation, is an accounting convention rather than a theory of choice.

5. **Nadia:** That objection rebounds, and fatally in a different way. If your principle is "execute the plan whose execution is on balance good," then you and I hold the same view: I rank plans, and I note that this act is a component of the plan I rank highest. The dispute was supposed to concern whether the act itself is rational, and on your formulation that phrase is doing no work. You have not defended the claim; you have retired it.

6. **Reyes:** It is doing work, and here is where the difference shows. On your view the agent may now form the intention to pay, foresee that she will abandon it at the drop-off, and abandon it — every node rational, the whole worse than the alternative she refused. On mine the plan is assessed once, and its rationality is not re-litigated at each node. Your account yields an agent whose locally rational choices are systematically at odds with what she herself says is good for her over time, and you are obliged to call that a feature.

One of them also dragged in Kavka's toxin puzzle: Nadia to argue that the case is empty, since if you know you would have no reason to pay later you cannot now intend to pay; Reyes to reply that the toxin case concerns agents unable to form intentions they lack reasons to act on, which the stipulated transparent agent is not. I left that out of the numbering, but I would like a verdict on both uses of it.
