In September I became chief risk officer of a mid-sized logistics firm, six months after a ransomware incident and a prolonged port closure nearly destroyed us. My remit is now to persuade a board that keeps asking why it should pay, every quarter, against events that may never arrive. The device that has worked best in my memos is Machiavelli's image from chapter 25 of The Prince: fortune is like one of those raging rivers which in flood sweeps away trees and buildings, before which everything yields, and yet when the weather is fair men can raise defences and barriers so that the waters, rising again, are carried off by canal and are less unrestrained and less dangerous — fortune, in the translation I quote, "shows her power where valour has not prepared to resist her." I have used this to argue for a settled, defensive posture: build the dykes in calm weather, hold capacity and reserves, and do not gamble.

A colleague who works on Renaissance political thought says I have borrowed the river's prestige to argue the opposite of what the chapter concludes. He points out that the same chapter holds fortune to be arbiter of only about half our actions, leaving the other half or a little less to us; that a prince is undone less by the flood than by a manner of proceeding that no longer accords with the times; that no man can bend his own nature when circumstances turn, so that the cautious man is ruined when the moment calls for audacity; that Italy is compared to an open country without barriers precisely because it lacked proper valour rather than proper earthworks; and that the chapter closes by likening fortune to a woman who yields to the young and the bold, from which Machiavelli draws the recommendation that it is better to be impetuous than cautious.

Could you audit the river analogy as I have been deploying it? I would like the mapping made explicit — what answers to the river, to the fair weather, to the dykes and canals, to the flood, to the undefended plain — followed by an assessment of where that mapping fails when the hazards are ransomware, counterparty failure, and regulatory change rather than seasonal water. Please distinguish the disanalogies that genuinely threaten my argument from the decorative ones, and state what the analogy quietly imports: the recurrence and stable form of the hazard, a quantitative division of labour between luck and agency, the assumption that preparation is exhausted by barrier-building in advance, and the assumption that the damage is exogenous rather than partly produced by the institutions it falls on. Say also whether building dykes is even virtù in Machiavelli's sense, or whether for him virtù lies closer to seizing the occasion the flood creates.

Then give me a verdict: does the defensive conclusion survive once the smuggled features are removed, and can the river and the woman be read as two halves of a single account of virtù, or do they license opposed advice that my memos have been equivocating between? If the river is the wrong instrument for justifying reserves to a board, tell me what analogy would do that work honestly, and what it would have to leave out.
