I'm one of three people who run a filmed seminar series in a mid-sized philosophy department — forty minutes, one working camera, a basement with terrible acoustics, mostly grad students plus a couple of emeriti who always stay on for the Q&A and never let anyone finish. Next term's guest is a political philosopher whose book last year tried to rescue meritocracy from the usual luck objections by redefining what desert actually attaches to. I'll be interviewing her and I want you to act as an experienced political philosopher and interviewer who has genuinely read the desert literature rather than the newspaper version of it, and help me build the question list. Think carefully and start with a short brainstorm — half a page at most — mapping out where her view is under the most pressure and what the standard replies are going to be, then turn that into the questions.

Her view, as best I can reconstruct it: what you deserve isn't what you produce, but how much you produced over and above what you'd have produced had you made none of the sacrifices you actually made. She calls it shortfall desert, and the baseline is supposed to be a "reasonable counterfactual" rather than the agent's own best available alternative, precisely so the theory isn't hostage to how imaginative or self-serving people are about what they might otherwise have done. Two load-bearing claims. First, she says this answers the Rawlsian point about the natural lottery: on her account raw talent never earns you anything by itself, so the gifted person who coasts has a shortfall of zero and deserves no more than their baseline, which she thinks lets her keep the word meritocracy without recieving the usual complaint. Second, she admits institutions can't observe counterfactuals, so she proposes estimating each person's baseline from proxies for circumstances — things like household income and school quality at fourteen. She also holds that shortfall desert generates a pro tanto claim on distributive shares that need can override in hard cases.

Now the questions. I want eight to ten with real teeth, and for each one, a single line telling me what she's most likely to come back with and the follow-up that stops that answer working. Flag the two I shouldn't lose even if the room runs out of time. Nothing generic — I don't care what drew her to the subject or what she's working on next. The pressure points I want covered: the severely ill or badly-brought-up person who lands a modest job has an enormous shortfall, so does she really want to say they deserve more than the brilliant surgeon who got there easily, and if the shortfall gets capped or normalised somehow, what is the cap appealing to; the person who sacrifices everything and produces nothing, whose shortfall is zero or negative — is desert then zero, and can she live with that given her whole pitch is that meritocracy shouldn't be purely output-based; path dependence, since once you're rewarded for a shortfall your future no-sacrifice baseline rises and the same sacrifice counts for less next time, so the theory seems to penalise the people it has already rewarded; what counts as a sacrifice at all — refusing a bribe, caring for a relative, training hard at a sport you'll never be good at, picking an unfashionable research topic — and whether her filter is secretly normative in a way that lets output back in through the back door; the regress worry that the capacity to defer gratification and stick with things is itself an unequally distributed natural asset, so she's relocated the arbitrariness objection rather than answered it, and where the principled stopping point is; whether the view is really desert or just incentive design wearing a moral costume, and if it's the latter what she loses — in particular whether she can still say someone deserves more than the rules entitle them to; the proxy move, where an economist in the audience will insist this is just the old equality-of-opportunity trick of scoring effort relative to the circumstances you started from with the word desert stapled on the front, and relatedly whether a golf handicap, which lets players compete without saying the handicap winner deserves the trophy, isn't a better model of what she's actually built; and Young's point that meritocracy was coined as satire with merit defined as IQ plus effort, so if she's dropped the IQ term entirely is she still a meritocrat or has she given up on merit and kept the word for comfort. Finish with one question about what would make her abandon the view — a case, an argument, or something about measurement — and don't let her answer it with a shrug. Write the whole thing in British English, spellings and idiom both, since I'll be reading these aloud to a British room, and don't bother with citations or a reading list; I want questions that stand on their own content rather than name-dropping.
