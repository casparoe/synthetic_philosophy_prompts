You know the formal epistemology literature on Dutch books and money pumps cold — think of yourself as a philosopher who's taught this material for years and has firm views about which objections actually land and which are just noise.

Here's the situation. A few weeks back at a party my friend Ravi ran a little book on whether our department gets its hiring line restored this year. Our other friend Tess told him she put a 60% chance on it being restored and then, separately and without noticing any tension, a 60% chance on it not being restored. Ravi sold her two bets at sixty cents each: one paying a dollar if the line comes back, one paying a dollar if it doesn't. She took both. She's down twenty cents no matter what the dean does. Ravi and my friend Claire got into it right after and I honestly couldnt tell who was ahead. Here's the exchange:

1. Ravi: She was guaranteed to lose twenty cents whatever happens. That's what irrationality looks like — the Dutch book argument shows incoherent credences are irrational, end of story, and Tess's didn't sum to one.
2. Claire: It shows something narrower than that. It shows that if your credences are your betting prices, incoherence costs you money. Tess's real failure was never seeing the two bets as a package — I asked her afterwards and she said she'd have turned down the combined bet instantly, pay $1.20 now and get $1 back for sure. Blaming her credences misdiagnoses what went wrong.
3. Ravi: That's a distinction with nothing on either side of it. Nobody at a party is offered the package. Bookies offer bets one at a time and she took each one, and rationality has to be judged on the decisions people actually make, not on offers they were never given.
4. Claire: Then call her bounded rather than irrational — and notice your argument proves too much going the other direction. I turn down every bet you offer, because you'd only offer one you figured favored you. Refusing a bet with positive expected value is also a sure loss in expectation, so if being exploitable is what makes you irrational, being cautious is irrational too, which means exploitability can't be the criterion.
5. Ravi: No, there's a clean asymmetry. Your refusals cost you nothing in any world, you end up exactly where you started. Tess ends up twenty cents down in every world. The criterion isnt "can be made worse off," it's "can be made worse off no matter what happens," and only incoherent credences allow that.
