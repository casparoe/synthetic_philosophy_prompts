I do biostat work at a mid-size pharma and there's a rule of thumb everyone operates under: you don't get a drug through on one trial. You run two adequate and well-controlled pivotal studies, each one has to stand on its own and clear the bar by itself — usually a one-sided 2.5% in each — and one trial that's twice as big doesnt count as a substitute. Pooling the two isn't the point either; the point is that there were two of them, separately.

My PI thinks this is pure frequentist superstition. His line is that two n=400 trials carry strictly less information than one n=800 trial, so the regulator is demanding a weaker design and calling it more rigorous, and if you actually cared about the posterior you'd run one big study with a defensible prior and be done. Our regulatory affairs person says he's missing what the number two is for, and that it was never about information.

So — what theory of evidence does the two-trial requirement actually presuppose? I want the implicit view spelled out rather than a name slapped on it. What does it take the object of assessment to be: a dataset, a procedure, a body of work produced by particular people with particular incentives? What is it trying to hold down, and why does it think two separate runs buy something one big run can't? Then tell me whether my PI is right that it's dominated, or whether the rule is tracking something a posterior probability just cannot express.

There's also an inconsistency I can't resolve. The same framework will accept a single pivotal study plus "confirmatory evidence" when the effect is dramatic or a second trial would be unethical, so two isn't two. And nobody ever states what joint false-positive rate the whole thing is supposed to hit — it feels like a target that exists only in the negative, as a refusal. On top of that the whole setup treats approving a dud as vastly worse than delaying something that works by two years, which looks like a value judgement wearing a statistical costume. Is the practice internally coherent, or is it several incompatible epistemologies bolted together?

What would have to change — what gets submitted, what counts as one study, how a failed replication is handled — if we swapped it for an openly Bayesian accumulation rule? And where does the psych replication mess land in all this: that field never had a two-study rule and it came apart anyway, so is that an argument for the rule, or evidence that two was never the load-bearing part and the load-bearing part was preregistration and no file drawer?
