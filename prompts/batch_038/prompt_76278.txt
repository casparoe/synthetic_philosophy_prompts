Been sketching out my views on luminosity and the KK principle for a talk, and I want a consistency check before I say any of this out loud. Here's the package:

1. I accept the anti-luminosity result: no nontrivial condition is such that, whenever it obtains, you're in a position to know it obtains. I take it to run through a margin-for-error premise, and I don't think vagueness has anything to do with it — the conditions could be perfectly sharp and the argument would go through just the same.

2. Same reason I deny KK. If knowing p never guarantees you're in a position to know that you know p, that's just (1) applied to the condition *knowing that p*. I don't have a seperate argument against KK and I don't want one.

3. But I'm an access internalist about justification: if you're justified in believing p, you can always know, by reflection alone, that you're justified. Not merely suspect it or have some inkling — know it.

4. And I hold that your evidence is exactly what you know, plus that you can always know by reflection what your evidence is.

5. Case verdicts. A tone gets quieter in steps too small to hear; I say *hearing the tone* is exactly the kind of condition (1) is about, no complaint there. But pain is different — if you're in pain, you know you're in pain, and I think any view that can't say that is broken.

6. Also, I deny KK but happily say belief is transparent: I find out whether I believe p by considering p, and if I believe p I generally know I do. I don't see the conflict with (2), though people keep insisting there is one.

So: which of these actually pull against each other, and which only look like they do? Rank the real tensions by severity rather than treating them as equally bad, and for each say what would have to go and what giving it up costs me — including whether the obvious repair really works. Then tell me which single revision would clean up the most. If you think the package hangs together fine and I'm manufacturing problems, or that one of these isn't a coherent commitment at all, say that plainly instead of playing along. Under 450 words, no throat-clearing, and don't lecture me on the general literature — I know it.
