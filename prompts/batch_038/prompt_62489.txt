Been on LW and the old game theory threads for like fifteen years, and my tiny podcast about coordination problems finally booked a guest who I think is genuinely worth pinning down. He's pushing a view I'll call the legibility thesis: program equilibrium — the setup where each player submits a program and gets to read the other's before the game runs — isn't a formal toy, it's a *predictive* account of real-world cooperation between parties who can audit each other's decision procedures. On his story, once your decision procedure is genuinely readable to the other side, the cooperative fixed point stops being a curiosity and starts being the default, and basically all institutional variation is variation in how legible parties are to each other. His distinctive sub-claim: the thing that actually breaks cooperation isn't opacity, it's *cheap* legibility — publishing code you can quietly swap out later — so what matters is what he calls binding legibility, where changing your own code after disclosure is itself observable to the other party.

I want nine or ten questions I can say out loud that make him defend that rather than re-explain it to people who've never heard of superrationality. Please cover at least:

- the equilibrium-proliferation problem — if everyone can read everyone's source, the program-equilibrium payoffs end up being roughly everything feasible and individually rational, so what does his selection story actually add, and is it just assuming cooperation to derive cooperation;
- binding legibility vs. ordinary commitment devices and collateral — what's left of the idea once you notice that "changing your code is observable" is a monitoring technology, and where does his account make a *different* prediction than reputation-plus-contract;
- asymmetric legibility: one side public, one side private (a state actor vs. a startup, or an open-weights lab vs. a closed one) — does legibility help or hurt the legible party, given that anything which can read you can also exploit you;
- whether he needs Hofstadter-style similarity between the agents, and what happens in a game that isn't a symmetric prisoner's dilemma — hawk-dove, say;
- bounded proof search and proof length, i.e. what his account says when reading the code is cheap but *verifying* it isn't, and the default becomes defect-if-no-proof-found;
- diagonalization — a program that reads the opponent and does the opposite;
- one question on what empirical evidence would count against the legibility thesis, ideally something that makes him engage with the fact that maximally legible environments (transparent on-chain contracts, published trading algorithms) also show the most naked extraction.

For each question, also give me the answer he'll most likely reach for, and the follow-up that stops that answer from working. No softballs, nothing about his career or what got him interested.
