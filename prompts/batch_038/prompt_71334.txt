I am six months into my fourth novel, and I have reached the point where the legal architecture of the book has to stop being scenery and start being argument. The central strand concerns a hearing officer in a state department of agriculture — a woman in her late fifties, meticulous, not a villain — who must decide a citation brought against a poultry transporter. I have been reading my way through American animal law in order to write her fairly, and what I keep finding is a structure I cannot yet articulate cleanly. The principal federal statutes governing the treatment of farmed animals at slaughter and in transport cover cattle, calves, horses, sheep, goats, and swine, but not the birds that account for the overwhelming majority of animals killed for food in this country; the statute governing conditions in laboratories excludes animals raised for food and fiber; the implementing rules for long-distance transport excuse handling that accords with what is normal and accepted in the livestock industry; and nearly every state anti-cruelty code exempts customary, common, or accepted agricultural practices. The same legal order, meanwhile, will imprison a man for years for what he did to one dog, and several civil codes now declare animals to be sentient beings rather than things. My hearing officer applies all of this without ever noticing that she is doing so.

I would like you to tell me what theory of value this body of practice presupposes. Reconstruct it as an explicit position, in your own voice, and be precise about which of its components the law actually requires and which it merely happens to sit comfortably with — I want to know where the practice is philosophically underdetermined, so that a contractarian, a utilitarian with a very high discount rate, and a rights theorist with a restricted list of rights-holders could each claim it as their own. I am particularly interested in the work done by the reference to custom. My own working hypothesis, which I would like you to test and, if it does not survive, discard: the exemption clauses are not really about species at all, but about the difference between idiosyncratic and systematic harm — cruelty is prohibited when an individual deviates from a role and licensed when an institution performs its role, so that the wrong being tracked is deviance rather than suffering. That would explain the asymmetry between the dog and the hen better than an appeal to species membership, because the dog is individualized, named, and inside a household, while the hen exists only as a population. Assess that hypothesis against the alternatives, including the two-tier division of moral labor that Robert Nozick entertains in *Anarchy, State, and Utopia* — welfare aggregation for animals, side constraints for persons — and tell me whether the custom clauses amount to switching off even the aggregationist tier, or to something more like letting the beneficiaries set the exchange rate at which their own costs are counted.

Two further things. First, whether the parts of this practice presuppose consistent doctrines: can a legal order recognize sentience in one provision and defer to industry custom in the next without contradiction, and is there any principled difference between punishing the individual who tortures an animal while licensing the institution that does the same thing at scale? Second, what would have to change — in the reasoning and in the interior life of my hearing officer — if the presupposed theory were replaced by a consistently utilitarian one, or by an equal-consideration view: what would she be obliged to say out loud in her own opinion that she currently never has to say? Please work only from the description I have given and from what you already know, without a bibliography or citations; if I have misstated a legal detail, correct me rather than building on the error, and flag any place where your reconstruction depends on a fact you are not confident of. Nine hundred to twelve hundred words, in your own analytical prose, organized under a few headings, and please do not spend it summarizing the standard debate — I want the diagnosis.
