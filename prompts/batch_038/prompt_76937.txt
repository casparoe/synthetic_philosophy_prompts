I'm maybe two-thirds of the way through a novel set in a country I invented — a small coastal territory that throws off a European empire in the 1870s and then has about forty years to work out what it is. One of my set pieces is a national ceremony the new government quite openly stages: a made-up founding date, made-up songs, made-up uniforms, a procession that nobody's grandmother ever saw. My editor's note in the margin was "this reads like you're illustrating a thesis," and she's not wrong, because I've been reading Hobsbawm and Anderson side by side (funnily enough both books came out in 1983) and I've been blending them into one soup without noticing that they're not obviously saying the same thing. So before I revise I want to get sharp about where the two accounts actually pull apart rather than where they harmonize, and I want a case I can steal structure from.

Build me a thought experiment — one paragraph, realistic, no science fiction, set in a country of your own devising so nobody gets to grade you on the history — in which the invention-of-tradition account and the print-capitalism account come apart in a specific direction: elites deliberately stage a brand-new ritual and it takes, genuinely and durably, in a population where Anderson's mechanism is simply absent. Absent for real, not winkingly: negligible literacy, no vernacular newspapers or cheap print of any kind, no broadcast media (so keep it in the nineteenth century), no single spoken language across the whole territory, and no mass party or state school system to carry it inland. And it has to be felt rather than merely performed — three generations on, people defend the ceremony, weep at it, will walk for miles to be at it, and would resist its abolition, with no enforcement and no penalty whatever for staying home. Don't dress up an actual invented tradition: no Bastille Day stand-in, no royal pageant with the serial numbers filed off.

Then show me the machinery. Tell me what in Anderson's account generates the expectation that this shouldn't happen, and what in Hobsbawm's generates the expectation that it should — I want the mechanisms, not a flat assertion that each man "would disagree," and if you think one of them doesn't actually predict anything here, say so and re-tune the case until the two genuinely pull apart, or tell me it can't be built and why not. Name the single feature of the scenario that's doing the work (my guess is it'll be something about oral repetition or the body or a church network standing in for the printing press, but tell me if I'm wrong), say what happens to the case if you delete that feature, and say how a committed Andersonian would attack it — including whether retreating to something vaguer like "circulation infrastructure in general" saves the thesis or just insulates it from ever being wrong. The whole point is that the case has to earn its verdicts out of the two theories rather than smuggle them in by stipulation, so if it only works because the description is rigged, that's a failure and I'd rather hear it. Please write the whole thing as continuous prose, like an email to me — no headings, no bullet points, no bold, no tables.
