Our department's graduate-organized speaker series has as this year's outside guest a moral philosopher whose position I can summarize as follows, though I'd rather you work from the position than from my summary. She defends a contractualism of Scanlon's kind: an act is wrong when it would be ruled out by principles for the general regulation of behaviour that no one could reasonably reject, and reasonable rejection is always a matter of comparing the complaints of individuals one against another, never of adding complaints together into a sum. What makes her version distinctive is how she handles the numbers. She refuses to say, as some contractualists do, that the greater number never counts; instead she holds that many small complaints become relevant to the comparison only when they are of broadly comparable weight to the competing individual complaint, so that a hundred people's mild inconvenience can defeat one person's mild inconvenience but can never, however numerous, outweigh one person's death. Her talk is billed under a title along the lines of "Numbers Without Summing." She is also known for a single stock response to the standard objections: she tells her interlocutor that the objection has already helped itself to the aggregative mode of reasoning her account is designed to displace, and that the case looks decisive only because it was described in consequentialist terms to begin with.

I have forty-five minutes for questions, and I want ten of them, arranged in three rounds that escalate in difficulty and are ordered so that I could plausibly ask them in sequence. For each question, please give me: a sentence or two of plain-language setup that a mixed audience of graduate students and faculty can follow without a prior seminar in metaethics; the question itself, short enough to be spoken aloud in under a minute; a note on the reply she is most likely to give; and a follow-up keyed specifically to that reply, whose function is to deny her the exit rather than to restate the challenge. No question may invite her to summarize her view, and none may be of the "what drew you to this topic" variety.

The ten should collectively cover the following pressure points, in whatever order you judge best: a case in which each of ten million people would suffer a trivially worse commute in order to prevent a single death, and the mirror case in which each of ten million could decline a trivial burden though their declines together kill one person — pressing whether her view can explain why the second is not an "each-we" dilemma in which the many are severally forbidden what they jointly ought to do; the problem of where the threshold between relevant and irrelevant complaints is set, who sets it, and whether a view that locates it by stipulation is one that could ever be wrong; the objection that partially aggregative accounts of her kind get pushed either into permitting you to save fewer when you could save more at no additional cost, or into the all-or-nothing conclusion that you should help everyone or no one; whether a complaint should be assessed from the individual's position before a risk resolves or from her position afterwards, given that the ex ante route looks like expected-value reasoning of the sort she claims to reject while the ex post route seems to license policies that kill identified people for gains that were reasonable when adopted; whether aggregation re-enters through the back door, since what often makes a rejection unreasonable is the total cost to everyone else of complying with what the rejector demands; and why trade-offs among the competing interests of a single person over time are unproblematic on her account when trade-offs between persons are not. Reserve one question for the strongest case she could make in her own defence — put it to her as a steelman and ask whether she is willing to own it — and one near the end for what would have to be true, in her judgment, for the restriction against summing complaints to be abandoned rather than refined.
