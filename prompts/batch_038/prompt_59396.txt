I cover forecasting and institutional design for a general-interest science magazine, and I am writing a long feature on the proposal known as futarchy: the electorate continues to settle questions of value while betting markets settle questions of belief, with elected representatives defining and managing an after-the-fact measurement of national welfare, and a proposal becoming law whenever a market clearly estimates that it would raise that welfare. After three years of reporting on internal decision markets at firms and on governance experiments run by a handful of blockchain-based organizations, I have come to think the proposal is substantially underweighted in public debate. What I now need is the other side of the ledger: a map of the view's exposure, so that my piece does not read as advocacy.

Tell me what would count against it. I am not looking for the objections I already collect from legislators and interest groups — that markets are unfeeling, that voters will not tolerate being governed by speculators, that nobody has ever tried it at scale. Those are questions of legitimacy and feasibility, and I want them kept analytically separate from defeaters that would show the mechanism cannot do what it claims.

Work through at least the following, and tell me where each ranks:

- The clean division of labour between voting on values and betting on beliefs. Defining a welfare measure, and revising it as the world changes, looks like an empirical and predictive enterprise in its own right. If specifying the target contaminates the market's job, or if the market's estimates quietly determine what counts as a good target, does the slogan's two halves survive contact?
- Discipline. Markets are supposed to be scored against realized national welfare, but the policies that are never adopted have no observable counterfactual, and every long-horizon welfare figure is itself a contested construction produced after the fact. Can the mechanism be checked at all, or does the scoring step reintroduce exactly the politics futarchy was designed to remove?
- Conditional estimation. The proposal depends on comparing welfare under adoption against welfare under non-adoption, typically with bets that are called off if the condition fails to obtain. What has to be true of the condition — resolvable, unmanipulable, promptly settled — for that to work, and which real policy questions fail those tests?
- Transfer of the evidence. The accuracy record for speculative markets comes largely from short-horizon, frequently repeated, cleanly resolved questions. Governance decisions are one-shot, decades-long, and thinly traded. Which of the conditions that make aggregation work are absent here, and how much weight can the proponent's own concessions about long-shot bias and excess volatility in long-dated aggregates bear?
- Reflexivity and manipulation. Where a trader can help bring about the outcome he bets on, is there any design fix, or only a hope that gains from manipulation are small relative to gains from truthful trading? What would demonstrate that the latter hope is false?
- Wealth weighting. Market prices aggregate willingness and ability to pay. Under unequal endowments, does the market's output track the welfare measure at all, or only the preferences of the well-funded? Would a defender say the values step handles distribution — and does that answer work?

Then tell me what futarchy predicts that its rivals — representative democracy, delegated expertise, algorithmic administration — do not, and describe the smallest realistic trial whose results I should treat as evidence against the view rather than as a bad implementation. Be candid about insulation: the design space here is enormous, so almost any failure can be attributed to the wrong welfare measure, the wrong resolution rule, the wrong liquidity provision, or an insufficiently clear market estimate. Has the proposal been qualified into unfalsifiability, and is that a genuine defect for something pitched as an institutional design program rather than a scientific theory? Where a committed defender would concede a point, say so and say what they would concede; where they would shrug, say what they would shrug at. Rank everything from fatal to merely awkward. I don't want sources or a reading list — I want the reasoning, developed far enough that I can judge its force myself. What would most persuade me personally is an argument that the two halves of the values/beliefs split cannot be prised apart, or that the mechanism is unscoreable in principle; tell me whether either is available.
