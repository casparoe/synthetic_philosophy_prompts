I have been working through Grice on what a speaker means by an utterance, and on the gap between what is said and what is implicated, and I have accumulated a set of verdicts that I cannot see how to unify. I would like to know what principle, if any, they add up to.

First cluster — who is meaning something at all:

1. An actor who says on stage that he will love her forever means nothing by it, and I would not hold him to it afterwards. The same actor running the line alone in his dressing room before the show likewise means nothing by it.
2. A diarist who writes, with no expectation that anyone will ever read it, that she will never forgive him does mean it, and I think she is committed to it.
3. A message in a bottle, thrown into the sea and never picked up, still means whatever its writer meant by it.
4. When I tell my dog he is a good boy, I mean it. He is a good boy.
5. A note left on the kitchen door for a housemate — "I ate the last of the bread, sorry" — means exactly what it says, though it is addressed to no one present.
6. If I mutter under my breath that she is going to be late again, intending my flatmate to overhear and conclude that I am annoyed, and she does conclude it — but only because she has separately noticed that I always mutter that phrase when I am annoyed — I still meant it, and she still understood me.
7. A speaker who lies in a language he mistakenly believes nobody present understands has lied, if somebody present does in fact understand.
8. Someone who says "Oh, brilliant plan" as the plan collapses means the opposite, and is not lying.

Second cluster — what is conveyed beyond what is said:

9. "Some of the plates are dirty," said by someone who knows all of them are, misleads; said by someone who has no idea about the top shelf, it misleads nobody, even though the hearer will still come away thinking not all of them are dirty.
10. "Some of the plates are dirty — in fact, all of them are" is a perfectly acceptable continuation, and so is "Some of the plates are dirty — not all of them, just the ones by the sink." Neither sounds contradictory; only the second sounds faintly redundant.
11. "She's from Ohio, so she's stubborn" is not made false by her being from Ohio and being stubborn even if the two have nothing to do with each other; yet the speaker has done something wrong. Unlike case 9, I think he is at fault whether or not he had any view about a connection, and adding "though being from Ohio doesn't make anyone stubborn" seems to retract the whole point rather than to clarify it.
12. "If some of the plates are dirty, we'll need to run the dishwasher again" invites no inference that not all of them are.

The pairs I cannot reconcile are (2), (3), (4) and (5) against (1): in several of my cases there is no audience capable of recognising anything, and yet I insist the meaning is there, whereas in the actor's case there is an audience and a real communicative setting, and I insist there is no meaning. And (9) against (11): the same sort of conveyed-but-not-said component, with opposite behaviour on cancellation and opposite behaviour on blame.

My tentative principle, which I suspect is too crude: a speaker means whatever a reasonable hearer who took him to be competent, cooperative, and as informative as the situation allows would come away believing — whether or not such a hearer exists, and whether or not the speaker is being sincere.

Please do the following. First, state at the top of your reply the principle, or small set of principles, that you think best captures these verdicts, in no more than three sentences, and then say which case motivates each clause of it. Second, tell me which of my verdicts that principle cannot accommodate and which I must therefore give up, and why. Third, apply it to a case I have not listed — the sarcastic note stuck to the office fridge, "Great job cleaning up," left by someone who knows that no one will own up to having read it — and to one further case of your own choosing. Fourth, say plainly whether a single principle can really govern both clusters, or whether I am running two different questions together.

Please keep the whole reply under 800 words.
