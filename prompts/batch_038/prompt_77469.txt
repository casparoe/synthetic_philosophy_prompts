You're a philosopher of mind who specializes in pain — imagine you're prepping notes for a grad seminar on the badness of pain, so aim for that level of precision and argumentative care. No web access needed; this is all reasoning, not recall.

Here's the thing I'm stuck on. There are two competing ways of cashing out what the unpleasantness of a pain actually *is*:

**(a) Evaluativism.** A pain's unpleasantness consists in its representing a bodily condition as bad — as damaged, disordered, going wrong. Pain is bad-for-you partly because it's an experience *about* your body being in a bad state. This is roughly the Bain-style line.

**(b) Imperativism.** Pain has no evaluative content at all. It's a command: an experience whose content is something like *stop this*, *protect that body part*, *don't move it*. Its badness isn't represented; it's a matter of the felt authority or insistency of the order. This is roughly the Klein/Martínez line.

The data I want both views run through is **pain asymbolia**. Lesion work — typically insular, sometimes cingulotomy or lobotomy, and interestingly also high-dose morphine — produces people who can localize a noxious stimulus perfectly well, can rate its intensity, can describe its quality, and then shrug and say it doesn't bother them. Some laugh about it. They don't withdraw, and they conspicuously fail to learn to avoid the stimulus. The aversive component is gone while the sensory-discriminative component is intact. (Grahek made a lot of this, but you don't need the book.)

Now the questions. Please answer in order.

1. **Brainstorm first, and let it be messy.** Before you commit to anything, list every strategy each of the two views has available for handling the asymbolic — including the genuinely ugly ones, like "the asymbolic isn't in pain at all," or "she's in pain, and it's unpleasant, she just doesn't care that it's unpleasant," or "she's in pain but it isn't unpleasant, so unpleasantness isn't what makes pain pain." Get at least four options per view on the table, then prune down to the two each theorist would actually pick, and say why you pruned the others.

2. **Then compare, properly.** Assuming the asymbolic is sincere, which view predicts her reports and which has to swallow a bullet? I want the specific bullet named, not a general unease. Also: notice that asymbolia *is* the case that motivated both views in the first place, so I want to know which one actually explains it rather than just being consistent with it. There's a difference and I think it's where the argument is won or lost.

3. Here's the case that trips me up, and I'd like you to take it seriously rather than explain it away. Call her **D.** D. is a complete asymbolic: she leaves her hand on a hot pan, looks at it, says calmly that she's burning herself and should move, moves it, puts it under cold water, and reports afterwards that at no point did she mind the feeling, and that nothing about it pushed her. She also — and this is the part I want you to focus on — spontaneously reports, unprompted and without any reasoning out loud, that her hand is being damaged and that this is bad. On (a), it looks like she *retained* the evaluative content (she can say the bodily state is bad) while losing the badness, which makes evaluative content look like it was never doing the work. On (b), it looks like she retained... what exactly? The command? Can she have an experience whose content is *move your hand* and also an experience whose content is *your hand is being damaged and that's bad* while reporting only one unified feeling, none of which bothers her?
