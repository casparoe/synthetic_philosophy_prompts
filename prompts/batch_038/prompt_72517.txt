You're a philosopher of mind who knows Dennett's taxonomy of creature-types really well and also isn't naive about how machine learning actually works — please think carefully and treat my half-formed idea skeptically before you build on it.

The idea. Dennett sorts minds into four cumulative rungs: Darwinian creatures, where natural selection is the only thing doing the learning; Skinnerian ones, where reinforcement reshapes behavior within a lifetime; Popperian ones, where the creature carries an internal model of its surroundings so it can try things out in there and let its guesses fail instead of failing itself; and Gregorian ones (named for Richard Gregory), where the crucial resources are external — language, notation, artifacts, culture. Everyone reads that as a ladder: each rung adds a new way of changing what the creature does next.

My suspicion is that each rung actually fixes two different things at once, and that once you pull them apart the ladder stops being a ladder. The two things: (a) where candidate behaviors get generated — in the genome, in the individual's history of consequences, inside a simulated model, or out in the shared environment; and (b) who pays when a candidate turns out badly — the lineage, the individual body, the individual's model, or the scaffolding itself. The four rungs happen to pair those up in the tidy way, but the pairing is contingent, and there are combinations with no name. A creature that generates candidates internally but whose errors are absorbed by an external structure that gets rebuilt, rather than by the creature. A creature whose candidates come from somewhere it has no access to but where the cost of failure lands entirely on it.

And the case that makes this not just taxonomy-games: current language models. From outside they look maximally Gregorian — trained on the whole accumulated external record, dependent on it, unintelligible without it. From inside the training process they look flatly Skinnerian, or worse, since the selection pressure is applied once, in aggregate, over a fixed dataset, and then stops; the deployed system doesn't get to have its guesses fail at all. So the two axes come apart in exactly the place where the ladder says they should be fused at the top. I want the paper to ask whether the taxonomy can be repaired by re-deriving it from the two axes (and if so, which of the resulting cells are occupied and which are empty), or whether the misfit shows that the rungs are tracking historical episodes in evolution rather than genuine levels of cumulative cognition, and should be given up as a framework.

I'm genuinely unsure which of those two horns is the better bet, so part of what I need from you is that you pick one and commit.
