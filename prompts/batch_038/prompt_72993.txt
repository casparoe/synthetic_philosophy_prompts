Ok so I need a referee. I spent three hours on Sunday in my friend Nadia's kitchen watching two people I like dismantle each other over the raven paradox and I still cant tell who was talking sense. I mostly drank tea and did the washing up. It was Rae (does philosophy, very stubborn) and Tomas (does statistics, more stubborn). It kicked off because someone said that Hempel's example — you supposedly confirm "all ravens are black" by looking at a green apple — is meant to be a paradox, and Rae said it isn't one, it's just a badly described piece of evidence. Roughly in order, this is how it went:

1. Rae: the paradox only exists if you imagine an observer with no background evidence whatsoever. Real confirmation happens against everything else you know, and against what I actually know — that ravens exist, that they're black, that the world is stuffed with other things — a white shoe tells me nothing. So the puzzle is an artefact of pretending to be a newborn.
2. Tomas: that's not a dissolution, it's a change of subject. The paradox is generated by three principles: a report of an A that is B confirms "all A are B"; if a report confirms a hypothesis it confirms every logically equivalent restatement of it; and whether it confirms depends only on the report and the hypothesis, with no background smuggled in. Keep all three and the green apple confirms. Drop the third and you haven't answered anything, you've declared the game off. And the newborn case is the clean one, not the cheating one: strip the background away and the apple really does raise the probability, because it eliminates one candidate non-black raven.
3. Rae: fine, but then "confirms" is worthless. On your account almost anything raises the probability of almost anything, and how much it raises it depends on how the universe happens to be populated — the same shoe is a big deal in one world and nothing in another, even though the logical relation between the shoe report and the hypothesis is identical in both. So you've stopped talking about the relation anyone wanted. And it cuts both ways: I read somewhere that if you start from total ignorance, seeing a black raven can actually lower the probability that all ravens are black, because it tells you ravens are a real species with variation in them. So your newborn picture is broken at both ends.
4. Tomas: the numbers. Take a world with a trillion ravens and hardly anything else, where nearly every non-raven happens to be black. There a green apple is worth far more than a black raven, because the apple is one of the very few non-black things around, and under the negation of the hypothesis a decent chunk of the non-black things would have to be ravens. He said Rae's "a shoe can't tell you anything about birds" isn't a logical insight, it's a hunch about the population ratios of our world wearing a logic costume. On the black-raven business he said that's an artefact of one particular prior, not a general result, and she shouldn't be allowed to use it.
5. Rae: the description of the evidence in the puzzle is incoherent, and that's the actual problem. Nobody in the thought experiment samples an object at random from the universe. You deliberately go and fetch something non-black, or worse, you point at a shoe you already knew everything about, in which case nothing new arrives and nothing updates. Confirmation is sensitive to how the observation was produced, and the puzzle only looks paradoxical because it describes a report with no procedure attached to it.
6. Tomas: if procedure matters, then the same sentence confirms the hypothesis under one procedure and doesn't under another, so "the green apple confirms" isn't a fact about the apple and the hypothesis at all, and you can't even state what you're testing. Also, move 5 is move 1 wearing a hat. Then he made a specific claim I couldn't follow: he said conditioning on the object being non-black doesn't reduce the likelihood ratio anyway, because if you take a deliberately chosen non-black object and find it isn't a raven, the odds shift by exactly the same factor as they would for a random draw.

Then Rae said they'd spent two hours agreeing with Hempel and arguing about nothing, Tomas said no, the equivalence principle is definately the real culprit and everything else is noise, and I had to catch a train.
