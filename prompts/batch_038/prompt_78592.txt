So, full disclosure: no philosophy training, I write backend services for a living. But our team chat went off the rails last week and I want to mine it for a joke to close Friday's demo with.

Here's the setup. Someone pasted an argument about insects — my understanding is roughly that even if you put a really low probability on any individual bug being sentient, there are so many of them that the expected amount of suffering still comes out enormous, so you should change your behaviour anyway. Then a colleague of mine actually wrote a script that counted the woodlice under the patio slab by the bike racks, extrapolated to the whole office garden, and multiplied by a made-up credence of 0.001 to work out whether mowing the lawn on Monday was permissible. Someone replied that the entomology number is a trillion trillion give or take several orders of magnitude, so his woodlice sample is doing no work at all.

That's the bit I find funny and want the jokes built on: the whole moral verdict is being carried by whichever quantity is measured worst, and lowering your credence doesn't rescue you because they just say there are more insects than you thought. Punchlines should turn on that multiply-a-tiny-probablity-by-an-absurd-count move, not on the fact that we call insects bugs and also call defects bugs — you can use the software pun as set dressing, but if the laugh depends on it, cut it. And it shouldn't sound like I think the argument is just "maybe bugs have feelings, whoops"; someone who's actually read the scale argument should recognise it.

Give me exactly five attempts, numbered, one or two sentences each, and nothing else — no intro, no explanation of why each one lands, no sign-off. Five that a philosophy person would nod at beats five that read as generic nerd humour, even if it means a couple are a bit dry.
