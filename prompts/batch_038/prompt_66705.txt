The dispute over whether perception is cognitively penetrable has run for some four decades without convergence. One camp holds that perceptual experience is in principle a readout of the sensory input and that beliefs, desires, and background theories can affect what we see only indirectly — by moving the eyes, redirecting attention, altering sampling time, or biasing the report that follows the experience. The other camp holds that high-level cognitive states routinely shape the character of experience itself, and that treating the perceptual system as sealed off is a residue of an overly modular picture of the mind. Both sides appeal to the same families of paradigms: lightness and color judgments under manipulated assumptions about illumination or surface identity, apparent size, slant, and distance judgments for objects whose meaning or affordance has been altered, and effects of hunger, fear, reward, or motivation on how bright, large, or near things look. Each side reads the resulting literature as vindicating it, and each regards the other's positive findings as confounded.

My working hypothesis — offered as a hypothesis to be corrected, not a conclusion — is that the impasse is neither primarily empirical nor merely verbal about the word "penetration," but structural. The term is being used to cover at least three distinct relations between a cognitive state and a perceptual episode:

1. A semantically evaluable dependence: the content of the experience responds to the content of the cognitive state in a way that can be right or wrong about the world — someone who comes to believe a surface is a tomato sees it as redder, and the redness tracks what the belief says.
2. A causal dependence routed through the perceiver's own activity: the belief changes where the eyes go, what gets attended, how the stimulus is sampled, or what the perceiver does, and those changes alter the input that the visual system then processes normally.
3. A dependence at the level of the judgment or report downstream of the experience: the experience itself is untouched, but the perceptual belief formed from it, or the decision rule applied to it, is not.

On this hypothesis, the two camps are not arguing about the same item on the list. The encapsulation camp largely wins by definition against relations 2 and 3, because it defines them as not penetration; the penetrability camp's positive evidence is usually offered as relation 1 but is nearly always attackable as 2 or 3; and the attacks succeed often enough that neither side accumulates findings the other is compelled to accept. The result is a debate in which the criterion, rather than the data, does the work.

Please diagnose the dispute rather than adjudicate it. In particular:

(a) Say whether this three-way split is the right decomposition, and if not, supply a better one. Tell me explicitly whether I have omitted a live fourth relation — for example, top-down influence on the processing that computes a representation without changing its content; or influence via learned statistical expectations, where the pressing question is whether such influence counts as penetration at all or is simply what a well-tuned visual system doing; or the case of a cognitive state altering which of several equally available percepts is selected in an ambiguous or bistable display.

(b) Determine whether the residual disagreement, once the taxonomy is fixed, is substantive or stipulative. Concretely: suppose a defender of encapsulation were shown a clean case of relation 1 — belief-influenced perceptual content, with gaze tracked and equated, attentional load manipulated and measured, sensitivity separated from response criterion, and the belief manipulation delivered without any change to the stimulus or to the subject's opportunity to sample it. Would she concede penetration? Would the opposing camp accept the demonstration as clean, or is there a further requirement — semantic evaluability, directness, absence of learned association, absence of any mediating imagery or inner speech, or something else — that can always be invoked to reclassify the result as not-yet-a-case? I want an honest verdict on whether either position is genuinely exposed to evidence or has been rendered immune to it, and, if immune, whether that immunity is a defect or the legitimate consequence of asking a partly conceptual question with partly empirical instruments.

(c) Separate out the boundary problem. How much of the stalemate is really about where perception ends — early versus late vision, whether object recognition or category assignment belongs to the perceptual system or to what comes after it — and how much is about penetration? If the boundary were fixed by explicit stipulation before any experiment was run, would the remaining disagreement become decidable, or would it dissolve into a dispute over which processing stages are properly described as the perceiver's doing rather than the mind's?

(d) Say what would count as progress given your diagnosis, and rank the candidates by how likely each is actually to move the dispute: a criterion both camps could endorse before seeing any data; a formal model of the visual pathway that fixes the perception–cognition boundary non-arbitrarily; a pre-registered experiment whose outcome both camps agreed in advance to classify identically, with the classification written down beforehand; or abandoning binary penetrability for a graded, parameterized notion of permeability indexed to a specified route. Tell me which one you would want attempted first, and why the others would be expected to fail.

(e) Assess how much of the philosophical motivation survives your diagnosis. Much of the interest in the question comes from the thought that if perception is penetrable, experience cannot serve the role it is standardly given in epistemology — an independent check on belief, not itself shaped by the theories it is supposed to test. Does that consequence follow from relation 1 alone, or does it require something stronger, such as penetration that is invisible to the subject or uncorrectable by further evidence? And if the empirical dispute were settled decisively in either direction, would the epistemological stakes genuinely shift, or were they never hostage to it?

Ground your answer in what each camp actually says and what each treats as a paradigm case, not in a survey of the literature. Where you think my hypothesis is wrong, say so directly and explain what is doing the real work in the stalemate instead.
