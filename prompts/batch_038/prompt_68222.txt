so I just got put on our health system's allocation committee — the group that decides how one fixed pot of money gets split between treatments — and as part of onboarding they made everyone write down where they stand ethically, so the room knows what it's actually arguing about. Mine is below. I want a sanity check before it goes in the binder, because I have a nagging feeling it doesn't hang together and I can't see where.

1. I reject utilitarianism for the usual reason people give: there's no social super-person who experiences the total, so when a policy burdens one group and benefits another, nobody's loss is actually made up for by somebody else's gain. Every benefit and every burden lands inside one separate life, and adding them up treats people as if they were stages of a single career.

2. Inside one life that objection has no grip. Me taking a wretched year of rehab now in exchange for decades of function later doesn't need any further justification, because the one who pays and the one who gains are the same person.

3. Though I don't think personal identity is some deep extra fact sitting under the psychology. It's just overlapping chains of memory, character, intention — and it comes in degrees. There's a lot less of it between me at 17 and me at 80 than between me now and me next spring.

4. On the committee I want to rank options by summed expected benefit (QALYs, basically), because every alternative I've heard sounds like unmeasurable hand-waving.

5. Two votes from my first month. First: a proposal that would have delivered a modest extra benefit to about forty patients, but only if we dropped funding a treatment one patient urgently needed. I voted no. Second: a program that gives a big benefit to a handful of people while exposing everybody in our catchment area to a very small added risk of a bad outcome. I voted yes. Both felt obvious in the moment and neither feels obvious now.

6. I don't think equality, or priority to the worse off, has any intrinsic pull. A distribution matters to me only because of how big each person's complaint is, never because of the pattern.

Which of these actually pull against each other, and how hard? I'm pretty sure a few pairs only look bad — tell me which apparent ones are fine, because I don't want to fix what isn't broken — and rank the genuine ones by severity rather than treating them all as equally ugly. For each real one, say what has to be given up and what giving it up costs me elsewhere in the list, then name the single cheapest revision that buys the most consistency. I'm not attached to any particular item, so don't soften the verdict to spare my feelings. Please keep the whole thing under 600 words.
