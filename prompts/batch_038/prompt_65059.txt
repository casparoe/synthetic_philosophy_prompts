I have no academic training in philosophy or history. For about a year I have been working through a translation of Thomas Aquinas's short book On Kingship and whatever parts of the Summa Theologiae I could find freely, on my own and badly, and I have arrived at a question I cannot settle by myself.

Here is what I take him to hold about tyranny. He separates two things that both get called tyranny: power seized wrongly at the outset, by a usurper, and power lawfully held and then used badly, for the ruler's own advantage rather than the common good. On the second kind he is cautious to the point of looking passive. It is often better to put up with a moderate tyranny than to move against it, because the move tends to make things worse: the attempt can fail and leave the tyrant more savage than before, the struggle can tear the city apart, and the man who comes next may be worse than the one removed. Private individuals are not to take this on themselves; redress belongs to public authority, and where the community holds the right to appoint its ruler, that same community may restrain or depose him, and a ruler so deposed has no complaint, since he has been unfaithful to the charge he was given. If there is no human remedy — if the ruler acknowledges no superior and the people are too weak or too frightened to act — then the only recourse left is to God, who can turn a tyrant's heart or remove him, and who sometimes permits tyrants as a scourge for a people's own sins, so that amendment of life forms part of the cure. I also think, and this is where I am least sure of myself, that in the Summa's question on sedition he reverses the charge: a tyrannical regime is itself seditious, because the tyrant deliberately sows suspicion and division among his subjects in order to hold them down, so upheaval against such a regime is not sedition in the proper sense. If I have that backwards, tell me plainly.

Now the case. I have stripped out the news details on purpose, because I want a case rather than a story. A man seized power in a coup twenty years ago, abolished every check on it, and is now killing his own citizens by the tens of thousands. There is no organised opposition, no free press, no unit of the army willing to move, and no election through which he could be voted out. Nobody appointed him, so the clause about the community that appointed him deposing him appears to have nothing to grip.

(A) A single private citizen, unknown to any organisation, who gets close to him and kills him.
(B) A strike from a distance by another country's government: a weapon launched from outside the territory that kills him and almost nobody else. Suppose the intelligence is as good as it can realistically be — near-certainty that the regime comes apart within weeks, that no worse man takes his place, that there is no civil war and no round of reprisals. Suppose also that this foreign country has suffered nothing at his hands: none of its citizens harmed, none of its interests touched. It acts because the killing is happening.
(C) His own senior officers, who command real forces and hold the capital's garrison, arrest and execute him, then hand power to a civilian body and schedule elections.

Which of these three would he permit, and on what ground? I can see him refusing (A) without hesitation. I cannot see whether (B) and (C) come out differently, because his tests seem to point in opposite directions in each case: (C) has public authority inside the polity but no warrant from the community and no declared war; (B) has a sovereign's authority and something like a just cause, but no jurisdiction over that territory and no injury of its own to avenge.

His reasons for holding back look almost entirely forward-looking — the worse successor, the civil strife, the reprisals. If I stipulate that the outcome is good, does the objection simply evaporate? Is that the right way to read him, or is there a standing objection that survives a good outcome, something about who is entitled to wield the sword or about presuming to judge a man who is not your subject? His treatment of killing in the Summa, including the case of killing in self-defence and what a private person may and may not do, seems to bear on this and I do not know how to apply it. Does his account of unjust law — that an unjust law is more an act of violence than a law and does not bind the conscience, except perhaps to avoid scandal or disorder — do any work here, or does it settle only whether the tyrant's subjects must obey him, not whether anyone may kill him? And on (B): does a sovereign's authority to wage war stretch to punishing a ruler for what he does to his own people when that ruler has done the sovereign no wrong at all, or is this simply a hole in the framework where the honest answer is that he has no view? One more thing I cannot judge: would it matter to him that the killing is done at a distance, by someone who never sees the man and runs no risk from him? I have an intuition that it would, and I cannot tell whether the intuition is his or mine.

Finally, tell me how much of your answer is Aquinas and how much is me. Mark which of your claims rest on something he actually wrote, pointing roughly to which work or discussion rather than giving me references, and which are extrapolations he would have no basis for making. And if you think the whole exercise is a parlour game — that there is no honest answer to what a thirteenth-century friar would rule about a stand-off weapon, or that I have got him wrong somewhere in a way that changes the result — say so at the outset rather than playing along politely and correcting me at the end. I would much rather be told the question is bad than be given a confident answer to it.
