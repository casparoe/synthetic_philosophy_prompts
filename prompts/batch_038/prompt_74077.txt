I have been the Arbiter of a long-running play-by-email nomic for nine years — a game of about thirty players, built on a heavily modified descendant of the original Suber ruleset, in which proposing and voting on changes to the rules is itself a move, and in which a single rule authorizes the amendment, repeal, or transmutation of any other rule in the set. Last month the game stopped being playable, and I need a document I can send to both sides and stand behind.

Here is what happened. A player carried a proposal by the required two-thirds margin. Its text did three things at once. First, it declared a particular scoring rule permanently immune from any future amendment, repeal, or transmutation. Second, it provided that every amendment adopted under it takes effect retroactively, as of the first day of the current cycle, so that scores already recorded are recomputed. Third, it repealed the rule specifying when a proposal that has passed becomes part of the ruleset — that is, the proposal removed the very provision under which its own passage gives it force — and it amended the amendment rule so that the electorate for future amendments consists only of those who were members on the day of passage.

The roster has split into two camps, and six weeks of thread-shouting has produced nothing.

Camp A, who call themselves the flat readers, hold that the ruleset contains no internal hierarchy: every rule stands on the same footing, the amending power is plenary because the rule conferring it says so and nothing else says otherwise, and retroactivity, entrenchment, and self-repeal are all within it. On their view the Arbiter's office is ministerial — record what the voters passed, and if that dissolves the game or renders it unplayable, the dissolution is a legitimate outcome and the only remedy is a new game started by whoever is left. Validity, they say, is a matter of procedure and nothing else.

Camp B, the derivative readers, hold that the authority to amend is conferred, not original, and therefore cannot be turned against its own source: a power granted by a rule cannot be used to withdraw the granting rule, entrenchment is not available because no rule confers a power to bind players who joined later and were not party to the vote, and retroactive effect is excluded because the set defines the force of an amendment in terms of the moment of passage. They insist that refusing to enter the three clauses is not activism by the Arbiter but the only reading on which the rules have determinate content at all.

I have persuaded one articulate member of each camp to work with me on a joint statement, and I want you to draft the document that this adversarial collaboration should produce. It needs to be something both of them could sign without feeling ambushed.

Specifically:

1. A numbered section of claims both camps can sign — about what the ruleset is, what the amending rule does and does not say, what the Arbiter's office consists in, what would count as the game having ended, and what both agree the three clauses were trying to accomplish. These must be substantive enough to constrain the later disagreement; no padded truisms.

2. A disagreements table. For each point of dispute, give the disagreement stated as A would state it and as B would state it, each in wording its own camp would accept as accurate and the other would not call a caricature; a column saying whether the disagreement is genuine or verbal, with reasoning; and a column saying what actually turns on it. I expect some of this dispute to be verbal — about what "valid" or "the rules" is doing in each camp's mouth — and if so I want that isolated and said plainly rather than papered over.

3. The cruxes: the sub-questions such that a settled answer would move at least one camp. My own suspicion is that one crux is whether the ruleset's authority derives from the players' continuing consent or from the internal structure of the text, and that another is whether a conferred power can be exercised to withdraw the conferring rule. Sharpen these, add any I have missed, and say what would follow for each camp from each possible answer.

4. For every crux, a concrete test both camps would agree bears on it — where possible a controlled proposal with specified text that we can actually run inside the game, plus what each camp predicts will happen, recorded in advance of the vote. Also give at least one argument-based test rather than a game-based one.

5. A short closing section on what I should do about the three clauses in the interim, with reasoning each camp can accept — or, if no such reasoning exists, say so and give the least-bad provisional ruling each can live with, and say which camp is being asked to swallow more.

One epistemic request, and it matters more than the formatting. There is, I understand, a serious body of work on self-amending rulebooks and legal self-reference, and I cannot tell from where I sit which parts of this quarrel that literature treats as settled and which parts are genuinely live and contested among people who work on it. Please mark that distinction explicitly throughout — in the agreed-claims section especially — and do not present a contested position as the consensus merely because it makes the document neater. Likewise, flag any place where you think my characterization of camp A or camp B is uncharitable or simply wrong, and any place where a formulation you propose quietly favors one side; I would rather catch that now than have it discovered after both have signed.
