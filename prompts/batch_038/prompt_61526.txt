I am in the last weeks of a seminar on the metaphysics of probability, and my seminar paper defends a reductive view I want stress-tested rather than expounded, since I suspect my reading group has become too polite about it.

The view: objective chance is not a further ingredient in the world alongside rational belief. The chance of an outcome at a time just is the credence that an ideally rational and ideally well-informed agent would assign to that outcome, on the basis of everything admissible at that time. Chance remains objective on this account, because it is fixed by the idealized epistemic situation rather than by anyone's actual opinions; and the Principal Principle becomes, in effect, a trivial truth about that agent rather than a substantive bridge principle connecting two independent things. I prefer it to frequentism because it makes sense of single-case chance attributions, and to propensity accounts because it does not multiply dispositions in nature; I prefer it to a best-system account because it seems to explain the authority of chance over belief instead of merely codifying it.

Tell me what would have to be true for this position to be false. I want the defeaters ordered from fatal to merely awkward, and for each I want to know whether a committed defender of the view would concede that it counts against them or wave it off, and what they would say in doing so. Please consider, at minimum: whether the ideal agent can be characterized independently of chance without either trivializing the account or delivering the wrong chance values; what happens in a deterministic world, where the ideally informed agent's credences look like 0s and 1s even though statistical mechanics assigns probabilities that plainly do explanatory work; whether the account collapses into a best-system analysis and so loses the distinctness I am claiming for it; and how it handles a case I keep returning to — a coin that will be caught and flipped again if it lands tails, so that the ideal agent is certain the process eventually produces heads while the chance of heads on the first toss looks for all the world like one half. Say explicitly whether my view risks anything at all, or whether every counterexample can be absorbed by further refinement of the idealization, and if it is that well insulated, whether insulation of this kind is a defect in a purportedly reductive account.

Give me exactly five numbered items, ordered from most to least damaging, each one or two sentences long, with no introduction and no conclusion. Each item should name the defeater, state the condition under which it would bite, and mark it as conceded or resisted.
