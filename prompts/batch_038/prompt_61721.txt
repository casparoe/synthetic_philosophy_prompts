I run a small irregular reading group here in Seattle that has been working through the philosophy-of-history literature for about two years, mostly people who came to it from forecasting, statistics, and software rather than from academic history. Last month we got stuck on a question I have not been able to let go of since, and I have drafted a short essay about it that I would like you to read as a referee would. The trigger was a routine disagreement of the kind you have probably seen a hundred times in comment threads: someone asserted that the outbreak of a general European war in 1914 was inevitable given the alliance system and the mobilization timetables, and someone else answered that this is unfalsifiable post-hoc storytelling. Both sides, I think, were making claims that are more interesting than either realized, because they were disagreeing about a quantity neither of them had defined. I have tried to define it. What follows is the draft, roughly as it stands; I have not shown it to anyone else yet, so please do not spare it.

The draft is called "Forks and Reconvergence: On What Inevitability Claims Are Actually Claiming." It runs as follows.

Professional historians have mostly treated counterfactual history as a disreputable genre. The most influential version of this attitude, from the middle of the twentieth century, holds that the historian's business is what happened, and that speculating about what might have happened is a game, enjoyable but unhistorical, which is why so much of the genre reads like a parlour exercise about noses and weather. The objection is usually pressed as an epistemic one: counterfactual claims have no evidence, because only one history is actual, so they cannot be checked. I think this objection fails, and fails on the historians' own ground. To say that a particular decision in a particular chancellery caused a war is to make a claim that is at least in part a claim about worlds in which that decision was not made. Causal explanation and counterfactual dependence are entangled in ordinary historical writing to such a degree that the historian who disowns counterfactuals while offering causes has not adopted a more rigorous standard; she has adopted an unexamined one.

But if counterfactual reasoning is legitimate in history, the distinction between responsible and irresponsible counterfactual history has to be located somewhere other than verifiability in principle. I propose that it is located in two quantities that are usually run together. The first I will call fork sharpness: at a candidate juncture, how crisply the world divides into distinguishable continuations, and how well those continuations can be enumerated from the actors' own options, correspondence, and constraints. The second I will call reconvergence rate: how quickly processes that are causally independent of the juncture — demography, coal geology, existing property institutions, wage and price levels, the logistics of moving armies — pull the diverging branches back toward the same coarse-grained description. A counterfactual is informative only inside the window between the fork and reconvergence.

This reframes the inevitability debate in a way I find clarifying. To say that industrialization in Britain was inevitable is not to say that no decision mattered; it is to say that the branches forked by any particular political decision reconverge on an industrializing macro-description within a generation, because the facts that drive industrialization are invariant across the branches. To say that a war was contingent is to say that fork sharpness was high and reconvergence slow over the relevant horizon. Both are empirical claims about the structure of the branching, and both can be argued for using branch-invariant facts, which is exactly what good historians already do when they insist that a given outcome was over-determined.

It also seems to me to dissolve a persistent confusion between determinism and inevitability, which I suspect is responsible for much of the heat in these arguments. Determinism is a thesis about the uniqueness of the future given the total state of the world. Inevitability is a thesis about the robustness of a coarse description under a range of perturbations to that state. A perfectly deterministic world can be maximally contingent at the level of description historians actually work at, because deterministic dynamics can amplify small differences without limit; and a world containing genuine chance at the micro level can be entirely inevitable at the macro level, because the noise averages out. Any historian who moves from the second claim to the first, or who thinks that conceding determinism means conceding inevitability, has made an error that has nothing to do with historical method.

Two test cases. The July Crisis of 1914 has very high fork sharpness — the sequence of decisions is documented almost hour by hour and the alternatives were live options for the men holding them — and, I claim, relatively low reconvergence over a five-year horizon: neither the alliance structure nor the mobilization timetables by themselves generate a general war in 1917 or 1919 in the absence of the particular sequence of July 1914. By contrast, the unification of China under Qin, or the Meiji restoration in Japan, have low fork sharpness in the relevant sense and fast reconvergence: a great many different domestic sequences land on the same macro-outcome because the external pressure and the internal resources are doing the work. The familiar objection about Cleopatra's nose then gets a precise answer rather than a dismissive one. The antecedent is not the problem. The problem is that such counterfactuals are almost always stated without a reconvergence horizon, and without a horizon the claim is not false but empty.

The practical consequence, which I take to be the payoff, is that counterfactuals should be written as branch pairs with an explicit horizon, and the historian's task becomes arguing that the horizon is short or long. This is a discipline that can be imposed on students and on book reviewers, and it explains why the good counterfactual collections read like serious comparative history while the bad ones read like fiction.

That is the draft. I would like a genuine reader's report, not a summary, and I want it organized around five specific questions, though please weave them into continuous prose rather than answering them as a numbered list. First, do the two quantities earn their keep, or is the fork-and-reconvergence pair a bit of decorative pseudo-formalism draped over a point that could be made more honestly in ordinary language about sensitivity to initial conditions and robustness of outcomes? I am genuinely uncertain about this, and I suspect the answer is that it is halfway between. Second, is the paragraph on determinism doing something illegitimate? In particular I want you to examine whether I have helped myself to something about amplification and chaotic dynamics that does not belong in a discussion of historical description, and whether my claim that the level of description settles the question is far too quick. Third, pick one of the two test cases and push back on it specifically; I have a nagging feeling that the 1914 case is doing rhetorical work that it cannot support, since a great deal of the argument for slow reconvergence there depends on facts about the alliance system that I have simply waved at. Fourth, and most importantly, tell me the strongest objection I have failed to consider. I know roughly where the weak joint is — my entire argument rests on the inference from a historian's causal claim to a commitment about counterfactual dependence — but I would rather you develop the objection than assume it is the one I have in mind, since there are several ways to attack that inference and I do not know which is fatal. Fifth, flag anywhere that I have characterized the historians' side uncharitably or inaccurately; I wrote that paragraph about the mid-century dismissal partly from memory of the polemics and I may have built a strawman out of it.

Please do not send me references, a bibliography, or a reading list — I have the shelf behind me and what I want from you is your own reasoning about whether the argument holds. Lead with your bottom line in the first two or three sentences: does the central move work, and would you tell me to keep the framework or strip it out. Then work through the rest in whatever order you think is most useful. Write it as continuous prose, the way you would write a letter or a long email to a colleague — no headings, no bullet points, no bold text, no tables, no numbered questions echoed back at me. Something in the range of seven hundred to a thousand words would be ideal.
