I'm trying to get clear on what it actually means to treat a non-human thing as a historical actor, and I'd rather work through a case where the move went badly than a clean textbook one. The case is the Sahel famines from the late 60s through the mid-80s and the encroaching-desert story that grew up around them.

Here's my recollection, so correct me where I'm off: after the drought and famine of the early 70s, a picture hardened — UNEP types, donor agencies, magazine covers — of the Sahara marching south at something like tens of kilometers a year, driven by local overgrazing and fuelwood cutting, with nomadic herders cast as the perpetrators and the failed rains as the trigger. It got institutionalized fast: the UN desertification conference in Nairobi in 1977, national plans of action, and eventually a whole treaty in the 90s that's still running. Programs followed from the picture — sedentarizing pastoralists, well-drilling, fixed grazing blocks, greenbelt tree planting. Then from the 90s on, rangeland ecologists and geographers, plus satellite greenness data, pulled the rug out: the desert edge tracks rainfall and moves back again, the Sahel re-greened when the rains recovered, high year-to-year variability is the normal condition of these systems and mobility is the adaptation to it rather than the cause of ruin, and the drought itself looks mostly like an ocean-temperature story with a side of sulfate aerosols rather than a local-overgrazing story.

What I want is an analysis of that whole arc through the non-human-actor question, in both directions. Specifically:

1. Sort out what work the word actor was doing. The narrative seems to slide between at least four slots — background condition, trigger, co-author, protagonist — and I want to know which slot the desert was actually placed in at each stage, and where the slippage did damage.
2. Was this environmental determinism in the sense people usually worry about? I suspect it's stranger than that: nature got agency, the herders got agency (as villains), and the state and the market got let off entirely. Argue for or against my read rather than just noting it.
3. Run it against Collingwood's insistence that history proper is the re-enactment of thought-driven action, and against Braudel's longue durée / événement split. Tell me which of those genuinely buys traction here and which is just decoration.
4. The causal claim collapsed and the frame survived it. What does that say about whether these frames are the kind of thing that can be falsified at all, versus something else — a moral device, a funding instrument, a way of allocating blame?
5. Contrast with a case where the non-human cause is solid, smallpox in the Americas say. What's the real difference: evidence, or the kind of claim being made?

Bottom line I need: does this episode argue for dropping climate-as-actor language from history, or for keeping it under tighter conditions? Pick one and defend it. If it's conditions, give me the ones you'd actually apply to a manuscript, not aspirations. Don't hedge and don't hand me a balanced survey — take a position and own it.
