I sit on a municipal review board that decides whether contested conditions count as health conditions for the city's employee benefit plan and for accommodation requests. Stuff like adult ADHD, infertility, obesity, gender dysphoria, chronic fatigue, age-related hearing loss, being a carrier of a genetic variant with late onset, short stature. We get maybe thirty of these a year, they're always appealed, and I'm tired of the arguments going in circles because half the table thinks "is it a disease" is a factual question with an answer and the other half thinks it's purely a political one. Two clinicians, a lawyer, two patient reps, me, no philosophy PhDs, and we have about ninety minutes per case before we have to vote.

So I want a decision procedure we can actually run: the questions to ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, which questions get settled by data (population frequencies, whether some mechanism is doing what it's there to do) and which ones have to be marked as our call about harm and whose interests count, and where the procedure has to hand the decision back to a person rather than pretend it computed something. I want it grounded in the naturalism vs normativism debate about disease — use the ideas, don't lecture us on the history, and no reading list, we're not going to read it.

One specific thing I want it to catch: a party leaning on a statistical or functional fact to smuggle in a value judgment, or the reverse, treating a straight value call as if the biology forced it. If the procedure needs a named step for that, give it one.

Then run it on three cases: adult ADHD in someone who manages fine at work but not anywhere else, age-related hearing loss in a person who doesn't experience it as a loss at all, and infertility in a single man who wants treatment. And commit — give us a verdict on each with your reasons, including one where the result is going to annoy half of us. Finish with what the procedure can't do and where it will reliably give a bad answer.
