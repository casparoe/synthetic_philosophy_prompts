I have no scientific training and never took a philosophy course, but for the past year I've been working through, on my own, the question of what makes something alive. What started it was reading about laboratories assembling cell-like structures out of biological parts, and about what we would be obliged to do if a probe sampling the plume of an icy moon came back with something that appeared to be metabolizing. I built an argument to sort this out, and now it seems to prove far too much, and I cannot locate the break.

(1) A definition of "alive" is worth having only if it is true or false of a thing in virtue of how the thing is, independently of what anyone cares about.

(2) Every criterion on offer comes in degrees and leaks at both ends. Maintaining an ordered structure far from equilibrium by consuming free energy: a flame does it, a virus sitting in a vial does not. Reproduction: a mule cannot, a crystal propagates its own arrangement, a bacterial spore can lie dormant for years doing nothing measurable. Inheritance with variation: viruses manage it only by borrowing the machinery of something else — and the definition I most often meet, the one built on the capacity to evolve by natural selection, seems to make the evolving lineage the living thing, in which case no individual organism ever was one.

(3) So no criterion is both necessary and sufficient, and no list of criteria is either, since a conjunction inherits every exclusion and every admission made by its members.

(4) If a property cannot be spelled out in conditions that do not already assume it, there is no such property in the world — only a habit of ours.

(5) So being alive names nothing real, and there is no fact of the matter about whether a virus, or a lab-built cell-like construct, is alive.

(6) Yet on that footing we could have no reason to sterilize a spacecraft before sending it anywhere, and no grounds for calling anything detected in an ice plume a discovery of life.

I do not accept (5), and I refuse (6) outright. My instinct is that (2) is simply true and (4) must be the weak link, but (4) has the feel of a platitude about definitions, which is exactly what makes me distrust the instinct. I also notice that the same argument runs, essentially unchanged, against "species" and against "planet," which strikes me as evidence against it rather than as a discovery about those words.

Tell me which step to reject, why it looked unobjectionable before being examined, and what the rejection costs — including whether (6) can be preserved without conceding that (5) has a truth value. Then tell me what would change your verdict: which argument or consideration would push you toward accepting (4), or where your own reasoning here is most likely to fail.
