I'm a staff engineer at a company that ships a consumer AI assistant used heavily by families coordinating care for an elderly parent. I have a background in distributed systems and product infrastructure, and no formal training in philosophy: everything I know about moral theory I have picked up in the last year from reading Henry Rosemont's *Against Individualism* and Roger Ames's *Confucian Role Ethics: A Vocabulary*, plus a fair amount of skimming. In three weeks I'm giving a 25-minute invited industry talk with roughly 60 minutes of Q&A in a university philosophy department's applied-ethics seminar series, and I would like you to build me a prep sheet for that question period.

The thesis I'm defending is this: when an AI system acts inside a family, its obligations should be modeled as role-indexed rather than impartial, and the standard design assumption — one uniform user, one consent gate, identical treatment of every human the system encounters — is the position that needs justifying, not the default. My argument sketch, which is what I want the objections aimed at:

1. Our system is already role-indexed in practice; disclosure, escalation, and reminder behavior already differ by inferred relation. The only real choice is whether we do it deliberately and can defend it, or accidentally and cannot.
2. The Confucian texts I've read treat role obligation as surviving outright conflict with impartial justice, not as a regrettable override of it. I plan to cite the Analects passage at 13.18 in which a son's refusal to testify against his father for stealing a sheep is offered as the real content of uprightness, and the Mencius passage at 7A35 in which Shun, asked what he would do if his father murdered a man, lets the minister of justice make the arrest and then abandons the empire and flees with his father on his back.
3. Metaphysically, role ethics denies that there is an antecedently formed individual who then enters relationships, so demanding that role-obligations be justified to a hypothetical impartial chooser already assumes the thing in dispute.
4. Empirically, our A/B tests show that role-indexed disclosure reduces care-plan abandonment and reduces escalations to crisis services, while uniform consent-gating produces workarounds — adult children sharing credentials, parents granting consent under duress. I read the workarounds as evidence that the uniform model misdescribes the user.
5. The design consequence is a schema change: relations become first-class typed objects with obligation-bearing edges, and a human is represented as a bundle of role-instances rather than a single account with a single preference vector.

The audience is not engineers and contains almost nobody who works on AI systems. As best I can tell from the seminar's past programs and the RSVP list: four or five Kantians and contractualists, one of whom writes on treating persons as ends and clearly considers industry ethics talk a species of ethics-washing; two people working on algorithmic fairness with a consequentialist orientation, who care about disparate impact, ground-truth labels, and auditability; a law school visitor who works on health privacy and will hear a data-minimization argument in everything I say; two comparative-philosophy graduate students who know the Chinese texts far better than I do; and one care ethicist who has published on Confucianism and care ethics together and is, I expect, the single most dangerous person in the room. Nobody there wants to hear about engagement metrics, and at least one of them will suspect that role-indexing is a retention feature wearing a philosophical costume.

What I want from you is a prep sheet, not a defense of my view. Specifically:

- Nine or ten objections I will actually get from this audience, ordered by likelihood multiplied by damage. Phrase each one the way that particular person would phrase it, in the first person, in their vocabulary — Kantian, fairness-technical, care-ethical, legal — not in mine and not in a generic "a philosopher might object" register.
- For each: a reply I could deliver in under ninety seconds without any philosophy training, an explicit note on where that reply is soft and what follow-up it invites, and a flag saying whether I should concede it in the first five minutes of the talk or hold it.
- The two concessions I should make up front to buy credibility, and the single reply that has to land or the whole thesis collapses.
- The one question I am hoping nobody asks, with an explanation of why it is close to fatal and what the most honest fallback answer is.
- Three short deflection scripts for exegetical questions I cannot possibly field — I already expect to be asked about the classical doctrine of remonstrance at Analects 4.18 and why gentle remonstration is any protection when the party doing the exploiting is the adult child holding the account credentials, about the hierarchical and gendered structure of the traditional relations, and about whether Xunzi rather than Mencius is the right Confucian for a system that needs generalizable policy. I want graceful ways to park these and hand them to a collaborator.
- Anywhere in my sketch that I am using a Confucian term in a way a specialist would immediately call a misuse — for instance, if treating filial piety as a permission for data disclosure is exactly the move that literature warns against — tell me plainly, because I would rather find out from you than in the room.

Please do not include a bibliography, a reading list, or citations; I have no time to read anything new and I would only be bluffing if I named sources aloud. Paraphrase the primary texts rather than quoting them, and keep the philosophy accurate but pitched at someone who can hold the concepts and cannot produce the scholarship.
