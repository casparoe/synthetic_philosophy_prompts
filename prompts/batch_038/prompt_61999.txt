I'm about three years into a novel that follows four generations of a family running a small weaving concern in a mill town, and I've hit a wall I didn't see coming: I can't work out whether I'm allowed to let my characters' religion do any actual work in the plot, or whether that's always going to read as a cheat.

For research I read a long weekend-magazine essay by a historian of economics, and he summed up Weber's Protestant ethic argument roughly like this:

"Weber's thesis is that Protestantism caused capitalism — specifically, that Calvinist predestination produced the kind of person the modern economy needed. The mechanism was psychological: if you can never know whether you're among the elect, the anxiety becomes unbearable, so pastors pointed believers toward success in their worldly calling as a sign of election, and once you believe that you start working methodically, stop spending on pleasure, and pile up capital. But the deeper point is philosophical, and it's the one that matters. Weber showed that beliefs and values can be causes, which is exactly what the materialists had denied, so the argument over whether ideas move history got settled by evidence rather than by assertion. And the evidence was quantitative: in religiously mixed parts of Germany and Switzerland, Protestants were heavily over-represented among owners, managers and skilled workers, and that pattern combined with the doctrinal argument established the causal chain. He also gave us the iron cage — the religious motive burned off but the machinery of work and accumulation stayed, and everyone born after it is locked in."

Later in the piece he says the record has since demolished all this: the Dutch and English trade figures don't bear it out, Catholics did as well as Protestants once you control for starting wealth, and the over-representation Weber opens with is explained away by religious minorities being pushed into finance and skilled trades anyway. So the thesis is refuted, though everyone agrees Weber deserves credit for putting the question of whether culture can cause anything back on the table.

My own suspicion is that last sentence is doing something sneaky — that the whole business is a just-so story where you describe a culture in economic language and then announce you've explained the economy. But I don't trust my suspicion and I don't want to build a book on a bad argument.

So, going sentence by sentence through his summary: what would a Weber scholar sign off on, what's a forgivable magazine simplification, and what's actually wrong? I particularly want to know whether "Protestantism caused capitalism" is anything like the claim Weber thought he was making, and whether the anxiety-and-sign-of-election story is his or the essayist's. Also — he seems to use the same statistics twice, first as the proof and then as the thing that got debunked, and I cant tell if that's sloppy or if there's a distinction I'm missing.

Then the part I care about most: are the refutation and the methodological question even on the same level? If the history fails, does anything follow about whether beliefs and values can be causes at all — or is the essayist smuggling a live question about explanation into the obituary of an empirical claim? And what would make something like "his doctrine of election made him keep better books" a genuine causal explanation rather than a redescription of the same events in grander words? Is there a test for that, or does it come down to what you're willing to count as a mechanism?
