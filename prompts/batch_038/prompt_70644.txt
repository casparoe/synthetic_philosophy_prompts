My brother Danny is marrying Priya on Saturday and I've been given the toast. Theres a complication, or four. Danny and Priya were actually legally married fourteen months ago at a registry office, in a hurry, because Priya's mother was already very ill and they wanted it done while she could still be told — she died in March, and she never once saw them get married. So Saturday is a party with a ceremony bolted onto the front of it, and the ceremony is being run by Danny's old college roommate, who got ordained off the internet, which I'm fairly sure doesn't hold up in that state. Everyone in the room knows all of this.

Also in the room, at the head table: the man who taught Danny and me philosophy as undergrads and is now a professor somewhere. He's twice explained to me, very kindly, that Saturday's vows are void — not an abuse, where the procedure is fine but the speaker doesn't mean it, but a misfire, where there's no accepted procedure and nobody qualified to run it, so the words do nothing at all, exactly like a line spoken by an actor in a play. He'll be listening for whether I've understood that those are different failures.

Here's my problem. I write novels. Twenty years of making sentences that cannot possibly count — a character in chapter nine forgiving somebody who isn't there, a vow spoken by nobody — and still people write me letters saying those pages rearranged their actual marriages. And as I understand it his own field turned on this decades ago, on something like the grounds that a vow only works because it's a copy of vows said before, so being a repeat can't be what hollows it out; that's what lets it land.

Write the toast. I want the professor to hear that he's been answered and everyone else to just hear something warm and slightly funny, not a paper. Exactly five numbered sections that read straight through in order, two or three sentences each, about two minutes out loud, no lead-in and no notes to me afterward, just start at 1 and stop at 5. Rules: the word symbolic cannot appear anywhere; nothing can call the registry office the real wedding or Saturday a re-run of it; there has to be something in there for Priya's mother, who isn't in the room and never saw any of it; one joke that works whether or not you've ever heard the word performative; and section 5 has to be the bit where people can actually lift a glass.
