I'm auditing a phil of mind seminar and the instructor handed out a two-page orientation sheet on the Searle–Dennett fight about whether beliefs are *really* about things. Here's the sheet's summary, more or less verbatim:

> Searle's Chinese Room is meant to show that a system running a program can never understand anything, because the man in the room shuffles symbols without any idea what they mean, and those symbols get their meaning only from us outsiders who read the printouts as English. Dennett's answer is the two-bitser: think of a little coin-sensing device whose physical state normally means "a U.S. quarter went in," but ship the very same device to Panama and put it in a machine there and that identical physical state now means "a quarter-balboa went in." Nothing inside the device changed, so what the state is about is fixed entirely by how it's hooked up and read. Dennett's conclusion is that beliefs are exactly like the two-bitser: they have no content of their own, only whatever content we choose to assign to them, so there is no such thing as a state being about something all by itself. On this the two men actually agree — Searle just thinks that's a catastrophe and Dennett thinks it's no big deal. Searle's positive view is that intentionality is produced by living brain tissue the way bile is produced by the liver, which rules silicon out automatically however clever the program is. So the whole "original vs. derived intentionality" business turns out to be a quarrel about how to use the word *real*, and neither side has an argument the other is obliged to answer.
