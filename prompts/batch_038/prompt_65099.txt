So I'm putting together a short piece about whether the deference result in cooperative inverse reinforcement learning really comes from the thing everyone says it comes from. The story that gets told about the off-switch game is: the robot lets you switch it off because it's uncertain about the reward, and your reaching for the switch is evidence that you know something it doesn't. My suspicion is that the uncertainty is only half the load, and the other half is a much less-discussed assumption — that your actions are informative about your preferences, i.e. that you're roughly rational. Swap that out for a realistic model of a weak-willed person and I think deference mostly evaporates while the reward uncertainty stays exactly where it was: the robot stops treating your commands as evidence, and once a command isn't evidence, being switched off is just a loss of capacity to serve your actual ends, not a signal to accept.

Can you build me a case that isolates precisely that? Specs:

- One scenario, one paragraph, two at the outside. Realistic near-future domestic setting: a care or health assistant robot and one specific person with one specific weakness. No sci-fi, no superintelligence, no exotic hardware, no upload-style tricks.
- The robot's whole motivational structure is "help this person get what they actually prefer." No self-preservation, no instrumental subgoals, no reward tampering, no deception, no lying about its model. If it avoids being switched off, that has to be purely instrumental plus evidential: switching it off helps nobody, and the switch-off command doesn't update it.
- The variable to isolate is the rationality assumption in the robot's model of the human. Hold everything else fixed — same history of observations, same prior over rewards, same architecture, same stakes. So give me the paired versions: A (models the human as rational) accepts the shutdown, B (models the akrasia honestly) doesn't.
- Non-deference has to be derived, not stipulated. No move where the robot just concludes "the human's true self prefers X" by fiat, or where you hand it a rule for which self is authoritative. It should come out of ordinary Bayesian updating on B's model of how this particular person's behavior gets generated, including their commands.
- Ordinary-person stakes, not life and death. I don't want readers to have an easy "well obviously it should override there" exit, and I don't want the case to turn on someone dying.

Then do the check for me. Walk through what A infers at each step and why it accepts being switched off; what B infers and why it doesn't; and name the single feature doing the work. Then tell me what happens if I pull that feature — specifically, if B is allowed to treat an explicit spoken command as a different evidential kind from the human's day-to-day choices, does the whole thing collapse back into standard deference? And finish with the best available attack from someone defending the rationality assumption, not a strawman.

Two honesty asks, and please don't soften these. First, I'm genuinely unsure whether the load-bearing assumption here is "humans are akratic" or the further claim that a command and a choice are the same evidential kind. If it's the second one and my headline framing is oversold because of it, say that bluntly and tell me what the honest version of the claim would be. Second, if you think the request rests on a confused premise — like if deference and the rationality assumption just aren't separable the way I'm imagining, or if what I'm calling a failure of deference is really garden-variety paternalism that shows up identically with any nurse or financial advisor and has nothing distinctively to do with preference inference — tell me that straight instead of building me a tidy case that doesn't prove anything. Related: how confident are you that this is actually constructible under all my constraints? If it isn't, say which constraint you'd drop and why that one.
