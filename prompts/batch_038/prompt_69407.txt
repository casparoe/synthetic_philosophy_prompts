I am in Year 13 and my EPQ concerns how one ought to choose between voting systems. I would be grateful if you would write an essay on one particular controversy within that subject, which I set out below. I intend to read the essay closely and argue with it, so I would much rather it committed itself to a position I can attack than that it surveyed the ground impartially. Please write the essay in British English.

The controversy is about what to make of the Gibbard–Satterthwaite theorem once one accepts it. As I have encountered it, the theorem is introduced as bad news: any sensible rule that selects one winner from three or more candidates can be manipulated by misrepresentation, unless one voter is a dictator. From that, people seem to draw quite different lessons. Some say strategy-proofness has been exposed as an impossible ideal and should therefore stop being the benchmark against which rules are judged, leaving us free to choose a rule for other virtues — proportionality, say, or the tendency to elect the candidate who would beat every rival in a head-to-head contest. Others say the theorem is bracing rather than disabling: manipulation cannot be eliminated, so the honest task is to adopt the rule in which lying pays least often and least well, and to treat each successful manipulation as a flaw in the institution rather than cleverness on the part of the liar.

A case from my own school may help to make the dispute concrete. Last term we elected the head of the student council by the alternative vote, with five candidates. About a dozen of us sincerely preferred a candidate I shall call N, who had almost no support beyond our group and who, we were confident, would be eliminated in the first round. The real contest was between S, whom we disliked most, and T, whom we found merely tolerable. We therefore put T first and N second. T won narrowly. Afterwards, three things were said. One of our number said we had done nothing wrong, since voting tactically is what people do in every general election, and no rule can prevent voters from answering a different question from the one the ballot appears to ask — namely, what will work rather than what do I prefer. A second said we had corrupted the result, because the published totals now recorded a level of support for T that had never existed, and because everyone else in the school was misled about what the school thought. A third observed that we could only have acted as we did because a straw poll in the common room the week before had told us N was hopeless. I should like the essay to say whether that last observation rescues the alternative vote or condemns it.

I should also like two claimed escapes from the theorem to be assessed, both of which I have heard advanced. The first is that one can leave ranked ballots behind altogether: my politics teacher argues that with approval voting you simply mark every candidate you find acceptable, so there is no ordering to falsify and nothing to lie about, the theorem's assumptions no longer apply, and the problem dissolves. A pupil in my class objected that you can mark your favourite alone, that doing so is often to your advantage, and that the lying has therefore merely migrated from the ordering of the candidates to the placement of your threshold. The second escape is randomisation: our mathematics teacher mentioned the rule that selects one voter at random and elects that voter's first choice, which he says is genuinely immune to manipulation, though he agrees it would be absurd to run an election that way. The essay should say whether an exit from the theorem that no one would ever walk through counts as an exit at all.

Finally, there is a proposal of my own that I have not been able to make precise, and it is the part of the essay I most want written well. I want to distinguish between rules that are transparently manipulable and rules that are only opaquely manipulable. A rule is transparently manipulable, as I mean it, when a voter can identify a profitable misrepresentation from information that is generally available before the poll opens — opinion polls, previous results, the visible strength of each campaign. A rule is opaquely manipulable when a profitable misrepresentation exists in principle, but identifying one would require knowledge very close to the full ballot profile. My suggestion is that only the first is a genuine defect. On this view the theorem is no tragedy: it establishes that lies are possible, not that lies pay, and what we ought to be measuring is how much information a rule demands of a would-be liar before dishonesty becomes rewarding. Please take this seriously — either develop it into a criterion that could actually be used to rank rules against one another, or show where it breaks down. Two difficulties occur to me and I should like both addressed. First, a voter who lacks full information may still have a positive expected gain from lying, in which case opacity seems to shade into transparency by degrees rather than marking a real boundary. Second, a rule that cannot in fact be exploited may still be damaged if its voters believe it can be, and I do not know whether the criterion should be sensitive to belief as well as to possibility.

A few requirements of form. The essay should run to roughly 1,400 to 1,700 words. It should assume that I already know the statement of the Gibbard–Satterthwaite theorem and of Arrow's theorem, and should not spend words restating either. I do not want a bibliography, a list of further reading, footnote apparatus, or the titles of particular papers and books; I want the argument, not the scholarship, and any names of results should be given only where they are already part of ordinary discussion. It should end with a clear verdict on the question I began with — whether strategy-proofness should remain the benchmark by which voting rules are judged — and I would rather have a committed conclusion I disagree with than a balanced summary that ends by observing how subtle the matter is.
