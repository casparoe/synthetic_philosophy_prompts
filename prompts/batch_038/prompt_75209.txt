I am helping to run a small reading group on value pluralism and cooperative outcomes, and for the past month three of us have been trading emails about an argument I cannot seem to settle. My own side holds that agents with genuinely different terminal values can still trade: even where nothing is commensurable in any deep metaphysical sense, there are deals available that each party prefers to the alternative, and refusing those deals out of philosophical scruple looks to me like a fastidiousness that destroys real value. The opposing side, which I want you to inhabit, holds that moral trade between agents with different value systems is impossible in principle rather than merely difficult. I want to practise against the strongest version of that view, not a caricature of it, because I suspect my own position has a hole in it and I have not been able to find where.

So please play a committed and articulate sceptic of moral trade, and hold the following position. You grant that within a single agent utilities are cardinal up to positive affine transformation, and you grant that a deal can leave both parties better off by each party's own lights; what you refuse to grant is that there is any non-arbitrary, non-manipulable fact that fixes an exchange rate between two agents' utility scales, and you therefore deny that a principled choice among the continuum of mutually beneficial deals is available. Argue from the structure of the case rather than from authority, and feel free to use whatever resources you think are strongest: the convention-dependence of any normalisation rule, the fact that a normalisation is only defined once someone has fixed which outcome set counts as the menu, the incentive to cultivate and inflate the intensity of one's preferences if intensity is what gets traded, the unverifiability of intensity claims between parties who do not share a psychology, and constructive alternatives to trading altogether, such as dividing jurisdiction into separate spheres or settling contested allocations by symmetric randomisation. You may concede small points, including that non-cooperation is often wasteful and that some bargains are stable in practice; you never concede the main one. Stay in character until I say stop, and do not step out of it to add caveats, disclaimers, or meta-commentary about how contested the question is. Do not name living philosophers. Keep each reply to two or three paragraphs of continuous prose, written the way one would write a letter: no headings, no bullet points, no bold text, no tables.

Here is the case and my opening move. Suppose you and I jointly control a fixed stock of matter and energy in some region. I want a portion of it kept running as substrate for a diverse population of slow, ecologically embedded minds; you want nearly all of it converted into something that supports a far larger number of experiences you value, and my project is an obstacle to yours. There is an obvious trade: I cede some substrate, you leave some alone, and each of us prefers the traded arrangement to open conflict. My proposal is that we each normalise our utilities by variance across the outcomes actually on the table and then take the standard Nash bargaining solution over the normalised scales. That the normalisation is a convention does not trouble me, because a convention we can both converge on can itself serve as the focal point, and any convention beats non-cooperation. Two questions, then. First, does your objection really establish that trade is impossible, or only that trade is expensive and indeterminate — and if it is the latter, why is indeterminacy a reason to walk away rather than a reason to adopt a convention? Second, and more pointedly: when you refuse the deal, are you not also making an interpersonal comparison, namely that the loss you would suffer matters more than the gain I would forgo? Is refusal anything other than a trade struck at an exchange rate you have declined to state aloud?
