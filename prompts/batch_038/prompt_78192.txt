I do contract reference work — discographies, franchise histories, institutional timelines — and for the better part of two decades my job has been to decide, for other people's projects, when one thing stopped being the same thing and something else began. What surprises me is how rarely that decision is made by looking at what actually persisted. When the owner of the Cleveland Browns moved his football operation to Baltimore in 1996, the league's settlement left the name, the colors, the records, and the history in Cleveland with a franchise that was formally deactivated for three seasons, while the relocated operation — the same owner, the same coaches, the same players — was entered in the record books as an expansion club that began play in 1996. Every person went one way and the identity went the other, and the reason was an agreement rather than anything about the world. The arrangement has since been copied in other leagues. The music business supplies a mirror image: an oldies act ends up with no surviving original members performing under the name, while several founding members tour the same repertoire under a different name, and which group is the band is settled, if it is settled at all, by whoever holds the trademark. Partisans of each are equally certain the other is the impostor, and neither certainty has anything to do with who is standing on stage. I once had to draw a timeline for a small daily whose entire staff resigned and started a competing paper across town; the masthead was bought by a chain that filled it with unrelated content, and every reference source still describes the chain's product as the paper founded in the nineteenth century, because that is what ownership of the masthead says.

I know this neighborhood touches the Ship of Theseus, including the variant in which someone gathers up the discarded planks and reassembles them, which is essentially the rival band. But the classic puzzle asks what continuity does or does not preserve, and what I keep encountering is something narrower and, to my mind, stranger: the question is closed by a document — a registration, a charter, a league settlement, a court's allocation of a mark — and afterward everyone in the record speaks as though a substantive question about persistence had been answered rather than assigned, so that a later reader can't tell that there was ever a decision to make. Nothing in the resulting history marks the spot where the continuity ran out and the paperwork took over.

So: is there an established concept for this specific move, as distinct from the general puzzle about replacement? I'm not after a label to use at parties; I want the carving — what the phenomenon is, what it isn't, and where its edges run. Please answer as exactly five numbered items, one or two sentences each, with no introduction and no conclusion, in this order: (1) the established concept that comes nearest to what I've described and the precise respect in which the description departs from it; (2) a second near neighbor and its own point of departure; (3) whether anything in the literature names this thing specifically rather than the general territory around it; (4) if not, a term you would coin, with a definition tight enough to exclude ordinary cases where the paperwork and the people happen to point the same way; and (5) one boundary case of your own choosing, stated concretely, with your verdict on which side of the line it falls and why.
