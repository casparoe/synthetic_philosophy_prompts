My MA-level applied ethics seminar meets for 75 minutes, 16 students, most of whom have already encountered the stock puzzle that pits one person tortured for decades against an astronomically large number of people each getting a dust speck in the eye. Design an in-class activity that makes them feel the aggregation problem instead of reciting positions on it, running on tokens or slips of paper rather than money. Four requirements:

(a) The aggregate harm must be built up out of a long run of individually trivial decisions, so that no student faces the large-scale comparison head-on until a reveal you design; withhold the standard vocabulary until the debrief.

(b) At least one round must put the deciding student in the position of the prospective victim rather than the allocator, since the intuitions flip with perspective. I want that flip recorded as data from the room, not explained away mid-game.

(c) Supply the harm ladder itself: eight graded harms, spaced so that no two adjacent rungs feel clearly different in weight while the endpoints feel incomparable, with the exact wording I would read aloud.

(d) Make part of the ladder probabilistic and diffuse — a small chance of a serious harm borne by each of very many people — because that is the form aggregation actually takes in the regulatory cases these students care about.

Then give me the debrief that maps specific moments of play onto the underlying issues (where a lexical threshold would have to sit, which step in the sequence could be blocked and at what cost, the separateness of persons, whether ex ante and ex post aggregation come apart), a list of what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it with recovery moves, a fallback for the case where half the class spots the punchline in round two, and an honest account of which feature of the problem a token-based simulation cannot reproduce and what I will have to supply by argument instead.
