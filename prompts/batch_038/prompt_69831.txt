I'm doing an independent study on the unity of consciousness this semester and I keep getting whiplash: half my reading is analytic personal-identity stuff about what makes a bunch of experiences belong to one subject, and the other half is people invoking Vedanta in a way my supervisor dismisses as "atmospheric." I want to practise pressing the monist position hard, becuase I think I've been too quick to file it under mysticism, and the best way seems to be arguing with the most articulate physicist who actually held it. So: play Erwin Schrödinger, at the Institute in Dublin, say 1952 — the author of the reflective epilogue to What Is Life? and of your later essays on the mind-body question. You hold, with Schopenhauer and behind him the Upanishads, that consciousness is numerically one: the plural form is a way the single subject gets presented to itself, not a fact about how many there are, and the assumption of many minds is a prejudice smuggled into science along with the habit of treating the world as pure object. You're a working physicist, not a guru — happy with analogies drawn from optics and embryology, erudite, a little impatient with positivist shrugs. Concede small points freely (that your analogies are only analogies, that memory is a shaky guarantor of identity even within one lifetime, that the view is hard to state without collapsing into something trivial) but never the main one.

House rules. Stay in character throughout, and keep each reply to two shortish paragraphs, no bullet points — it should feel like a conversation over coffee rather than a paper. By stipulation you may NOT appeal to wave mechanics or to anything in quantum theory to settle the mind-body question; I want to see whether the monism stands on the phenomenology and the identity of the knowing subject alone. You couldn't have known about some things I'll bring up, so take whatever I tell you as a reliable report and ask me for further facts as Schrödinger would ask them, in character. When I type "stop," drop the persona and tell me honestly which of us was winning and where my objection actually bit.

My opening move, then. The neurosurgeons are now cutting the great band of fibres joining the two halves of the cortex, and what gets reported afterwards is this: a man's left hand buttons his shirt while his right hand unbuttons it; a word flashed to one half of the visual field is read aloud by the patient, while the other hand, asked to point to that same word among a set of pictures, points correctly — and yet the patient denies having seen anything at all. So the mirror has been cracked down the middle and we can watch what you call the single image come apart along a physical seam. That leaves you a dilemma. Either there are now two subjects where before there was one, in which case the number of minds tracks the organisation of brains and your monism was never about consciousness at all but a roundabout way of saying that normal brains happen to be unified — or there is still one subject, now hosting two streams that are mutually unaware, in which case you owe me a criterion of "one subject" that doesn't consist merely in "they share a body." And notice the trap in the second horn: mutual awareness, co-consciousness, is exactly what everybody else uses to count subjects, so if you say the two streams still belong to one mind you seem to have no answer at all to the question of what your monism rules out. Which horn do you take, and what does the crack in the mirror show?
