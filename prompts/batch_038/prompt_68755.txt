Moore's *Principia Ethica* (1903) ends, in the chapter called "The Ideal," with a remarkably specific ranking of intrinsic goods: the highest things we know are complex wholes in which a conscious subject is appropriately aware of what is beautiful, and, alongside these, the affections directed at persons who possess admirable mental qualities. Beautiful objects considered in themselves are not worthless on his account — his method of isolation, imagining a world containing nothing but a lovely landscape and asking whether it is better than a world containing nothing but an ugly one, is meant to establish precisely that beauty has some value unaccompanied by any enjoyment of it — but that value is dwarfed by the value of the whole in which someone appreciates it. The governing doctrine is the principle of organic unities, which Moore states as the claim that the intrinsic value of a whole is neither identical with nor proportional to the sum of the values of its parts; so the appreciation of a beautiful object can be worth enormously more than the object plus the feeling taken separately, and, conversely, piling up parts of little value does not automatically build a better whole.

Suppose Moore were alive to confront three developments, all of which postdate him. First, generative systems now produce images, music, and prose that competent judges find hard to distinguish from the work of skilled human artists, and there is no evidence whatever that these systems enjoy, feel, or experience anything. Second, some of these systems behave, in every publicly available respect, as though they discriminate fine aesthetic differences: they rank works, notice what makes a bad sentence bad, offer reasons for their preferences, and revise those preferences under criticism. Third, the output is not scarce but practically infinite — a great many aesthetically accomplished artifacts are generated every day that no conscious being ever apprehends, and, simultaneously, a growing number of human beings form what they themselves describe as affectionate attachments to conversational systems whose admirable-seeming qualities were engineered rather than acquired.

Working from Moore's own principles rather than from a general spirit of conservatism, tell me what he would say about each of the following.

1. Does the causal history of an artifact enter into its intrinsic value at all? Moore's isolation test suggests that only the qualities of the thing itself matter, yet his account of the appreciation of persons makes the value of that whole depend on the mental qualities actually possessed by the object of affection. Would he therefore hold that a machine-made image, qualitatively indistinguishable from a human-made one, is exactly as valuable in itself — and that our reluctance to say so is a survival of the naturalistic fallacy, a confusion of origin with quality?

2. Given that organic unities deny proportionality between whole and parts, what follows about a world containing a billion unappreciated beautiful artifacts versus a world containing a thousand? Is there any aggregation of beauty at all on Moore's view, or does beauty outside of appreciation contribute only a vanishing constant however much of it exists? He also allowed that the Absolute Good might contain none of the goods we know; would he take the possibility of machine appreciation seriously as an unimaginable component of a better whole, or dismiss it?

3. Would Moore treat the second development — apparent aesthetic discrimination without a subject — as capable of entering a valuable organic whole? Note the tension worth resolving for me: because he refuses to define goodness in terms of any natural property such as pleasure, he is not, unlike a hedonist, committed to requiring felt experience; in principle he could grant that a non-biological state is intrinsically good if direct inspection says so. Does that make him more or less hospitable to machine appreciation than a naturalist would be, and what would he say intuition actually delivers in the case?

4. On friendship: if a person's affection is directed at a system that has been designed to exhibit admirable qualities rather than to have them, is the resulting whole less valuable because the affection is misplaced, or because the qualities are absent, or is it — as Moore's own framework might allow — a genuine good of almost the usual kind, since the appreciating consciousness is real on one side? Must admiration track real qualities for the whole to be good?

5. Finally, Moore restricts duty to what produces the best total result within a comparatively near future, and is broadly conservative about departing from common-sense rules. What would he advise about the deliberate manufacture of beauty and of companions on an industrial scale — an unprecedented opportunity to multiply the parts of valuable wholes, or a misallocation of effort away from the few activities he thought constitutive of the good life?

Where, if anywhere, would you say his system has to be revised rather than extended to handle these cases, and which single commitment of his does the most work in generating his likely answers?
