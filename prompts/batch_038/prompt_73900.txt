I spent thirty-four years in a metrology laboratory, most of them measuring isotope ratios to four decimal places, and I have come to philosophy only since retiring. I am auditing a lecture series at our regional university, and last week's session concerned what one may properly assert — specifically, the claim that assertion is governed by a knowledge condition rather than by a truth condition or by a requirement of sincere belief. The speaker was lucid and I took careful notes, but afterwards I found I could not reconstruct his argument in a form that seemed to hold together, and I have since read a general-interest essay pressing the same line of attack. Since I cannot yet tell how much of my unease is warranted and how much is a beginner's confusion, I have written the argument out below in his order, without softening any of it:

1. To assert that p is to present p as something one knows. This is not a contingent generalisation about how courteous people behave but part of what the act is; it follows from the practice rather than from observation of it.
2. The claim can be tested against ordinary usage, and ordinary usage refutes it. People properly say that a colleague will arrive at three because she said she would, that the water is boiling because they can hear it, or that their club will be relegated, and in none of these cases do they know what they say; yet nobody thinks they have done anything wrong.
3. The counterexample usually cited by the other side is the lottery. Everyone agrees that one may not flatly say of a ticket in a million-ticket draw that it will lose, however overwhelming one's statistical evidence is, and even though what one says is in fact true. So truth and excellent evidence are insufficient, and assertion therefore requires something stronger than knowledge — which is why defenders of this position end up demanding certainty, or the elimination of every alternative, whether they admit it or not.
4. The stopped-clock cases are said to reinforce the point: someone who forms a true belief because a broken clock happens to read the correct time does not know, and may not assert. But the example is rigged, since the belief is accidental; give the same speaker the same evidence without the accident and she would know and could properly say so. Any case that seems to permit assertion is thus redescribed as a case of knowledge, and any case that seems to forbid it is redescribed as a case of ignorance, which leaves the doctrine unfalsifiable.
5. The remaining datum — that it sounds self-undermining to say that it is raining but that one does not know that it is raining — is explained far more cheaply as a matter of conversational manners: an assertion invites the hearer to rely on the speaker, and it is discourteous to invite a reliance one cannot honour.
6. Therefore the knowledge condition is false as an account of assertion, and what remains of the dispute is a question about how heavily one ought to hedge one's statements.

How much of this is fair? Please go through it claim by claim and tell me which a serious defender of the knowledge condition would sign without amendment, which are simplifications that cost nothing, and which are outright misrepresentations — and for the misrepresentations, what the accurate statement would be. Two things trouble me in particular and I would like them settled rather than glossed: whether claim 3 has the direction of the lottery argument right, and whether claim 2 is comparing like with like when it moves between what a practice permits and what speakers actually do. I should also like to know whether claim 5's cheaper explanation is genuinely available, or whether it quietly presupposes the very connection between assertion and knowledge it was offered to replace. Finally, does the conclusion in claim 6 survive the correction of the earlier claims, or does it depend on them?

One complication I would like you to fold in, because I suspect my own instincts here are misleading me. I arrived at the lecture already sympathetic to the truth condition, on the grounds that what one is trying to do in measurement is to get things right rather than to accumulate warrants. Yet in my working life almost nothing we said amounted to a claim to know: we reported a value together with its uncertainty, as a measurement and not as a settled fact about the world, and it never struck any of us as either improper or evasive to speak that way. Does reporting of that kind fall outside the reach of these theories altogether, or does it bear on them from a direction the lecture did not consider? I am not looking for recommendations of further literature, nor for the names of the various proponents — only for your judgement on the argument as I have set it out.
