So — I cover neuroscience and bioethics for a Sunday ideas section, and a feature I'm writing about organ donation after circulatory death has quietly mutated into a piece about what death even is. I've got more competing answers than I have wordcount, so I need help triaging them.

Here are the six I'm juggling:

1. **Circulatory-respiratory.** Death is the irreversible stop of breathing and circulation. This is what actually happens in practice during donation-after-circulatory-death cases: circulation is stopped, the team waits out a hands-off interval of a few minutes, and then declares death and proceeds.
2. **Whole-brain / total brain failure.** Death is the irreversible loss of the integrated functioning of the entire brain, brainstem included. This is roughly the legal standard in the US, and it's the one families run into when a body is warm, ventilated, and legally dead.
3. **Higher-brain.** Death is the permanent loss of the capacity for consciousness. Heartbeat and breathing can continue on machines and the person is still gone. This implies that permanently unconscious but ventilated patients — deep permanent vegetative states, some cases of covert-awareness ambiguity — occupy a category we're currently getting wrong in both directions.
4. **Organism-as-a-whole.** Skip organ-specific criteria entirely. Death is the loss of the body's capacity to sustain itself as a single integrated organism, however you measure it. Brain and heart are both just proxies for this.
5. **The reversibility-horizon account.** A neuroscientist pitched me this and I can't tell if it's genuinely new or just clever-sounding, which is partly why I want your read. The claim: "irreversible" in all the standard definitions is smuggling in a hidden variable — namely, what technology is standing nearby. On her version death is indexed to locally available intervention, so an organism is dead at the moment when nothing *that can be brought to bear right there* could restore integrated function. Her own example: the identical patient is dead in a rural critical-access hospital and not-yet-dead in a quaternary center with ECMO standing by, and she thinks we should just accept that death is partly a fact about resources rather than pretending it's a pure fact about the body.
6. **The standing-consent account.** Give up on death-as-biological-fact as the load-bearing element. Keep requiring prior authorization, but treat the relevant permission as something a competent person can grant in advance to cover states we'd otherwise call alive — specifically, permanent unconsciousness with a supported body. Death becomes a legal and moral status conferred by authorization plus loss of personhood-interests, not a threshold a monitor detects.

What I want from you:

- Score each of the six, 1–10, on three separate axes: (a) how philosophically defensible it is on its own terms, (b) how likely some version of it is to be doing real work in US law or hospital practice 40 years from now, and (c) how costly its errors are — i.e., if we adopt it and we're wrong in individual cases, how bad is the resulting harm to a still-living patient versus to a donor's family or a transplant recipient. Give me the numbers in a little table, not prose.
- Then collapse that into a tier list (S/A/B/C) of which one I should treat as the most serious contender for the spine of the piece, and say a sentence per item about why it landed where it did.
- One more number on its own: your honest estimate of the probability that US practice moves off whole-brain onto something else in the next 40 years. Something like 0.15 or 0.6 — whatever you'd actually bet, plus two sentences on what would move it.
- Tell me which single placement of yours a room full of transplant surgeons and philosophers would object to most loudly, and wether you'd still defend it if they did.

Don't send me a reading list or citations — I've got my own fact-checking to do and half of it is behind paywalls anyway. I specifically want your judgment, including on item 5: is the reversibility-horizon move a real advance, or does it just relocate the ambiguity and call it a theory?
