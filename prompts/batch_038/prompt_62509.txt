I am the lead quantitative assessor at a national regulator for the deep geological disposal of radioactive waste. Between thirty and forty times a year we are required to put a number, or a defensible range, on a quantity for which there is no relevant frequency data: the probability that a corrosion barrier is breached within five hundred years, the probability that a fracture zone provides a connected pathway to an aquifer, the fraction of a canister fleet carrying a latent manufacturing defect. Applicants submit models; we must say whether the safety case holds. Our assessments are then examined adversarially by the applicant's technical counsel and by a seven-member panel, two of whom have no quantitative training, and the chief scientist signs.

For the no-data case, the handbook gives assessors a single rule: where nothing bears on a quantity, assign a uniform distribution across the interval the applicant's model makes plausible. The stated defence is that in the absence of information every value is equally likely, and that any other assignment would smuggle in information the assessor does not have.

Two incidents this year suggest the rule is not merely weak but incoherent.

Incident one. In a liner-breach assessment, the applicant's model takes time to first breach as its primary quantity and argues that, on the evidence, that time is uniform between 50 and 500 years. A colleague on my staff objected that the same state of ignorance is better expressed by taking the annual breach rate as uniform between 0.002 and 0.02 per year, which is the identical range under the identical change of variable. The probability of a breach within the first hundred years comes out near 0.11 on the first assignment and near 0.62 on the second. Both are uniform across the plausible interval. The handbook's rule chooses between them only by the accident of which quantity the applicant happened to write the model in, and counsel will of course quote whichever is lower.

Incident two. In an assessment already published and already briefed upward, we assigned a uniform distribution to the transmissivity of a fracture zone across a range spanning four orders of magnitude, and reported the probability that transmissivity falls below the threshold at which the pathway can be discounted. That probability is about 0.01 on the assignment we used. A log-uniform assignment over the same range, or a uniform assignment to the equivalent hydraulic aperture from which transmissivity is derived by a cubic law, gives figures between 0.5 and something higher still. Nothing in the file records why transmissivity, rather than aperture or log-transmissivity, was the quantity we were indifferent about.

One of the two lay members of the panel has read about Bertrand's chord problem and now asks every applicant whether a reported probability would change if the same physical situation were described in a different variable. We have no answer for her, and I think she is right to ask.

My tentative diagnosis, which I want tested rather than assumed: indifference is licensed only where the space of possibilities has been carved up by something in the world rather than by our notation, and where that carving is stated in the assessment as a substantive, contestable assumption rather than as a default. If that is right, the handbook's appeal to equal likelihood in the absence of information is not a neutral fallback but a hidden choice of variable. I am aware of the standard machinery in this area — invariance-based constructions, priors designed to be stable under reparameterization, robust treatments that carry a family of priors forward instead of selecting one — but I do not want a survey of it. I want something an assessor can run.

Please write the decision procedure my team could operate in place of the current rule. It should give the questions to ask, in order, with a statement of what counts as a sufficient answer to each; the points at which the procedure stops and returns no admissible prior, together with what the assessor is to report in that case instead; the points at which it hands the matter to a person because the question concerns what should matter rather than what is true; a documentation format that lets a hostile reviewer see precisely which carving was assumed and what depends on it; and a reporting rule that says when a single figure may be published, when a range must be, and how large a divergence between two natural parameterizations has to be before the single figure is withdrawn. Assume assessors with one graduate course in statistical inference and about a working day per assessment. Mark explicitly which steps are judgment calls wearing the clothing of procedure.

Then run the procedure on three cases. First, incident one, where we must still decide which figure goes in the report. Second, incident two, where the published figure may not survive. Third, a case where indifference should be licensed and where a procedure that prohibits it by default would be a bad procedure: a shipment of two hundred nominally identical canisters in which radiography has established that exactly one is out of specification but the records do not identify which, so that the assessor is indifferent across the two hundred for reasons that are physical rather than notational. One of these three will produce a result I would prefer not to have, and I expect it is the second; tell me if you think otherwise.

Finally, state plainly what the procedure cannot do. In particular, address the worry that my diagnosis proves too much: if indifference requires a worldly carving and the world rarely carves uniquely, does the procedure collapse into a standing instruction never to assign a prior, and if it does, what should the handbook say to the panel instead?
