I teach a graduate seminar in the philosophy of technology and am under contract for a chapter in an interdisciplinary volume on the epistemics of forecasting about advanced AI. One of my co-contributors, a policy scholar whose work I otherwise respect, argues throughout her chapter that the emergence of a recursively self-improving system should be understood by analogy with a fission chain reaction approaching criticality, and she draws a fairly drastic practical conclusion from it: because a supercritical assembly passes from merely warm to beyond all intervention on a timescale shorter than any institutional response time, the safety and governance work must be completed before the threshold is approached, and the threshold must be treated as a bright line rather than as a region. She calls this the control-rod point. Several of my students find the analogy persuasive in a way I cannot quite account for, and I suspect it is carrying more weight than its structure can bear.

Could you audit it for me? I would like the correspondence laid out explicitly — what plays the role of the neutron, of the multiplication factor, of the fissile material and its enrichment, of the moderator, of the geometry that determines whether a given mass is critical, and of the control rods — followed by a sorting of the disanalogies into those that genuinely threaten her conclusion and those that are merely picturesque. Two candidate disanalogies I keep returning to, and would like weighed seriously rather than waved away: first, in a chain reaction the multiplication factor is fixed by the material properties of the fuel and the geometry, so that supercriticality is a standing condition of an assembly one can inspect, whereas in the self-improvement story the analogous quantity — how much further capability one round of improvement actually buys — is precisely what is in dispute, which makes me worry the analogy assumes the conclusion it is offered to support; second, that a chain reaction does not change what the fuel is, since each generation of neutrons meets uranium no better at fissioning than the last, whereas self-improvement is supposed to alter the substrate itself. I would also like your view on the distinction I take the physicists to draw between reactors and weapons: a power reactor is steerable only because a small fraction of the neutrons sustaining the reaction are delayed by seconds relative to the fissions that produced them, and because rising temperature feeds reactivity back negatively, whereas a weapon is deliberately run prompt-critical so that the process completes before any mechanism can respond. If that is right, which of the two is her analogy an analogy to — and is she entitled to move between them as she does, invoking the reactor when she wants the image of a threshold that can be instrumented and approached, and the weapon when she wants the image of a point of no return?

Finally, two things I would not want the audit to omit. The analogy carries a substantial cultural payload — a secret wartime project, a race among states, a single decisive afternoon, an invention that cannot be un-invented — and I want to know how much of the persuasiveness of her argument derives from that import rather than from any structural fit between source and target. And I would like you to say, if you can, what a better analogy would have to represent, and whether one exists that is neutral on the very question at issue, namely the speed and controllability of the process: a research organization that progressively automates parts of its own work, a compounding process constrained by a reinvestment bottleneck, an epidemic with a reproduction number above or below one, or something else. Do not, however, hand me a replacement that begs the question in the opposite direction, toward slowness and tractability; if the honest conclusion is that no analogy in this neighborhood is neutral, say so and explain why. I would like a verdict on whether the control-rod point survives the audit in any weakened form, since my seminar includes two historians of the Manhattan Project and they will notice immediately if the physics is handled loosely.
