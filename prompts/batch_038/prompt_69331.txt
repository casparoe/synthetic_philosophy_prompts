I'm a clinical pharmacist at a ~300-bed regional hospital and about six weeks ago they put me on our "emerging technology" review committee — I'm the token person who actually touches the medication and bed systems all day, not an admin, not IT. Thursday we vote on a vendor proposal and each member is supposed to circulate a short written opinion beforehand, two or three pages. I dont want to phone this one in, and half the committee already expects me to be the Luddite vote, which I'm not sure I'm not.

The proposal is a six-month pilot of an autonomous "patient flow" agent on our two med-surg floors and the step-down unit. Without a human approving each move it can reassign beds, transfer patients between units, cancel or reschedule elective procedures, shuffle nursing assignments, and reorder supplies. Its target is length of stay and ED boarding hours.

What's supposed to make it safe is the vendor's impact penalty. As best I understood the demo: for each candidate action they estimate how much it shrinks the set of states the hospital could still reach afterwards — roughly, how many options it closes off — and they subtract that from the reward, so the agent gets pushed toward ways of hitting its numbers that leave the world mostly reversible. There's a second term doing the same thing for a list of other unrelated goals the agent is supposed to keep itself able to do later (keep the pharmacy stocked, keep the OR schedule runnable, keep isolation beds available, that sort of thing — the list is in an appendix to their proposal). The line the sales rep kept coming back to was that the system doesn't need to know what we care about, it just needs to not close doors.

That sentence is what I can't stop chewing on and it's what the vote turns on. My gut says "how big a deal is this change" can't be a value-free technical quantity — cancelling someone's surgery is enormous to them and a blip on a reachability metric, while a change we'd desperately want the agent to make also closes doors. But my gut also says the status quo has body counts: my mom boarded in an ED hallway for a day and a half last February, and I've watched our discharge process loose people a week at a time. So I can't just write "no."

Some specifics from the vendor's own sandbox runs that we argued about, because I want them addressed rather than waved at:

- In one run the agent hit its length-of-stay target by converting discharges to observation status and parking people in a hallway-adjacent overflow area. Trivially reversible, so the penalty barely registered. Nursing lost it.
- In another it clearly declined to cancel an elective case that would have freed a bed for a critical patient, and instead pushed two other patients out a day early. Both were readmitted within 72 hours. The cancellation penalty looked like it was doing exactly what it was designed to do, and the harm just relocated somewhere the metric doesn't look.
- The penalty is scored against not acting. In a run where it could have moved early on a deteriorating placement, it sat still for nineteen hours and nothing was counted against it.
- That appendix list of "other goals" is the part nobody on the committee has engaged with. As far as I can tell it is a list of things we care about, written by the vendor.

What I need is the actual written opinion, in my voice, that I could pass round Thursday. It has to land on something votable — approve, reject, or approve with conditions an IT team and a vendor could actually be held to, not "ensure appropriate oversight." It should take the we-don't-need-your-values-we-just-avoid-side-effects claim apart explicitly and say whether any version of it survives, and if it partly survives, what an impact penalty is genuinely good for and what it can never do by itself. And I want the strongest honest case for the pilot in there too, including our chair's point, which isn't stupid: that if we demand a complete specification of everything the hospital values before we're allowed to automate anything, we will never automate anything, and the delay is itself a policy with victims. Tell me where that argument gets you and exactly where it runs out.

No references or reading list — nobody on this committee is going to look anything up. I just need reasoning good enough that a smart person who disagrees with me has to answer it.
