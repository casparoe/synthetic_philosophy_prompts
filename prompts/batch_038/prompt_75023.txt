I have no academic training in philosophy. I spent eleven years instrumenting physical systems — sensors, control loops, maintenance records — and I have been reading on my own for about as long, and I have run into a disagreement that appears to go in circles. I want a diagnosis of its structure, not a verdict on which side is right, and not a map of who holds what; I am not looking for names or titles, and I will not be checking any.

The problem keeps recurring in two places for me. One is practical: you write down what a system should protect or maintain, and years later the system is running on a much better model of the world in which the things you named either do not appear at all or appear as composites of other things. The other is something I read about in the history of science: what did a term pick out before the theory that defined it was replaced. Both look to me like one problem, which I have come to think of in two halves. The first half I call inaccessible information: everything an observer knows about a system arrives through a narrow channel — a fixed set of measurements, a record that has already discarded most of the world — and some of what would settle the matter never comes through, sometimes not even in principle. The second half I call ontology identification: deciding which things the words in your account pick out on the far side of that channel, and whether those things survive a better account.

Three positions recur, and they do not converge.

The first holds that you fix your terms inside the model you currently have, and you supply rules for carrying those terms into richer models later — rules for coarse-graining and fine-graining, so that what was one component becomes an aggregate, or what was one aggregate becomes several components. On this view existence is always relative to a model and the real work is constructing the maps. The objection raised against it is that the maps are precisely what is in question: you cannot write the translation rule until you already know what you were pointing at.

The second holds that there is a fact about which patterns in a system are real, fixed not by any particular description but by how much those patterns buy you in prediction and economy compared with a complete but unusable account of the underlying detail. On this view you do not need translation rules; you need to locate the invariants, and the invariants are what everyone was groping toward all along. The objection raised against it is that this makes what exists depend on what is worth computing for an observer with particular limits, which seems to smuggle the observer back into the world.

The third holds that you should refuse to talk about what no access could ever settle, and confine your ontology to what the accessible data pin down plus whatever organization is needed to state it. The objection raised against it is that it either collapses into a shrug about everything beyond the data, or it is unstable, since what counts as accessible is itself fixed by some account of the channel, which is a model again.

Where I am stuck is that the three agree on every case and still do not move. Here is a case I constructed, because the real ones drag in too much machinery. Suppose all you have is a counter that ticks whenever something passes a point. Nothing else, ever. There are two readings of the far side: one source firing at a varying rate, or several independent sources pooled into the same channel. The channel destroys exactly the information that would separate them, and no amount of additional data from that same channel will restore it. Put that to the three positions and you get three answers — one says both readings are live and the choice between them is a bet to be encoded in the translation rules; one says the pooled process is the only real pattern and the question about sources is empty; one says there is nothing to say beyond the tick record — and each camp tells me the others have misdescribed the case rather than disagreed about it. No one is persuaded and no new argument ever turns up.

My hypothesis, which I hold loosely and expect you to attack: they are not disagreeing about ontology at all. They are disagreeing about what a successful identification has to deliver — a procedure, a truth-maker, or a guarantee against error — and each is grading the others against a standard the others never accepted. On that reading the counter case is not evidence against anybody. It is a case in which all three standards fail at once, and each camp reads the shared failure as the peculiar failure of its rivals.

Is that right? If it is not, tell me what the disagreement actually is: a verbal dispute over the word exists, a hidden empirical or formal question — for instance whether some class of such cases is decidable in principle, or whether the pooling is identifiable given auxiliary assumptions — a difference about which intuitions count as data, a difference about where the burden of proof sits, or simply a hard problem that is being mistaken for a confused one. Then tell me, given your diagnosis, what would count as progress: a distinction someone has not yet drawn, a formal result, a change of question, a new kind of case. And if your conclusion is that no progress should be expected, say why, and say whether the problem is ill-posed or merely unsolved.
