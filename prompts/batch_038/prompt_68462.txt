I'm a program officer at a small private foundation that funds theoretical fundamental physics — quantum gravity, early-universe cosmology, that kind of thing. Our board has an unusual mandate: we're allowed to fund work that won't be testable on any reasonable timescale, and we actually do, roughly a third of our portfolio. The problem is I have no defensible way to choose within that third. Right now it comes down to whichever applicant writes the most stirring prose about beauty and inevitability, and one of our reviewers — a working physicist, quite hostile to the whole post-empirical idea — writes back that we're funding theology. Both of those outcomes are embarrassing at board meetings.

I want to build the case around the non-empirical confirmation stuff in the philosophy of physics — Dawid's framework, as I've read it: the reasoning from there being no viable alternative to a theory within a well-mapped local space of possibilities, the reasoning from a theory unexpectedly cohering with or explaining things it wasn't built for, and the meta-level reasoning from the track record of that kind of inference in physics generally. Turn that into a triage procedure I can actually run.

Here's the shape of my job. Forty-odd proposals a cycle, and I get maybe twenty minutes of my own read on each before it goes out to external referees; I then write a one-paragraph rationale that a board of non-specialists can audit, and my director signs off. So the procedure has to be something I can run in that window without doing the physics myself, and every step needs to say what a *sufficient* answer looks like in writing — not "assess whether the theory space is exhausted" but the specific sentence in the proposal that would satisfy you and the specific sentence that would fail you.

What I need:

- Ordered questions, with a stated pass/fail for each and the points where the procedure stops at "no" versus where it hands the call to a human because the question is really about portfolio risk or who deserves a career, not about confirmation.
- Explicit machinery for the obvious abuses: "no alternatives" that just means "only eleven people work on this, eight of them from two schools"; coherence that the same group manufactured after the fact; a framework flexible enough to absorb any observation it meets. Build the tests in as steps, not as warnings in the preamble.
- A rule for distinguishing "not testable on our horizon" from "not testable in principle," and what to do when the proposal is silent about the difference, which most of them are.
- A default for missing information — I'd rather have a conservative default than a hole in the procedure.
- Honesty about which steps are judgment wearing a procedure's clothes, flagged as such, because I'd rather defend three soft steps than pretend there are none.
- A one-paragraph template for the board rationale that a hostile reviewer can't trivially knock down.

Then run it on three hypothetical proposals: (1) a quantum gravity program whose applicants argue the core problem has gone twenty years without a serious rival approach, and where the subfield is about fifteen researchers, mostly trained in two groups; (2) a proposed measure over a string landscape, built to fix one fine-tuning problem, whose applicants claim it also falls out of an unrelated consistency condition and lands on a numerical coincidence nobody put in; (3) a small, very elegant formalism I personally love and have staked some credibility on, whose central selling point is that it explains why the other programs fail, and which makes contact with no conceivable observation inside fifty years. On the third one, give me the answer the procedure actually produces, even if it's a flat no — and if you think the procedure is wrong rather than my instinct, argue that separately rather than bending the steps to rescue the proposal.

Finish with what the procedure cannot do: things I'll be tempted to use it for and it will misfire on. And don't give me a bibliography or a reading list — I can't chase sources and neither can the person covering me next month.
