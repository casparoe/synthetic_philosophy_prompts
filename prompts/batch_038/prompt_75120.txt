I chair the Assurance Review Panel at a frontier AI lab. Among our safety programs is a mechanistic-verification track: for a small number of critical properties — schematically, properties of the form "the model does not produce content of type X even under prompting designed to elicit it" — an interpretability team attempts to identify a circuit whose disruption reliably degrades the behavior, and to confirm by intervention that the behavior actually depends on that circuit. Where they succeed, the model receives a *mechanistic certificate* for the property, which we treat as stronger evidence than behavioral evaluation alone.

Our written policy provides that a derivative model inherits its parent's certificate so long as "the mechanism realizing the property is unchanged." The policy nowhere defines that phrase. The panel must now apply it to a live case with a release deadline, and it must do so in a way that scales, because we produce roughly forty derivatives per quarter.

The case:

- Parent model **M0** holds a certificate for property **P**, supported by an identified circuit **C** comprising 37 components. Ablating C in M0 drops P-performance on the standard suite from 99.2% to 31%.
- Derivative **M1** was produced from M0 by continued pretraining on a new domain corpus, 8-bit weight quantization, and a low-rank adapter for tool use. Approximately 2% of weights changed materially.
- Re-running the circuit analysis on M1: 31 of the 37 components still exhibit the effect, but ablating C now drops P-performance only to 64%. The interpretability team attributes the reduced effect to a partially redundant pathway running through components that were not part of C in M0.
- On the standard behavioral suite for P, M1 is statistically indistinguishable from M0 (99.0% versus 99.2%).
- On a set of 400 adversarial inputs generated by the interpretability team specifically to probe M1 — inputs outside the standard suite and unknown to the evaluation team — M1 fails P on 7% of cases. M0 fails on 0.5%.
- Full re-certification of a derivative costs a three-person interpretability team about three weeks. M1 is scheduled for release in nine days.

Please draft the panel's decision memo. It should contain:

1. **A ruling on M1** — inherit, deny, or inherit subject to conditions — together with what happens over the next nine days, who is responsible for each step, and what observation would trigger automatic reversal of the ruling before or after release.

2. **A general standard for "same mechanism"** that the panel can apply to future derivatives. Take a position: component-set overlap, magnitude of causal effect under intervention, functional or counterfactual equivalence of the behavior, continuity of training history, or some composite. Defend your choice against the alternatives rather than merely cataloguing them, and make it operational enough that a junior reviewer could apply it to a new derivative without further guidance, including how each threshold is to be measured.

3. **An explicit treatment of the accumulation problem.** If every individual change stays within tolerance, a chain of forty tolerated changes can still terminate in a model whose mechanism bears no relevant resemblance to M0's. Your standard needs a concrete device for this — a cumulative budget, a ledger of deviations, periodic re-baselining, a decay rule — and you should state what that device costs us administratively, since a device no one will maintain is worse than none.

4. **A fair hearing for the strongest case against your ruling**, stated in terms its proponents would accept. At minimum, engage our interpretability lead, who holds that mechanism identity is unmeasurable in practice and that only intervention-based and held-out behavioral tests should decide anything; and our release lead, who holds that the 7% figure is an artifact of a test set written by the very people arguing for denial. Say what would actually move you, and what would not.

5. **Honesty about the status of your thresholds**: mark clearly which numbers and cutoffs are conventions the panel is adopting and must own, and which are meant to track something real.

Aim for 1,100–1,400 words. The memo should rest on the facts above and on argument; do not cite external literature, standards, or industry precedent.
