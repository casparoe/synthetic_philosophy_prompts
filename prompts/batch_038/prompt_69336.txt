You're a political philosopher, but write this like a normal person talking — no jargon dumps. I've been chewing on the Ulysses story for a while now: guy gets tied to the mast so the version of him that hears the singing can't act on it, and people keep using it to explain why a constitution should be hard to change. Two details bug me. First, the ropes aren't what saved him, the wax in the sailers' ears is — he couldn't get untied later because nobody could hear him order it. So the binding works by stopping the message, not by making anybody stronger-willed. Second, he told them beforehand that if he begged to be let go they should tie him tighter. The ropes escalate.

Here's a case I made up in my head. A town charter says it can only be ammended by an 80% vote, and also that every twenty years that 80% rule renews itself automatically unless 80% vote to scrap it. So it isn't one dead guy's rope — the dead hand keeps re-tying itself, and each generation sort of consents by doing nothing.

Write a dialogue between two people arguing over whether that's still a Ulysses contract at all. One says the self-renewing part actually fixes the complaint, since it's a standing choice rather than a one-off act by people long dead. The other says it makes things worse, because inaction is now what binds you, so the consent is hollow. Both should be sharp — nobody wins because the other is dumb — and I want them to also fight about which piece of the story the 80% rule actually is: the wax (blocks the current majority's voice from getting through), the ropes (blocks their hands), or the tighter-bonds clause (a rule about rules). Think hard first and jot down three or four sentences brainstorming the strongest version of each side, then write the dialogue. Don't make them agree at the end, but each should give up one real point by then.
