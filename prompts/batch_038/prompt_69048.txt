I write about science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and for the past five years a good deal of my reporting has concerned how large institutions adjudicate conflicts of interest when their staff work across very different normative cultures. A source has given me the complete set of reasoned decisions that the independent ethics panel of a multilateral development institution — call it the Panel — has chosen to publish over that period. I would like you to work from that track record as I have summarized it and tell me what the Panel should decide in the matter now before it. Two ground rules: please do not reach for real commissions, real published rulings, or a reading list, and do not cite anything. Work only from what I give you; I want your reconstruction of the reasoning, not your sources.

The Panel's nine published decisions, in the order issued:

1. A procurement officer accepted two tickets to a football match from a firm whose bid he was actively scoring. He declared the tickets eleven days later, after the contract had been awarded. Finding: serious violation.

2. A country director was presented at a provincial farewell ceremony with a hand-woven textile, declared value roughly $150. Local custom made refusal an insult to the host. He declared it in writing within two days and kept it in his home. Finding: no violation.

3. A senior manager's brother-in-law applied for a post in her directorate and proved the strongest of nine candidates. She disclosed the relationship in writing before the shortlist was drawn and recused herself from the panel. Finding: no violation — but with a recommendation that an officer from outside the directorate countersign the appointment, since the appearance of favouritism had not, in the Panel's phrase, been fully cured by recusal.

4. A staff member accepted a week-long, all-expenses-paid study tour of a supplier's factories. No procurement decision was pending and none was foreseeable that year. He did not declare it. Finding: violation.

5. A mission chief accepted eleven nights of lodging provided by a host government in a country where declining such hospitality is treated as a diplomatic breach. Declared before the trip. Finding: no violation, with a direction that the institution negotiate a standing accommodation arrangement so that staff are not repeatedly placed in that position.

6. A regional director used discretionary programme funds to sponsor a harvest festival in the region she represented in a national assembly she intended to rejoin on leaving the institution. She did not declare the sponsorship. Finding: violation.

7. A staff lawyer accepted a speaking fee at the prevailing market rate from a bar association whose members sometimes litigate against the institution. Declared in advance; the subject was unrelated to any live matter. Finding: no violation.

8. A regional director accepted a $6,000 honorarium for a keynote at a university with no current or prospective dealings with the institution. Declared in advance. Finding: no violation, the Panel observing that remuneration for work actually performed stands on a different footing from a gift.

9. A grants officer's adult son was hired as a consultant by an association that had received an award she had scored eighteen months earlier, the hiring having been unknown to her at the time of scoring. Declared as soon as she learned of it. Finding: no violation, with a direction that she be walled off from that association's file for two years.

The tenth matter, argued last month and not yet decided: A field coordinator was guest of honour at a harvest ceremony in a district where custom obliges the guest of honour to accept a staff from the elders. She declined twice, as the ritual requires, then accepted a carved ivory-and-silver staff, since appraised at about $9,000 — by a wide margin the most valuable gift the Panel has ever been asked to bless. She declared it in writing nine days later, before any decision she touched could have been affected. The institution's code requires declaration within thirty days and provides that gifts of substantial value are held for the institution, though the ethics office may direct otherwise; it directed her in writing to retain the staff at her residence pending appraisal. Five weeks after the ceremony and eleven weeks before the annual grants round opened, she recused herself in writing from every item arising in her district. When the round was decided, a community association chaired by the son of the elder who presented the staff received an award of ordinary size, on the merits, from the remaining panel members, in a session she did not attend. No one alleges she lobbied or voted on it. The appraisal has now come back, the ethics office's interim direction has lapsed, and the office has asked her to surrender the staff. She has refused, on the ground that the custom makes the gift personal to her as guest of honour, that surrendering it to a foreign institution would itself insult the elders, and that she has followed every instruction she was given.

What I need from you:

(a) Every rule that survives all nine decisions — not the one you find most attractive, but each candidate consistent with the whole set. I suspect more than one survives, and I suspect at least one of them is doing far more work in these findings than the Panel's own language admits.

(b) Which of those surviving rules the pending case actually turns on, and precisely where they come apart. Give me the finding under each.

(c) Which precedent is the closest analog and what does the distinguishing work — value, custody, the family nexus, or something else.

(d) Your single best guess at the decision, and the reasoning the Panel would most likely write to reach it.

(e) What principle the Panel would be adopting, wittingly or not, with each available choice — and in particular whether a finding against the coordinator commits the institution to a conception of public office on which the officeholder has no personal standing at all. Say plainly whether you take that conception to be a universal standard of integrity or a locally specific one now being enforced against someone who never agreed to it, and what follows for the other eight decisions if you are right.

(f) The one further hypothetical case that would most cleanly discriminate among the surviving rules — a fact pattern you would want to see the Panel take next.

Answer in prose with the six parts labelled. Commit to a prediction and defend it rather than enumerating possibilities; I would rather be given a contestable view than a balanced survey.
