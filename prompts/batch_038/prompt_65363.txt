I am assembling a small anthology of speculative fiction about social ontology — stories that make an abstract debate felt rather than argued — and I would like you to contribute one. The question I want the story to press on is whether generations are real kinds at all. We speak of Boomers, Millennials and the rest as though they were populations with a shared nature, but it is not obvious what that nature could be: is a generation a historical kind, united by common descent from a stretch of events; a natural kind with some underlying structure that a competent enquiry could in principle detect; a socially constructed kind, existing only because it is named, counted and acted upon, and no less real for that; or a merely nominal convenience that pollsters, journalists and market researchers slice out of a continuum and then report as a finding? Sitting alongside that are the boundary cases — people born within a year or two of a cut-off, whose classification seems to turn on nothing but a number — and the question of whether the kind does any causal work of its own, or merely tracks age, period and circumstance all at once and lets us mistake the tracking for an explanation.

Please set the piece in a near-future administration in which cohort membership has become a fact of legal consequence. A Directorate of some kind issues certificates of determination, relied upon for a pension schedule, for the composition of jury panels, and for eligibility under a compensation scheme for those whose schooling was interrupted by a particular crisis. Determination is supposed to be mechanical: it follows from date of birth and nothing else. The complication is that the Directorate has recently acquired a formational inventory — a long structured interview combined with an audit of records, which reports which events actually shaped a person — and in a small minority of cases the inventory contradicts the register. Your protagonist is a determination officer of some twenty years' standing who has never had a file overturned, and the case in front of her is a woman born eleven days on the wrong side of a boundary, raised by parents who moved deliberately to somewhere the defining events of her registered cohort never reached, who is applying to be reclassified. She gains nothing either way; she says the certificate simply misdescribes her life. Around them, give me a colleague who wants the inventory quietly suppressed, on the grounds that the Directorate's whole legitimacy rests on dates being beyond argument, and a claimants' advocate who argues that if the categories are manufactured then the scheme is unjust to everyone the manufacture happens to exclude.

A few requirements. Aim for roughly 2,200 to 2,600 words, in third-person limited and the past tense, and write throughout in British English — spellings, idiom and institutional vocabulary all British. The metaphysics must be carried by what the characters do, decide and fail to decide, not by anyone delivering a lecture about kinds; there should be no essay hiding inside the dialogue. Do not name real philosophers or sociologists, and do not invent academic citations, footnotes or studies — if the Directorate has a founding paper, keep it offstage. Resist the easy satirical move of mocking generational marketing language, which is the obvious joke and a tired one; the story should take both the officer and the claimant seriously. The familiar generational labels may appear in passing, but the boundary at issue should be an invented one, since I do not want the plot hinging on a real cut-off year that readers can dispute. Finally, the ending should leave the underlying question genuinely open while making one particular answer cost your protagonist something concrete and specific.
