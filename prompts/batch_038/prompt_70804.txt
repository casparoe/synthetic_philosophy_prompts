For eleven years I published under the name Vera Lund; my legal name, still used by my family and now printed on the spine of my collected edition, is Margaret Ashe. I have been keeping a notebook of the sentences this double life forces on me, together with the verdicts I cannot shake, and I want to know what account of names and identity statements they add up to.

A. The magazine profile ran the sentence "Vera Lund is Margaret Ashe." It is true, and it was the entire news value of the piece; yet I maintain that it reports nothing about the world that "Margaret Ashe is Margaret Ashe" does not also report.

B. When my mother says "Margaret is upstairs" and my agent says "Vera Lund is late with the sequel," they are not saying the same thing — and I do not mean merely that the two sentences would move me differently. What differs is which of my two public lives the sentence drags along with it, and those lives are facts about the world, not private furniture in two heads.

C. A reader who has read everything published by Vera Lund and has never encountered the name Margaret Ashe learns something from "Vera Lund lives in Tucson" and nothing whatever from "Margaret Ashe lives in Tucson," though both are true, both are about me, and I would report either as saying that I live in Tucson.

D. In an unfinished novel a woman meets the same person twice — once as a recluse whose photograph she has studied for years, once as a voluble stranger at a wedding — and concludes that the recluse is a fraud while the stranger is the most honest person she has ever met. She has never seen either name in print. I hold that her two beliefs contradict one another in content, that she is not irrational in holding both, that no amount of reflection could disclose the contradiction to her, and that the usual repair — her beliefs are really about names — is unavailable, since there are no names in her head.

E. In a published novel I called one woman "Nell" in the family chapters and "Dr. Eleanora Vance" in the hospital chapters, and during revision I swapped the two at will: every swap left what the story says untouched and altered only what the reader felt. I take that as proof that, at least within fiction, substituting names that designate the same thing preserves what is said.

F. In a draft I destroyed there was a character called Ismay. "Ismay does not exist" I call true, and true in virtue of those chapters; "Ismay is Ismay" I can call neither true nor false — I want to say it fails to say anything at all.

Is there one account of what an identity statement reports, and of what a name contributes, that delivers all six of these verdicts? Set out the two or three candidates you would actually defend; for each, say what it makes true, false, or truth-valueless in A through F; and say plainly which of my verdicts it forces me to abandon and what abandoning them would cost my practice. If none survives, identify my weakest verdict and argue me out of it. Then apply your preferred account to a case I have deliberately withheld: a young scholar writes that Vera Lund never existed, she was Margaret Ashe all along, while writing in the same essay that Vera Lund wrote my second novel. I want to call both sentences true — can the account permit me that?

Please close with a table, one row per candidate account, with these columns: Account; What it says a sentence like A reports; Verdicts delivered (list from A–F); Verdicts I must abandon; Cost to my working practice (high, medium, low); One sentence on why it wins or loses.
