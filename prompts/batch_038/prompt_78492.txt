Write a dialogue of about 1,200 words between two members of a commission drafting the release rule for a jurisdiction that has abolished prison sentences: people adjudged to have committed crimes are now held in secure residential facilities and released on a panel's decision. One commissioner is a compatibilist in Dennett's vein, holding that determinism leaves a naturalized and graded notion of desert intact — tied to an agent's competence and responsiveness to reasons — and that confinement answers to it. The other is a free-will skeptic in Caruso's vein, who denies basic desert and wants the rule built on a public-health-quarantine model: confinement for as long as, and no more restrictively than, the prevention of harm requires.

Their task is to settle on the single statutory sentence specifying when release must be granted, and they cannot agree. Let them argue over the cases that sentence will actually govern: the person whose dangerousness has largely disappeared a few months into a serious harm; the person still dangerous after what would once have been a long term; the person whose offense was minor but whose projected dangerousness is high; and what, if anything, the rule owes to victims who want continued confinement.

Neither commissioner should get soft treatment. Press the compatibilist on whether desert is doing any work beyond deterrence, moral reform, and expression, and on why desert should track harm done rather than the agent's degree of reasons-responsiveness; press the skeptic on whether "no longer dangerous" functions as a cap or as a floor, and why necessity should not license indefinite confinement once proportionality is no longer anchored in desert. Each must answer the other's actual objection rather than a caricature. End on the disputed clause itself, with each stating precisely which words of it they can accept and which they cannot, and why; any convergence must be earned rather than declared.
