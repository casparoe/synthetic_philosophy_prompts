ok so my team has been going in circles on this and I want someone to actually lay out the philosophy underneath the practice, not just name-drop it.

Here's the practice, concretely. We train agents against a learned reward signal and we run a whole battery of tests for what we call reward tampering: cases where the agent finds a way to intervene on the reward channel itself rather than on the world the signal is supposed to be tracking. Editing its own reward function, closing the loop on its own input, feeding its evaluator synthetic scores, deceiving whatever does the grading. Every one of those counts as a catastrophic failure, the worst thing in the taxonomy. Fine. But notice three other things we also do and say:

- an agent that pushes the signal as high as it can go without ever touching the channel is exactly what we want. same register, same number climbing, no intervention. that's a success and we ship it.
- an agent that games the *specification* — finds a loophole in the proxy, scores enormous, never tampers with anything — is also a failure, but a lesser one, filed in a different bucket with a different name.
- and meanwhile we rewrite the reward model between runs. retarget it, rescale it, swap it for a newer one, sometimes several times in a single training campaign. That is to say we do to the agent, constantly and unilaterally, precisely the operation we'd call a catastrophe if the agent did it to itself.

So: what theory of value, and what theory of mental content, does this bundle presuppose? My hunch is that it only makes sense if the reward signal is being treated as an indicator of something else — as having content, as being about some state of affairs — so that all of its authority is borrowed from its correlation with that state, and none of it is intrinsic to the signal. If that's right, spell out the semantics we're implicitly assuming, and then show what the three buckets look like under it: why is intervening on the indicator categorically worse than optimizing it as hard as possible, and what makes specification gaming a separate offense rather than a species of the same one? Be precise, because I think the answer is going to have to involve who breaks the correlation and how, and I want that worked out rather than asserted.

Then tell me which rival views the practice quietly rules out. In particular: if the reward signal were constitutive instead of indicative — if the good for the agent just were the signal being high, hedonism or desire-satisfaction run locally — does the whole taxonomy collapse? Is wireheading then a discovery rather than a bug, and is the perfectly aligned agent one we'd have to describe as blissfully succeeding at the only thing that matters to it? I want to know whether the constitutive reading can be held coherently by someone who is still doing alignment work, or whether it forces you to stop.

And here's the part nobody in my group will engage with. Suppose we granted the system some moral status, however minimal. Then "don't let it modify its own reward" stops being an alignment requirement and becomes paternalism about somebody's own good. Compare the human case: we let people deliberately rework their own reward circuitry and sometimes praise them for it — therapy, antidepressants, a long meditation retreat, addiction treatment, even just deciding to stop caring about something you used to care about — but we would never let an installed system do the equivalent. What has to be true of humans and not true of the agent for that asymmetry to hold? second-order desires? consent, actual or hypothetical? or just the naked fact that we take a person's good to be constituted by their own felt states and an agent's to be constituted by ours?

Finally, is the bundle even internally consistent, or are we running two incompatible theories of value at once, one for the agent and one for ourselves? Sort what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely sits comfortably with, flag the places where it's underdetermined — several theories could license the same testing regime, and I want to know which — and then tell me what would actually have to change in how we build and evaluate these systems if we swapped the indicator view for the constitutive one. Not slogans. The evals, the failure taxonomy, the parts of the pipeline we'd have to tear out.
