Half my class was raised on the idea that anyone can make it if they hustle, and the other half comes from towns — or families, or countries — where making it gets you cut back down to size. It's a first-year seminar I'm calling something like "Status, Envy, and Getting Ahead": 22 students, one 80-minute block, room with movable tables. I assigned short readings on both sides but I dont want to just discuss them, because discussion is exactly the register where this whole thing hides. People are very good at defending a norm in the abstract that they enforce without thinking in practice.

So: design me an in-class game that makes them feel the difference between a norm that celebrates whoever pulls ahead and a norm that levels whoever pulls ahead — and specifically I want them to feel why the second one isn't just petty envy, and why the first one isn't just freedom.

Things I want baked into the mechanics, not the narration:

- The load-bearing feature is sanctioning cost. It should be cheaper, in game terms, to knock somebody down than to lift somebody up, and cheaper still than to build your own standing from scratch. I have a hunch that asymmetry is the actual engine of the cut-down culture, and I want them to run into it during play rather than be told.

- There should be one way to get ahead that's mostly luck and one that's mostly effort, and the group should be able to see which is which. Because the fight they always want to have is whether the tall person deserved it — and I want the game to make them discover that the answer doesn't settle what the group does next.

- Also: a student who's doing badly should have some way to gain standing that has nothing to do with succeeding. Give that route a cost, and let them find out what the cost is.

- No real money, and no candy if you can help it. Index cards, a whiteboard, a shared pot of something purely abstract is fine. Status has to be public and legible though — the whole point is that everybody can see who's tall.

- Nobody should end up genuinely embarrassed. I want the sting to land on a game role, not on the nineteen-year-old holding it. Tell me explicitly how you're insuring against that, since it's the likeliest way this blows up on me.

- They should not hear the name of either norm until the debrief. I want the reveal to land as a small shock of recognition.

Then the debrief. I want it to separate at least three things my students currently mash into one lump: resentment of somebody who did better than you, an actual commitment to equality, and policing whether somebody still counts as one of us. Map specific moments from the game onto each of those. And don't let it end tidy — I'd rather it end on whether the person who got celebrated and the person who got cut down are being judged by the same standard or by two different ones, and what it would take for them to say which.

Last thing: tell me what predictably goes wrong the first time I run this. I'm already expecting at least one table to refuse to sanction anybody out of politeness, and I want to know whether that's a bug I should engineer out or the single most interesting piece of data I'll get all semester. And be straight with me about which feature of these norms a paper-and-pencil game simply can't reproduce, so I know what I'm going to have to supply by hand afterwards.

Please write the whole thing in American English.
