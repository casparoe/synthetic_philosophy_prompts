I have a fifty-minute section with second-years in a rational choice course. They've had two weeks of expected utility theory — completeness, transitivity, independence — worked through with decision trees, and every one of them has read something mentioning Kahneman, so they will arrive expecting a "humans are irrational" story. I want to teach the Allais paradox, and I want them to leave with close to the opposite: that independence is the sure-thing principle wearing mathematical clothes, and that what's actually contested is what an axiom is supposed to be — a constraint on coherent preference, or an empirical generalization that a show of hands can refute. Plan the section for me; don't deliver it.

Specifically: the order the pieces must go in and what depends on what — in particular, whether the common-consequence decomposition of the two pairs comes before or after I collect their choices, given that showing it first kills the hook and showing it second leaves half the room unable to retract what they just picked. Which figures to use: the textbook million against five million, Allais's own hundred million francs against five hundred, which will sound like a joke, or a scaled-down ten-dollar version; what the carrying example has to have for the reversal to bite, and what it must lack. The misconceptions in the order they'll surface — that it's just risk aversion over huge sums, that probability weighting already dissolved it, that you can always bend the utility function to fit any choice, that independence is a technical convenience with no normative force — and the exact point in the sequence to head each off. One closing question that would tell me whether they can now spot an Allais-style violation in a pair they've never seen. And what I should openly refuse to cover at this level.
