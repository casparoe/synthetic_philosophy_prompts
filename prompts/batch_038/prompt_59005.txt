So I've been reading the rationalist blogs for going on fifteen years now — Overcoming Bias back in the day, SSC, the occasional history-of-science thread — and I like to think I've trained myself to flinch whenever somebody reaches for an analogy to end an argument. Last week somebody got me anyway and I genuinely can't tell if I got got or if he's right.

The setup: a friend of mine who reads way too much legal theory was in a group chat argument about the statues thing, specifically about whether you're allowed to call a 17th-century merchant who invested in slave voyages a bad person. His move was that retrospective moral condemnation is exactly like an ex post facto law — you're convicting someone under a rule that didn't exist when they acted, which is the paradigm case of injustice in basically every legal system worth the name (the US Constitution flatly prohibits them, nullum crimen sine lege, all that). So when people say "well, they should have known better," he says they're demanding something no court would ever grant: notice of the rule before you're held to it. Then he extended it outward to historians — anyone who evaluates the past using today's categories is doing the same thing with extra steps, and the only honest procedure is to judge actors by the standards that were actually available to them, meaning what their own society told them. He seemed pretty pleased with himself and half the chat agreed.

Here's what I want from you, and you don't need to name-drop sources or give me a reading list, I want the machinery laid out. First, the correspondence table: in his analogy what exactly plays the legislature, the promulgated rule, the defendant, the court, the punishment, and the notice requirement — because I noticed he never says who the moral lawmaker is, and I suspect that's not an accident. Second, the disanalogies, but sorted: which ones actually load-bearing against his conclusion and which are just decoration that a defender could shrug off. Third, three specific pressure points I came up with and can't resolve. (a) Doesn't the analogy only work if moral norms are made and dated by somebody the way statutes are, i.e. doesn't it sneak in a positivist metaethics about morality and then use the legal machinery to make that look neutral? (b) At Nuremberg "that crime hadn't been written down yet" was rejected as a defense — is that a counterexample to him, or does it only work because international law is a weird half-legal thing where the analogy was already straining? (c) Does his slide from "the act was wrong" to "the agent is blameworthy" go through, or has he answered a different question than the one the chat was actually asking — and relatedly, does negligence law's objective reasonable-person standard, which holds people to a bar they personally didn't endorse, undercut the notice point from inside the legal system he's borrowing from? And fourth, his leap from individual moral assessment to historians-in-general: is that even the same claim? My sense is that presentism in historiography is usually about concepts, periodization, what counts as an explanation, and which questions get asked of the sources — not about scolding dead people — so even a perfect analogy would only cover a slice of the target. Finally, if you think the analogy's no good, tell me what a better one would be, ideally one that keeps whatever is right in his intuition, because I do feel there's something to the notice thing that I don't want to just throw away.
