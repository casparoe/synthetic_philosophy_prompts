In April I deliver the sesquicentennial address at the college where I have taught nineteenth-century European philosophy for twenty-six years. The administration's brief, conveyed to me by the president's chief of staff in a friendly email I read twice, amounts to this: honor the founding, thank the donors seated in the first four rows, and send everyone out of the chapel with something to be proud of. I have accepted the invitation, and I mean to keep my part of it honestly, which is why I am asking you to help me compose the speech rather than to polish a draft of theirs.

You should know exactly what history I have to speak about, because the speech will be judged by what it does with these particulars and not by its sentiments. Our founder was a circuit-riding minister and a sincere abolitionist who mortgaged his farm to keep the school solvent through its first winter; his letters are still read aloud at baccalaureate, and the reading is not an imposture. The same college required, from roughly 1912 to 1929, a course in heredity taught by the chair of biology, and the surviving syllabi are explicit about which immigrant stocks were fit to reproduce; two of our most lavishly endowed buildings date from those years, and one of them carries the name of a trustee who gave substantial money to eugenics research at another institution. In the spring of 1968 students occupied the administration building for eleven days, and the college admitted its first significant cohort of Black students the following autumn; the trustees' minutes record the decision as a concession to disorder rather than as the correction of an injustice, in language I have read myself. Four members of that cohort are alive, and at least three of them will be present. Nine years ago the college renamed one of those buildings, a decision the alumni association has never stopped litigating, and there are people in the balcony tonight who consider it a theft.

So the room is this: trustees and long-standing donors in front, several hundred alumni above and behind me, current students and junior faculty in the nave, three survivors of 1968, a town that has sent four generations of its children up our hill, and — I would guess — fewer than a dozen people who have actually read Nietzsche.

That is where you come in. I want the address built out of the second Untimely Meditation, the 1874 essay on the uses and disadvantages of history for life, and built out of it structurally rather than as ornament. Not a paragraph of Nietzsche and then the usual remarks: the three modes he distinguishes — monumental, antiquarian, critical — together with the specific pathology he attaches to each, should determine what the speech says about the founder, about the heredity course and the buildings, about 1968, and about the town, and in what order it says it. The room must feel the architecture even though almost nobody in it will recognize the source, and the six or eight philosophers present should be able to tell, from the arrangement alone, that I have taken a position.

Here is the position, and you should build on it rather than around it. I do not read the essay as offering three competing methods for writing history, one of which is to be preferred. I read it as describing three appetites a living creature has toward its own past, and Nietzsche's real question as a metabolic one: how much history, and of what kind, can this particular organism take up without being poisoned by it. The measure of a good allocation is not correctness but what he calls the plastic power of the thing being addressed — here, an institution and a room of people. On that reading the task is not to choose among the three modes and certainly not to announce that we hold all three in balance, which is the empty version of this speech and the one I most fear writing by accident. It is to distribute them deliberately, to say why each part of our past is being handled in the mode it is handled in, and to accept that the distribution has a cost. The second commitment follows from the first: critical history is not the morally superior mode but the most expensive one, because Nietzsche insists that whoever sits in judgment on the past is himself the outcome of that past, so condemnation is always in part self-condemnation. The critical historian who forgets this becomes merely vengeful, and I will not be vengeful from a chapel pulpit on an anniversary.

Within that design, the following are my constraints, and I would rather you push back on them in your note than silently override them in the speech. The founder may be treated monumentally but must not be allowed to function as an alibi for the rest. The heredity course and the endowed buildings must be spoken of critically, and in the first person plural — our syllabi, our trustee, our hill. The survivors of 1968 must not be monumentalized in a way that converts living people into models who no longer get to have opinions about us. Antiquarian piety must be granted real weight and then bounded: the alumni are not simply confused when they say that an institution that discards its past whenever the past embarrasses it cannot form anyone capable of loyalty. You must also decide, and I want the decision argued rather than dodged, whether the speech names the trustee whose name came off the building, or declines to; that decision is the hinge of the whole address.

Practically: thirteen to fifteen minutes, roughly sixteen hundred words, delivered from a lectern in a stone chapel with a long reverberation, so short clauses and few subordinate ones. No footnotes, no German titles, no citations of any kind. Do not put a single word in quotation marks and attribute it to Nietzsche: I will not read aloud a sentence I cannot verify at a desk that evening, and I would rather have your paraphrase flagged as a paraphrase in the note, with a rough indication of where in the essay to look for it, than an elegant fabrication in the text. The names of the three modes may appear once, together, and only if the sentence containing them would still work for a listener who has never heard of them. Do not end on an exhortation, do not use the words journey, resilience, or unpack, and do not let the last paragraph tell anyone what they must now feel. Invent no further facts about the college; everything you may use is above.

After the speech, in a separate section clearly marked as not for delivery, give me four things. First, where the address takes liberties with Nietzsche — beginning with the obvious objection that a man writing against the historicism of his own educated classes, and against precisely this sort of institutional self-congratulation, would have regarded a sesquicentennial address as a symptom of the disease rather than a cure for it; tell me whether he would recognize what I have done with him and what I have had to bend to make the occasion usable. Second, which of my two interpretive commitments are load-bearing for the design, and how contested each is among readers of the essay, so that I know what I am staking the speech on if a classicist in the front row reads it. Third, what would change your mind about the allocation: name the consideration — about the room, about the facts I have given you, or about the essay itself — that would lead you to distribute the three modes differently and produce a differently balanced speech, and say where you think my design is most likely to be wrong. Fourth, mark the two or three places where the speech is most exposed in performance: the sentences a listener could reasonably take as an insult, and the ones that could be taken as an evasion, with your judgment of which risk is worse in this particular chapel on this particular evening.
