I'm 73 and I spent my working life in atmospheric physics — mostly designing instruments that went up on satellites and stared down at clouds, which sounds more romantic than it was. Since I stopped working I've taken to reading philosophy at night, badly, with a dictionary. Mostly it's been free will and the philosophy of science, and then lately stuff about war, because of my daughter.

Here's the situation, and I want your honest opinion rather than comfort.

My daughter Kate spent six years in the air force flying what they call remotely piloted aircraft — she sat in a trailer in Nevada with a lot of screens and she was very good at it. She's out now, doing something completely different, and mostly she seems fine. But over the holidays my brother Frank, who did two tours back in the eighties in a country I won't name, got onto her about it, kindly, the way Frank does, and it turned into an argument that hasn't ended.

Frank's line is basically this: a soldier on one side and a soldier on the other have the same right to shoot at each other, always, and that's what makes war different from murder. Neither of them is personally doing anything wrong; the rightness or the wrongness belongs to the politicians who started it, and to nobody in uniform. He says this is old, it's how the thing has always been understood, and Kate should just accept it and stop picking at it.

Kate's line is that Frank's rule only makes sense if both people can actually hurt each other. She says: in six years nothing was ever aimed at her, not one single thing; she drove home every night and cooked dinner; the men she watched on a screen could not have reached her even if they'd known she existed. So whatever it is that makes one armed person permitted to kill another armed person, she thinks it must have something to do with each of them being a live danger to the other — and she's not sure it ever applied to her, which she thinks puts her in a worse position than Frank, not a better one.

Now here's where I'm stuck, and I think I'm muddling two separate things without being able to tell them apart. Part of me wants to say Frank is right, because the alternative looks horrible: it would mean you have to settle whether the war itself was a legitimate one to fight before you can say whether any particular shooting inside it was allowed, and you'd be judging every private and every corporal by whether his government happened to have a good cause — which seems both impossible for him to know and an open invitation for the winning side to hang the losing side's soldiers as common criminals. But part of me thinks Kate has her finger on something Frank's tidy little rule just steps over, and that the rule was built for two armies standing in a field shooting at each other and is now being stretched across something it was never made to cover.

And there's a third thing she told me, which may be the real reason she's picking at it. One strike in particular: three men on the roof of a compound with rifles, she was told they were lookouts, there was a decision to make fast and she made it. Afterwards she got the impresion from people on the ground that they might have been guarding that compound against a different armed group than the one she'd been briefed on — that is, their rifles may have been pointed at nobody she was fighting for. She says: on Frank's rule that shouldn't matter a jot, they were armed men in a war zone and that's the end of it. But she can't make herself believe it doesn't matter, and she wants to know whether she's just being sentimental or whether the rule genuinely fails to cover that case too.

So who's right, or what's the right way to hold all of this? I'd especially like to know, first, whether Kate's objection — that neither she nor they were ever in any danger from each other — actually undermines Frank's rule, or whether the rule was never based on danger in the first place and she's attacking a straw version of it. And second, whether there's any way to keep the part of Frank's position I want to keep, that you cannot make foot soldiers answer for their country's reasons, without also accepting the part that makes three men on a roof simply disappear. And whether doing it with a joystick from Nevada is a difference in kind or only in degree, since Frank says men have definately been killing from a comfortable distance ever since someone invented the bow.

Please answer in plain prose, like a letter — no headings, no bullet points, no bold, no tables. I read on a tablet and all that formatting makes my eyes go funny. Take your time, nothing here is urgent, but I do want you to come down somewhere at the end instead of telling me both sides have a point.
