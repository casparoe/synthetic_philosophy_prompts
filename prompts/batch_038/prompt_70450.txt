So my friend Dara and I got into it last night — three hours, kitchen table, empty bottle of wine, nobody won — about why some countries are rich and others aren't, and I'm honestly still buzzing about it. She's a third-year econ PhD student doing development, I did a bunch of history and philosophy in college and never shook the habit, so we came at it from completely different angles and somehow ended up more stuck then when we started. What frustrated me wasn't the disagreement, it was that at some point I stopped being able to tell what the disagreement was even about. Every move one side makes, the other side has had an answer ready for twenty years, and nobody budges.

Here's roughly the terrain as I understand it, so tell me where I've garbled it. The institutions crowd says the deep cause of the income differences is property rights, constraints on the state, contract enforcement — and their signature move is an instrumental variable: early European settler mortality in the colonies as an instrument for what kind of institutions got installed, on the theory that where Europeans died fast you got extractive states and where they survived they rebuilt something like home. Then a critic went back through the underlying mortality numbers and argued that a lot of them are really troop mortality on military campaigns rather than settlers, and that once you recode, the first stage mostly evaporates; the original authors said the recoding is wrong and the results survive. Meanwhile the geography people say the disease environment hits health, productivity and schooling directly, so the instrument might just be picking up geography with institutions riding along as a passenger; the human capital people say the colonists who survived were disproportionately literate Europeans, so the same instrument is measuring education; and the culture people say none of this works without beliefs about work, trust, time and innovation, in one notorious version without selection for those habits across generations. Then there's the pile of within-country cases everyone throws at each other — the two Koreas, the twin towns on the US-Mexico border, the east-west German split — which feel decisive for institutions and do absolutely nothing inside a cross-country regression, plus the obvious reverse-causality worry that rich countries can afford good institutions, plus the fact that we get one world and one history and no way to randomize any of it. Dara's line all night was that only the identification approach licenses a causal claim at all; mine was that she was buying precision about a question nobody asked at the expense of the question people actually care about, which I couldnt state cleanly enough to win with.

Please act like an expert economic historian and philosopher of social science who is equally at home in identification arguments and in the philosophy of causation and explanation, and think this through carefully. Start by brainstorming out loud about what kinds of thing a stalled debate like this could be, then commit to a diagnosis. Which is it: a verbal fight over what counts as "the" cause when everything is downstream of everything else; a clash of evidentiary standards where credentialed causal inference and comparative-historical mechanism-finding simply don't recognize each other's results as results; three or four different questions traveling under one label — a variance decomposition of cross-country income, a mechanism story for how a given place got where it is, and a policy-relevance question — that never actually meet; or a genuine hidden empirical question about direction of causality and lags that is unsettled only because the data will never exist? My hunch, offered as a hypothesis to be knocked down, is that it's mostly the third with a real chunk of the second, and that the whole settler-mortality exchange is a fight about a tool that both camps secretly agree matters less than they pretend. Ground the diagnosis in what each side actually argues rather than surveying the field, and don't give me a reading list or citations — I want reasoning, not references. Then tell me what would count as progress on your diagnosis: a distinction, a kind of evidence, a reformulation of the question, a formal result — or make the honest case that no progress should be expected, and say whether that would be a failure of the discipline or the correct response to the shape of the problem. American English please, and be concrete rather than diplomatic; if you think one side is closer to right about something, say so and say why.
