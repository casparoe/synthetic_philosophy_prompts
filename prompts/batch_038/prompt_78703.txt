A friend and I argued for most of last night about the following and got nowhere, so I should like a more careful treatment than either of us managed.

The practice I have in mind is the ordinary two-sided quote of a dealer or market-maker. For the same instrument, at the same moment, the firm publishes a price at which it will buy and a higher price at which it will sell. It does not regard itself as holding one fair value at which it would trade in either direction, and it does not treat the gap between the two numbers as a temporary error to be competed away; the gap is the firm's income. Meanwhile, incoming orders are assessed one at a time, as they arrive, against the firm's current book and current information. The firm does not consider, and has no obvious reason to consider, the sequence of trades to which its quoting gives rise.

Yet the same industry is fanatical about coherence elsewhere. If the prices of two related instruments imply a risk-free profit, those prices are described as wrong, the discrepancy is traded away in seconds, and the whole apparatus for pricing derivatives rests on the requirement that no such profit be available. So we have, inside a single institution, a stern insistence that prices hang together across instruments, alongside a relaxed acceptance that the price of one instrument is not a single number at all.

My friend's position is that there is nothing philosophical here: the gap pays for holding inventory and for the risk of trading against someone better informed, and that is the end of the matter. My suspicion is that the practice encodes a substantive and rather unusual theory of belief and of agency across time, and that the two halves of it are in tension.

So: what does the two-sided quote presuppose? Does maintaining an interval of acceptable prices commit the dealer to a view on which rational belief is a range rather than a point, and does it thereby rule out the familiar picture of an agent who has one number for each uncertain question and stands ready to buy or sell accordingly? Does assessing each order in isolation, while knowing perfectly well what sequence of such assessments one's own quoting will generate, commit the firm to a view on which the rational unit of choice is the moment rather than the plan? Consider also the other side of the transaction: a customer who crosses the spread twice, buying and then selling back the same thing, loses a sum he could have computed in advance, and we treat anyone who knowingly walks into that as a textbook case of practical irrationality. The dealer sits on the other side of precisely that sequence, and its own published prices look rather like the belief-state such arguments are designed to rule out. How should that be resolved?

Please distinguish what the practice strictly requires from what merely sits comfortably with it; say whether its parts presuppose consistent things, given that the same firm must report one fair value for the position in its accounts and one number against which its risk limits are set; and say what would have to change if we imposed strict single-price coherence instead, meaning one figure at which the firm stands ready to trade in either direction, revised only as its evidence is revised. Would markets survive that, and what exactly would the firm lose?

Finally, tell me what would change your mind: which consideration, if I pressed it, would force you to my friend's deflationary answer rather than mine, and at which step is your reconstruction most likely to have gone wrong. Please write in British English.
