I am a third-year doctoral student in biostatistics, and for the past year I have been sitting in on an informal philosophy-of-religion reading group attached to our university's humanities centre: eight of us, mostly non-philosophers, moderated by a graduate student in the department. This month's topic is divine hiddenness, and the moderator circulated as background a transcript of a podcast segment by a Christian academic who argues that hiddenness is the objection to his own theism that keeps him up at night. I have read enough of the primary literature to know that the argument has changed shape over the decades and that the operative notion is non-resistant nonbelief rather than merely honest or reasonable nonbelief, but I do not trust my reading of the transcript. Our discussion last week followed the host's framing almost entirely, and his version struck me as tidier — and more conclusory — than anything I encountered in the papers. What I want is not an adjudication of theism versus atheism but an accuracy audit I can bring to the next meeting: which parts of the gloss a specialist would endorse, which are tolerable popular simplifications, and which are distortions that change what the argument actually requires.

The segment runs roughly as follows. "Schellenberg's hiddenness argument is the best argument against the existence of God, and it is the one that should worry Christians most. Here is the idea. If God exists, God loves each of us the way a good parent loves a child, and a good parent does not hide from a child who is looking for her. So if God exists, God would make sure that anyone honestly open to a relationship with God believes that God exists; God would never leave such a person in the dark. And of course there are such people — sincere, reflective nonbelievers who have looked and found nothing, and who would welcome a relationship with God if they thought there were a God to have one with. You do not even have to argue about whether they exist: you can see it in the demographics, in societies like Sweden, Japan, and China, where thoughtful and decent people simply grow up without any inclination toward theism. So there is non-resistant nonbelief, and were there a perfectly loving God there would not be. Notice that none of this depends on any claim about suffering, which means the familiar answers to the problem of evil do not touch it. The theist's replies are correspondingly few. You cannot say that these people are really resisting God, because that assumes what is at issue and converts every case of honest nonbelief into secret sin. You cannot say that God has reasons we cannot see for staying hidden, because that concedes that nothing could count against theism, which is to stop treating it as a claim about the world. What is left is either to deny that God is perfectly loving or to accept that a perfectly loving God is content to let sincere seekers spend their whole lives without him. Both are hard pills, but I think honest theists have to swallow one."

Could you work through that claim by claim — there are about a dozen separable moves in it — and sort each into accurate, defensible simplification, or distortion, saying in a sentence or two what turns on the difference? In particular I would like to know: where the proponent of the argument would himself object to being described this way, since my impression is that several sentences are framed to please atheists rather than to state his view; whether the parent-child comparison is doing quiet work that the argument cannot afford; whether the demographics sentence belongs to this argument at all or is borrowed from somewhere else; whether the two dismissals of theistic replies fairly describe what those replies claim and where the burden of argument actually sits; and whether the closing dilemma survives the corrections, or whether there are exits the host omits that working theistic philosophers genuinely take and what those exits cost. Please also tell me which single distortion does the most damage — the one I should make sure the group does not carry away with them — and finish by rewriting the passage as a paragraph that a non-specialist audience could follow and that a specialist would judge fair. I would rather not have a reading list or a set of references to chase; I cannot look anything up this week, and what I need is the reasoning laid out directly.
