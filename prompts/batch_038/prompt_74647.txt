So my roommate and I have been going in circles for about three hours on the wrong kind of reasons thing and neither of us can state his position without it collapsing, which is infuriating. Help.

His view: there's no such thing as a wrong kind of reason. A reason is a reason. If a powerful demon will wreck my life unless I sincerely admire a saucer of mud, then I have a reason to admire the mud, and any theory that denies this has quietly redefined reason to protect a favorite account of value. He thinks the whole literature is a defense racket for the claim that something is admirable just in case there's reason to admire it.

My view: there's obviously a difference between considerations that have something to do with the mud and considerations that don't, and the difference does real work. But every time I try to say what the difference is, he hands me a case that either slips through it or that I want to describe the other way round.

Here are my verdicts. He shares most of them, which is the annoying part, because I don't think his principle generates them and I can't produce mine.

1. Demon and mud: the demon gives me a reason to want to want to admire the mud. It does not give me a reason to admire the mud. He says I'm just refusing to call a thing what it is.
2. Someone offers me ten grand to find their awful amateur play funny. Same shape as 1, I think, but weaker — because I notice I can sometimes actually succeed at this one through repeated exposure, whereas no amount of trying gets me to admire mud on demand. I don't know whether that difference in psychology is doing philosophical work or is just a fact about me.
3. My friend's terrible new haircut, which she is delighted with. Here I flip: I think I have a genuine reason to admire it, not merely a reason to want to want to. But the considerations doing the work — she's happy, she's my friend, it costs me nothing — are not about the haircut at all.
4. Same structure, higher stakes: a guy I know is dying and is deeply attached to a hideous porcelain figurine his mother left him. I think there's something like a requirement to find it charming. He says no, there's a requirement to say it's charming and not wince, which is a completely different thing. If he's right about this one, what was I doing in 3?
5. Belief seems to behave differently from all of the above. I can't get myself to believe something I'm confident is false for money, and I don't feel the pull of a reason there — the offer feels simply beside the point. So maybe belief has no wrong kind of reasons, only the illusion of them. But then I'm not sure admiration is different and I'm just being less honest about it.
6. Fear is the one that wrecks me. A harmless spider in a jar, plus a threat if I don't fear it. I feel like fear can be had for the wrong kind of reason in a way belief can't — but if that's true, its not a split between two kinds of reason, it's a split between attitudes, and I need a story about which attitudes have which property.
7. The billionaire and the toxin: a million dollars if I form, by midnight, the intention to drink something that will make me violently ill for a day and is otherwise harmless. I think I can't form that intention on the basis of the money, and I've been assuming that's the same phenomenon as 1 and 2. He says it isn't, it's about intentions being self-undermining rather than about kinds of reasons, and honestly I can't tell our accounts apart.
8. Admiring a person versus admiring a feature of them. A historian lays out for me that some strategist was brilliant and also monstrous. I think I can admire the brilliance, and that I'd be doing something wrong if I admired him, full stop. So maybe the right-kind/wrong-kind talk is just a clumsy way of marking the difference between admiring a property and admiring a thing that has the property — in which case my whole distinction is a category confusion.
9. Self-management: I think it's completely fine, not merely excusable, to arrange my life so that I come to want to run in the morning — buy the shoes, sign up with a freind, move near the park. But I think it's not fine to just decide, on the spot, that I want to run. In both cases the consideration in play is entirely about me and my future welfare. I can't keep those apart and I think I should be able to.
10. And I'll admit the motive: I think admirable-just-in-case-there's-reason-to-admire is worth saving, so I'm worried my distinction is doing the work of a prejudice rather than marking a joint. He keeps saying that out loud and it lands.

What I want is a principle, or a small set of candidate principles, stated precisely enough that I could apply it to a case I haven't thought of. Tell me what each clause is there for and which of my verdicts it's there to deliver. Then tell me which of 1–10 it can't get, and please don't strain to save all of them — I'd rather have a simple account that costs me two verdicts than a baroque one that costs me none, and I want to know which two you'd spend. Also: does it have to be attitude-relative, one rule for belief and another for desire, admiration, fear, and intention, and if so is that a defect in the account or just what the territory looks like? And which of these three readings do you think is actually right — that the wrong-kind cases are reasons for a different attitude, reasons of a genuinely second-tier kind, or not reasons at all — because I've been sliding between all three and he's caught me at it.
