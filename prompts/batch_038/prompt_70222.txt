I am preparing a two-page handout for the closing session of a graduate philosophy-of-language seminar. The students have worked through Austin's lectures on performatives and Searle's classification of illocutionary acts, and my difficulty is that they have retained the family names and the phrase "direction of fit" without retaining the structure that generates them: when a case is hard, they reach for whichever label sounds nearest. I want to hand them a decision tree that sorts a disputed utterance by its illocutionary force, and I want the tree constructed out of the logical space rather than out of Searle's headings. Please produce the following.

**1. The tree.** Five to seven branching questions, ordered so that each node can be answered by someone holding only a transcript of the utterance and a neutral description of the circumstances in which it was made. No node may ask what the utterance is called, what verb the speaker would later use to describe herself, or how the speaker now feels about it. The first node must not be "is this constative or performative?" — say briefly, in a footnote under that node, why that axis is unavailable to us. The axes I want represented somewhere in the tree, in whatever order you judge defensible, are: direction of fit; whose future conduct the act bears upon; whether the act would still be complete if the hearer understood it perfectly and remained entirely unmoved; whether the hearer's uptake is constitutive of the act or merely a hoped-for consequence of it; whether an accepted conventional procedure governs the act, such that its violation renders the act void rather than merely insincere; and whether the speaker has the standing to perform this act in these circumstances.

**2. The leaves.** At minimum: promise, prediction, threat, warning, offer, command, request, assertion, apology, declaration of the institutional kind (sentencing, pronouncing, naming), and at least two further leaves that the space makes available but that no one standardly occupies. Every leaf carries one sentence stating its most awkward commitment — the thing a defender of that leaf has to say and would rather not.

**3. Two explicit diagnostics.** First, list every pair of leaves your tree cannot in fact separate, and state what further fact would separate them, or why the distinction collapses at this level of description. Second, list the combinations of answers to your nodes that lead nowhere, and for each say whether the cell is empty because the position is incoherent, because it is unattractive, or because no one has simply bothered to occupy it.

**4. A worked pass through five cases.** For each, trace the path node by node, name the exit leaf, and say whether that exit matches the verdict you would defend; where it does not, identify the specific node at fault rather than patching the case.

- A landlord, whose tenant is three weeks behind, says: if the money is not in the account by Friday, I will start eviction proceedings. My class is evenly divided on this one.
- An incumbent politician tells a rally: when I am re-elected, taxes will come down.
- An officiant conducts a beach ceremony; it later emerges that his licence lapsed the previous month and neither party knew.
- A says "I'm sorry" to B; B hears it clearly but takes it for sarcasm.
- An actor playing a judge says "I sentence you to five years" in the third act, to an actor playing the defendant.

**5. One closing note.** State which single node in your tree a student under time pressure is most likely to get wrong, what the wrong turn looks like from the inside, and which leaf it spuriously delivers them to.

Two constraints on the deliverable: no reading list, no citations, and no history of which philosopher introduced which term — the handout must stand on its own. And no node may be answerable by someone who already knows which leaf they want to reach.
