I write historical novels and a festival's asked me to run a session for their book group crowd — about 18 people, mostly readers rather than historians, one long table, 90 minutes. I want an activity that makes them feel why the old line about history being written by the victors is half true and half a bad slogan, without me saying either half up front. They shouldn't hear the word victors until the debrief.

Make the event up — a strike, a siege, a treaty, whatever, but nothing real; I dont want anyone fact-checking me at the bar afterwards. The trick I'm after: the winning side's paperwork should be the fullest, dullest, most dependable record that survives, while the losers leave a handful of vivid scraps that are half myth. Then give them a second mechanism that has nothing to do with winning at all — a copying or translation bottleneck with its own incentives, perhaps — so the argument splits into at least two separate causes instead of one. Each group has to produce a single account under a hard word limit, and a fresh set of players writes the official history from that summary alone, never seeing the archive. Give it a small stake so they're trying to get it right rather than writing something lovely.

What I want back: kit list, roles, timings to the minute, a debrief that maps what happened onto the distinctions — especially between a source lying and a source never having existed — and gets them to see that bias and thinness need different fixes. Plus what predictably goes wrong the first time you run it on non-academics, and which bit the activity cant reproduce that I'll have to supply myself.
