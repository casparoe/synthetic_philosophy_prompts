I've been drafting a paper for a little applied-epistemology workshop my department is hosting in the spring, and I'm at the stage where I can't tell anymore whether I've actually said something or just arranged familiar anxieties in a confident order. The topic is the erosion of understanding through reliance on AI systems, but I'm desperate to avoid the standard hand-wringing — you know the genre: attention spans, deep reading, students outsourcing their essays, cognitive offloading is just writing all over again and Socrates complained about that too. I want a term of art that does real work. Below is the current draft, about nine hundred words, cut down from something much worse. Three specific asks at the bottom.

---

**Verification debt**

Every engineering org of a certain age has a file that still runs in production. Nobody wrote it, nobody alive can explain it, and the one person who used to understand it retired in 2011. The correct engineering response is not to read it and the correct response is not to rewrite it; it is to build a test harness around it and never touch it. The org has converted a comprehension problem into a checking problem, and that is genuinely the right move — right up until the day the inputs shift in a way nobody anticipated, at which point the harness tells you the output is fine, because the harness was written for the old regime, and there is no one to ask.

I think something structurally identical is happening to knowledge, and I think we currently lack a word for it that isn't either nostalgic or apocalyptic. I'll propose one: **verification debt**. A community has verification debt with respect to some body of results when (i) the community can check those results, (ii) checking is cheaper and more reliable than understanding them, and (iii) the understanding-based alternative has not merely been forgone but is now more expensive and less trustworthy than the check. Clause (iii) is what separates this from plain ignorance and from the old worry about lost crafts. Lost crafts are just absence. Verification debt is a live, functioning arrangement that keeps paying its bills, and that is exactly why nobody services it.

The distinction I most want to hold onto is between two things people routinely slide between. The first is individual deskilling: I can no longer do long division, or read Latin, or integrate by parts without looking it up. This is real but small, and crucially it is recoverable — a department can always retrain one grad student, and it will do so the first time it becomes worthwhile. The second is communal verification debt, where there are still people who understand the material, but the process that produced those people ran on the pre-debt regime. You cannot apprentice into a skill that nobody is being paid to practice at the level required. And here's the asymetry that makes the whole thing worth writing about rather than tutting over: every individual step toward it is locally correct. Checking is faster. Checking is less prone to error. Checking scales. There is no moment at which anyone makes a mistake, and yet the community arrives somewhere it cannot get back from. This is why I don't think the debate should be conducted in the register of virtue, laziness, or intellectual character. Verification debt accumulates as an artifact of pricing, not of failing.

The reason the price signal misleads is that verification and understanding answer different questions, and I want to be blunt about the difference rather than mystical. To understand why something is the case is, on the account I'm assuming, to have some command of the surrounding counterfactuals — to know which changes to the setup would change the result, and roughly how. To verify is to confirm that a conclusion follows, given a frame. Verification is model-relative; understanding is what lets you notice the model has stopped applying. An automated checker asked whether a derivation is valid answers a question internal to the frame the checker was built inside. It does not, and structurally cannot, tell you that the frame quietly stopped being the right one — that the data distribution moved, that an idealization that used to be harmless is now load-bearing, that the theorem's hypothesis is technically satisfied but satisfied in a degenerate way nobody intended. Verification debt is dangerous not because the checks are bad. It's dangerous because good checks are silent about exactly the failure mode that matters, and silence from a reliable instrument is indistinguishable, from inside, from everything being fine.

This generates a self-undermining dynamic I take to be the paper's main claim. The people able to notice frame failure are disproportionately the ones who learned the material the hard way, before the check existed. That's a cohort fact, not a permanent fact. As the cohort exits, the capacity to notice frame failure is drawn down at the same rate as the capacity to reconstruct the results — because they were the same capacity. So verification debt is not a stock you can choose to pay down later. It's a stock whose interest rate rises with time, since the population that could pay it down is shrinking, and the checking infrastructure meanwhile gets better and more trusted, which suppresses precisely the alarm that would justify the expenditure.

I want to close with the condition under which the debt is genuinely unrecoverable, because otherwise the analogy oversells. With legacy code you can usually refactor: rewrite the module, and comprehension comes back at a price. The epistemic analogue of refactorability is whether the result can be restated in terms whose meaning the community still controls. Sometimes it can. A lot of what looks like opaque machinery is just notation nobody has bothered to normalize, and normalizing it is a weekend's work for someone with the right taste. The debt binds only where no such restatement is available — where the check succeeds by exploiting structure that has no human-scale description at all, so that "understand it later" is not deferred but empty. I suspect a good deal of current anxiety is about the refactorable kind, and that this is worth saying out loud even though it weakens my own case, because the genuinely non-refactorable cases will then stand out when they arrive.

Okay, the three asks:

**One, accuracy and slippage.** Read it like a hostile commentator, not a friendly one. Where does the three-clause definition let me equivocate? I'm specifically worried about clause (iii), and about the places where I glide between "no one in the community can do X" and "it is now expensive for the community to do X" — those are different claims with very different empirical commitments, and I suspect I've leaned on the second while rhetorically cashing out the first. If that slippage is load-bearing for the argument, tell me, because then the paper is in trouble and I'd rather know in February than at the workshop.

**Two, rank the objections by how much they actually hurt.** I know some of the obvious ones already: that writing, printing, calculators, and refereeing were all accused of the same thing and understanding survived; that the whole thing is unfalsifiable because any successful check gets described as fragile-in-waiting; that I've smuggled in a claim about what understanding *is* (the counterfactuals gloss) and the paper collapses if that gloss is wrong; that verification debt might be perfectly benign if everyone stays productive and happy; and that the cohort argument proves too much, since it would equally condemn any field where expertise concentrates in an aging generation. But the one I can't answer, and which I think might be fatal, is this: if the systems doing the checking are themselves capable of frame-awareness — if they can notice that an idealization has become load-bearing — then the model-relative/model-external distinction I'm trading on just dissolves, and my "main claim" reduces to a contingent bet about capability timelines dressed up as a structural point. Tell me whether that objection is fatal, merely very damaging, or whether there's a version of the paper that survives it. If a version survives, sketch what the surviving thesis would have to say, because I'd rather rewrite around it than defend something I don't believe.

**Three, is any of this interesting?** Blunt answer preferred. I have a nagging sense that "verification debt" is just a new label for a worry that's been stated better elsewhere, and that the workshop audience will have heard the shape of it within ninety seconds. If that's right, tell me which single move is doing the only original work here — I'd guess it's either the pricing-not-virtue framing or the claim that the notice-capacity and the reconstruct-capacity are literally the same capacity being drawn down — and tell me whether the paper should be rebuilt around that move with everything else cut. Related: I haven't done a lit review and I'm deliberately not asking you for one, so please don't hand me a reading list. But if my coinage collides with an existing term that means something different, flag the collision so I can rename it.
