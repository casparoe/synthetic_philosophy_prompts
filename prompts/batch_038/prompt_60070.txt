ok so i run a philosophy-of-mind seminar and the last session is the extended mind thesis (Clark and Chalmers plus the usual pushback), and instead of a wrap-up summary I want to close the session by reading a poem aloud. You're the right person for this: a working poet who has also sat through far too many of these seminars, so you know the debate cold but you also know that a poem about a debate is almost always terrible. Think carefully about the machinery before you write anything.

The case is mine, I made it up for the session, it isn't from any paper. An old couple, married fifty-some years, who had quietly split the storage between them: he held the routes, the faces, how to fix the boiler; she held every date, every name, the whole account of how they met, which cousin was which, what year the kitchen got done. She dies. About a year later he's at a dinner party and somebody asks how the two of you met, and he starts telling it and reaches for the second half of the sentence — the half that was never his to hold — and it isn't there.

What I want: a dramatic monologue in his voice, 28 to 34 lines, loose accentual lines of fairly even length, no rhyme scheme required. The formal trick is that the poem runs on two voices. In the remembered first half, whenever he states something, the next line is hers — set off by an indent, recognizably not his register (brisker, more concrete, she is the one who produces actual numbers and actual names). No dialogue tags, no quotation marks, the reader should just feel the second voice arriving. Plant at least three hard concrete facts in thier indented lines — a year, a place, a person's name. Then around line 18 or 20, the indented lines stop coming. He keeps going, and he fills the gaps himself in his own unindented line, and at least twice what he says flatly contradicts one of the facts she planted earlier. Crucially: the poem must never flag the error. No "or was it," no self-correction, no hesitation, no italics. He is confident. That's what makes the thing work.

Also, if you can land it, end on the first indented line in the poem that is actually his voice — him doing her job, badly.

Her indented lines should quietly carry the conditions the thesis needs (with her constantly, easy to reach, never second-guessed) as behavior, not as a list. And no jargon anywhere in the verse: don't use "extended mind," "parity principle," "cognition," "externalism," don't name any philosopher or any -ism in the poem itself. The idea has to be in the form.

The philosophical job the form has to do, so you know what you're aiming at: someone who knows the debate should be able to put a finger on particular lines and say *that's* the parity intuition — if those dates had been in his head we would call this memory loss, we would call it a cognitive deficit and not merely an inconvenience — and *that's* the coupling-isn't-constitution reply — he never had those beliefs, he only ever had a reliable habit of asking, and a habit of asking can end without any mind getting smaller. The wrong guesses are the hinge between the two: they are the best available evidence that the content really was out there, and they are also exactly what the skeptic will point at. Leave that tension open, don't resolve it and don't editorialize; the dinner-party question can stay wrongly answered.

Before the poem, do a short brainstorm out loud: name the three facts she holds, say which two he gets wrong and how, and give the line number of the turn with one sentence on why you put it there. Then the poem. Then, after the poem, give me a structured summary as a single JSON object with exactly these fields:

- `her_facts`: array of strings, the concrete facts planted in her indented lines, each with the line number it appears on.
- `his_errors`: array of objects with `he_says` (his line), `she_had_said` (the earlier line it contradicts), and `line_number` for his version.
- `turn_line`: integer, the line where the indented voice stops arriving.
- `key_lines`: array of objects with `line_number`, `voice` (one of `his`, `hers`, `his_in_her_slot`), `move` (one of `parity_intuition`, `coupling_not_constitution`, `constant_and_unchecked_access`, `two_way_traffic`, `the_error`), and `note` — one sentence on what the line is doing, aimed at a reader who has done the reading.
- `verdict`: object with `does_the_poem_take_a_side` (one sentence), `what_it_concedes_to_the_skeptic` (one sentence), and `what_a_specialist_would_say_is_still_missing` (one sentence).
