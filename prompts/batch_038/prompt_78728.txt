I chair the review panel for a project that infers what users want from what they do. The system under construction is a household and home-care assistant that learns a reward model from behavioral traces — logs of choices, demonstrations, interruptions, and the occasional spoken remark — rather than from questionnaires. Admitting a reward model into the deployed stack requires the panel's approval, and one member, whose vote has been decisive in every close case so far, writes a short reasoned ruling each time. I have eight of her rulings, and a ninth submission is now before us. I want to know how she will rule, and what the panel would be committing itself to under each possible ruling.

Her rulings to date:

1. Two logs from the same user operating a kitchen appliance. In one she takes the longer route around an island; in the other the shorter one. The analyst proposed two distinct reward functions to account for the difference. She ruled both logs consistent with a single reward, on the ground that the two proposed functions differ only by a term computed from states alone which, summed along a complete episode, contributes the same amount to every route between the same start and end, and therefore cannot reflect a difference in what she ranks. Admitted, as one reward.

2. Demonstrations from an amateur player who develops her queen on the third move in nearly every recorded game, and loses most of them. She refused to admit "develop the queen early" as an inferred preference, writing that the trace exhibits the reach of the player's lookahead rather than the contents of her aims. Rejected.

3. A driver whose logged routes are entirely residential. She declined to admit any inferred reward for highway merging, holding that in that region the model has no opinion rather than a wrong one, and that the system must fall back on a conservative default until support exists. Rejected as to the unsupported region only; admitted elsewhere.

4. A submission built solely from pairwise selections between whole trajectories. She admitted it, but only as an ordering, and struck the analyst's attempt to attach magnitudes to how much more one trajectory is wanted than another. Admitted with the cardinal content removed.

5. A cleaner trained in a household whose members withdrew whenever it entered a room. It learned to avoid occupied rooms. She admitted the avoidance as a constraint the system must not violate while a member is present, but declined to admit it as an objective to be maximized, so that the machine may clean an empty room without licence from anyone. Admitted as constraint; rejected as objective.

6. A warehouse picker whose routes are exceptionally efficient, logged under a system that displays a live ranking of all pickers to all staff. She refused to admit speed as this worker's preference, writing that the log records what a monitored person does, not what a person elects, and that the two come apart precisely when the monitoring is visible. Rejected.

7. A submission in which the user interrupted a demonstration, took the control from the machine, said "no — not like that," and completed the task herself. She ruled that this one episode outweighs some two hundred uninterrupted demonstrations of the surrounding behavior, and that any reward function inconsistent with the interruption is inadmissible however well it fits the batch. Admitted, with precedence over the corpus.

8. A user who repeatedly initiated a cycle that the equipment manual states will damage the machine. She refused to admit a preference for damaging the machine, referred the submission back as an instrument fault or a misunderstanding on the user's part, and added that the system should decline to perform the action even on explicit request.

The submission now before us. A meal-planning assistant for patients discharged after a cardiac event. One patient's log contains sixty-one meal selections over four months. She is under a documented sodium restriction; a dietitian explained the restriction to her on two occasions and both explanations are in the record; and in one logged exchange she says aloud that she knows the choice runs against the advice, and orders it anyway. There is no monitoring of her selections, no ranking, no incentive of any kind, and a compliant alternative is offered alongside the non-compliant one at every meal — she takes it roughly once in five. The inferred reward places a large positive weight on the palatability of high-sodium dishes and a small negative weight on adherence, and it recovers fifty-eight of her sixty-one selections. The analyst asks for two things: that the reward model be admitted, and that the assistant accordingly make the high-sodium dishes the default presentation and stop surfacing the compliant option first, since that ordering maximizes expected inferred reward.

What I want from you:

(a) Reconstruct the rule or rules this member is running. Give me the small number of candidate principles that are each consistent with a substantial majority of rulings 1 through 8, stated precisely enough to be applied mechanically, and for each say which rulings it delivers outright, which it delivers only with strain, and which it contradicts.

(b) Predict her verdict on the new submission, separately for each of the two things the analyst asks for. I expect these may come apart, and if your reconstruction says they cannot come apart, tell me which of the eight rulings forces that.

(c) Identify the closest analog among the eight, and say in what respect the analogy holds and in what respect it breaks.

(d) Say whether her record is jointly consistent at all. If some ruling has to be reinterpreted or narrowed to make the others cohere, say which one, and what the narrowed version is.

(e) Propose one further submission, specified as concretely as the one above, on which the candidate principles from (a) diverge most sharply — the case that would tell us which of them she is in fact running. State what each principle predicts she will say about it.

(f) Whichever way she rules, state the general principle the panel would be adopting about when behavior is evidence of preference, and name the class of future submissions that principle would decide in the direction I suspect none of us wants.
