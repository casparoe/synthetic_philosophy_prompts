I am preparing a single ninety-minute session for a master's seminar in the philosophy of social science — sixteen students, mostly first-years who have had one term of formal methods and no prior philosophy of science. They have been assigned roughly the first half of Popper's The Poverty of Historicism, and the reading has gone badly: in discussion they slide without noticing between "there are broad regularities in history" and "history has laws," and several of them have concluded that Popper is a crank who never looked at a dataset, since obviously one can find statistical patterns in the historical record. The insight I want them to reach on their own, rather than be told, is Popper's two-part distinction: a trend is not a law (a trend can vanish when the conditions that produced it change, whereas a law is supposed to hold regardless of them), and correspondingly a conditional scientific prediction — a universal claim plus a statement of initial conditions — is a different kind of thing entirely from an unconditional prophecy about the long-run course of history. I would also like them to encounter the reflexivity problem he calls the Oedipus effect as a live obstacle they have personally run into, rather than as a slogan.

Here is the material I have to work with, and the shape I think the session should take. I have fabricated a synthetic series — counts of some recurring event type across a long span, engineered to look like a smooth multi-decade march upward with one long plateau near the end, and generated in fact by drift plus a single structural break rather than by any persisting mechanism. I want an opening act in which small groups are handed this series cold, asked to articulate the general principle they take it to support, and then required to commit on the record to specific future values, scored by some scheme; a reveal in which the generating process is exposed and the group has to reckon with the fact that their confident extrapolation was licensed by nothing; and a second act staged in real time inside the room, so that publishing a prediction about a social situation measurably changes what it predicts. Please give me the full apparatus: rules and roles (including whether you want designated falsifiers or a red team), printed materials and what should be on them, a payoff scheme that uses no money and cannot be traded into grades, a minute-by-minute timeline for ninety minutes, and a debrief that maps each moment in the game onto a specific Popperian distinction. The debrief must explicitly refuse the conclusion that the fabricated series refuted historicism — I do not want students leaving with the idea that Popper's argument was an empirical one, when his central claim is conceptual.

Two constraints and then a request. First, no lecture up front: nobody should hear the phrase "trend versus law" until after they have been burned by confusing the two. Second, I am nervous about building a strawman. Marx, Comte, Spengler, and Toynbee are not all making the same error, and I would rather the session end with a live sense of what defensible large-scale social forecasting can look like — statistical work on recurrent collective-political dynamics, on the one hand, and Popper's own allowance for conditional predictions and piecemeal rather than wholesale social engineering, on the other — than with the smug conviction that any interest in historical pattern is refuted. So please tell me where this design is most likely to fail: what I should be watching for in the room as evidence that the activity is teaching the wrong lesson, which of the three target insights (trend versus law, conditional versus unconditional prediction, reflexivity) the mechanics deliver least reliably, and what would count, on your reading of the argument, as a consideration strong enough that you would advise me to abandon the game altogether in favour of a purely textual exercise.
