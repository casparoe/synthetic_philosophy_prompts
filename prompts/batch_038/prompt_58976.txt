Our working group is drafting the methods chapter for a distributionally weighted cost–benefit framework, and for four months two of my co-authors have been locked in an argument about Harsanyi's aggregation theorem that has produced nothing but longer footnotes. The stalemate reproduces one in the literature that has run for decades, and I want a diagnosis of what kind of disagreement it is, not a summary of the theorem.

Both sides accept the mathematics without complaint: given that individual preferences over prospects satisfy the von Neumann–Morgenstern axioms, given that the social ranking over the same prospects satisfies them too, and given a Pareto condition, the social utility function must be a positive affine combination of the individual ones. Where they diverge is what has been shown.

One co-author holds that this is one of the rare cases in which a substantive normative conclusion follows from individually innocuous premises — formal rationality plus Pareto — and therefore that additive aggregation is not a convention we adopt but a result we are entitled to. The other holds that the theorem is close to arithmetic dressed as ethics: linearity of expectation does the work, the object being aggregated is an ex ante expected utility over prospects rather than a ranking of realized consequences, so the classical utilitarian claim about summing well-being never appears; and the premise that the social evaluator is itself an expected-utility maximizer over the same lotteries already presupposes the consequentialist, additive-in-expectation structure the theorem is supposed to deliver. She adds two further charges. The impartial-observer device that fixes each person's affine scale — an evaluator who has an equal chance of being anyone — seems to install equal concern and interpersonal comparability by construction rather than deriving them. And when people disagree about probabilities, unanimity over prospects can be produced entirely by differences in belief, so a Pareto premise applied to common beliefs may license a social ranking that says nothing about outcomes.

My own hypothesis, offered as a hypothesis: the two are answering different questions under one heading — one asking how to rank prospects under uncertainty, the other asking what social value consists in — with the word 'utilitarianism' doing double duty as the name of both, plus a background disagreement about whether a formal assumption counts as substantive.

Is that right, or is there a hidden formal question carrying the dispute — for instance, whether one can pass from ex ante to ex post aggregation without giving up something each side wants — in which case the impasse would be a sign of an unresolved technical problem rather than a verbal one? Diagnose it from what each side actually claims, distinguishing wherever possible a dispute over the object of the theory, over the status of a premise, over the meaning of a label, and over unargued starting intuitions. Then tell me what would count as progress: a distinction, a new impossibility result, a reformulation of the question, or a demonstration that no progress should be expected and why. I also need to know what this implies for my chapter — whether the theorem can be cited as a justification for additive aggregation or only as an explication of what we have decided to mean by it.

Please say how confident you are in the diagnosis overall, and single out the one claim in it you are least sure of. If you suspect you have mis-stated either camp's position, flag that explicitly rather than smoothing it over.
