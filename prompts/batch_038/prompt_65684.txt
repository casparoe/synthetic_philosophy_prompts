I sit on the seven-member charter review commission of Aldermere, a city of roughly 180,000, and this spring we must certify the electorate for a consolidation referendum that will decide whether Aldermere merges with the adjacent unincorporated township of Northgate into a single metropolitan government. The state statute is unhelpful in exactly the way one would predict: it requires approval by a majority of the qualified voters of each affected territory, voting separately, and then says nothing at all about who the qualified voters of a territory that has no municipal franchise of its own are supposed to be. Northgate has about 19,000 residents, of whom some 11,000 are registered county voters. It also contains a state college whose 2,600 undergraduates are overwhelmingly registered at their parents' addresses in other states, and an industrial park employing 4,300 people who commute in from three surrounding counties, pay a township payroll tax, and receive nothing beyond roads and fire coverage. Aldermere has for eleven years permitted non-citizen residents to vote in its school-board elections, and there is a live proposal to extend that here; Northgate has about 1,400 adult non-citizen residents, most of them there for more than a decade. Two of my colleagues are retired engineers who want a rule they can administer by October. One is a former county attorney who insists the only defensible course is the narrowest franchise — the 11,000 registered voters — because anything broader will be litigated and could void the result after the fact.

What I cannot get the commission to see clearly is that our problem is not primarily a legal one, and I want a document that says so. Every electorate we might certify produces a different answer on consolidation, and the government that consolidation would create is precisely the body that would thereafter hold plenary authority over its own franchise questions — so in fixing today's voter list we are, in effect, choosing the demos that gets to choose the demos, and we have no authority to do that which was not itself handed to us by a body whose membership was settled without asking anyone. The people we exclude are not bystanders: if consolidation passes, Northgate ceases to exist as a jurisdiction, the commuters will have helped dissolve a government that taxed them, and the students will acquire a city they leave in three years; if it fails, all of them remain subject to a county government none of them had any hand in constituting. Two procedural escape hatches have been floated at our last two meetings and I want them assessed rather than waved at — an advisory ballot open to everyone who lives or works in the territory, and a weighted scheme under which each territory's total is normalized so that population differences cancel. I came to municipal administration after a doctorate in political theory, and I have found that the vocabulary I was trained in is unusable in a room where people want to know what form to print.

Please draft the written opinion I will circulate to the commission ahead of our April 9 meeting. It should recommend one certification rule, or a primary rule with a clearly subordinate fallback; specify the certification deadline and the concrete administrative steps between now and the November ballot; and set out the reasoning in language a reporter from the local paper could quote without distorting it. Most importantly, it should confront the circularity head-on instead of retreating behind our county attorney's procedural minimalism: explain whether legal safety is genuinely the right criterion here or a way of laundering a substantive choice about who counts, and say what follows for the commission's legitimacy either way. State whether the two escape hatches above are coherent devices or theater, and why. Include the strongest case against your own recommendation — I expect it to be either that admitting the commuters lets people vote on a government they will never be subject to, or that whatever we allow here will be cited as precedent in the next boundary fight in the county — and answer it rather than conceding it. Close with the exact text of the motion I should ask the commission to adopt. Keep it between 1,100 and 1,400 words, organized under headings, with no footnotes, no citations, no suggested reading, and no named philosophers: the memo has to stand entirely on argument about this case.
