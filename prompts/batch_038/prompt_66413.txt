I cover science and ideas for a monthly magazine, and I am midway through a feature on first-mover advantage: why moving first is sometimes decisive, sometimes ruinous, and how anyone is meant to tell which is which. One strand of the piece concerns a proof template from combinatorial game theory that gets invoked well outside mathematics — in arguments about preemption in technology races, about whether defensive positions are inherently favored, and about whether an actor with more resources and more options can always adopt whatever plan a rival could have adopted. To get that strand right I went to a Saturday lecture series last month at which a combinatorialist and an industrial-organization economist shared a stage, and I stayed for the argument they resumed afterwards beside the coffee urn, which I have transcribed from my notes. Neither is a colleague, neither knows I am shopping for a referee, and I have no stake in the outcome; I want an adjudication, not an ally.

Here is the exchange, in order, as fairly as I can render it. I will call the combinatorialist Vera and the economist Owen.

1. Vera laid out the template on Hex, the connection game in which players alternately place stones of their own colour and win by linking their two assigned sides, and in which a draw is topologically impossible. Suppose the second player had a guaranteed win. The first player places a stone anywhere at all, and thereafter plays by the second player's supposed winning plan, answering each of the opponent's moves as the plan prescribes; if the plan ever calls for a cell her opening stone already occupies, she plays somewhere else instead and now simply has a second free stone. Three premises carry the argument: the two players always have the same menu of moves available, an extra stone of your own colour can never be a liability, and the game cannot end level. Since the plan guarantees a win for whoever follows it, both players are now guaranteed to win, which is absurd; so the second player has no forced win, and because the game is finite and cannot be drawn, the first player must have one — on any board size, without anyone's knowing how. She added that the argument goes back to Nash, who never published it.

2. Owen said that this is exactly the sum of its content: the second player cannot force a win, purchased by assuming away everything that makes a competition interesting. The premises are precisely what is contested in any real race. In the preemption models he works on, moving first commits capital, discloses your approach to a rival who can then route around it, and can leave you stranded when the underlying technology shifts; there are equilibria in which the leader would gladly pay to be the follower. So when people outside mathematics appeal to strategy stealing to argue that the better-resourced side can always copy any plan the other side could have run, they are borrowing the prestige of a proof for a claim it never made.

3. Vera replied twice over. First, "an extra move can hurt" is not a counterexample to the theorem; it is the observation that the game under discussion is not in the theorem's class. Chess is like that — in zugzwang positions a player would pay to pass — which is why nobody runs this argument on chess. Go is like it once the second player receives compensation points, because the opening position is then no longer symmetric. Neither is a refutation; both are a failure to satisfy hypotheses, which is what hypotheses are for. Second, non-constructive is not the same as inert. Knowing that the first player wins Hex disposes of a family of claims before anyone checks them: no drawing resource exists for either side, and a searcher who believes she has found one has already made a mistake. The proof tells you which chair to sit in and what to hunt for, even when it cannot tell you the moves.

4. Owen said that this is where it fails at the table. Hex players neutralize the theorem with the swap rule: after the first move the second player may simply take over that position, colours and all. The convention exists because the existence proof is unplayable; a theorem whose only practical output is a rule change designed to conceal our ignorance is trivia with good manners. And its reach is smaller than it appears: in every case where the conclusion is strong enough to matter, the premises are strong enough to make the conclusion unsurprising.

5. Vera said the reach is not small, and offered Sylver coinage — Conway's misère game in which players alternately name positive integers that are not sums of non-negative multiples of numbers already named, and the player forced to name 1 loses. The game is unsolved in general, largely because positions can admit infinitely many legal moves; yet a theorem of Hutchings establishes that opening with any prime of five or more wins, without exhibiting the continuation. And in the finite positions containing what is called an ender — a number so large that playing anything else would rule it out at once — the player to move wins by a strategy-stealing split: if some other available move wins, make it; if none does, play the ender, a move the opponent could have made unavailable at any moment anyway, and force them to make one of the moves you have just established loses for whoever makes it. That is non-obvious content, produced by the same trick, in a game where the extra-move premise looks false on its face.

6. Owen said Sylver coinage is his exhibit rather than hers. It is misère: taking the last available move loses, so an extra move certainly can hurt — which is why the argument has to be corralled into a special family of positions and why it still leaves everyone ignorant of move two. And the ender argument is not theft at all; it is an ordinary two-case disjunction. If the template must be renamed every time its premise fails, it is not a theorem with a domain but a temperament.

What I need is a verdict rather than a summary. Take the six turns in order and say of each whether it advances the argument, restates a premise in new clothes, or overreaches, with a sentence or two of reasoning; then tell me which single turn does the most work and whether it succeeds. Say whether there is a genuine disagreement here or two people using strategy stealing to mean different things — a theorem about a class of games on one hand, a transferable intuition about competitive structure on the other — and, if the latter, which of the two readings each of them keeps sliding into. Name the strongest point each makes that the other never actually answers, and tell me what both of them leave out: something about what the template does and does not establish, or about how a proof that yields no procedure can nonetheless change what a rational player ought to do, that neither raised. If my notes garbled a step — a premise I dropped, a conclusion I inflated — say where and give me the corrected version; I would rather hear it from you than from a reader. Please do not send me to the literature: everything should be decidable from the argument as I have reported it, and if a turn turns out to depend on a premise I never stated, tell me what is missing instead of guessing at sources.

One last item, and it is the one my editor will actually read. My draft currently carries this sentence: the same trick proves that the first player can force a win at Hex, at Chomp, and at tic-tac-toe, though in none of the three cases does it say what the winning moves are. Is that defensible as it stands? If it is not, repair it in prose a general reader can follow and a combinatorialist would not wince at.
