Question (400 words max): Jade turned out to be not one mineral but two -- jadeite and nephrite. Does that discovery show that identifying natural kinds is not a matter of carving nature at its joints? Discuss, drawing on at least one essentialist/semantic account of kind terms and at least one alternative.

Student answer:

Kripke and Putnam hold that natural kind terms are rigid designators whose reference is fixed by a microstructural essence, so that water is necessarily H2O. Putnam's Twin Earth brings this out: Oscar and Twin Oscar are internally identical, but because the stuff in their lakes is XYZ rather than H2O their words pick out different substances, which shows that meanings ain't in the head. On this picture the joints are set by chemistry, and any sample sharing the essence belongs to the kind regardless of how it looks to us or what we call it.

Jade looks like a counterexample. Jadeite and nephrite have different crystal structures and different compositions, so there is no single essence for the term to latch onto; jade is really a disjunction of two kinds that we lump together because of shared appearance and craft use. So the essentialist is wrong that ordinary substance terms generally correspond to joints.

The joints story can be patched up, though. Lewis says some properties are more natural than others, and the perfectly natural ones are the ones science converges on, so a kind is natural to the degree that science ends up employing it -- jade just isn't high enough on that scale. Quine made a similar point when he treated naturalness as similarity within our innate quality space, and Lewis formalised that insight. Boyd's homeostatic property cluster account, which he developed for artifact kinds like jade, says a kind is real when a cluster of properties is held together by underlying causal mechanisms; jade fails because no mechanism keeps jadeite and nephrite together. Dupré's promiscuous realism goes further and denies that there are joints at all, so on his view the jade case is exactly what we should expect.

So the discovery doesn't refute essentialism so much as show that jade was never a kind term to begin with, which is what the essentialist should have said all along. But this is compatible with the pluralist point that which groupings matter depends on our interests.
