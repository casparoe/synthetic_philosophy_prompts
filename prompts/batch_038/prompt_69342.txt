I'm in a reading group this semester on weakness of will and honestly one picture is doing nearly all the work in our discussions, way more than any argument does: Odysseus and the mast. Everyone reaches for it. My roommate, who is the kind of person with four different commitment apps installed, deployed it on me last night to argue that binding yourself hard is just obviously rational and carries no autonomy worries whatsoever — set up the auto-transfer, hand a friend your passwords, let the app delete the distracting stuff at 9pm — because, his words, the version of you begging to be untied isnt really you, it's just the Sirens doing the talking. He mentioned his other seminar is reading Elster's Ulysses book and seems to think that basically settles the matter. What bugs me is that we've also spent three weeks on hyperbolic discounting and nobody in the group has said a single word about how that and the mast story are supposed to fit together, which feels like the whole question.

So can you do a proper audit of the analogy for me? I want the correspondence table first — mast, ropes, the wax, the crew, Circe's warning, the fact that the episode has a known endpoint maybe an hour away — mapped onto the self-control case, with what each element is actually being asked to do. Then the disanalogies, and I care much more about the ones that threaten my roommate's conclusion than the trivia. A few I've thought of that I'd like you to check, sharpen, or throw out: in the story the impairment is local and total, the man lashed up there is temporarily out of his head and everybody agrees he'll be fine tomorrow, whereas hyperbolic discounting says the reversal happens at every scale, this evening versus tonight, this month versus next, so there may be no unbound self standing outside the problem to sign the contract in the first place — every self wants to tie up the next one. Also the crew are a third party with their own interests and their own immunity, and in real life the enforcer is an app company or an employer or a state, none of whom have wax in their ears. Also the ropes cost something and the analogy treats that cost as a rounding error. And there's a wrinkle I genuinely can't work out: Odysseus didn't want to miss the song, he wanted to hear it and survive hearing it, so the mast there is preserving the value of an experience rather than blocking a temptation, which seems like a very different kind of commitment from the ones my roommate is describing, but I dont know how to cash the difference out.

Then give me a verdict: does "bind yourself hard, no autonomy problem, the later you has no standing to object" survive once the disanalogies are on the table, or was the analogy carrying the conclusion the entire time? And if you think ropes are just the wrong source, propose and develop a better one — someone in the group half-suggested that willpower is less like being tied down and more like a reputation you're building with yourself over a lot of small repeated rounds, where each broken personal rule makes all the others cheaper to break, and I'd like to know whether that actually does better explanatory work or just sounds nicer. One last thing and please take it seriously: keep seperate what's genuinely settled among people who work on this from what's contested, and don't hand me a contested picture as though it were the consensus. Some of the stuff that gets waved around in these conversations — like the idea that self-control is a limited resource that runs down as you spend it — I've read doesn't replicate very well, and I'd much rather you flag that than quietly build on it.
