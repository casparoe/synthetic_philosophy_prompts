I chaired a panel last month where two of the speakers got into a standoff I couldnt referee, and I'd like a second opinion on it. The topic was whether habitat destruction is bad, all things considered, for wild animals.

Owen opened like this: most wild animals are tiny short-lived things — larvae, hatchlings, insect nymphs — and the overwhelming majority die within days of existing, of starvation, predation, or parasites. So an intact habitat is a machine for churning out vast numbers of brief bad lives, and draining the marsh mostly switches the machine off. His conclusion was that people who claim to care about wild animal suffering should be at least ambivalent about habitat loss instead of treating it as an obvious harm.

Priya said the argument rides on a premise Owen never argued for — that those lives are net negative. If the typical short life is briefly worth living, then habitat loss destroys an enormous amount of positive value instead and the sign flips. Her charge was that he's smuggling a welfare claim in under cover of a mortality claim.

Owen came back that her side is unfalsifiable comfort. Nobody can measure the balance of pain and pleasure in a square meter of leaf litter, so in the absence of evidence we should go with what we do know, which is that these animals get produced in such quantities precisely because nearly all of them die fast and badly.

Priya said mortality rates are simply silent on net welfare — a life can end badly after a week and still have been worth starting — so Owen has swapped an empirical question for a prior wearing an empirical costume.

Then a grad student in the audience threw in something neither of them could absorb: when habitat is degraded rather than removed, the animals don't disappear, they pile into what's left, so the suffering gets exported and concentrated rather than eliminated. Owen said that obviously helped him, since crowding makes the survivors worse off. Priya said it obviously helped her, since it means habitat loss destroys value instead of preventing it. Same fact, both claimed it, and I didnt know what to say. We ran out of time.

Can you adjudicate this move by move? Rate each of the five from clearly wrong to clearly right with a line of justification each, tell me where the smuggling charge actually bites and where it doesn't, whether Owen's base-rate appeal is an empirical claim or a dressed-up prior, how one fact about crowding could seem to favor both sides and what that reveals about the argument, and then name what the two of them are jointly leaving out that would change the shape of the dispute.
