For an independent study on epistemology outside the Western tradition, I have been reading about the Nyāya school, and before I submit my paper I would like to check my understanding against a summary written by someone more expert than I am. In the first lecture of a public course I watched online, the lecturer introduced Nyāya as follows:

"Nyāya was the school of logic among India's classical darśanas, and it built its whole epistemology around four pramāṇas, that is, four proofs: perception, inference, comparison, and testimony. On this view, a genuine claim to know something had to be proved by one of the four, and that list became the standard framework for Indian philosophy as a whole. Nyāya's theory of inference runs to five steps rather than the three Aristotle needed, chiefly because the conclusion is asserted at the beginning and then restated at the end — a redundancy that shows the tradition never acquired a sense of logical economy. Comparison is simply argument by analogy of the ordinary kind. Testimony meant above all the authority of the Veda, which is why Nyāya ranked it last: what scripture says has to be checked against perception and inference before it can count as knowledge."

Much of this sounded convincing, and I have already borrowed two of its ideas for my draft — in particular that pramāṇa means something like proof, and that Nyāya's list of four was shared by the other schools. Two things still make me uneasy, though. I have a vague recollection of reading that the Buddhists recognised fewer than four pramāṇas, and the five-step inference strikes me as though it may be doing something Aristotle's three-step version does not, rather than merely repeating itself.

Could you work through the summary sentence by sentence and tell me which claims a specialist in Nyāya would endorse, which are defensible simplifications for a general audience, and which are simply incorrect? And where the lecturer moves from a claim to a verdict — from the five steps to a judgement of inferiority, or from the character of testimony to its ranking — I would like to know whether the verdict survives once the claim underneath it is corrected.
