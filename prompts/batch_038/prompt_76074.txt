Last spring I toured Halden, Norway's maximum-security prison, as part of a comparative criminal-justice project, and I have not been able to reconstruct the theory of punishment the place actually runs on. Let me describe the practice as concretely as I can, and then ask you to do that reconstruction.

Halden has no watchtowers, no barbed wire, and no bars on the windows; the perimeter wall is the only conspicuous security feature. Officers are unarmed and carry keys, and much of their shift is spent cooking, playing sports, or making music with inmates — interaction is treated as official duty and defended as security through contact. Surveillance cameras cover the grounds but not the cells, the cell corridors, or the common rooms. Cells are single rooms with a refrigerator, television, desk, and private shower; ten or so share a kitchen and living room with real cutlery and a games console, and inmates buy groceries and cook for themselves. They are locked in overnight, roughly twelve hours, and are paid a small daily sum as an incentive to leave their cells for work, school, or training. Healthcare, dentistry, schooling, and library services come from the same municipal providers that serve the surrounding town, at the same standard. Inmates vote.

The governing doctrine, as the staff described it, is that the sentence consists solely in the deprivation of liberty: the state may take a person's time and nothing else, so any further hardship is a defect rather than a feature, and life inside should differ as little as possible from life outside. Ordinary sentences are capped at twenty-one years, since there is no life imprisonment — but for the gravest offenders a court may impose preventive detention, extendable indefinitely in multi-year increments for as long as the person is still judged dangerous. Foreign nationals serving sentences that end in deportation are held in a separate institution, on the stated ground that they are not going to be reintegrated into Norwegian society.

Set against this the American practice I know: mandatory minimums and three-strikes statutes, life without parole, consecutive sentences running into centuries, truth-in-sentencing rules, solitary confinement used as routine behaviour management, prison labour at a few cents an hour, felon disenfranchisement, and in some jurisdictions an explicit policy that conditions should be austere because austerity is part of what the sentence conveys.

Please tell me what the Norwegian package presupposes, working from the description rather than from names and labels.

1. What theory of punishment does it require — and is there one, or several bundled together? The official justification for normality is lower recidivism, but the same architecture could be licensed by a rights-based constraint on state power, by an expressive view of what the state owes a person it confines, by a welfare-state continuity thesis, or by managerial self-interest in calm institutions. Separate what the practice strictly presupposes from what merely fits it well, and say what would happen to the practice if a rigorous study showed the recidivism advantage had vanished. Does it survive, and what would that tell us about what was really doing the work?

2. What does the claim that the state may take time and nothing else presuppose about time as the currency of penal harm? Is the assumption that time is the only harm the state may inflict, or that it is the only harm the state can measure and control? Note that the practice appears to treat a year as a fixed quantity of harm regardless of how the hours are filled — which sits oddly with the enormous investment in making them pleasant, and with a twelve-hour nightly lock-up in a comfortable room.

3. What view of character, and of the persistence of a person over time, does the combination presuppose? Rehabilitation requires that the person who leaves be relevantly different from the one who entered; preventive detention requires that present dispositions reliably predict future ones; proportionality requires that the person serving be the same person who offended. Can one account of identity and character serve all three, or is the practice committed to something inconsistent here — in particular, to the thought that if the institution really transforms people, present risk is a poor guide to future risk, and if present risk is a good guide, the transformation has not occurred?

4. Does the practice presuppose that the state may permissibly attempt to remake a citizen's character, and is that compatible with the fact that participation is nominally voluntary while release depends on demonstrated change?

5. What does the separate deportation prison show about the theory? I suspect it reveals that reintegration is understood as an internal transaction between a polity and its members — which would mean the theory is structurally unavailable for non-members, and that systems which disenfranchise and exclude presuppose something different about the offender's standing. Reconstruct that presupposition and say whether it is a commitment or a practical concession.

6. Which rival views of punishment, responsibility, or evidence about persons does this practice make it impossible to hold while working inside it? And where is the practice philosophically underdetermined — where would several incompatible theories equally license everything I described? Tell me what further institutional facts, rather than arguments, would discriminate among them.

7. Finally, run the substitution in both directions. If Norway adopted the American presuppositions, which specific features would have to be dropped or inverted, and in what order? If an American jurisdiction adopted the Norwegian ones, what would have to go beyond amenities — the sentencing law itself? Identify anything in the American system that would survive the substitution intact.

You do not need to cite or recommend sources, and please do not build the answer around a list of named positions. Flag any part of my description that you think is load-bearing in a way I have not noticed.
