Von Neumann's little book *The Computer and the Brain* (the Yale Silliman lectures, published posthumously in 1958) stacks up a bunch of quantitative claims about nervous tissue: neurons fire all-or-none so the system looks digital at the elementary level, but graded potentials, transmitter chemistry and muscle control are continuous, so the whole thing is a *mixed* machine; the individual elements are roughly a thousand times slower than the vacuum tubes he was comparing them against; the precision per element is low, so whatever the system computes gets done with much less arithmetical depth than we'd demand of a digital machine; that shortfall is paid for by enormous parallelism and redundancy; memory isn't organized by addresses the way a computer's memory is but works statistically; and the signaling code of the nervous system probably has a logical structure unlike any human language.

Here's what I want: design a pair of artificial systems that differ in exactly ONE of those parameters and are matched on all the others. Matched means matched — same total element count where that's relevant, same aggregate throughput, same energy budget, same training regime, and the same demonstrated competence on a battery of tasks you name explicitly (pick something motor-perceptual and something that requires holding a lot of stuff in mind, so the battery isn't rigged).

The catch, and it's the whole point: von Neumann's own analysis treats these parameters as linked. Low precision forces wide parallelism, wide parallelism plus slow elements forces shallow serial depth, and a shallow serial architecture pushes you toward statistical rather than addressed memory. So you can't just declare a system that differs on one of them — you have to show me how the others stay put, and engage his linking argument head-on rather than hand-waving it.

Then give me a single discriminating experiment: one task, or one perturbation, that you could actually run on both machines, which comes out differently for the two. The difference can't just be "one is slower" or "one is better overall" — it has to be a difference in *how* they break or in what one of them turns out to be unable to represent at all.

Hard constraints: no consciousness, no qualia, no appeals to what it's like, no Gödel sentences, no intuition pumps about understanding, no magic hardware beyond what you've already described. One paragraph per system, one paragraph for the experiment. If it can't be said in that space it's probably not a real design yet.

After the scenario, do the checks: state which parameter is doing the work and why the experiment is sensitive to it and not to the others; tell me what happens to the experiment if you relax that parameter back toward the shared value — does the case collapse, or does it just weaken; and say how someone who thinks the whole parameter list is only a loose formal analogy, not a set of real joints in nature, would attack what you built. Finish with an honest note on whether you actually held the other paramters fixed or whether you quietly let one slip, because that's the failure mode I care most about.

For orientation, since it fixes what counts as cheating: I want this as a diagnostic — something that would tell me which of von Neumann's tradeoffs is a genuine constraint on any information-processing system and which is just a contingent fact about wet tissue. A pair that stipulates the difference instead of generating it from the architectures doesn't do that job.
