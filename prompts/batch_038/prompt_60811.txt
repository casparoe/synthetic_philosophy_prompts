I am a third-year PhD student working at the intersection of aesthetics and political philosophy, and I have encountered a sentence that functions in my department as a conversation-stopper but that I cannot pin down analytically. It comes from Du Bois's 1926 address to the NAACP's Chicago conference, published as "Criteria of Negro Art," where he insists — paraphrasing rather than quoting, since I want the claim and not the rhetoric — that all art is propaganda and ever propaganda, whatever the purists may complain. The sentence has entirely detached from its occasion and now travels as a slogan.

Last week it was deployed twice in my presence, in opposite directions, and in both cases with the confidence of someone settling a matter rather than opening one. The first was a fellow student in a reading group on the relation between aesthetic and moral value: because all art is propaganda, she argued, there is no such thing as autonomous aesthetic value, and so the question we had been discussing — whether a work can be aesthetically excellent while morally repugnant — is malformed, since the aesthetic and the political cannot be prised apart in the first place. The second was a curator on a panel about our university gallery's new funding rule, which requires that supported work address a stated social issue: Du Bois's sentence, she said, shows the rule to be harmless rather than restrictive, since the work will carry a message whether or not the funder asks for one, and asking merely renders an unavoidable choice explicit instead of covert.

Before I try to adjudicate between these uses, I need to know what the sentence can actually be made to mean. Please lay out the distinct theses it might express, ranging from the trivially true through the substantive and defensible to the flatly false. Do not confine yourself to assertions about artworks: consider conceptual claims about what art is, descriptive claims about what artworks do to audiences, normative claims about what artists owe their publics, claims about the political neutrality of aesthetic judgment, and the possibility that the sentence is not a claim at all but a rhetorical repositioning of the speaker relative to his critics. For each reading, tell me what it commits one to, what would count against it, and whether it is strong enough to do the work each of my two interlocutors needed — specifically, whether any reading really collapses the autonomy of aesthetic value, and whether any reading really makes a message-requirement on funded art inert rather than limiting. Where the two uses come apart, say so explicitly; I suspect each needs a reading the other cannot share, and that suspicion is what I want tested.

Two further things. First, the same address dwells at length on beauty as a good that the mass of human beings are unjustly choked away from, which sits awkwardly beside the slogan: tell me which readings are compatible with that emphasis and whether the tension forces a weaker reading than either of my interlocutors wants. Second, distinguish the reading that is plausibly Du Bois's own, given the polemical target the sentence names, from the readings later users have extracted from it — I am writing about the argument he was making, not about the slogan he inadvertently left behind. What I want is a numbered menu with verdicts that I can bring to the next meeting; reasoning only, please, with no references or reading list.
