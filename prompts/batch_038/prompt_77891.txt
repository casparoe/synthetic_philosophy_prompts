I cover science and ideas for a British broadsheet, and I sit on the paper's editorial standards panel — a standing committee of five (two senior editors, the readers' editor, our in-house legal adviser, and myself as the elected journalist member) whose decisions on contested usage and evidential standards bind the desks until the panel revisits them. Next month we take up the hardest question in our remit, and the chair has asked me, precisely because I do not come from the foreign desk, to prepare the paper that will be circulated in advance. I would like you to draft that paper for me.

The question is what the newspaper must have in hand before it says, in its own voice, that a campaign amounts to genocide. Our existing house rule, adopted after a different controversy some years ago and never revisited, is that we may use the word only where a competent international tribunal has so found, or where at least two national governments have made the determination through their own formal processes. I have come to think this rule is incoherent rather than merely conservative, and I want the memo to argue that, not just note it.

The test case before the panel has these features, which I set out because any rule we adopt has to survive contact with something like them — not because I am asking you to adjudicate the facts. A UN-mandated fact-finding mission has published a report finding reasonable grounds to believe that measures intended to prevent births within a population were ordered at a high level, and that children have been transferred out of that population in significant numbers; the report nevertheless declines to use the word genocide, describing the campaign instead in the vocabulary of crimes against humanity. Killing on a mass scale is not established: the death toll is fiercely disputed, and most of the documented physical destruction is of settlements, hospitals and food stores rather than of bodies. The state in question denies that the victims constitute a national, ethnic, racial or religious group at all, maintaining that they are adherents of a political movement, and it points to its own census categories, which fold them into the majority, as evidence. A well-regarded scholar has meanwhile argued in print that the special-intent requirement can be satisfied by inference from the births-prevention and child-transfer measures taken together. Survivor organisations are asking us why we describe their experience in the report's language and not in theirs. The foreign desk has a despatch ready in which the proposed headline carries the word and the body text does not currently support it.

The memo needs to do six things.

First, recommend a decision: adopt, amend or abandon the existing rule, and say plainly what we should do with that despatch.

Second, formulate whatever replaces the rule as an operable test — something a sub-editor working to an eleven o'clock deadline can apply without having read the memo — and state honestly what that test costs us in the cases where it produces silence. A rule that cannot be applied under pressure by someone other than its author is not a rule, and I would rather have a crude one that holds than an elegant one that evaporates.

Third, give the legal adviser's position its strongest form and then answer it. The best version is not that courts are more careful than we are, but that the word smuggles in an assertion about a perpetrator's state of mind which we cannot ourselves verify, and that the paper's credibility in the rare cases where it does use the term is a common resource that one premature use depletes for everyone. If you reject that argument, say what specifically it gets wrong; if you think it partly right, say which part of the rule it should shape and which it should not.

Fourth, address the denial of group status head-on. Distinguish the question of whether the treaty's list of protected groups should bind us as a moral boundary from the question of whether it binds us only as a jurisdictional one, and tell the panel what to do when a state contests that the victims form a group at all. Related to this: Lemkin's own conception of the crime was considerably wider than the treaty that emerged from the drafting, and the destruction of a group's culture and institutions was among the things the drafters declined to carry across. Should a formulation of that wider kind be available to us, and if so under what name? A neologism invites the charge of evasion; the received word invites the charge of overstatement.

Fifth, deal with the asymmetry in the evidence — the killing unestablished, the births-prevention and child-transfer measures comparatively well documented. The treaty treats those acts as sufficient in themselves; I am not convinced our readers' moral intuitions do. Say whether we should write to the law or to the intuition, and how to handle the gap between them in copy that has to be understood on first reading.

Sixth, recommend actual language: a headline and an opening paragraph for the despatch in question, and, if you think a two-tier usage is workable, a standing formula for cases where we judge the moral concept satisfied but the legal one unestablished. If you think two-tier usage is a mistake, explain why in terms a desk editor will accept rather than in terms a philosopher would.

Please write in British English, at something like twelve hundred words, as a document the panel could adopt or amend: recommendation at the top, reasoning beneath it. Argue from the structure of the concept and the architecture of the treaty rather than from authority — no footnotes, no bibliography, no citations of case law, and nothing that turns on the holding of a particular judgment. And do not resolve the memo into a formulation that amounts to both sides having a point: we meet for ninety minutes and need a bottom line.
