I am a second-year graduate student in philosophy, and I have been drafting a section of my qualifying paper on how to think about value in worlds that contain infinitely many good and bad episodes. My advisor is sympathetic but impatient with technical apparatus, and last night I spent close to three hours arguing about the draft with a friend of mine who works in economic theory. She is the sharpest person I know on this material and she is also, I think, being deliberately uncharitable in places, which makes it hard for me to tell which of her objections are lethal and which are merely annoying. Rather than continue the argument over coffee, I would like a cooler assessment of the draft itself. It is rough. I have deliberately stripped out the citations, since this is a working sketch and not a submission, and I would rather not be told that my reading of some particular author is off; what I care about is whether the structure of the argument holds.

The draft runs as follows.

Section 1. The problem. Suppose the world contains infinitely many planets, or infinitely many days, each of which our actions can make better or worse. If value aggregates across locations, then any act with an unbounded footprint has an infinite total value, and comparing two such acts means subtracting one infinity from another. The result is undefined, so it looks as though we can never say that one such act is better than another. Call this the paralysis worry. Any proposal in this area has to answer it without collapsing into something worse.

Section 2. The case that motivates me. A civil servant in an already infinite world can add one more contented person whose life contains a fixed positive amount of good. If our axiology says the total is unchanged and hence the act is neutral, it has badly misdescribed the situation. If it says the act makes things better, it has given up on aggregation, since the sum before and after is the same infinite quantity. I treat this as the touchstone: a proposal that cannot distinguish adding one contented person from doing nothing has failed, whatever else it gets right.

Section 3. Three repairs I reject. First, discounting: weight the value at distance n by a factor shrinking in n so that the sums converge. But the discount rate is arbitrary, any positive rate makes the remote future practically irrelevant, which is precisely the attitude we were trying to avoid, and discounting by temporal distance does not even address a spatially infinite world unless one picks a privileged center. Second, averaging: compare acts by mean value per location. This does cure the paralysis worry, but it cures it by amputation, since any act affecting only finitely many locations now has zero mean effect, and the census case returns in a nastier form: the addition of a contented person and the addition of a wretched one are both invisible. Third, evaluation inside a finite window: rank acts by what they do inside some bounded region. But the ranking then depends on the window, and enlarging the window can reverse it.

Section 4. My proposal, which I call the Frontier Rule. For an act A and a finite region R, let V(A, R) be the value A produces inside R; this is well defined because R is finite. Fix an exhausting sequence of regions R1, R2, and so on. Then A is at least as good as B if the difference V(A, Rn) minus V(B, Rn) is non-negative for all sufficiently large n, and A is better than B if that difference is eventually at least some fixed positive amount. The first clause blocks the reversal problem, since a mere early advantage does not suffice, and the margin requirement in the second clause blocks the case of an advantage that is always positive but shrinks toward zero. The census case is handled: the act that adds a contented person exceeds the null act by a constant positive amount at every stage of the exhaustion.

Section 5. My friend's two objections, and my replies, which I suspect are weak. Objection one, order dependence: the rule needs an exhausting sequence, and in a homogeneous infinite cosmos neither the origin nor the ordering is privileged. Her example is a universe of planets indexed by the integers, with act A improving planets 0, 1, 2, and onward by a fixed amount each, and act B improving planets minus 1, minus 2, minus 3, and onward by the same amount. Under an exhaustion expanding symmetrically in both directions the difference is always zero and the acts tie; under an exhaustion advancing rightward first A wins; under one advancing leftward first B wins. She adds that the verdict is also sensitive to granularity, since regrouping the planets into pairs or triples changes whether a difference oscillates or stabilizes. Her conclusion is that the rule is not a claim about value but about bookkeeping. My reply is that the physically distinguished exhaustions are those tracking the agent's light cone or causal horizon, since only those correspond to regions the agent could affect. She says this smuggles in agent relativity at the level of the axiology rather than the decision rule, and I have not paid that cost honestly. Objection two, that the rule does not escape infinite aggregation but relocates it: when the difference between two acts converges, the rule delivers a verdict, but that verdict is a sum of infinitely many contributions, so an infinity of individually negligible improvements can outrank one enormous finite one; and when the difference diverges, the rule can only say that one act is better, with no way of comparing two acts that each diverge, so the rule goes silent or incomparability-generating exactly in the cases where the paralysis worry originally bit. Her illustration is a pair of acts that agree everywhere except that one adds a contribution falling off like the reciprocal of n squared at planet n and the other adds a contribution falling off like the reciprocal of n: both beat the baseline, but the rule cannot rank them against each other.

Section 6. My concession, which I have not yet worked out. I suggest handling divergent differences by comparing the order of growth of the difference rather than its limit. She says this is infinite cardinal arithmetic with extra steps, and that I have simply moved the problem one level up.

Four requests. First, please tell me where the draft is flatly wrong rather than merely underargued, especially in Section 3, where I worry that my dismissal of averaging is too quick, and in Section 2, where I may be assuming without argument that the census case is a genuine constraint rather than an artifact of a picture of value as a sum. Second, and most importantly, I would like to know whether the Frontier Rule is something I have actually constructed or something I have rediscovered under a new name; if there is a standard criterion in decision theory, growth theory, or the theory of infinite series that does the same job, or a standard impossibility result that shows no criterion can satisfy what I am asking for, I need to know that now rather than at the defense. Third, please assess Section 5 specifically: does the light cone reply work, is the granularity point genuinely damaging or is it a restatement of the ordering point, and is her second objection fatal or does it merely show that my rule is incomplete in a fixable way. Fourth, please tell me whether any of this would interest a reader who does not already care about population ethics, since my private worry is that the whole thing is technically fussy and philosophically inert, and I would rather hear that from you than from my committee.
