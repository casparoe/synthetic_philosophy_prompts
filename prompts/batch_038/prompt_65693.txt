I am a final-year student at a secondary school, and I edit the philosophy section of our quarterly journal. Our readers are sixteen- and seventeen-year-olds who have completed one year of physics and no philosophy at all. For the next issue I would like to publish a diagnostic flowchart on the epistemology of scientific thought experiments, and I am hoping you can write it.

The question the flowchart must answer for a reader is not "what are the famous theories?" but rather: when a physicist or philosopher asks you to imagine something — two stones of different weight tied together and dropped, what you would see if you could keep pace with a beam of light, a tiny gatekeeper sorting fast molecules from slow ones, water climbing the walls of a spinning bucket — what does the imagining actually justify, and where does the justification come from? Our readers can find summaries of the standard debate on their own. What they cannot find anywhere is an instrument for sorting their own reactions, so that is what I need.

Please observe the following requirements.

1. Construct the logical space before you construct the tree. Open by identifying three or four questions that are genuinely independent of one another — for example, whether the conclusion of a thought experiment can outrun what is already contained in its premises; whether the warrant it supplies is empirical or a priori; whether the imagining itself, as distinct from the argument it could be written up as, does any justificatory work; and whether a thought experiment must be recoverable as a valid argument in order to be worth anything. Then lay out the grid of combinations those questions permit, and for each cell say whether a real view occupies it. For every unoccupied cell, say in one clause whether the absence is because the view is incoherent, because it is unattractive, or because nobody has simply explored it yet.

2. Give the tree itself as indented text, with six to nine branch nodes and eight to twelve leaves. Each node must be phrased as a question a reader can answer from their own reactions to cases, without any prior familiarity with where the answer leads; no node may contain a philosopher's name, the label of a position, or a term of art that would need defining. At least five nodes must be keyed to a specific imagined situation drawn from this list: Galileo's two stones, Einstein chasing a light beam, Maxwell's demon, Newton's bucket and Mach's retort to it, Schrödinger's cat, the Einstein-Podolsky-Rosen setup, and Twin Earth.

3. Each leaf should carry the recognized name of the position if it has one, or the word unnamed if no one defends it, followed by one sentence saying what the view claims and one sentence stating the commitment the view has the hardest time defending. Among the leaves I need to see: the view that a thought experiment is nothing over and above an argument in vivid dress, with no epistemology of its own; the view that some thought experiments deliver genuine a priori insight into how nature must behave; the view that their force comes from mentally running a model or simulation of the system rather than from any inference; the view that thought experiments have no distinctive epistemology and inherit whatever reliability the empirical generalizations they deploy happen to have; and at least one leaf holding that a bad thought experiment is not different in kind from a bad inductive argument.

4. Add a section on two tests that every leaf must be made to pass. The first is redeployment: the same imagined situation gets run in opposite directions by the two sides of a dispute, as with Newton's bucket and Mach's use of it. For each leaf, say in one clause what that view is committed to predicting about a thought experiment used this way — that it loses its force, that the force splits between the two readings, or that nothing about the case has changed. The second is disagreement: two of our readers hear the same thought experiment and one reports that it settles the matter while the other reports nothing at all. Say what each leaf makes of that variability.

5. Flag any pair of leaves that your nodes cannot actually separate, and say which question would be needed to separate them if it exists. Do not quietly merge them and do not pretend the tree does more work than it does.

6. Close with a two-question short form for readers who will not walk the whole tree, and identify which two nodes you would keep and why.

Two constraints on presentation. I need no bibliography, no reading list, no footnotes, and no citations of any kind, and please do not invent any; where a historical episode matters, describe it in your own words rather than quoting anyone. Aim for nine hundred to twelve hundred words, in plain prose, with no jokes and no rhetorical questions directed at me.
