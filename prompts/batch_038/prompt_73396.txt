So, I write novels for a living — third one comes out in the spring — and a few months back I was grouseing to a friend who teaches eighteenth-century stuff about a review that basically said my book was beautiful and morally empty. She laughed and mailed me Rousseau's Letter to d'Alembert on the Theatre, the 1758 one where he's arguing against d'Alembert's suggestion that Geneva build a playhouse. I expected a cranky genevan pamphlet about actors and got something that keeps me up at night, because half of it is aimed squarely at me. Not the Geneva politics so much as the psychology underneath: his insistence that the whole business of a play is to please, and therefore it can never reform an audience, only mirror and flatter back whatever that audience already approves of; the long section on Molière's Misanthrope, where his complaint is that the honest man is the funny one and the smooth liar gets the audience's sympathy; the claim that the spectator's pity gets used up in the seat, so that weeping at a performance is a cheap substitute for actually helping anybody; and the bit where he says forget the theater, plant a post in the public square, gather the citizens around it, and let them be the spectacle to themselves. Also his weird ranking that tragedy is less dangerous than comedy because the heroes are too remote to imitate, whereas comedy's characters look exactly like the people in the stalls.

Here's what I want: a real reading of The Picture of Dorian Gray against that letter. Not a theme-hunt, an actual argument about whether Wilde's novel vindicates Rousseau or is itself the thing Rousseau was warning about — a beautifully made object that flatters a reader's appetite for beautiful corruption, so that we in the armchair are exactly the Genevan public he didn't want in the theater. Work through the scenes where I think the two texts rub against each other: Sybil Vane, who is an actress and therefore double-damned in Rousseau's book, and the night she acts badly because she's fallen in love for real and can no longer pretend — does Rousseau's account of the stage predict that failure, or does it have no room for it? The yellow book Lord Henry hands over, which operates in the novel as pure contagion, the one text that actually changes somebody, which is the mechanism Rousseau says theater doesn't have. Lord Henry as a man who has converted his entire life into spectatorship and never once does anything. Dorian's cataloguing of jewels and perfumes and tapestries, all those exquisite sensations that leave him exactly as rotten as he was. Basil, the one character who believes art carries moral weight and gets a knife for it. And the ending, where Dorian attacks the portrait instead of himself — is that Rousseau's diagnosis of the spectator made literal, the man who would rather punish the representation than change the thing it represents?

Two constraints. I'm putting this on a festival panel in about six weeks in front of a mixed crowd, half of whom haven't read either text, so keep the plot recap to a minimum but tell me which chapters or scenes you'd point at and in what order, and tell me which of my guesses above are wrong or forced — where the Rousseau lens actually distorts Wilde rather than illuminating him. Wilde's preface denies outright that books are moral or immoral, only well or badly written, so the novel is arguably refusing the entire question Rousseau is asking; I want you to take that seriously and say where my reading breaks, not paper over it. And no bibliography, no recommended further reading, no citations — I can't chase those down and neither can half the panel. Just the reading, the scenes, and a blunt final answer to the question I actually care about: if Rousseau were in the room, what would he say about the difference between Sybil's bad performance and my own books?
