Q3. Tomas works nights in a bonded warehouse. Every pallet that leaves goes onto the floor scale, which shows the weight to the nearest kilo. Two years ago there was a bad labour dispute at the firm, and there were credible reports that scales at two other sites had their firmware altered so that any true weight of 900kg or more was displayed as 899, i.e. so that overloading went undetected. Tomas's scale was checked after the dispute and found to be unaltered, and nothing he can see from a single reading would tell him whether a scale had since been altered; detecting it would need a certified test weight, which the night shift doesn't have. Tonight's pallet weighs 640kg. The display reads 640. Tomas forms the belief

  L: this pallet is under 900kg

and then reasons explicitly from it to

  N: this scale is not concealing an overload right now

(where a scale conceals an overload right now just in case the true weight is 900kg or more and the display reads 899).

(a) [10] State the tracking conditions for knowledge and apply them to L. Say explicitly what you are holding fixed when you evaluate the counterfactuals, and say why that choice matters.
(b) [8] Apply the same conditions to N. Does Tomas know N?
(c) [7] Given your answers to (a) and (b), what does this case show about the closure of knowledge under known implication? Then set out the skeptic's use of closure against the tracking theorist, and the tracking theorist's reply.

And here is the student's script, unedited apart from the numbering:

"(a) Nozick replaces the justification condition in the traditional analysis with two counterfactual ones. S knows that p only if p is true, S believes that p, if p were false S wouldn't believe that p (the sensitivity condition), and if p were true S would believe that p (the adherence condition). L is true and Tomas believes it, so adherence is satisfied straight away: adherence just says the belief would be there when the fact is, and here both are actual. So everything turns on sensitivity. The thing to hold fixed when we run the counterfactual is the state of the firmware, because that's an actual standing fact about the situation and not part of what L is about; what we vary is the weight of the pallet. So consider the closest situations in which L is false: the pallet is 900kg or over, the scale is still the unaltered one it actually is, so the display shows the true weight, and Tomas forms the opposite belief. L is therefore sensitive, and adherent, and true, and believed, so Tomas knows L. If instead you varied the firmware at the same time as the weight you'd get the other verdict, but that's just choosing a more distant counterfactual situation for no reason. Worth noting too that this isn't a Gettier case in the ordinary shape: nothing Tomas infers from is false and there's no coincidental match, so the case isn't doing work against justified true belief at all.

(b) N is true, since the pallet is 640kg, and Tomas believes it. But the belief is unsafe. The tampering is a live possibility that he has no way of ruling out from where he stands, so there are plenty of nearby situations in which he forms the very same belief on the very same basis — reading the display — and it is false, because in those situations the scale has been altered and an overloaded pallet is going out. A belief that could easily have been false is a belief that doesn't track the truth, so Tomas does not know N.

(c) L entails N, by the definition of concealment that the question gives, and Tomas sees the entailment and infers N from L. So he knows L, he knows that L entails N, and he doesn't know N: knowledge is not closed under known implication. That's the point the tracking theorist wants, because it lets them block the skeptic without conceding anything. The skeptic argues by modus tollens: Tomas doesn't know N; if he knew L he would know N, since he deduced N competently from L; therefore he doesn't know L. The reply, as with Dretske, is that the altered-firmware possibility is not a relevant alternative in this context, so it doesn't have to be ruled out for Tomas to know L, and that's what rejecting closure comes to. The deeper lesson is that on this account inference can't extend knowledge at all. Any belief Tomas reaches by deducing it from L will fail to track the truth, because the closest situations in which the inferred claim is false are situations in which L is false too, and in those he keeps believing L. So the view makes deduction a merely preservative business, which is a cost, but a cost the tracking theorist accepts in exchange for avoiding skepticism."
