ok, I need someone who actually works on philosophy of history / historiographical method to push back on me here, not a generalist survey. Think it through properly: start with a rough brainstorm of every candidate correspondence you can spot before you commit to a table, and don't sand the edges off just to stay even-handed.

Setup. I'm writing the methods chapter of a master's thesis. When I told my supervisor I wanted to defend something close to Ranke's old instruction to show the past as it actually was, he reached for Carr's fishing image in What is History? — roughly: the facts of the past are like fish swimming in a vast ocean, and what the historian catches depends on where he chooses to fish and on the mesh of the net he chooses to use, so by and large he gets the kind of facts he wants. My supervisor's use of it was blunt: the spot is chosen, the mesh is chosen, the net is chosen, so there is no such thing as a neutral catch, and the whole Rankean project is naive. Then a guy in my seminar pushed the same image further in a Hayden White direction: you never actually encounter an uncaught fish, and what you hand over at the end is a story you assembled out of scales and a couple of bones, so the net doesn't merely select the fish, it constitutes them.

I want an audit of the analogy as an argument, not a biography of it.

1. Correspondence table, explicit: ocean, fish, net, mesh size, fishing location, the catch, the fisherman, plus anything else that earns a slot (the boat? the market? the quota? the fisherman's skill?). For each, say what in historiography it is supposed to stand for and how tightly it stands for it.

2. Sort the disanalagies into ones that actually bite against my supervisor's conclusion and ones that are decorative. My guesses at the load-bearing ones: fish are the size and number they are whether or not anyone nets them; mesh is checkable against the catch, since you can haul a finer net over the same water and see what you missed; fishing has an internal success condition, you caught fish or you didn't and you can tell which; the fisherman's purposes are external to the ocean; and nets don't get better at catching precisely what they were built to let through. Tell me which of these a Carr-ian can simply absorb and which ones do real damage, and where Elton's complaint about the historical-fact / fact-of-the-past split is a better counter than anything I've come up with.

3. This is the bit I most want nailed down: does the analogy smuggle in the realism it is being used to attack? It looks to me like the ocean image quietly grants a determinate stock of past facts waiting under the surface, plus a sense in which gear improves, plus the idea that a catch is judged against what's in the water. If that's right, the picture supports Carr's own moderate claim — interaction, dialogue, the historical fact as distinct from the fact of the past — and actively resists the stronger sceptical reading. Argue for or against that and give me a confidence level rather than a shrug.

4. Can the image be stretched to do what my seminar guy wants, a net that constitutes the fish, without ceasing to be an analogy anyone would recognise? Say exactly what has to be altered for that to work, and whether what's left is still an argument or just a switch to a different picture wearing the same clothes.

5. Propose a better analogy if one exists. I keep reaching for map projection — every projection distorts, some distortions are fatal for some purposes, the territory is indifferent — or for the paleontologist rebuilding a skeleton from fragments with informed imagination. Say which you'd actually use, what work each can and can't carry, and what conclusion each licenses that the fishing one doesn't.

Finally, the epistemic ask: tell me what would change your verdict. Which consideration would get you to say the fishing analogy does carry the full sceptical conclusion after all, and where is your own reasoning here most likely to fail — I'd rather be shown the weak joint than handed a confident answer.
