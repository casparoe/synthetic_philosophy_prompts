I've been arguing with a colleague about lottocracy, and he produced the following, which he takes to show that the case for sortition consumes itself. I could not object to any single step while we were working through it, and I cannot accept the end point either.

(1) A body that makes binding decisions for everyone is entitled to do so only if the procedure by which its members are chosen gives no group an unfair advantage in determining what is decided.
(2) Elections give such an advantage: they filter for money, name recognition, party backing and articulateness, so elected legislatures are recruited from and answerable to a narrow stratum.
(3) So elected legislatures are not entitled to make binding decisions for everyone.
(4) Selection by lot gives no such advantage, since everyone's chance of serving is the same and nobody has to defeat anybody in order to get there.
(5) So a sorted legislature is entitled to make binding decisions for everyone.
(6) But what gets decided depends not only on who sits in the chamber. It depends on who sets the agenda, who drafts the questions put to the chamber, and who supplies the briefings and testimony it works from. If any of that falls to elected officials or to a permanent staff, the advantage excluded by (1) reappears one level up.
(7) So a sorted legislature is entitled to decide only if its agenda and its information supply are themselves produced without giving any group an advantage.
(8) Producing them without advantage is itself a determination of what is decided, and so falls under (1) on the same reasoning — and likewise for whatever produces that, at every further level.
(9) Either the series stops, in which case the stopping point is a choice over which nobody's chances were equalized, which is exactly what (1) forbids; or it does not stop, in which case no legislature ever begins to operate.
(10) Therefore (1) is false. And since (3) was derived from it, elections are not disqualified after all, and lottocracy has nothing over them.

Which step would you reject, and what does rejecting it cost? For what it is worth, I want to keep (2) and (4), which look like the best-supported claims in the argument, and I would rather not simply abandon (1), since something like it is what made sortition attractive to me in the first place. If all three can survive, show me how; if they cannot, tell me which goes, and what the lottocrat then owes at the level where the series in (8) threatens to run.
