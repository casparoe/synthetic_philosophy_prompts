I sit on the patient-safety committee at a mid-size hospital and we've been stuck on the same argument for about three meetings now, so I want to try to settle it by pulling the language apart instead of shouting more cases at each other. Two things came through the same month. Nurse A programmed a pump with the wrong concentration, the patient coded, and died. Nurse B programmed the exact same error on the same kind of pump, and a pharmacist caught it before anything went in. As far as we could reconstruct, the two situations matched on everything we normally score: same training, same shift length (both past twelve hours, both covering a second unit), same documented failure to do the independent double-check, no prior events, and no difference in what either of them was aiming at. Our chair wants both handled identically; the risk manager wants A terminated and B coached. The chair ended the last session by saying "well, it's the thought that counts," and that's been treated as if it resolved something, even though every other time I've heard that sentence in my life it's been about a bad birthday present.

So here's what I want from you: take that sentence and tell me what it could actually be claiming, as a menu rather than a single gloss. I can already see some of the shape of the space but not all of it — it might be a claim about what makes an act morally good versus a claim about what makes an agent blameworthy; it might be an epistemic point, i.e. that judges and committees can only ever get at intentions so we should stick to intentions; it might be a narrower claim about how to set sanctions without touching blame at all; it might be the strong view that consequences contribute literally nothing to moral assessment; or something weaker about the quality of the will being the only thing that's really "up to" the person. For each reading you give me, say whether it's a platitude nobody would dispute, whether it's substantive but defensible, or whether it's just false, and say what has to be true for it to work. Where a reading makes the sentence come out true, note what the sentence then fails to say — because part of what's driving our fight is that people keep reading it as if it were doing more work than any single reading can do.

Two more things. First, tell me which of your readings the chair actually needs in order to get from the proverb to "treat A and B the same," and whether that reading, all by itself, licenses the conclusion, or whether he's smuggling in extra premises about fairness or about what a policy can administer. And second, is there any reading that would instead license the risk manager, and if so is it the same sentence doing different work or a different sentence people keep confusing with it? When I tried the analogy of two drivers who both roll through a red light while texting, one of whom kills a kid and one of who doesn't, somebody waved it off with "that's just the moral luck thing, nobody really believes that," which I'm pretty sure is a dodge and not an answer. Please keep it to the philosophy of the sentence — I don't want advice on how to structure our disciplinary process, I want a list of what the words could mean and which of those meanings survives contact with my two nurses. Oh, and keep it as a list I can hand around at the next meeting, since half the committee thinks this is entirely a matter of feelings and I'd like to show them its not.
