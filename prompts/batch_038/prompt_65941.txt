Next month our estuary bird-monitoring programme closes after twenty-two years, and I have been asked to give the address at the final gathering — the last of the monthly data-entry evenings, which the funder has agreed to let become a small farewell. I founded the programme, and I will also be the person saying goodbye to it.

The room will hold four groups who share no vocabulary. About thirty volunteer recorders, most of them retired, several of whom have counted the same six sites every winter since the first. Two former graduate students whose theses were built on our counts, now working in conservation elsewhere. The regional director of the funding body, who signed the termination memo and is attending entirely in good faith. And a reporter from the county paper who covers local nature news and will want one good line.

The termination memo was, as far as I can judge, correct. It held that twenty-two years of expenditure could not enter the calculation on either side; that the only question was whether another year of counting would do more good than the same money spent on habitat work at three other sites; and that it would not. I agree. I have said so publicly, and I mean to say so again from the front of the room — I am not going to ask anyone to believe the programme should have been saved.

That agreement is what makes the speech difficult, and why I am commissioning it rather than writing it myself. Nearly every consoling thing I could naturally say turns out to be either untrue or a species of the very reasoning I have just conceded to be beside the point. Claiming that the years were not wasted because of what they yielded is retrospective accounting of exactly the kind the memo rightly declined, and it is weak on the facts as well: the dataset is deposited, and in four years nothing has been done with it beyond two citations that misread it. There is one genuine bright spot — in the sixteenth year our counts picked up an invasive predator at a single site early enough for it to be cleared — and the memo's defenders have already begun citing that as proof the programme paid for itself. I think this is the misuse of a lucky observation, and I do not intend to use it, though I recognise that refusing the only piece of good news available will be the hardest thing to justify to the recorders.

So: please write the address. Five to seven minutes as spoken, in plain English, deliverable by someone who is not a philosopher and who will be visibly tired. It must honour twenty-two years of cold mornings without asserting that they were a sound investment, and without letting the director suspect that I am relitigating his decision under cover of sentiment. And it must give the recorders something true to hold. Here is the part I want thought through most carefully. Several of them kept counting long after it was plausible that the counting mattered — partly because my co-founder Moira, who died in the eleventh year and whose name is on the hut, had asked them to, and partly because by then it had stopped being an investment and become the way they spent their Saturdays. My suspicion is that the memo's fault was not arithmetic but category: it priced a portfolio where what was actually being closed was a practice, and a set of promises. Whether that suspicion is sound, or merely a sore loser's consolation, is what I want the speech to work out in public, honestly, and in language a county reporter can follow. I would also like it to take a position — without ever using the word — on whether those recorders were irrational at any point.

No jargon, no named theories, no allusions that only the two academics will catch. Close on something the recorders can do on Monday. Afterwards, add three or four sentences telling me which consoling lines you deliberately kept out and why, so that I can defend the choices if the reporter asks.
