so I'm drafting my quals answer on why pain is bad and before I commit to it on paper I want to know whether my package actually hangs together. Roughly:

1. Pain is bad for the person feeling it, and that badness is intrinsic to the felt character — nothing extra has to hook onto the experience for it to be bad. Not a desire, not a judgment, not a disposition to act.
2. People with pain asymbolia really are in pain. I take their reports at face value: they can tell you where it is, how intense it is, and that it hurts. (They also still jerk back from some stimuli, which I don't think is doing any work here.)
3. But their pain isnt bad for them, or isnt bad in the way mine would be. They don't mind it, and some of them seem amused by it.
4. That's exactly why I like the case: it pins the badness to the affective dimension rather than the sensory one.
5. And I think that affective dimension just is a kind of felt aversion — a wanting-the-feeling-to-stop that's baked into how the feeling feels.
6. Except wanting a feeling to stop can't be enough to make it bad. I want the mosquito whining by my ear to stop, and that isn't bad in anything like the way pain is.
7. Also, half of me worries the whole edifice rests on sand, since what's damaged in these patients probably isn't a pain module but something more general about how threats get tagged as mattering at all.

Go through these and tell me which ones genuinely can't all be true. Rank the conflicts by severity instead of listing them as equally bad, and for each say what I'd have to drop or water down and what that costs me downstream. And flag the pairs that only look like they conflict — I don't want to go repairing something that isn't broken.
