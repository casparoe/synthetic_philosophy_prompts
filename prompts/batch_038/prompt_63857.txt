Our after-school cosmology seminar ended with an argument that seems to prove far more than our teacher intended, and I have not been able to locate the break in it. (1) If the universe expands forever toward an empty vacuum state with a nonzero temperature, then thermal fluctuations continually assemble ordered systems out of equilibrium. (2) Assembling a single momentary brain is a far smaller entropy decrease than assembling an entire cosmos like ours, and smaller decreases are exponentially more likely. (3) So in such a universe, observers who hallucinated their past and their surroundings vastly outnumber observers with genuine ones. (4) Therefore a member of that population selected at random is almost certainly a hallucinating one. (5) I have no reason to think I am anything other than a member selected at random. (6) So I am almost certainly hallucinating — including the telescope data and the statistical mechanics that carried me to (1). (7) So none of my empirical beliefs is justified. (8) And because the same reasoning could be run against any cosmological theory whatsoever, no claim about the universe as a whole can ever be justified. Three classmates named three different culprits, which makes me suspect none of us has actually found it. I would rather abandon (8) than (2), since (2) looks to me like nothing but counting. Which step fails, why did it seem acceptable until I wrote it out in this order, and what would I be giving up if I rejected it instead of the others?
