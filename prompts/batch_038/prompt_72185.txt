It was about the precautionary principle. It came up because of a story on gene-drive mosquitoes — Dana works in public health and she was going on about how PP is the only responsible way to think about releasing self-propagating organisms, and I said PP is famously incoherent, and she said I was being a philosopher.

The actual disagreement is narrower than "is PP good." Dana's line was that the standard attack — that PP is either vacuous, because once you have any probability estimates at all ordinary cost-benefit analysis already tells you what to do and PP adds nothing, or else it's absurd, because a maximin rule under uncertainty would forbid basically every technology including the ones that saved millions of lives — that attack only bites on *decision-rule* versions of PP. Her claim is that PP isn't a decision rule at all. It's an evidential rule. It doesn't tell you what to do, it tells you who has to go generate data before an activity is allowed to proceed. So it operates on the *inputs* to cost-benefit analysis instead of competing with it, and it never faces the vacuous-or-absurd dilemma, because a burden of proof isn't a weight and isn't a probability threshold in the decision-theoretic sense. And the asymmetry — that proponents bear the burden rather than opponents — isn't a bias smuggled in, it's just what "precautionary" means.

I pushed back and said that's relabeling. A burden of proof is a threshold, and a threshold has to sit somewhere. Put it at 50/50 and you've got nothing distinctive — you've got the ordinary civil standard and PP is doing no work at all. Put it anywhere else and you've had to decide how bad the harm is and how much you care about it, which is exactly the decision-theoretic content she was trying to quarantine. So the burden-shifing reading inherits the dilemma: vacuous at 50/50, arbitrary-or-secretly-a-decision-rule everywhere else. She said "arbitrary" was doing a lot of work in my mouth, and that legal systems set non-50/50 burdens of proof constantly without anyone calling them incoherent.

Then we both got foggy and one of us dredged up a third option: a tripwire version (I think it's Sunstein's but don't quote me on that), where PP sets neither a weight nor a threshold — it just names lines in the world, such that if you cross this line, this predetermined response fires, whatever the probabilities said. Which is really a rule about when to stop deliberating and act, i.e. a precommitment device for genuinely imponderable uncertainty. We never figured out whether that's a real third category or just a decision rule with extra steps.
