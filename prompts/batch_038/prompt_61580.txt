I'm part of a working group on how machine-learning systems ought to model themselves, and one member has been leaning on an analogy I find suggestive but unstable. His claim is that designing a capable agent that reasons about its own reasoning is structurally the same problem as designing a formal theory that proves things about its own proofs. He states the correspondences fairly explicitly: the agent's belief set is the theorem set, deliberation is proof search, a belief about its own current or future reasoning corresponds to a sentence asserting that the theory derives some given formula, a self-model that is both complete and accurate corresponds to a theory that proves its own consistency, and self-deception or incoherence corresponds to inconsistency. From this he draws two conclusions. First, that no sufficiently capable agent can possess a complete and accurate model of itself, since Gödel's second theorem forecloses it, and that the program of engineering such self-models should therefore be abandoned rather than repaired. Second, that the right design response is the one the theory case suggests: build systems that are deliberately incomplete about themselves and defer outward, to a stronger system, a human overseer, or an ensemble, in the way one establishes the consistency of a theory by climbing to a stronger one.

Could you audit the analogy for me? I would like: (i) the mapping laid out explicitly, including any correspondences he has not stated but that his two conclusions tacitly require; (ii) the disanalogies, separated into those that bear on the first conclusion and the second respectively and those that are merely decorative — I would particularly like to know whether belief behaves enough like derivability for the diagonal construction to go through at all, whether an agent whose stock of accepted premises shifts each time it updates on its own outputs qualifies as the fixed, recursively axiomatizable system the theorem presupposes, and what, in a physical self-model, plays the role of a system's capacity to name and manipulate its own syntax; (iii) what the source domain quietly imports that the target lacks, including anything about the trust one must already place in the stronger theory for a relative consistency proof to be worth having; (iv) a verdict on each conclusion: does it survive once the disanalogies are on the table, and if it does, does the analogy carry any of the weight or is the conclusion supported by independent argument he could have given directly; and (v) if the analogy fails for the purpose he is putting it to, a better one, with a candid statement of what the replacement can and cannot support. I am far less interested in whether Gödel's theorem is correct than in whether incompleteness is the right shape of obstacle here.
