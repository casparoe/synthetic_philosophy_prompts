**UNKNOWABILITY IS SELF-CONCEALING: A SECOND-ORDER READING OF THE KNOWABILITY PARADOX**

**1. The proof and what it's supposed to show.** Take the knowability principle, KP: for every proposition p, if p then possibly-Kp, where K is an idealized knowledge operator (factive, distributing over conjunction) and the diamond is plain metaphysical possibility. Instantiate KP with the Moore-style conjunction p ∧ ¬Kp. You get that this conjunction is possibly known. But if it were known, then both conjuncts would be known, and knowing the second conjunct entails that nobody knows the first, contradiction. So the conjunction is not possibly known. So, by KP, it's not true. And since p was arbitrary, if p then Kp: every truth is known. Add the obvious premise that some truths are in fact unknown and you get that KP is false.

Two framing points I want to keep visible, because the rest of the paper leans on them. First, the result as Fitch actually published it (Theorem 5 of his 1963 paper on value concepts, which grew out of a referee report Alonzo Church sent him in 1945) runs the other way round: contingent ignorance entails necessary ignorance. If there are truths nobody knows, then there are truths nobody could ever know. Fitch's own use for it had nothing to do with anti-realism — he wanted to block a conditional-fallacy problem for his informed-desire analysis of value. Second, the live philosophical complaint isn't just that anti-realists overlooked a counterexample; it's the one you find in Kvanvig and in Brogaard and Salerno, namely that moderate anti-realism doesn't seem to be *expressible* as a thesis strictly weaker than naive idealism. My target is that second complaint.

**2. What I don't want to do.** I don't want to run a restriction strategy. Tennant restricts KP to Cartesian propositions, ones whose knowability is consistent, and the standard complaint is that the restriction looks tailor-made to block the derivation. Edgington inserts an actuality operator so that KP says of a true p that possibly someone knows that p is actually true, and again the complaint (Williamson's, as I understand it — please correct me if I've garbled this, I'm fuzzy on the details) is that the resulting principle either doesn't do the work anti-realism needs or falls to a parallel version of the paradox. Brogaard and Salerno, if I've got them right, charge that the standard derivation illicitly applies a necessitation-like rule to a line that still depends on the KP hypothesis. I'm not going to adjudicate any of that. I want to argue the paradox teaches something about the *epistemic status of unknowability claims* rather than about the extension of knowable truths.

**3. The result.** Call a truth *self-concealing* if knowing it destroys the possibility of knowing that you know it. Claim U: for no p can anyone know both p and that p is never knowable.

Suppose an agent knows p and knows ¬◊Kp. Factivity on the second gives ¬◊Kp. But the first gives Kp, hence p is known, hence ◊Kp, since actuality entails possibility. Contradiction. So U holds.

Three things are worth noting about U. (i) Its assumptions are a strict subset of what the Fitch proof needs — just factivity for K and the inference from φ to ◊φ. It requires no distribution of K over conjunction and no unrestricted KP, so it's more robust than the paradox it's read off of. (ii) U entails that knowledge of a given truth's unknowability is only ever available to agents who don't know that truth: unknowability is knowable from outside a question, never from inside it. (iii) U says nothing about the general claim. You can know that some truth or other is unknowable, because you can know an existential generalization without knowing any witness. So the denial of KP is knowable in general and unknowable in every particular. That asymmetry is the whole content of the paper.

Contrast with unprovability. For the Gödel sentence we can, in a stronger theory, prove that the sentence isn't provable in the weaker one; unprovability is surveyable from a higher vantage point. On U there is no analogous vantage point for unknowability, because the vantage point you'd need — knowing p — is exactly the one that falsifies the claim you're trying to survey.

**4. Why this answers the expressibility complaint.** The moderate anti-realist is committed to: some truths are such that no possible agent ever knows them. Section 3 says this commitment is real, coherent and knowable, but essentially anonymous — it can never be discharged into a witness. That, I suggest, is the correct diagnosis of why the thesis feels inexpressible. It isn't that KP can't be weakened without collapsing; it's that the weakening is unsurveyable from the inside, and we mistake unsurveyability for unstatable-ness. Naive idealism, by contrast, makes a claim that is witness-by-witness checkable in principle: for each truth there's a knower, and the anti-realist's own objection is that we can't find one.

**5. A restriction I'm tempted by but don't trust.** If U is right, the Fitch proof only bites on propositions that are already about knowledge or knowability, since it needs ¬Kp to be part of the target. So restrict KP to epistemically unmixed propositions, ones containing no epistemic operators: KP*. The derivation is blocked because p ∧ ¬Kp is out of range. Unlike Tennant's restriction this isn't defined by reference to the proof; it's defined by subject matter, and it's independently motivated if KP was ever meant as a claim about the world's transparency to inquiry rather than a claim about the closure properties of K. Obvious worry: this is just a type solution, and there's an argument (Carrara and Fassio, in Theoria — I haven't been able to get hold of it) that typed restrictions on knowledge are not available. Also I'm not sure KP* is stable under embedding, or whether I've secretly reintroduced the paradox by allowing mixed propositions to be unknowable while insisting they're never instances of the principle.

**6. Open.** Is ¬◊Kq knowable at all, for any q? U permits it, but only for an agent ignorant of q, and knowing that q could never be known seems to require ruling out every possible epistemic situation, which looks like a job for somebody who knows a lot about q. Also: does U survive a temporalized reading of the diamond, on which KP says p can come to be known after now? My guess is yes, since a later knower makes the earlier claim false, but I can't see whether an indexical-from-here reading gives an escape.
