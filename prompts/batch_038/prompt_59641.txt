I am revising a short essay in social epistemology — currently about 1,400 words, condensed below — and I would like critical comment on the argument rather than encouragement. Please read the whole draft before responding, and please do not supply references, a bibliography, or names of works I ought to consult; I want your own reasoning about the argument, not pointers to the literature.

**Working title: Manufactured Consilience: The Bystander's Problem with Information Cascades**

1. The setup is familiar. A sequence of agents must each choose between two options, one of which is correct. Each agent acts in turn, sees everything the earlier agents chose but nothing of the evidence behind their choices, and receives a private signal that is better than a coin flip though far from certain. The first agent follows her signal. The second weighs her signal against the first agent's choice. By the time the third agent acts, two agreeing choices outweigh a single contrary signal at any level of signal reliability above chance, so she follows the crowd — rationally, and against her own evidence. Thereafter the sequence conveys no new information: each subsequent agent faces an overwhelming public record and suppresses a private signal that is never revealed. The group can lock onto the wrong option and remain there indefinitely, and a comparatively small amount of fresh public information can overturn the whole thing. Nothing anyone does here is irrational; every step is correct Bayesian reasoning. That is precisely why it is called rational herding.

2. The moral usually drawn concerns aggregation. Information distributed across the members of the group — many private signals, each of some value — fails to be pooled, so the collective decision is far worse than the information the collective possessed. On this telling the losers are the agents inside the sequence, and the disease is a failure to combine.

3. I want to argue that this framing mislocates the harm, and that the mislocation has consequences for what a remedy must look like.

4. Consider someone who is not in the sequence at all: a later arriver, a member of another group, a reader who simply encounters the fact that very many people hold some belief or chose some option. What does widespread agreement tell her? The evidential force of agreement depends almost entirely on the independence of the agreeing parties. Ten people who arrive at a belief by ten separate routes provide a great deal of evidence; ten people who arrived by copying one another provide roughly as much as the first two or three. But the bystander occupies exactly the position the agents inside the cascade occupied with respect to one another's private information: she can observe the outputs of the process, not its structure. Agreement and echo are, from where she stands, the same observation.

5. So the more interesting damage a cascade does is not the bad decision it produces but the epistemic condition it imposes on everyone downstream: it manufactures apparent consilience. It converts a small quantity of evidence into something that presents itself as a large quantity, and it does so invisibly. The bystander who treats observed agreement as corroboration is applying an inference rule that is normally sound; she is not careless. She is being reliably misled by a reliable rule. The victim population of a cascade is therefore much larger than the standard model suggests — not merely the agents who suppressed their signals, but every consumer of the resulting consensus, whose credences are inflated by an amount they have no way of measuring.

6. This relocates the target of institutional design. The familiar remedies — collecting opinions privately before discussion, requiring written independent estimates before pooling, appointing a devil's advocate, withholding senior members' views until the end — all operate on the sequence, trying to preserve independence at the point of formation. If the cost is borne at the point of consumption, remedies must also address consumption: what the hearer should do when independence cannot be verified.

7. My proposal is a genealogy condition on corroboration. Agreement between sources corroborates only insofar as the hearer has positive reason to think their belief-forming processes were independent; absent such reason, the hearer should treat n agreeing sources as worth at most one, not n. The default is non-corroboration, defeasible only by evidence about the history of the beliefs rather than about their content. What keeps this from being a counsel of despair is that the relevant history is cheap to elicit and comparatively hard to fake: not "why do you believe this?", which invites a rehearsed reconstruction, but "what did you have in front of you when you settled on this, and when did you settle on it?" Two sources who can show that their formation preceded any exposure to each other are genuinely corroborative; two who cannot have not established agreement but redundancy.

8. There is an objection I cannot yet answer, and it may be fatal. Conventions of justification are themselves learnable by imitation. Once asking after the genealogy of a belief becomes the recognized form of due diligence, agents can satisfy its letter — producing plausible histories of independent formation — while its diagnostic value decays. The remedy is thus exposed to a second-order cascade: a cascade not about what to believe but about what counts as a properly independent route to belief. My response is that this decay is slower, because the script concerns the shape of a person's past information rather than the content of her conclusion; imitating a conclusion is free, whereas maintaining a set of mutually consistent, mutually checkable past informational states is expensive and constraining. I am not confident the response survives scrutiny, and it is the part of the draft I most want attacked.

9. A limit worth stating: for many beliefs formed under time pressure or in diffuse informational environments, no genealogy can be reconstructed afterward, because nobody recorded when they came to believe what. There the condition collapses into a blanket discount — treat widespread agreement as approximately one source. That is a large practical conclusion, and it may simply be correct: in high-volume environments, consensus should be near-worthless by default.

10. On the standard story, the cascade is a tragedy about the loss of pooled information among the people making the decision. On mine it is a tragedy about legibility: the structure of a group's agreement becomes unreadable from outside, and everyone reading it from outside over-believes. The aim of design, accordingly, is not to make people agree less but to make the shape of their agreement visible.

Please comment on the following, in this order of priority.

1. **The load-bearing question.** Does the distinction I draw between a failure of aggregation and a failure of calibration do real work, or is it a relabeling? The observation that a cascade leaves private signals unrevealed already entails that an outsider cannot read the group's evidence out of its actions. If my central claim is that same fact viewed from a different end of the process, then the essay offers a change of camera angle rather than a discovery, and it should say so plainly. Tell me which it is; if it is a relabeling, tell me whether anything substantive still follows from the change of angle.

2. **The rationality objection.** A fully rational bystander knows that cascades happen and therefore already discounts observed agreement. On that line no miscalibration arises among ideal reasoners, and my argument can only concern people who discount insufficiently — which would make the essay about a bias rather than a structural feature, and sharply reduce its interest. Press this as hard as you can, and say whether any version of my claim survives it, for instance one on which even the ideal reasoner is worse off because the information required to calibrate the discount has been destroyed rather than merely neglected.

3. **The originality worry.** I suspect a reviewer will say the whole point is the familiar independence condition on jury-theorem-style results transposed to testimony: agreement carries weight only if the agreeing parties are independent, and correlated votes break the aggregation. If that is the right diagnosis, say so and tell me what, if anything, the draft adds beyond applying a known condition to a known phenomenon. If you think it adds something, state precisely what.

4. **Technical accuracy.** Check my description of the canonical model in the first paragraph: the claim that the sequence stops transmitting information once a cascade begins, the claim that a single contrary private signal is outweighed at any signal reliability above chance once two agreeing choices are on the record, and the claim about how easily a cascade can be overturned. Flag anything overstated or false and propose corrected phrasing.

5. **Is the proposal operational?** Does the genealogy condition in the seventh paragraph amount to more than "ask people for their evidence" — a practice well known to elicit confabulated justifications?
