So — three months from now I have to actually write the last third of my novel, and I've got a philosophy problem I can't charm my way out of. I want help deciding where to spend my thinking time. Not a survey, and definitely no bibliography — I'm not going to chase references, I want you to argue.

The book: there's a manuscript that is a person. Not metaphorically. A bound typescript, ~400 pages, left behind by a dead physicist; anyone who reads it straight through, in order, at normal speed, ends up in a conversation with it. Second strand: the physicist's brain was pulled out at the autopsy and kept — jars, then slices, then mailed off to various labs, the real Einstein business, roughly — and one of my characters has a slide of it on her desk and talks to it. The two strands are supposed to rhyme: mind as a pattern that can be sliced, distributed, put in a drawer, restarted. That's the engine of the whole thing. (I got the bug from Hofstadter and Dennett's The Mind's I, the 1981 collection — the Dennett piece where he's separated from his own brain, and something about Einstein's brain in there too, though I might be folding another Hofstadter thing into it; doesn't matter for what I'm asking.)

Here's my problem: I can afford maybe twelve hours a week for thinking before I start drafting, and there are about eight live questions about whether a text could be a mind, and they are not remotely equally load-bearing. My list, rough:

1. The restart problem. Every time someone opens the book and reads it through — same person waking up, a copy, or a brand new one? If the same reader reads it twice in a year, did anybody die in between?
2. Simultaneity. Two people read it in different cities on the same afternoon. One mind having two experiences, or two minds? Does it change if they read at different paces?
3. The unread interval. Shut in a drawer for forty years: asleep, dead, or neither — and does it matter that nothing at all is happening *to* it?
4. Interpreter-dependence. The book's mental states exist only because a reader is doing the interpreting. Is that any different from how we attribute minds to brains on the basis of behavior, or is it the fatal difference?
5. Speed. Parsed by a machine in a millisecond versus read by a human over six hours — same experience played fast, or does rate change what it's like?
6. Skips and damage. A reader who skips a chapter, reads the footnotes out of order, or a copy with pages torn out — gaps in a life? Does a damaged copy have dementia?
7. Self-knowledge. The book can't be wrong about what it says; the sentence is right there on the page. If there's no room for self-deception or an unconscious, is that alone enough to disqualify it as a mind?
8. Whether any of this makes it something you could wrong.

What I want: tell me which of these I actually have to settle before I can write the ending, in order, and defend the order. The ending turns on a character deciding whether closing the book kills someone, so I'm weighting by (a) how many of the other questions hang off each one — upstream vs downstream — (b) how far I can get on it with armchair thinking alone, since I have no lab and zero intention of learning neuroscience, and (c) the cost of getting it wrong: does the novel become incoherent, or does it just become philosophically unfashionable, which I can live with. And tell me straight which ones I should consciously hand-wave. "Fudge these three, nail that one" is a much more useful answer to me than eight even-handed paragraphs.

My hunch, which I want attacked: #4 is the root and everything else is downstream of it, because if interpreter-dependence lands then the restart and simultaneity problems are just costumes it's wearing. But I suspect I believe that because it's the most fun one to write, not because it's the most load-bearing, and its fine to tell me I'm wrong. Also: if there's a question a sharp hostile reader could use to break the book that isn't on my list, add it and slot it into the ranking.

Give me a ranking of the four worth my twelve hours, a short line on what I should deliberately fudge and why, then finish with one last line in exactly this form so I can paste it into my notes:

Verdict: <the single question>
