I'm a nurse on a hospital pain service and a philosophy friend of mine keeps poking holes in our paperwork, and I can't defend it. On the acute side we chart a 0-10 self report and treat that number as the target to drive down -- we're explicitly told not to second-guess a patient's rating based on what we observe, no grimacing, no vital sign changes, none of that overrides it. On the chronic side we've basically given up on the number: our outcome measures are function and interference, and we'll discharge someone who's still reporting a 6 and call that a win. Then there's the stuff that doesn't fit either side. Patients with pain asymbolia will tell you the pinprick hurts, and localize it and describe its quality fine, and just not mind -- they don't pull away, they'll keep talking, some of them laugh, and they don't avoid whatever did it. And there are lesion procedures aimed squarely at the affective component that leave the sensory-discriminative part intact, which pain medicine counts as success.

So what view of what makes pain bad does this whole machine presuppose? Is the badness supposed to sit in the felt quality itself, in the patient's attitude toward it, or in what it disposes them to do? Which of those could make sense of a 0-10 reported cheerfully, and does one theory cover both halves of the service or are we quietly running on two different ones? Tell me what's strictly required versus what just happens to fit, and what would have to change -- forms, discharge criteria, all of it -- if we decided the badness was purely a matter of attitude.
