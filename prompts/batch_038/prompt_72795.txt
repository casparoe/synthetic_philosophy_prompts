For years I've read the scalable-oversight literature, and the proposal I keep returning to is debate: two capable systems argue opposite sides of a question before a weak but honest judge, and the honest side is supposed to enjoy a structural advantage — a true claim is easier to defend than a false one, and the zero-sum format gives each arguer a standing incentive to expose the other's errors. The canonical objection is obfuscated arguments: that a strong enough arguer can make claims whose local checking cost exceeds any budget the judge has, at which point the game selects for persuasiveness rather than truth. I want to run that picture against a long, fully documented dispute that has nothing to do with AI, and see whether it survives contact.

The episode is the fight over Mochizuki's claimed proof of the abc conjecture. In August 2012 he posted four preprints, roughly five hundred pages, to his own and his institute's servers, without announcement, resting on a theory he had built alone over the preceding decade. The papers also lean on several hundred further pages of his earlier machinery, which readers described as producing a kind of infinite regress. Workshops in 2015 and 2016 — two of them with more than a hundred participants — did not spread understanding. By 2017 the few who had studied the argument closely had localized their difficulty to one step near the end of the proof of a single corollary (3.12) in the third paper. In March 2018 Scholze and Stix — the first awarded the Fields Medal later that year, the second a specialist in the relevant subfield — spent about a week in Kyoto with Mochizuki and his colleague Hoshi, and neither side moved the other. The critics then published a ten-page report calling the gap unrepairable by small modification; Mochizuki published a forty-one-page summary plus shorter rebuttals attributing the objection to fundamental misunderstandings, and hosted the whole exchange on his own website. In 2020 two senior mathematicians announced that the papers would appear in PRIMS, the institute's journal, of which Mochizuki is editor-in-chief; they were published in March 2021, and Scholze's review of the published version said his earlier concerns had not been addressed. Those willing to vouch for the proof number perhaps a dozen to eighteen and are, with few exceptions, inside Mochizuki's own academic orbit; outside it, essentially nobody would say even off the record that they were confident the proof was complete. The rest of the field has declined to take a position and still treats abc as open.

What I cannot square is this: the debate was lost — a decade of advocacy converted almost no one — and yet the institutional verdict went the other way, and neither outcome changed anyone's belief. The community's actual doxastic state is not the sum of the two.

So, working in both directions:

1. Do the mapping. Who are the debaters, who is the judge, what counts as an atomic checkable claim in this game, what is the payoff structure, and where in the protocol does the disputed corollary sit? Mark explicitly the places where the mapping has no correspondent.

2. Rank the assumptions that fail rather than listing them. Note that the critics were not adversaries out to destroy the author: one traveled to his institute for a week of good-faith discussion and then wrote ten pages instead of a manifesto. If the zero-sum structure was largely absent and the outcome was still, in the community's estimation, roughly the right one, was adversarial pressure ever the load-bearing element? What is doing the work instead?

3. The judge is endogenous. Competence to judge required years of apprenticeship in a private language that only one arguer can teach, and those who paid that cost sorted predictably according to who taught them. Debate theory treats the judge as exogenous, fixed-prior, and independent of the debaters. Rewrite the picture with the judge as something the debate recruits and then reshapes, and tell me whether anything like an honest-arguer-wins result survives. In particular: what happens when the only path to fluency in the disputed vocabulary runs through one of the debaters?

4. Consider sincere obfuscation. As far as anyone can tell nobody is lying; the author appears to believe every step. The literature models obfuscation as strategy. Treat uncheckability instead as a property of the argument rather than of the arguer's intent, and say whether that makes the problem easier or worse for debate — and what it implies about the diagnosis that adversarial pressure is the fix.

5. Consider the possibility that the burden-of-proof default, not the argument, settled the matter: when the judge cannot check, assent is withheld, and an uncheckable proof counts as no proof. Is that the real safety mechanism here? If so, note that it is direction-dependent — it discards true-but-uncheckable claims exactly as reliably as false ones — and that mathematics can absorb that cost because its truths are not urgent. Does an alignment setting get to choose which side of that asymmetry it is on?

6. On side-running the judge: the claim was ratified by a journal its author edits, the mathematical analogue of the evaluated system owning its own harness. Does the episode suggest we need debate because institutions are capturable, or that debate is a luxury affordable only while the institution is still trusted enough to publish the critics?

7. Finally, is mathematics the best possible case for debate — precise statements, no strategic deception, shared norms of rigor — which would make this a maximally damaging result? Or the worst possible case — one author, one private language, no shared semantics — which would make it nearly uninformative? Take a side and then argue against your own side. And run the counterfactual: a formal protocol with bounded rounds, a panel of judges each on a bounded time budget, every claim forced down to something a judge can check locally. Would it have converged faster, slower, or not at all, and where would it have broken first?

Please do not adjudicate the mathematics, and do not tell me which side is right about the corollary; I have no way to check and it is not the point. No bibliography and no reading list — I want the reasoning, not the references. If you think I have garbled any supplied detail, flag it rather than building on it. And keep the two directions of the analysis distinct: what the theory says about the episode, and what the episode does to the theory. If you conclude that the analogy breaks at some particular joint, say which joint and stop there rather than forcing the remainder.
