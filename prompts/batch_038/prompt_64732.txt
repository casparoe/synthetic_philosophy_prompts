I'm drafting a chapter on the ethics of habitat loss, and I've come to think that the question everyone in this area argues about — does habitat destruction reduce wild animal suffering? — is malformed rather than merely hard. I'd like a careful explanation of why it bundles together several claims that need to be pulled apart, and what turns on pulling them apart.

For this purpose, please adopt the following three-way distinction. I coined these labels for the chapter, and they are not standard in the literature, so don't try to match them to a source:

- **Terminal harms**: the deaths and acute suffering of animals killed or displaced during the destruction event itself — starvation, exposure, predation during dispersal, collisions with vehicles and structures, and the stress of crowding into fragments that are already occupied.
- **Equilibrium effects**: the change in the expected welfare of a typical life in whatever state replaces the old one — higher or lower per-capita food and shelter, altered predation pressure, changed exposure to parasites, disease, and weather extremes, edge effects, and the delayed species losses that continue for some time after the habitat is gone.
- **Composition effects**: the change in which kinds of animals make up the population — specialists giving way to generalists, slow-reproducing species giving way to fast-reproducing ones — and so a change in how many lives are lived and how each of those lives is distributed over time.

Please explain the following:

1. Why these three can point in opposite directions within a single case, so that the answer to the question as posed depends on which of them the questioner has in mind, and why the temporal mismatch between them (hours, decades, generations) makes any single verdict on "habitat destruction" ambiguous about its own time horizon.

2. At exactly what point the analysis stops being ecological and starts turning on contested commitments in population ethics — specifically on whether total welfare or per-life welfare is the right thing to aggregate, and on whether harming animals that exist and preventing animals from ever existing are morally on a par. Set out the strongest version of the argument that this asymmetry makes habitat destruction bad in itself even for someone who believes most wild lives are net negative, and then identify where that argument is most vulnerable.

3. Why composition effects resist resolution in principle rather than for want of data: whether the shift from specialists to generalists improves or worsens the total picture appears to depend both on the welfare profiles of the species that gain and lose and on the aggregation rule being used, so that perfectly good ecology underdetermines the verdict.

Please write in American English and work at the level of mechanisms and argument structure. I am not looking for citations, reading lists, or quantitative estimates; a schematic case — say, a temperate woodland converted to row-crop agriculture — is enough to make the distinctions concrete.
