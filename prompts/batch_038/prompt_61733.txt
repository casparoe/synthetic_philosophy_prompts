In my philosophy-of-history seminar we spent two weeks on multi-causal explanation, and afterwards two of my classmates carried an argument out into the corridor and never finished it. Their case was Japan's surrender in August 1945. I could not tell who was winning, so I have written the exchange out as faithfully as I can.

Ishita: the bombs were not a cause of the surrender in any interesting sense; the Soviet declaration of war was. After Hiroshima the government spent three days verifying what had happened and debating, and did not surrender; it accepted the Potsdam terms only once the invasion of Manchuria was under way. An event that leaves the outcome untouched and is then followed by a change cannot be the cause of the change. Add to that that the leadership had been privately seeking Soviet mediation of a negotiated peace, so the Soviet attack destroyed the one policy they actually had, and that the emperor's rescript invoked the new weapon as his reason while saying nothing of the Soviet attack — the bomb's causal role is largely rhetorical, a story told to a public that could not be told the truth.

Noah: you have read sequence as causation. The bomb and the Soviet entry each closed a different door — one made continued resistance physically unendurable, the other removed the mediation that made endurance worth betting on. You cannot award the effect to the second door and deduct it from the first, when it was the first that made the second decisive.

Ishita: then neither is a cause. The only honest formulation is that the conjunction caused the surrender, and choosing one of its members is narrative emphasis wearing the clothes of explanation.

Noah: on that standard nothing ever causes anything, since every event sits inside an open-ended conjunction of background conditions. And the conjunction view does not favour you: a third weapon was already being assembled for use later that month, so one side's capacity to keep closing doors was renewable while the Soviet entry was a one-off.

Ishita: which is a claim about a future that never arrived — one untestable counterfactual substituted for another.

Noah: every causal claim in history is counterfactual; the alternative to a speculative counterfactual is not no counterfactual but a worse-specified one.

Please go through these six moves and say, for each, whether it holds up, holds up only partly, or fails, with a reason; then say who is closer to right overall and what both of them left out — including whether they are in fact disputing different explananda. I would like you to attach a level of confidence to each of your verdicts and single out the one you are least sure of and why.
