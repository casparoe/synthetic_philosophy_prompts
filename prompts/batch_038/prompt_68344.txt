Our lab has just been folded into an interdisciplinary project on the neural bases of animal welfare, and the piece that touches moral philosophy has landed on me. I am a fourth-year PhD student in comparative neuroanatomy, and my training is entirely quantitative: I am fluent in log–log regressions of brain mass on body mass across species, in the interpretation of residuals from those regressions as measures of relative or excess brain size, in encephalization quotients and the well-known arbitrariness in the choice of reference scaling exponent, in stereological estimates of total neuron number and of how those neurons are partitioned between the cerebellum and the pallium, in phylogenetically corrected comparative methods and the non-independence of species data points, and in the preservation artifacts and measurement error that plague museum-derived cranial volumes. What I do not have is any training in ethics. A philosopher collaborator has circulated a short set of claims about whether brain size, or neuron number, or neural complexity more generally, bears on the moral standing of a creature, and my PI has asked me to draft our response. I would rather not draft it in a vocabulary I only half follow, and I would rather not have it drafted at me in a vocabulary I cannot check.

So I want the debate she is gesturing at restated in my vocabulary, as faithfully as it can be. I think the positions in play include something like the following, but please correct me where I have garbled them: that moral status is grounded in sentience, that sentience comes in degrees, and that those degrees track neural capacity of some description; that what actually matters is not size but a set of capacities — associative learning, temporal integration, affective richness, something like self-awareness — for which size is at best a proxy measure with an unquantified reliability; that moral status is a threshold property rather than a scalar one, so that above the threshold there is nothing left to measure; that the capacities in question cannot be restricted to typical adult humans without also sweeping in certain non-human animals and certain humans who lack them; and that even granting degrees of capacity, degrees of capacity do not by themselves yield degrees of moral weight. Take these one at a time and give me their counterparts in the language of scaling exponents, residuals, cell counts, and phylogenetic correction. Where a position has no counterpart, say so plainly instead of manufacturing one to keep the table tidy.

Two constraints, and then the part I actually need. First, do not smuggle my framework's commitments in and afterwards present them as what the philosophers meant: if you find yourself treating moral status as a latent variable behind noisy behavioural indicators, or a threshold claim as a saturating dose–response curve, mark that as an interpretive choice you have made, not as a translation. Second, the residue report is the deliverable, not an appendix to it. For each position, tell me what carries over with its content intact, what carries over only at the cost of a distortion you should describe specifically, and what cannot be expressed at all in a vocabulary organized around covariation, allometry, and correction for shared ancestry. Then give me your judgement on the question I have to settle before I write a word: is that residue a limitation of my vocabulary — an inability, perhaps, to express normative relevance as distinct from statistical association, or to express a property that does not admit of degree — or is it evidence that some of these positions are less determinate than they sound, such that the disagreement thins out once one insists on saying what would count as a difference? Take a side where you can, and say what would settle it. Finally, run one item in the opposite direction: my PI holds that residual brain size, computed against a mammal-wide allometric baseline, is a defensible comparative index of neural investment. Give me the strongest objection to that confidence available from the philosopher's side, and make it an objection to the concept rather than to the statistics, which I am well equipped to handle myself. No bibliography or reading list, please — I want the arguments, and I have no way to verify either.
