I cover science and ideas for a national magazine, which in practice means I spend a good deal of my time watching contested research findings travel from journals into op-eds, panel discussions, and product marketing. I am currently reporting a longer piece on the boundary between science communication and commercial promotion, with a sidebar on persuasive techniques that readers should be able to spot, and there is one technique I keep encountering that I cannot name; my editor is already tired of me calling it "that thing where they argue against a fake objection." It runs roughly as follows. An advocate for a position — the head of research at a supplement company posting on the firm's own site, a climate policy analyst writing an op-ed, a start-up founder explaining why a new product is safe — volunteers, entirely unprompted, a paragraph of the form "critics will say X, but in fact Y." The X on offer is not a simplified version of the objection the advocate's actual critics are pressing. It is a different claim altogether, one that occupies the same slot in the argument while being much easier to defeat, and the rebuttal is clean and, so far as I can determine, factually accurate. The real objection goes unmentioned, and yet nobody in the exchange is obstructed from raising it.

What puzzles me most is what the technique buys. Following audiences afterward — in comment threads, at Q&As, in the letters I receive — I notice that their stated beliefs are often barely moved by the paragraph. What changes is their account of their own process. They report that they have already looked at both sides, or that the objection has already been dealt with, or that whatever else one thinks of the advocate, he was upfront about the strongest case against him, and they enter that as a mark in the advocate's favor. The paragraph seems to function less as an argument than as a credential the advocate issues to himself and the audience then holds on his behalf. I have also watched a related version in which a speaker, unasked, announces that the most powerful objection to his view is such-and-such, names something that is not the most powerful objection, refutes it, and is credited thereafter with candor by listeners who never learned what the most powerful objection actually was. Every sentence in these performances would survive a fact-check; nothing false is asserted and nothing is suppressed in the crude sense.

So: is there an established term or body of work for this, and if there is, what is it? The label is not really what I am after — the carving is — so tell me what the nearest concepts are and exactly how what I have described differs from each of them. I have considered and half-rejected several candidates already. Strawmanning, which as I understand it happens in the heat of an argument rather than in advance and at the advocate's own initiative. "Prebunking," the word the misinformation researchers I interview use, but which seems directed at protecting accurate beliefs against myths that are already circulating. Framing and choice architecture, which sound adjacent but describe something about presentation rather than about manufacturing a sense of completed inquiry. Gaslighting, which is plainly the wrong thing, since no one's trust in their own perception is under attack. Nudging, which I take to be intended benignly. If none of these is right and there is no settled name, coin one, define it tightly, and tell me where its edges lie. Handle the boundary cases explicitly: what happens when the objection planted in advance is one that no member of the audience would otherwise have entertained at all — does the technique then introduce the very objection it claims to be answering, and does that make it better or worse? How should the classification change when the volunteered objection is a genuinely weakened form of the real one rather than a substitute for it? And does the whole thing sit on the manipulation side of the line or the legitimate-persuasion side? It proceeds entirely through arguments, offered voluntarily, that the audience is free to check, and it may leave their beliefs untouched, so if it is manipulation I need to be told what the non-rational element is supposed to be, and whether the verdict differs when the target is the audience's belief rather than the audience's opinion of its own fairness.

Please write the reply in British English. I do not want a reading list or a set of citations for me to chase down; I want the conceptual map, and I would rather you be precise about distinctions than comprehensive about sources. Since I may end up quoting you in print, state how confident you are in what you claim, and single out the one point you are least sure of — flagging in particular any place where you are leaning on an empirical literature that is contested or thin, or reconstructing an argument rather than recalling a source you are certain about.
