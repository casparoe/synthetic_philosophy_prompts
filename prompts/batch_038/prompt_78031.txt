I am the elections and registry officer for the Kestrel Basin Commission, a body created three years ago by an interstate compact to manage a shared aquifer and the river it feeds. The Compact attaches a franchise to two of our instruments: a consultative basin-wide referendum every four years, whose results the Commission is obliged to answer formally, and two standing citizens' panels — one on withdrawal permits, one on allocation during drought — that are filled by sortition from the same roll. My office maintains that roll. The difficulty is that the basin is a hydrological unit while the affected population is not: some of our binding rules reach people well outside the boundary, and the boundary contains people whom our rules barely touch. We receive eight to twelve eligibility petitions a year, from individuals, from municipalities, and from companies; each determination is a public record, is cited as precedent in later petitions, and three have already been appealed to our general counsel. My staff is two people. I have a public policy background and no legal training, each written determination has to fit on two pages and be readable aloud at a public hearing, and anything departing from a prior determination needs the general counsel's signature.

Article 4 of the Compact was a political compromise and reads, in substance, as follows. Clause (a): persons ordinarily resident within the basin. Clause (b): persons subject to binding rules issued by the Commission. Clause (c): persons whose significant interests are materially affected by Commission decisions. Clause (4): an applicant qualifies by satisfying any two of the three, and no one may be excluded solely for failing the residence clause. Clause (5): no determination under this Article may be made by a vote of those already enrolled. I want you to turn this into a decision procedure my office can actually run, grounded in whatever the political theory of democratic boundaries has to say that is usable — the difference between being subject to rules and being affected by them, why those come apart, how much affectedness is enough to warrant a vote rather than mere consultation, and what becomes of the question when the people who would decide the boundary are themselves inside it. Please give me the questions in order, with a statement of what counts as a sufficient answer to each and what evidence discharges it; a rule for burden of proof and for provisional enrollment when an applicant cannot produce documentation; explicit instructions for handling legal entities, since several petitions come from corporations and agencies rather than natural persons, and for whether such a petition is routed to members, employees, or shareholders, or refused at the threshold; a short list of the points at which the procedure must stop and hand the question up, because what is at issue is a value judgment (what makes an interest significant, whether affectedness without subjection deserves a vote or a hearing, whether the roll can contain anyone not yet born) rather than a fact my office can establish; and a flag on every step that looks mechanical but is in truth a judgment call wearing a procedural costume. Include a two-page template for the written determination, and a rule for what to do when a new petition cannot be reconciled with an old one.

Then run the procedure on three petitions before us this quarter. First, roughly fourteen hundred seasonal farmworker households who live in employer-provided housing inside the basin from April to November, are fully subject to our well-siting and withdrawal rules, but are domiciled for tax and census purposes in another state; I expect your procedure to enroll them and I would like them enrolled, so if it does not, say so plainly rather than relaxing a test. Second, a hyperscale data center sited three kilometres outside the boundary that draws eleven million litres a day through an intake pipe inside the basin, is squarely subject to our withdrawal rules, and by any measure materially affects the aquifer, but is a legal person with no resident members. Third, a downstream coastal town sixty kilometres outside the basin whose shellfish beds have been damaged by nutrient loading in our permitted discharge, which claims affectedness and denies subjection. At least one of these three determinations should come out against a party I am sympathetic to and will have to defend at a hearing; I would rather the procedure be honest than comfortable. Finally, tell me what this procedure cannot do: where Clause (5) forecloses the obvious escape from the regress, why the roll's composition remains a political question after the tests have been run, and how the procedure will systematically misfire if used for anything beyond the two instruments it was written for. I do not want a reading list, a bibliography, or a survey of positions; I want something a colleague with no philosophy could pick up and apply on a Tuesday afternoon.
