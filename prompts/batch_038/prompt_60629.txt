I serve on the technical advisory panel for a county government that uses a risk score to triage which households receive an emergency assistance advance. Because the benefit is rationed, the score functions as a threshold rule, and the neighborhoods we serve differ substantially in their underlying rates of the hardship we are trying to predict. Last month a general-audience magazine ran a long feature on the mathematics of algorithmic fairness, and the panel chair has asked me to prepare a written response. Before I do, I need to know how much of the piece I can rely on. Here is the passage that has caused the most disagreement among my colleagues:

"In the mid-2010s a set of impossibility theorems proved something that ought to have ended the debate about biased algorithms once and for all. Any score that predicts well is calibrated: among the people assigned a given score, the fraction who go on to experience the outcome matches that score. Any score that treats groups equally has equal error rates: the same share of false alarms and the same share of misses in every group. The theorems show that whenever the groups differ in their underlying rates of the outcome — which in practice they always do — no score can be both. The exceptions the proofs allow are cases in which the score is perfectly accurate, which never happens, so the conflict always binds in the real world. That is also what resolved the famous dispute over the recidivism tool used in American courts: the investigative reporters had demonstrated that the tool was not calibrated, and the vendor's reply about error rates simply missed the point. The lesson is not that we need better algorithms. It is that fairness is not a mathematical property at all but a political preference, which means that oversight panels auditing these systems are performing a ritual with no fact of the matter behind it. If we want an escape route, though, there is one: stop drawing thresholds. Assign the scarce benefit by lottery, giving each applicant a probability of selection equal to their predicted risk. Because no cutoff is ever chosen, both criteria hold in expectation, and the impossibility evaporates."

My instinct is that the author gets the central conflict roughly right and then draws conclusions that do not follow, but I cannot locate precisely where the reasoning breaks, and two of my colleagues think the whole passage is sound. Please work through it claim by claim and tell me, for each: what a researcher working on this literature would accept without qualification; what is a defensible simplification that loses something important; and what is simply false. In particular I would like to know whether the passage is describing one result or conflating two distinct ones, since I have read that the incompatibility involving calibration and error rates is not the only such theorem and that the various results differ in exactly which conditions they pit against each other and in what the exceptional cases are. I would also like a straight answer on three points: whether the passage's account of the recidivism-tool controversy has the dispute running in the right direction; whether the lottery proposal actually escapes the trade-off or merely relocates it; and whether the move from these theorems to the conclusion that audits have no fact of the matter behind them is licensed by anything the theorems say.

Finally, tell me what survives of the passage once it is corrected. If the conflict is real but the author's diagnosis and remedy are not, what should I tell the panel about how to choose among these criteria for a rationed benefit like ours, and what would a careful researcher say the theorems do and do not settle?
