I sit on my university's institutional animal care and use committee, and I have been asked to draft the decision memo for Thursday's meeting, which covers two connected items.

The first is a protocol from comparative psychology. To study consolation-like behavior in rats, it repeatedly induces acute distress in a "demonstrator" animal and records whether a cagemate approaches and remains in contact. Analgesia would defeat the study, since the phenomenon under investigation is a response to unremitting distress. The investigators state openly that their aim is not biomedical: they want evidence that rodents register and respond to one another's suffering, and they describe the project as contributing to the case for taking animal welfare more seriously.

The second is a proposal to revise the committee's own charter. It obliges us to give "due weight to animal welfare," but its preamble grounds that obligation in the Kantian position that we owe animals nothing directly, and that the real duty at stake is guarding against the hardening of human sympathy that cruelty produces. The charter was drafted by a Kantian, and the committee has never resolved whether the operative clause and the preamble can both stand.

Members are split three ways. One faction says that if the preamble governs, the rats' distress counts only insofar as its infliction corrodes character, and that this protocol is unusual in tending to cultivate rather than corrode sympathy in those who run it — so approve. A second says that reasoning is self-defeating: it would license the very experiment whose stated purpose is to show that animal suffering matters — so reject. A third says the divergence between the two rationales is precisely what the charter revision should settle, and that we should decide the protocol only provisionally.

Please draft the memo I will circulate: a recommendation on the protocol — approve, reject, or approve with conditions, including any conditions and a timetable for re-review — and a recommendation on the charter wording, with reasons members could actually argue with. It should spell out what the indirect-duty rationale commits us to in cases where it and a direct-interests rationale come apart in both directions, treat the strongest of the three positions at its best rather than strawmanning it, and not rest on an appeal to regulatory requirements. Finally, please separate what is settled from what is genuinely contested among people who know this literature — about Kant's own view and about where the disagreement in animal ethics now lies — and do not let a contested reading pass for the consensus.
