**The line in the pain**

Most hedonistic utilitarians think pleasure and pain sit on a single scale, which means in principle any bad can be out-weighed by enough good. A rival camp disagrees. There is, they say, a threshhold of suffering, and above it no finite amount of mild pleasure compensates. Call it threshold negative utilitarianism. It's an attractive view: it explains why we won't trade one person being tortured for a trillion lifetimes of faint contentment, and it takes the sting out of several repugnant-conclusion style objections.

The standard worry is that the line looks arbitrary. Suffering comes in a continuum, so a sharp cutoff is unmotivated, and a vague one just hands you someone else's problem. There's also a technical worry: a coherent set of preferences over risky prospects that satisfies continuity can be represented by a single real-valued utility function, and genuinely lexical orderings aren't continuous. So the threshold theorist appears forced to give up continuity, which is a real cost.

I want to suggest the debate has been framed wrong. What the threshold theorist needs isn't lexicality but non-comparability. She isn't claiming that agony is infinitely worse than an itch — that would require a ratio scale, and pain measurement is at best ordinal. The 0-to-10 scales used in clinics rank reports; they don't measure how much more one report is than another. Her claim is that there's no fact of the matter about the exchange rate between the two domains at all. If that's right, the sorites worry dissolves: there's no cutoff to be vague about, because the view never asserted a cross-domain ordering in the first place.

But it buys something worse. Now the theorist must say that a world containing one episode of the worst suffering and a world containing one episode just barely below the line are on a par — not equal, not better, not worse. And parity interacts badly with transitivity. Walk down a chain of ever-milder episodes, each on a par with the one before, and "no worse than" propagates: the torture world ends up no worse than the faint-itch world. Call it the parity collapse. Escaping it means denying that "no worse than" is transitive across incommensurable pairs, which sounds like a bigger bullet than the one being dodged.

There's a second, more practical cost. In a world containing both widespread mild bliss and some real agony, the view can't say whether adding the bliss helps, because there's no exchange rate to compute with. So the theory goes silent exactly where policy needs it to speak — analgesia rationing, farm animal welfare, whether to fund pain relief or wellbeing enhancement. Which raises the question I'd end on: is the threshold view a genuine alternative to the single-scale view, or is it the single-scale view with one region where we've decided not to look?
