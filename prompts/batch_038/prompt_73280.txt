I co-direct a small forecasting-and-risk group, and for the better part of a year two of our senior members have been unable to agree on one sentence in a joint report: whether it is legitimate to assign a probability to a single, non-repeatable outcome — specifically, the probability that one particular drug candidate fails its one and only phase-III trial. Each believes the other's sentence is not well-formed rather than false. I would like you to draft the document a good-faith adversarial collaboration between them ought to produce.

To keep the exercise from collapsing into a survey, fix the two signatories as follows. The frequentist is a restrictive one in von Mises's line: probability is the limit of relative frequencies in an infinite collective satisfying both the convergence condition and randomness under every admissible place selection, and single-case attributions are pseudo-problems unless they can be cashed out in an explicitly named reference class. The subjectivist is a coherence theorist in de Finetti's line: probabilities are degrees of belief answerable to nothing but the avoidance of sure loss, there is no external probability for a belief to track — he denied flatly that probability exists as a feature of the world — and exchangeability is a substantive symmetry judgment an agent makes, not a metaphysical claim about limits. Both are fluent practitioners of the same calculus, both routinely report the same numbers, and both reject Popper-style propensities.

Please produce the document with the following sections, in this order:

1. **Shared commitments** — roughly twelve numbered items, one or two sentences each. These must be claims both parties would sign as written, not neutral platitudes: what they agree about the axioms and their interpretation, about finite relative frequencies as the only probabilistic evidence either has ever observed, about the representation theorem for exchangeable sequences and its asymptotic consequences for two agents with different priors, about proper scoring rules, about what the law of large numbers does and does not establish on its own, and about the drug-trial sentence in our report. Where an apparent agreement is in fact a joint opposition to propensity rather than a mutual concession, say so explicitly and mark the item.

2. **Residual disagreements** — a table with the columns: label / the frequentist's formulation in his own preferred terms / the subjectivist's formulation in her own preferred terms / verbal or substantive / if substantive, what would settle it. Each side's column must be wording that side would accept as its own view; no straw figures.

3. **Cruxes** — three or four, about a paragraph each. For every crux, state the precise sub-question such that settling it would move at least one party, the case or theorem or body of evidence both parties already accept as bearing on it, and each side's prediction recorded in advance of the answer. At least one crux should concern whether any finite evidence could ever separate two agents who agree on every frequency and on every betting price.

4. **Diagnosis** — about two hundred words. How much of this is a disagreement about what the world contains, how much is about the admissible use of a term in a report, and how much is structurally unresolvable because the two positions are empirically equivalent everywhere it matters.

5. **The sentence we can publish** — about one hundred words. The actual wording for the drug-trial probability that both would sign, followed by a footnote-length note on what each of them thinks that wording commits them to and what each thinks it leaves out.

Do not survey the interpretations of probability, do not propose a third position they should adopt, and do not soften the cruxes into claims that cannot fail. The drafting is the point.
