I'm a retired experimental physicist — thirty-five years of low-temperature stuff — and I've fallen down a philosophy-of-math rabbit hole since then. What's got me is an idea I keep chewing on: proof gives you certainty, but the same deductive machinery also gets used for uncertain, revisable estimates, and a big ingredient there is a default rule — absent any specific information about how X and Y correlate, just take E[XY] = E[X]E[Y]. Call it the presumption of independence. Number theorists do it constantly: treat "n is prime" and "n+2 is prime" as independent with the right marginal rates, add the usual correction for shared small primes, and you land on Hardy–Littlewood-style predictions. The proposal I've been reading is to formalize this with a "heuristic estimator" — something that takes partial information about a mathematical object and returns an expected value, subject to coherence conditions — but apparently no candidate satisfies all the intuitive conditions, and the open problem is whether you can capture the legitimate heuristic arguments without also licensing spurious ones. The people pushing it come out of AI safety and think a solution would eventually help with things like spotting deceptive reasoning in a trained model.

Say I could bankroll a tiny group: two people, two years, one logician and one philosopher, plus me as the anoying amateur. Which of these should they chase first, and which should I tell them to drop?

1. a no-go theorem showing the intuitive coherence conditions can't all hold, so people stop hunting a unicorn
2. mapping the trade-off frontier by deliberately weakening conditions
3. building the estimator out of computational notions — bounded reasoning, pseudorandomness, no small circuit detecting the correlation
4. skipping generality and stress-testing on concrete cases, real number-theory heuristics versus spurious ones
5. reframing it as epistemology — objective Bayesianism, exchangeability, imprecise probability — rather than a math object

Defend the ordering with explicit criteria: how much other work depends on it, whether two people can actually do it in two years, and whether succeeding would change how anyone argues.
