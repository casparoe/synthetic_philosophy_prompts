I am preparing a case for my graduate seminar in the philosophy of art, and I want the case built out of an institution's own paperwork rather than out of a treatise. Let me describe the practice as concretely as I can, since I want the analysis to bite on details rather than on slogans.

A national arts council awards project grants to individual artists and to small companies. Applications are scored against a published rubric of one hundred points, distributed as follows.

- **Reach and participation (30 points).** The projected number of participants drawn from communities the council designates as under-represented in the arts. Points scale both with numbers and with the depth of involvement: attending scores lowest, co-making scores highest.
- **Evidence of emotional effect (25 points).** The application must contain an evaluation plan using a short self-report instrument administered to audience members before and after the event, with items along the lines of "I felt moved," "I felt connected to the other people present," and "This has changed how I see something." Points are awarded for the rigor of the instrument and for the ambition of the projected change, never for any particular feeling.
- **Artistic innovation (20 points).** The degree to which the proposed work departs from the conventions of the form in which the applicant works. The guidance notes define innovation as formal novelty, and reviewers are instructed that accomplished work in a settled idiom scores low here regardless of its quality.
- **Delivery capacity and risk management (15 points).**
- **Legacy and documentation (10 points).** What survives the project: an archive, a training method, a dataset.

Two further features of the arrangement matter more than they appear to. First, applicants are directed not to discuss their own intentions or their own felt experience except insofar as these bear on the delivery plan; the guidance states that an artist's testimony about what a work means is inadmissible as evidence of what the work does. Second, the very same council administers a national prize for artistic achievement, awarded by a panel of practitioners who judge the submitted works alone and anonymously, with no access to audience data, participation figures, or evaluation reports. The council's statutes declare that the prize has no bearing whatsoever on funding decisions, and the two committees share no members.

With that practice in view, I should like a paper that addresses the following, in roughly this order.

**1. Reconstruct the theory of art the rubric presupposes** — not what its drafters would say if interviewed, but what the scoring structure commits them to whether or not they notice it. What must art be, and what must its value consist in, for it to make sense to allocate points in these proportions? In particular: does the second criterion presuppose that a work is a cause of measurable psychological states in recipients, or something weaker? Does it presuppose that feelings are comparable across persons and across projects on a single scale, and that the feeling in question is indifferent as to content, so that any documented change counts equally? Does the innovation criterion presuppose that departure from a shared idiom is what separates art from craft — and if so, what does that imply about why the audience's response is worth measuring at all? What does the exclusion of the artist's own testimony presuppose about where meaning lives?

**2. Set that reconstruction against Tolstoy's account in *What Is Art?*** — art as the deliberate communication, by external signs, of a feeling the artist has himself experienced; the degree of infection as the measure of a work's power, conditioned by the individuality of the feeling transmitted, the clarity of its transmission, and above all the artist's sincerity; and the value of the infection further governed by the content of the feeling, since only feelings that unite people are good. My suspicion, which I want tested rather than flattered, is that the rubric looks Tolstoyan at the second criterion and is in fact his opposite. Explain precisely where the resemblance holds and where it collapses. Consider at least these pressure points: Tolstoy's sincerity condition demands access to the artist's own felt experience, which the rubric declares inadmissible; his individuality condition concerns the particularity of a feeling, not novelty of form, and he treats obscurity, borrowing, and contrived effect as marks of bad art, whereas formal novelty of the kind the council rewards plausibly reduces the intelligibility on which infection depends; his judgment turns on what is transmitted, while the council's instrument is content-neutral by design and pays for ambition irrespective of direction; and his ideal of universal art — work accessible to the untrained laborer — is not obviously the same thing as reaching designated communities with documented participation.

**3. Distinguish what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits comfortably.** Is the council committed to a causal-psychological account of artistic value, or only to an administrative need for indicators that survive audit, with the philosophy optional? Be careful with the familiar defense that the numbers are mere proxies for goods that are not really numerical: does that defense survive the fact that points, and not the underlying goods, are what allocate the money? And where is the practice genuinely underdetermined — where could a formalist, an institutional theorist of art, a pragmatist about value, and even a Tolstoyan each recognize part of their own view in it?

**4. Assess the two institutions together.** Given that one body funds both the rubric and the prize and insists that they have no bearing on one another, is that insistence a candid acknowledgment of incommensurable standards, or a refusal to hold any theory of art at all? What does the arrangement presuppose about whether "art" names a single kind of thing with a single kind of value? Is it stable, or does one of the two tend to corrode the other over time — and in which direction?

**5. Run the counterfactual.** If the council were persuaded to adopt Tolstoy's account whole, what would have to change — not only the criteria but the architecture: who evaluates, whether artists' testimony is admitted, whether the content of the feeling is scored, whether professional artists are funded at all, whether reach would be replaced by a demand for unaided intelligibility? Then tell me which of those changes a contemporary council would find hardest to accept, and what that difficulty reveals about what the present rubric actually values.

A few requirements of form. Write in American English, in polished academic prose, at a length of roughly two thousand words. Argue rather than survey; I am not looking for a doxography of aesthetic theories but for the implicit theory of this one scoring sheet, extracted and judged. You may take my familiarity with Tolstoy's text for granted, so please do not supply a bibliography, a reading list, or page references — analysis, not citation. And do not end in equipoise: state plainly which account of art the rubric best fits, and whether Tolstoy's partial presence in it is a resource the council could develop or a confusion it should eliminate.
