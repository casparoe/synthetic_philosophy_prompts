I run a small seminar on chance and identity, and the Everett week always goes the same way: I explain the branching, everybody nods, and then somebody asks the one question that ends the class — if all the outcomes happen, what is the number actually for? I've never found a piece of fiction that sits inside that question instead of talking about it, so I want to commission one from you to hand out.

Here's the setup I'd like you to use, because it's specific enough that you can't just write a generic many-worlds story. Set it in a near-future city where Everett has been settled public fact for a generation and the municipal observatories log decoherence events the way a weather service logs storms. There's an insurance firm that writes branch-indexed contracts: you pay a premium once, and the payout lands in some branches and not others, according to the squared amplitude of each. The whole pricing book rests on that one number. Nobody at the firm can say what it's *for*. Every branch happens. Nobody's expected wealth is reduced, exactly — some successor of the customer gets paid and some successor doesn't, and both are equally real. So what is the customer buying, and what is the firm risking?

Your protagonist is an auditor or claims adjuster there who pulls an old contract out of the archive and finds it was priced by counting branches instead of weighting them: flat payout in each of a very large number of very low-amplitude branches. On the counting view it looks like a steal for the customer and a rounding error of exposure for the firm; on the weighting view it's catastrophic. And here's the part I really want you to build the plot around, because it's the bit that makes the counting view look broken rather than merely different: the counting price changes depending on which basis the observatory happens to log the split in, whereas the weighted price doesn't budge. The protagonist realises the firm has been selling one of these two things for thirty years and cannot tell which.

Work in, as scenes rather than as speeches: someone who insists there's nothing to be uncertain about and is told that's exactly the point; a consultant who claims that any rational agent who cares about their successors and satisfies a few modest axioms is *forced* to price by squared amplitude, plus a colleague who says the axioms were picked to get there, and specifically that the assumption about indifference to extra branches already contains the answer; someone who argues that in the interval between a measurement and looking at the result you really are uncertain which branch you're on, and that this rescues probability — plus the objection that this just relabels the weight as a credence without earning it; and one human stake, small and concrete, where a particular branch is one where somebody loses something they care about.

Constraints: 1,800 to 2,500 words. No whiteboard scenes, no character explaining many-worlds to a layperson, no Alice and Bob, no epigraphs, and don't put words in the mouths of Everett or DeWitt or anyone else real — if a real argument appears, let your own characters have it in their own words. Keep the physics broadly right; you can invent whatever observatory technology you need. And don't resolve the problem. The story should end with the protagonist still not knowing what the number means but having narrowed the field, and it should feel like the not-knowing is the honest outcome rather than a cop-out.
