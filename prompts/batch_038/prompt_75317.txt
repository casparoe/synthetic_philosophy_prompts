I'm a philosophy PhD student in bioethics co-teaching a session on the therapy/enhancement distinction, and instead of lecturing I want to hand out a dialogue built around a case I made up that I think breaks the standard moves. Here's the case:

A national insurer is deciding whether to cover "vigilan," a drug that modestly but reliably improves sustained attention in healthy adults, with no meaningful side effects and no dependence. It's already covered for diagnosed attention disorders. The proposal is to cover it for night-shift air traffic controllers and long-haul drivers during the circadian nadir, roughly 3-5am. Two things make it awkward: (i) their attention at 4am isn't below any statistical species norm — it's textbook normal human physiology, so on a Boorse-style account this is enhancement and not treatment; (ii) in trials, people on vigilan consolidated noticeably fewer memories of their shifts, so the drug may make them safer workers while making their own lives slightly worse.

Write the dialogue between the two advisers the board has asked for. One thinks the therapy/enhancement line is real and normatively load-bearing — but has to actually say where the baseline sits and can't just repeat "normal species function" and stop. The other thinks the line is at best a budgeting device dressed up as a moral distinction, and that what matters is whether an intervention expands the person's opportunities, or their own good all-in. Both should be at their best, no strawmen, and the memory wrinkle should force each of them to say something they hadn't planned to say.

I want genuine back-and-forth: they should hit each other's actual moves, including the baseline problem, adapted preferences, the rat-race worry if only some drivers take it and everyone else loses shifts, and whether a diagnosis invented to fit the job — shift-work sleep disorder — is just enhancement laundering itself as therapy. Also, don't let it end in a polite draw or a both-sides-are-right shrug: they should land somewhere definite, with one of them conceding a specific point and saying which. Then give me two or three sentences on who you think actually won and why — I want your verdict, not a summary of the options.
