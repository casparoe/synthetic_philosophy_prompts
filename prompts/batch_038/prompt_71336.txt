I teach a second-year ethics seminar of fourteen students, one ninety-minute session, in a room with movable tables and a whiteboard; my budget for materials is essentially a printer and a bag of poker chips. They have all encountered the trolley problem in high school and are thoroughly inoculated against it — the moment a scenario smells like a thought experiment they start hunting for the intended answer instead of making a decision. What I want them to leave the room having seen, rather than having been told, is that the question whether an agent can be under two obligations that cannot both be discharged is not settled by observing that one course of action is better on the whole. Two features in particular should surface from what they do: first, that the claim they did not act on appears to keep making demands of them after the choice has been made and is, by their own lights, justified; and second, that their own deliberation never yields the clean, unqualified verdict they expected to be able to rest in, even once they are confident about what to do.

Please design a staged deliberation with roles and real (small) stakes that produces this out of its own rules rather than out of a described scenario. The philosophical vocabulary — dilemmas, residual duties, anything from the reading — should stay out of the room until the debrief, and no framing lecture should precede the activity. Two structural requirements matter most to me. The obvious escape route, ranking the competing claims and simply doing the better-ranked thing, has to be unavailable to them in the moment, not merely discouraged: I would like the format of the task, the composition of the group, or the rules of what counts as a legitimate outcome to make a single all-things-considered ranking impossible or unusable for them, so that the conflict is something they run into while trying to do their job. And after the decision has been made and defended, something has to happen that leads the participants themselves to treat the unchosen claim as still in force, with a cost they actually pay in a later round of the same activity — the residue should be discovered, not announced. Alongside the concept, give me the full rules and role briefs, the materials list, a minute-by-minute timeline for ninety minutes, an accounting scheme for the stakes that does not require real money, and a debrief that maps particular moments from the run onto the structure of the argument (including where each familiar way of blocking the conclusion could be resisted and what resisting it costs).

I would also like three practical notes. Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this — the role that gets played too enthusiastically, the point where the room stalls, the student who sees through the mechanism early and says so — and what to do about each. Tell me what to say to the student who, in the debrief, shrugs and insists that all they experienced was a hard choice between two options rather than anything deserving a special name; I would like the activity itself to have planted something that answers this, but I also want the verbal fallback ready. And be frank about which feature of the target idea the activity cannot reproduce no matter how it is run, so that I know exactly what I will have to supply by argument afterwards. Finally, say whether the design survives being run with twenty-two students instead of fourteen, or whether it needs to be rebuilt at that size.
