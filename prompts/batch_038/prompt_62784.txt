Consider a proposal for rescuing the analytic–synthetic distinction that bypasses synonymy, meaning postulates, and semantic rules altogether, and instead defines analyticity in terms of revision cost: a sentence is analytic for an agent just in case the agent would retain it under any body of evidence whatsoever, so that abandoning it carries infinite cost, whereas every genuinely empirical sentence carries only a very large but finite cost. Explain the conceptual difficulty this proposal runs into, on both sides. On the first side, Quine's holism is itself a doctrine of degree: he allows that some sentences are far more central than others, that we will make drastic adjustments elsewhere in the system to preserve them, and even that revisions of logic have been seriously proposed for the sake of overall simplicity. So does the revision-cost account mark a boundary that Quine's picture lacks, or does it merely relabel the gradient he was already committed to — and if the latter, what exactly does the word analytic still buy us? On the second side, the account's crucial term is a counterfactual quantified over all possible bodies of evidence, and it is unclear what could make such a counterfactual true of a sentence other than a fact about the sentence's meaning, which is precisely what the proposal was designed to avoid appealing to. Assess whether these two horns are equally damaging, or whether one of them admits of a repair that leaves the proposal recognizably a defense of analyticity rather than a concession to Quine. Finally, say whether the proposal fares any better for sentences containing law-cluster concepts — terms whose content is fixed by a loose cluster of laws, no one of which is individually constitutive — than for sentences of the stock bachelor sort, and what that asymmetry reveals about where the real disagreement with Quine lies.
