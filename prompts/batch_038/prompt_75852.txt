I've spent two years defending a line about escalation of commitment that a behavioral economist colleague insists is a case of a philosopher falling in love with a formalism, and I'm now at the point of writing it up for a decision-theory audience, so I want to know exactly where it breaks before someone else tells me. The line starts from the dollar auction: an auctioneer sells a dollar bill, the highest bidder wins it, and the second-highest bidder loses whatever they bid with nothing to show for it. Everyone knows the textbook moral, which is that bidding marches past a dollar and both players end worse off than if neither had entered. My claim is that this moral has been drawn in the wrong place. Analyzed rather than narrated, the game has a mixed-strategy equilibrium in which the player who is currently behind is genuinely indifferent between raising and dropping out, and in which — for the standard version with a five-cent minimum increment against a one-dollar prize — the probability of dropping out at each round is five percent regardless of how high the bidding has already climbed. Escalation therefore occurs along the equilibrium path. It is what correct play looks like, not a failure of correct play, and the pathology belongs to the setup rather than to the players. I then extend the point beyond the parlor game. Whenever someone produces a real-world escalation — the failing division that keeps getting funded quarter after quarter, the lawsuit pursued well past the point where settlement was cheaper, the military commitment maintained after its original objective has evaporated — I hold that one of two things is going on. Either the situation is structurally uncapped in the relevant sense, so that persistence is not a mistake about the game but the game itself; or there are audience effects and reputational payoffs that make persisting instrumentally sensible for the individual agent even when it is ruinous for the organization footing the bill. On this account, what is called the sunk-cost fallacy is not a psychological mechanism at all. It is the name we give to cases whose structure we have not yet bothered to describe.

So tell me what would count against it. I want the observations, experimental results, and arguments that should move me, sorted by how decisive each would be, and with the ones that would falsify the core claim kept strictly separate from the ones that would merely force me to narrow it. Be specific about the shape of the evidence rather than gesturing at intuitions: I care above all about settings my two escape routes cannot absorb, where the endpoint is finite and commonly known to be finite, where there is no audience and no prospect of future interaction with the other party, and where the agent's own prior involvement is varied while the payoff structure is held exactly fixed. Note that my own analysis commits me to a testable prediction — indifference at each round should be invariant to the amount already sunk, since on the equilibrium account that amount is irrelevant — so a robust finding that escalation scales with the sunk amount rather than with the remaining gap to the prize would be something I could not redescribe away. Tell me how strong that evidence actually is, including whether the large bidding datasets from online penny auctions, where the rules are close to the textbook game and every bid is logged, support it or undercut it. I have also been pressing myself on the familiar result that people pour more money into a failing venture when they were personally responsible for the decision that started it, which looks like psychology with no structural counterpart whatsoever; say whether that is fatal for me or whether there is a redescription I have missed.

Then tell me what the position risks. What does it predict that the standard bias-based accounts do not, and what would a discriminating test between them look like — and if it turns out to predict nothing distinctive, say so plainly, since that is itself a verdict. I want an honest assessment of the insulation problem in particular: my two escape routes have the air of universal solvents, capable of dissolving any counterexample by reclassifying it as either an uncapped game or a hidden signaling game, and I would like to know whether the view has in fact been qualified into unfalsifiability, whether that is a genuine defect for a claim of this kind, or whether it is a legitimate idealization of the sort rational choice theory depends on everywhere else. Finally, separate what a committed defender should concede from what they should refuse to grant. I suspect I will have to give up the strong claim that there is no distinctive psychology of escalation; what I want to know is whether a weaker version survives — that escalation is rational-but-ill-posed within a well-characterized subclass of situations and genuinely biased outside it — and what it would cost me, argumentatively, to retreat there. For what it is worth, the thing most likely to move me is not another story about generals or chief executives, but a demonstration that agents facing identical information and identical payoffs escalate differentially in a way that tracks neither the structure of the game nor the presence of an audience.
