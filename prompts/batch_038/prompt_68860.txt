I work in comparative development, and I have come to accept a broadly Weberian position: that the religious and moral values a population holds can be an independent cause of its economic trajectory, not merely a reflection of its material conditions. I want a map of what would have to be true for that position to be false — not a summary of the standard debate over the thesis.

Please do the following.

First, rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, and in doing so keep apart three different targets that are usually run together: the general claim that values can cause economic outcomes, Weber's particular causal story (predestination and assurance-seeking, inner-worldly asceticism, the calling, systematic reinvestment), and the historical claim about where and when capitalism arose. Say which findings would kill one of these while leaving the others standing, and state what would have to be true for a values-based explanation to be doing causal work that a claim about material interests alone could not do.

Second, distinguish the objections a committed Weberian would concede as genuinely damaging from the ones they would wave off, and say why in each case.

Third, address the insulation worry head-on. If the position can always retreat to elective affinity and to being one contributing cause among many, does it risk anything? Is that an acceptable posture for a historical causal claim, or has the thesis been qualified past the point of falsity — and would you tell me if it had?

Then give me your verdict on three findings, classifying each as a refutation, a competing explanation, or a mechanism claim that leaves the thesis intact:

- a county-level study in a European state finds that Protestant share predicts firm formation, but the association vanishes entirely once school enrollment and literacy are controlled for;
- the same package of frugality, reinvestment, and formal record-keeping appears among ascetic merchant communities that had no contact with the Reformation;
- archival work shows that the merchants whose devotional writings the argument leans on were already prosperous before their conversions, and that their piety deepened after commercial success rather than before it.

Finally: what does the position predict that a purely materialist rival does not? I want one discriminating pattern I could look for in data, and an honest statement of how likely it is to turn up.
