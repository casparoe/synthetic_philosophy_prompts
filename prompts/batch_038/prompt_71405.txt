My roommate has this tic: whenever someone's stuck she grades their move as either JOOTSing — jumping out of the system — or not, and she's been doing it all week to people attempting the MU-puzzle from Gödel, Escher, Bach (the MIU thing: you start with MI, and you may append a U to any string ending in I, double everything after the M, swap III for a U, or delete a UU; the question is whether you can ever reach MU).

Her verdicts so far:

- guy writes a program that enumerates every derivable string and greps the output for MU: not a jump. "He automated the inside. He never left."
- guy proves that the number of I's in any theorem is never a multiple of three, so MU, which has zero, is unreachable: jump. "He stopped pushing strings around and started saying things about the whole pile of strings at once."
- guy, told MU is underivable, just decrees MU an axiom: not a jump.
- guy adds a rule that any I may be rewritten as a U, so MI turns into MU in one move, and can't say why he picked that patch then any other: not a jump. "He moved the walls and called it leaving the room."
- guy says the puzzle shows human minds aren't formal systems, so he's outside all of this: not a jump. "Announcing that you're outside isn't a way of getting outside."
- Gödel numbering, arithmetic acquiring a way to talk about its own formulas: jump, "but he lands in a new system with its own ceiling, so nobody ever actually gets out."

Here's the one she hasn't ruled on yet and I want your prediction. A classmate doesn't find the three's invariant. What he finds is that doubling can only ever hand you a count of I's that isn't a multiple of three, so the III-into-U rule can never burn a string down to nothing — and then he edits the doubling rule so it can triple as well as double, and gets MI → MIII → MU in two moves. His line afterwards: "the puzzle was never about MU. It was about noticing you'd been handed a rule set and were treating it like weather."

So, what does she say? I can see it going both ways and I want the readings laid out rather than just an answer. One reading says any hand on the rules disqualifies you, which puts him with the patch-the-wall guy. Another says what matters is whether you reasoned about the system instead of inside it, which puts him with the invariant guy, since his diagnosis is exactly that kind of move even though he never states it as a theorem and never reaches her "nobody gets out" point. There's maybe a third reading lurking in that Gödel-numbering comment, something about whether a jump has to buy you a lesson that transfers past the one puzzle you're standing in. Give me the full set of rules consistent with all six of her verdicts, say what each predicts about the classmate, then commit to the one you think she's actually running and explain why hers comes out that way instead of the others. And last: what case should I bring her next to split the survivors? I want something where two of the live rules point in opposite directions, with a note about which is which.
