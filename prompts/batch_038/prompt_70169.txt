I finished one of those big popular histories of the July crisis last week, and the afterword turns into a short essay about hindsight, and now I'm arguing about it with my brother, who read Taleb in college and won't shut up about it. Here's roughly what the afterword says — I'm paraphrasing closely, its maybe 300 words in the book and I've squeezed it down:

"Hindsight bias and what Taleb calls the narrative fallacy are two names for one thing, and psychologists use them more or less interchangeably. The effect was demonstrated back in the 1970s with a study built around an American president's foreign trip: people estimated the odds of various outcomes in advance, and afterwards a lot of them recalled their own estimates as much closer to what actually happened than they had been. That detail is the important one, because it shows this isn't a memory glitch at all — it's the mind converting 'it happened' into 'it had to happen.' Since the process runs below awareness, no amount of methodological self-discipline or transparent sourcing scrubs it out; the only real defense is to write your predictions down before the event, which is why forecasters beat commentators. And that means history cannot, strictly speaking, be an explanatory discipline. The historian always knows how the story ends, so any account she gives of why it ended that way is a narrative built backwards from the destination. The nearest thing to a control group the past can offer is the counterfactual, the historian's what-if, which is why the most honest recent histories spend nearly as much time on what didn't happen as on what did."

I half agree with this, and half of me thinks it's slippery in a way I can't put my finger on. Can you go through it claim by claim and tell me what's solid, what's a fair simplification, and what's actually wrong? I'm hung up on three things in particular. First, the interchangeability: are hindsight bias and the narrative fallacy really the same phenomenon, or are they carving out different parts of the same mess? Second, the paragraph jumps from a finding about people's recollection of their own estimates to a claim about inevitability — does that jump work, and are "it happened," "it could have been foreseen," and "it had to happen" three different claims that the author is sliding between without noticing? Third, the one my brother and I actually fought about: even granting everything above, does "the historian knows the ending" really show that history can't explain? I have a vague intuition that knowing the outcome might sometimes be an asset for explanation rather than a handicap — you can trace the process that actually led there instead of betting on which of fifty possible futures was coming — but I can't say whether that's right or just me being stubborn. Two smaller ones if you have room: does the write-it-down-in-advance remedy even make sense for a discipline whose events are already over, and is the counterfactual-as-control-group line something historians and philosophers of history would actually sign up to, or is it the author's slogan? Finally, it'd help to hear how two different readers would judge the passage — a psychologist who works on judgment and decision making, and a working historian — and where those two would end up disagreeing with each other rather than with the book.
