I am writing the assessed essay for a final-year module that pairs moral philosophy with the criminal law of consent, and I have reached a point where the reading has stopped helping. Over the last few weeks I have been recording my own verdicts on cases, in the hope that a principle would fall out of them. It has not. I would like you to try to extract one — or, if none exists, to tell me precisely which of my verdicts has to go.

My starting position, which I now think is unstable, is a two-clause test. Consent is valid only if: **(i) Disclosure** — the agent was neither ignorant of nor mistaken about any fact that she herself would treat as decisive; and **(ii) Room** — the agent had at least one alternative to consenting that is acceptable, in the sense that the loss it involves is not one the proposer is entitled to impose on her. Clause (i) looks as though it makes validity hostage to each agent's idiosyncratic standards. Clause (ii) looks as though it condemns ordinary hard bargains, since losing one's flat is not something an employer is entitled to impose, even though in the case below he imposes nothing at all. I am aware of the baseline problem for telling threats from offers, and of the law's distinction between deception as to the nature or purpose of an act and deception as to collateral matters; neither seems to sort the cases below, and I do not want an essay that simply recites either.

Here are the cases and my verdicts.

1. A surgeon tells a patient that a transplant has a 90% success rate when it is really 60%, because he is sure she will refuse if she knows, and because refusal means she dies. She consents. — **Invalid.**
2. The same surgeon, the same patient, the same operation: he states the risks accurately but never mentions that declining and managing conservatively is a recognised option, because he is sure she would take it and do worse. She consents. — **Valid**, though he behaved badly.
3. A supervisor tells a doctoral student that he will write her references so that she gets nothing, unless she sleeps with him. She agrees. — **Invalid.**
4. A different supervisor never mentions references, but over months makes it plain that the students who become close to him end up with posts, and invites one of them to his flat for wine with that understanding in the air. She goes, and afterwards insists she chose freely and would do it again. — **Invalid**, though I cannot say why without making 5 coercive as well.
5. A firm offers a graduate an ordinary salary for a job that will consume three years of her life. She has no other offer and her rent falls due in a fortnight. She signs. — **Valid.**
6. A pharmaceutical company recruits a healthy volunteer in a low-income country for a Phase I trial carrying a small risk of permanent liver damage, and pays her twenty times the local monthly wage. — **Valid**; indeed, paying her less would wrong her more.
7. The same trial, unpaid. She volunteers because the clinic running it is the only one that will treat her child's asthma, and the coordinators have let it be known that participants' families move up the waiting list. — **Invalid**, although the only difference from 6 is that the inducement is in kind rather than in cash.
8. Two students, both very drunk, have sex. She is enthusiastic at the time and says next morning that she would never have agreed sober, but does not feel wronged. — **Invalid**, and I am uneasy about it, since I do not know whose consent is supposed to count.
9. An 82-year-old with early dementia sells her house to her nephew for half its market value. She is lucid on the day, passes a capacity screening, and says she would rather he had it than the state. — **Valid.**
10. A 34-year-old with treatable depression, articulate and fully informed about the facts, refuses dialysis and will die within weeks. — **His refusal should not be honoured**; we should treat him.
11. A woman raised in a closed community, never told that she may decline, agrees at nineteen to marry a man her elders have chosen. She is content with the arrangement and turns down an offer of help in leaving. — **Valid**; I do not want a principle that reduces her own account of her life to a symptom.
12. She consents to sex on the express condition that a condom is used throughout; he removes it part-way through without telling her. — **Invalid**, and more seriously so than 13.
13. A man at a bar describes himself as a single architect. He is married and drives a taxi. She sleeps with him, learns the truth later, and feels violated. — **He wrongs her, but her consent was valid.**
14. A man with a treatable but easily transmitted infection says nothing about it and uses no protection. She consents to sex she would have refused had she known. — **Invalid.**

The pairs I cannot reconcile are 1 and 2; 4 and 5; 6 and 7; 9 and 10; 10 and 11; and 13 and 14. In each pair the cases look structurally alike to me and yet I am confident that the verdicts differ, which suggests my test is missing a distinction rather than merely misapplied.
