Last night a friend and I spent two hours on superrationality and got nowhere, which is roughly what has happened to everyone who has taken up the question since Hofstadter's columns in the early 1980s. I would like a diagnosis of the impasse rather than another retelling of it.

The positions, as concretely as I can state them. His side: in a one-shot prisoner's dilemma, a dominance argument shows that defecting is better however the other player acts, so any principle recommending cooperation on the ground that the other player is reasoning as I am confuses correlation with control — magical thinking dressed as game theory; and to the extent that it is not magical thinking, it collapses into evidential decision theory applied to a symmetric game, which makes it a label rather than a discovery. My side: the dominance objection begs the question, because it assumes from the outset that only causal dependence is decision-relevant. The superrationalist does not claim to influence the other player, but claims that some joint epistemic states are inadmissible when the players are relevantly alike and each knows that each knows it. And the terrain looks different in the Platonia dilemma, where a trillionaire tells twenty people that if one and only one of them sends a telegram by noon that sender wins a billion dollars: there, dominance reasoning leaves every player with no usable prescription, while symmetry plus randomizing at one in twenty appears to do genuine work. Between the two of us stand at least two further camps — one holding that the whole thing is a disguised empirical claim about how similar actual reasoners are, another that it rests on a metaphysical claim about agents being copies of one another.

So: what kind of disagreement is this? Is it substantive and normative, or largely verbal — and if verbal, over which term, and where do the two readings of that term come apart in a way that could be checked? Is the real crux about which epistemic states are admissible at the point of decision, about what a reason has to be connected to in order to bear on action, or about a hidden empirical premise concerning transparency among reasoners? Are the camps answering different questions — how to act, versus how to represent the decision problem — under one heading? I would particularly like to know whether the prisoner's dilemma version and the Platonia version are the same dispute or two disputes that have been run together, since my friend insists the second is just the first with a mixed-strategy solution smuggled in.

Finally, given your diagnosis, tell me what would count as progress: a case that separates superrationality from evidential decision theory in an asymmetric setting, a characterization of the epistemic conditions under which symmetry reasoning is licensed, some result about the continuous or mixed-strategy versions, or a reformulation that dissolves the question. If your view is that no progress should be expected, argue for that directly and say what the residue of genuine content is. I want reasons keyed to what each side actually says, not a history of the concept.
