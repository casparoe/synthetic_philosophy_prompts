I am a senior completing a twenty-page independent-study paper on Al-Ghazālī's attack on causal necessity, which I will present at my school's spring research symposium, and I have reached the comparative section. I need your help ranking the possible replies to him, and I want the ranking done against one criterion that I have fixed for the paper rather than against general plausibility.

The position being answered is the one Ghazālī defends in the discussion of causation in *The Incoherence of the Philosophers*: what we call a cause is only the occasion at which God creates the effect, the fire does not burn the cotton by anything in its own nature, and the connection we take to be necessary is nothing more than God's uniform way of acting, which he could interrupt.

My criterion is this: **how much of ordinary scientific language the reply allows us to keep, unmodified.** A reply scores 10 if a chemist who accepted it could go on saying without qualification or hedging that the acid dissolved the zinc because it donates protons, that the drug lowers blood pressure by blocking the receptor, and that the bridge failed because the weld fatigued. It scores 0 if that chemist would have to translate every one of those claims into a statement about what God, or a habit, or a pattern of events, reliably does. Please hold yourself to this criterion and do not let the ranking quietly slide into a ranking of which view you think is true; where your estimate of truth pulls against the criterion, note it separately.

The candidates, numbered so that you can rank them by number:

1. Averroes' counter-attack in *The Incoherence of the Incoherence*: that stripping things of their causal powers is stripping them of their natures, and that the philosophers' word "cause" never carried the meaning Ghazālī's argument needs it to carry.
2. The Ashʿarite reading on which God's custom is itself a real, stable, knowable feature of the world, and only the word "necessity" is surrendered.
3. Malebranche's occasionalism, which accepts Ghazālī's conclusion but restores lawlikeness by having God act through general volitions rather than particular ones.
4. Leibniz's pre-established harmony, on which each substance carries its own complete unfolding and causal claims are true in virtue of it.
5. Humean regularity plus counterfactual dependence: no necessity in the world, but "the acid dissolved the zinc" is made true by what happened and by what would have happened otherwise.
6. Neo-Aristotelian dispositional realism, on which powers are real, irreducible, and pointed at their manifestations.
7. My own proposal, which I call "graded occasionalism": causal necessity comes in degrees proportional to how well a regularity survives deliberate interventions, so that fire burning cotton is close to fully necessary while a stock rising on good earnings is barely causal at all.

For each candidate, give a score out of ten on my criterion and a sentence or two justifying it, and then present the ranked list. Alongside the scores, please answer three further questions:

- Which two of the seven do you think are the same view in different clothing, and am I entitled to list them separately in a paper of this length?
- Which single candidate's score would move most if I replaced my criterion with the opposite one — how much of religion's God the reply preserves, in the sense of a deity who is the genuine source of everything and is not constrained by the natures of things — and what does that tell me about whether the two criteria can be traded off smoothly?
- Is candidate 7 a real seventh option, or a redescription of one of the others? If it is a redescription, say which, and if it is genuinely distinct, say what it buys me that none of the six do.

I am not looking for suggestions about what to read next or for references to check; I want your judgment about the positions exactly as I have stated them, and I would rather you tell me that one of my summaries is unfair to its target than accept it and rank accordingly.
