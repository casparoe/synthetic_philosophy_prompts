I chair the exhibitions committee of a national history museum. At our meeting in three weeks we must settle the interpretive frame for a new permanent gallery on the collapse of the Weimar Republic; the document I describe below will be circulated to members a fortnight ahead, and I would like you to draft it.

The curator's script is organized around a single question posed on the entry wall: would Germany have taken the same path without Hitler? Visitors then move through eleven "decision rooms," each reconstructing a moment in which a handful of named people chose — the backroom brokerage of January 1933 that produced a cabinet of twelve containing only three members of the party Hitler led; the emergency decree issued after the Reichstag fire; the vote two months later transferring lawmaking power from the legislature to the cabinet; the merger of the chancellorship and the presidency upon Hindenburg's death. The structural material — defeat in the First World War, the terms of the peace, the hyperinflation of 1923 and the depression that followed 1929, the fact that German governments had already been ruling by presidential emergency decree under Article 48 of the constitution since 1930, the authoritarian traditions of the officer corps and the higher civil service, the disintegration of the party system — is gathered into a single antechamber that the script's own label text describes as the "backdrop."

Three committee members have filed written objections. A social historian argues that the frame reinstates a great-man reading of the period in precisely the place where a museum's authority is most likely to be trusted, and that visitors will leave believing eleven decisions caused it all. A member whose parents were survivors argues that the proposed compromise — enlarging the antechamber and adding a panel on how much of what followed was foreseeable — will strike many visitors as mitigation, an invitation to conclude that no one in particular was to blame. A third wants the entry-wall question struck out as malformed, on the ground that the conditions the antechamber invokes were themselves partly produced by that party and its leader, so there is no clean "without him" to imagine.

Please work within these constraints. The founding gift for the gallery requires that individual choice be given prominent place, and the donor's estate has been consulted on this point. Fabrication drawings are commissioned in five months, and re-scripting after that becomes prohibitively expensive. The interactive element under discussion is a dial on which visitors record their own estimate of how likely a comparable regime would have been without Hitler, with the running distribution of answers displayed on the wall beside them.

Draft the decision memo, roughly a thousand words. It must recommend one of three outcomes — approve as drafted, approve subject to specific amendments, or return the script for reframing — and if it recommends amendments, they must be stated concretely enough that the curator could execute them without a second round of committee work. It should supply a short test that an individual panel or decision room can be made to pass or fail, applicable by members who are not specialists in the period. It should say plainly what to do about the dial. And it should set out the strongest consideration favoring the outcome you recommend against, in a form its proponents would recognize rather than in a weakened version convenient to your argument. Where the memo takes a position on the counterfactual, it should be explicit about which counterfactual it is answering, since the committee's disagreement may in part be a disagreement about what is being asked. Write from reasoning alone; I am not asking for sources or references, and none are needed.
