I sit on the board of a sixteen-unit housing co-op, and last spring we codified a rule giving each household final, unappealable say over one designated matter inside its own unit. Within a month we were deadlocked: every proposal on the table either overrode someone's protected choice or was unanimously dispreferred to something else we were forbidden from choosing. Trying to understand what had happened to us sent me back to Sen's result that minimal liberalism, the Pareto condition, and an unrestricted domain cannot all be satisfied together, and I now want a clean constructed case to work through with the rest of the board, none of whom have any training in social choice.

Please design a thought experiment that generates the conflict to the following specifications.

- Two people, each decisive over one pair of alternatives within a sphere an institution would plausibly declare private; four social states in all.
- A realistic setting — a shared building, a research team, a street — nothing science-fictional, with the scenario itself no longer than a paragraph and readable aloud to a lay audience.
- Neither party's preferences may rest on moral disapproval of the other's conduct, and neither may be a busybody in the sense of caring about the other's conduct for its own sake. This is the point of the exercise: the canonical illustrations turn on one party's investment in what the other does inside the other's own protected sphere, and they get dismissed in discussion as cases about prudes and nosy neighbors. I want to know whether the conflict survives when that motive is stripped out.
- Every unanimity step must be a strict preference for both people, not a tie broken by fiat, and the cycle has to fall out of the two exercises of right plus unanimity rather than be announced.

After the scenario, run the check. List the four states, say which pair each person is decisive over, give the preference profile, and derive the cycle one step at a time, marking at each step whether the ranking comes from a right or from unanimity. Then tell me honestly which feature of the case is doing the work, what the verdict looks like if I delete it, and how the strongest available objection — that the sphere was never genuinely private, so the right was misassigned in the first place — would attack the case, and whether the case survives that attack. If you judge that my constraints cannot all be satisfied at once, say so plainly, show me as informally as possible exactly where the binding constraint enters, and then give me the closest case under the smallest relaxation. Please write the whole answer in American English.
