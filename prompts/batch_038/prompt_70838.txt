For thirty-four years I ran an analytical chemistry laboratory — municipal water quality for most of it, with a decade of industrial process analysis at the end — and since retiring I have been reading philosophy with something like the seriousness I once reserved for method validation. I audit a course at the local university and belong to a Tuesday reading group, and my daughter, who writes software for a living, pressed a popular book about language and mind on me at the last meeting. I have been working through it slowly, keeping notes in the margins as I used to keep laboratory notebooks. One passage has stopped me for the better part of a week. It runs as follows:

> Putnam's Twin Earth is the most famous thought experiment in the philosophy of language. He asks us to imagine a planet just like ours, with a twin of every person and every thing on it, differing in one respect only: the liquid in its rivers, kettles and drinking glasses is not H2O but a different compound, XYZ, which behaves exactly like water. The two Oscars are physically identical and think the same thoughts, yet when Earth's Oscar says "water" his word picks out H2O, and when Twin Oscar says it his word picks out XYZ. What a term means, then, cannot be settled by anything inside a speaker's head: meaning is fixed by the causal contact a community has had with the stuff itself. That disposes of the old picture on which a word simply means the description its users associate with it, and it shows there can be no such thing as a purely internal psychology of meaning. The lesson for our own century is plain enough: a system that has never stood in causal contact with water, that has done nothing but process descriptions of it, cannot so much as entertain a thought about water, however fluently it talks of rivers and rain.

My difficulty is that I feel both agreement and resistance toward this paragraph and cannot sort out which of my reactions attaches to which sentence. As a chemist I am inclined to think the case is less exotic than the book implies: any analytical method that responded identically to XYZ and to H2O in every macroscopic respect would, for every practical purpose I ever worked under, have counted them as the same substance, so the stipulation that they are distinct compounds seems to me to be doing quiet work that the passage never acknowledges. On the other hand, I suspect my objection may simply mistake what a thought experiment is for — it was never meant to be a feasible laboratory scenario. So I would like an audit rather than a summary of my own views: take the passage sentence by sentence and tell me what a philosopher of language working in this tradition would accept without demur, what she would treat as a simplification that costs something real, and what she would judge to be simply wrong. I am especially curious whether the book's account of what fixes meaning omits machinery that Putnam himself insisted on — I gather he thought most ordinary speakers are in no position to tell the two liquids apart, and I do not see how that squares with meaning being settled by a community's contact with the stuff — and whether the final claim about language-processing systems is something the earlier sentences actually support or merely something the author would like to be true. Please mark your confidence as you go; I would far rather know where you are reconstructing the position from memory of the literature than receive a smooth, uniformly assured verdict, and where philosophers themselves disagree about a claim in the passage, say so instead of presenting one camp as settled. Finally, if you think the task I have set you is ill-posed — if auditing a compressed paragraph cut loose from the argument surrounding it cannot honestly be done, or if my chemical objection is not merely mistaken but confused about what the case is supposed to establish — tell me that plainly rather than humouring an old technician.
