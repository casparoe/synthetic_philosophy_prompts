Third year in a philosophy of physics program, and there's an argument my department has been having since before I arrived that I can no longer tell is a real disagreement. I don't want a verdict on who's right — I want to know what kind of disagreement it is.

The topic is whether chaotic dynamics undercuts deterministic predictability in some deep sense, or whether the whole worry is a bookkeeping mistake about resources.

The first camp, call them the deflationists, tend to have an operationalist or measurement-theoretic streak. Their argument, roughly: in a chaotic system nearby states separate exponentially, so predicting some feature of the state a time t ahead requires something like λt more bits about the initial condition than predicting it right now, λ being the relevant Lyapunov exponent. No physical agent ever has more than finitely many bits about an initial condition — the apparatus is finite, the region is finite, and on some people's view the information content of a bounded region is bounded in principle and not just in practice. So the predictability that determinism is supposed to guarantee is never available to anyone in a position to check it, and the difference between such a system and a genuinely stochastic one with the same statistics isn't a difference anyone could detect. Some of them go further and say the idealization itself is bogus: a phase-space point specified by infinitely many digits isn't something the world contains, so uniqueness of trajectory is a property of our mathematics rather than of the system.

The second camp says the first is confusing horizons with impossibilities and trading on an ambiguity in "in principle." Their points: computed pseudo-orbits of a chaotic system are shadowed by genuine orbits from very nearby initial conditions, so a simulation isn't just drifting free of the truth — it's tracking a true trajectory of a nearby system in a precise sense, and for hyperbolic systems that's a theorem rather than a hope. What chaos kills is detailed long-range prediction; it leaves alone the things that are actually robust, meaning attractors, invariant measures, time averages, symbolic-dynamics structure, transport rates. And they add that the deflationists have changed the subject, because determinism is a claim about uniqueness of the trajectory through the actual state, and whether anyone could ever learn enough to exploit that uniqueness is an epistemic matter that doesn't make determinism false or empty. There's a sub-dispute in here that never gets resolved: whether shadowing is really available for the messy non-hyperbolic systems people care about, and if only locally, for how long — I've seen people claim the shadowing time grows only like the log of the precision, which would put the structuralists right back where the deflationists started.

My own hypothesis, and please treat it as one, I'm not attached to it: this is two disputes wearing a single coat. One is stipulative — what "in principle" quantifies over, i.e. which idealized agent the modal claim is about, a finite-information agent with unlimited time or an exact-real-number agent — and each camp picks a different one and then talks past the other. The other is substantive and formal: whether physical states have finite information content, and whether shadowing buys usable long-run trust outside the hyperbolic case. Those questions at least look like the kind of thing that could have answers, and I suspect neither side notices which one they're currently arguing about.

So: is that diagnosis right, or is the fault line somewhere else — a clash over which idealizations are legitimate, over what counts as a physical possibility, over standards of evidence, or over a formal result one side is quietly assuming? Tell me where the real break is, restate each side's strongest claim once you strip out the part that's mere stipulation, and say what would count as progress given your diagnosis: a distinction, a theorem, a physical argument about information content, a change of question. If your conclusion is that no progress should be expected and the question is ill-posed, argue for that instead — I'd much rather be told that than handed a survey of the two positions.
