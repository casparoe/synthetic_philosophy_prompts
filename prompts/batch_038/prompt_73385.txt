I am drafting the compensation rules for a statutory scheme covering people whose life expectancy has been shortened by exposure at work, and our working model follows English personal injury law closely. Since 1982 nothing has been payable for a shortened life as such, though the claimant's awareness that their life has been cut short counts towards damages for pain and suffering; a living claimant may still recover the earnings they will never receive in the years they will not live to see, less the living expenses they will no longer incur; and on the claimant's death the estate loses that claim altogether, leaving the dependants only the support they would have received plus a fixed bereavement sum. Critics have told our panel that the package is incoherent, since it compensates a dying man for knowing that he is dying while an identical life extinguished without warning generates nothing for the loss itself. Please set out the most important implications of the Epicurean argument that death is no harm to the person who dies, because no subject remains to be harmed, together with the main deprivation-based objections to it, for whether these rules are principled or arbitrary — and in particular for what the scheme is implicitly committed to about whether a shortened life is a loss to the person living it and, if so, when that loss falls. Answer as exactly five numbered points of a sentence or two each, with no introduction or conclusion.
