I spent most of Saturday night arguing with a friend about eliminative materialism, and the argument started from a long-form piece in a general-interest magazine that I read on the train down and lent him afterwards. Since neither of us works in philosophy of mind, we ended up trading intuitions rather than arguments, and I now suspect that a fair amount of what he was defending was actually supplied by the article rather than by the view. Here is the passage we kept going back to:

"Eliminative materialism, the position most associated with Paul and Patricia Churchland, holds that beliefs and desires do not exist. This is not the modest claim that we are often mistaken about what we believe, nor the humbler suggestion that 'belief' will eventually be sharpened into something more precise. It is the claim that the entire vocabulary of believing, wanting, intending and hoping picks out nothing whatever, in the way that 'phlogiston' and 'the luminiferous ether' picked out nothing: folk psychology is a theory that has simply failed, and when neuroscience matures we will stop talking about what people believe and start talking about the activation patterns of their cell assemblies. The Churchlands observe that this stagnant framework has never explained why we sleep, has never explained mental illness, and has added nothing to its stock of generalizations in two and a half thousand years, which is precisely what one expects of a theory on its way out. Two consequences follow, and the article is candid about both. First, since believing is a relation between a person and a proposition, and there are no such relations, there is no propositional content inside anyone's head; nothing a person thinks or says is true or false in any robust sense, and the eliminativist's own sentences are no exception, which is why the charge of self-refutation has followed the view since its inception and why its defenders have never had a satisfactory answer. Second, eliminative materialism is best understood as a radical behaviourism: to say that someone is thirsty is only to say that she is disposed to drink, and the Churchlands add that even the disposition-talk will eventually be superseded. In practice, of course, they go on asking what their colleagues want for lunch, just as the rest of us go on saying that the sun rises; on their view this is harmless shorthand, a useful fiction retained for convenience while the serious explanatory work is done elsewhere."

My friend took both consequences at face value and added an argument of his own, which is the one I would most like you to assess. He says that even if the Churchlands are entitled to brush off the standard self-refutation charge — he granted, grudgingly, that they might call it question-begging, on the grounds that it states its objection using the very vocabulary the theory says is empty — a second and better problem survives the reply. His version runs like this: the eliminativist's positive case rests entirely on the authority of neuroscience, but neuroscience is a practice conducted by people who form hypotheses, design experiments, weigh evidence, and revise their conclusions in the light of what they find; every one of those descriptions is an attribution of propositional attitudes. So the theory is not merely awkward to assert, it is explanatorily parasitic on the framework it is trying to overthrow: it borrows the credibility of an enterprise that can only be described in the idiom it declares vacuous. He thinks this is a genuine improvement on the old performativity objection rather than the same objection in a lab coat, and I was not able to show him otherwise, though I left the argument unsure whether I had been persuaded or simply out-talked. I should add that I came into the evening inclined to agree with him, and I am aware that this may be distorting my reading of the article.

Could you audit the passage for me, sentence by sentence where that is useful, and tell me how much of it a professional philosopher of mind would sign? Please write in British English, and bear in mind that I am offline and cannot chase anything down: argue from the substance of the positions rather than sending me to sources, and do not pad the answer with a reading list. Please organise the reply under exactly the following headings, in this order, with roughly the lengths indicated. **The skeleton** (about 120 words): break the passage into its discrete theses, numbered, so that we are clear what is being assessed and so that claims it runs together are separated out. **What stands** (about 150 words): which theses are accurate, including any that are accurate but incomplete or accurate only on a contested premise. **Where it distorts** (about 400 words): the misrepresentations, one by one, each with the corrected formulation and a note on whether the error is a common simplification, a conflation of two distinct views, or an overstatement of a modest thesis; I particularly want to know whether the behaviourism gloss and the useful-fiction gloss are things the eliminativists themselves would reject, and whether the slide from there is no propositional content to nothing is true or false in any robust sense is licensed. **The self-refutation question** (about 250 words): set out the standard charge, the best replies available to the eliminativist, and then give a considered verdict on my friend's bootstrapping argument — whether it is a real advance, whether it proves too much against his own side, and whether the eliminativist has a reply that does not concede the point. **Where I should end up** (about 120 words): given the corrected account, do the article's conclusions survive, and what is the most defensible position for someone who found the article's tone convincing but its reasoning shaky?
