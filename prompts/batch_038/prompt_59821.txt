I am three years into a dissertation on the normative foundations of voluntary obligation, and the chapter I am currently revising defends a position I have not encountered articulated in quite this form, so I want it stress-tested by someone who will not simply assume I have read the standard alternatives badly. The position, in outline, is that promissory obligation is at bottom an obligation incurred to oneself rather than to the promisee. In promising, you exercise a power over the shape of your own agency: you settle what you will do, and in settling it you acquire a duty to be the person whose word carries weight. Breaking a promise is therefore primarily a failure with respect to yourself — you become someone for whom an assertion about your own future counts for nothing. The injury to the promisee is real and morally significant, but on my account it is derivative, explained by the fact that the promisee participated in the act through which you took on this self-directed duty and so occupies a special position from which to complain when you fail in it. Two features are what drew me to the view. It explains why you cannot simply release yourself from a promise by ceasing to care about the promisee, something an unambiguously other-directed account has to work harder to accommodate. And it explains what is added when you promise to do something you already had independent moral reason to do — return the book, tell the truth, refrain from an affair — since the promise adds not a further reason of the promisee's making but a commitment concerning the constitution of your own agency.

What I want from you is a map of this view's exposure, not a survey of the literature on promising. Take the cases in rough order of the damage they do, and for each say whether the self-directed account delivers an acceptable verdict, whether it can explain the case away, and what the explanation costs. The ones I have in mind: a promise made to a stranger you are confident you will never see or hear from again, broken in circumstances where no one will ever learn of the breach; the promise to a person who dies before the time for performance, including promises concerning what will be done with their body or their papers; a promise that cannot be performed until after the promisor's own death; the promisee's release, which on my account ought to be embarrassing — if the duty is owed to me, why should their say-so extinguish it?; the parallel asymmetry between promising yourself and promising another, since if both are self-directed it is unclear why one admits of release by me and the other does not; promises extracted under duress, or made by someone not competent to make them; the promise accompanied by a secret reservation; and the promise whose performance would itself be a serious wrong. I am also interested in structural rather than case-based pressure. There is the long-standing worry that one cannot hold a claim against oneself, and I want to know what turns on whether that is a conceptual truth or an artifact of how we ordinarily describe rights. There are the puzzles in deontic logic concerning obligations derived from other obligations, which seem to bear on whether a self-directed duty can generate duties toward anyone else at all. And there is the material on standing to blame, since my account must explain why the promisee's resentment is appropriate in a way that a bystander's is not, and I am not confident I can ground that asymmetry without smuggling in a genuinely other-directed duty after all.

Two further judgments, delivered plainly. The first concerns insulation. I expect a critic to say that "the wrong to the promisee is derivative" functions as a formula that absorbs any counterexample: whatever intuition is produced against the view, I can redescribe it as an intuition about the derivative wrong. Is that a fair description of my position? And if it is, does it matter for a theory of this kind — I am aware that the standards for immunity differ between normative inquiry and empirical science, and I would rather be told whether what looks like unfalsifiability here is a genuine defect or simply what theoretical unification looks like from the outside. The second concerns comparative risk: what does my account get right that the practice-based line (which, whatever else it achieves, seems to locate the obligation in a social institution rather than in anything the promisor does) and the assurance-style line get wrong, and is any of it worth the costs identified above? Please rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, indicate for each whether a committed defender of my view should concede it or resist it, and say where the pressure ought to land hardest. My own guess is the release case and the self-promise asymmetry, on the ground that both seem to require a party with the standing to alter the obligation, and my account has no such party other than the promisor — who, by hypothesis, cannot alter it at will. If it turns out that only a substantially revised version of the view survives, show me the revision rather than reassuring me; a chapter I can defend is worth more to me than one I can enjoy.
