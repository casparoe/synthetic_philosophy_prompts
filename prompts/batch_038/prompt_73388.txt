I chair the Methods Committee of a national review panel that supplies the quantitative safety case for a proposed deep geologic repository for spent nuclear fuel. Seven appointed experts — two hydrogeologists, a corrosion chemist, two seismologists, a statistician, and a structural engineer — must produce one figure: the probability that radionuclide release to the accessible environment exceeds the regulatory threshold at some point in the next ten thousand years. Our statute is demanding in two ways at once. It requires a single number with a documented pedigree, not a range. It also requires that panelists work from one public data record and disclose to each other everything their estimate relies on, so no one can claim to have had access to evidence the others lacked.

We are deadlocked on procedure for the coming cycle. In the last cycle, after three rounds of full exchange, two panelists remained a factor of thirty apart, and the residue was not evidential: both had seen and accepted the same data, and both models reproduce that data about equally well. They disagree about how to treat quantities nobody has ever observed — fracture connectivity in the host rock, corrosion behavior on millennial timescales — and each characterizes the other's treatment as an assumption, not a finding. One member of the committee, a game theorist, wants to write a "disagreement screen" into the charter. His reasoning is that rational agents who share all relevant information and know one another to be rational cannot stay at odds, which he takes to be Aumann's agreement theorem; therefore a panelist who still differs after complete exchange has either concealed something or is not updating honestly, and that panelist's estimate should be reported to the board as "non-responsive" and set aside. He offers a remedy for the assumption dispute: we stipulate a common prior over the contested parameters in the charter itself, in advance, so that any surviving gap can only reflect bad faith. The rest of us lean the other way — independent anonymous elicitation, then an aggregation rule, with the spread reported to the board rather than suppressed — but we cannot settle on the rule. Our statistician notes that when estimates span orders of magnitude an arithmetic average is effectively decided by the highest of them and a geometric average by the lowest, so the choice of pooling rule quietly decides the license; she also prefers the geometric version on the grounds that it is the only one that behaves sensibly when the panel later updates on data everyone shares.

Please draft the memo I will circulate to the committee before our next meeting. It should recommend a protocol — one of the two, or a specified hybrid with the division of labor made explicit — and it should be usable: what the panel does in session, in what order, what appears in the report to the board, and, if any screen survives at all, what it looks like as criteria a chair can actually apply to a live disagreement. Engage the game theorist's argument rather than waving it off: state precisely what his result requires of the agents it applies to, whether our panel's situation satisfies those requirements once the statute's full-disclosure mandate is taken into account, and whether a prior written into a charter by fiat can be the sort of prior the result is about, given that the panelists in question do not actually hold it. Then give the strongest honest case for the protocol you reject. Our legislative oversight committee has already reprimanded us once for reporting ranges, on the ground that a range lets the board select whichever end suits the political weather, and there is a genuine accountability argument that the public is owed one number that an identifiable expert will stand behind in a hearing. I need to know whether that argument defeats your recommendation, and if it does not, what in the recommended protocol answers it. I am not looking for references or a survey of the literature; I need the reasoning and the procedure.
