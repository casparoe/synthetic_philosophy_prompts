**Draft: "Risk Attitudes or Units of Deliberation?"**

**1.** Risk-weighted expected utility theory, in the form Lara Buchak defends, evaluates a gamble with outcomes ordered by utility, o₁ ≤ … ≤ oₙ, and probabilities p₁, …, pₙ, by summing the utility increments between adjacent outcomes, each weighted by r applied to the cumulative probability of doing at least as well as the better outcome. The function r is continuous and strictly increasing with r(0) = 0 and r(1) = 1, and expected utility theory is recovered as the special case r(p) = p. Convex r encodes risk aversion: it downweights the increments at the top of the distribution, so the agent discounts further upside while continuing to care, undiminished, about escaping the worst outcomes. Because the weight attached to an increment depends on where that increment sits in the ordering of outcomes, and not merely on the state in which it obtains, the theory violates the sure-thing principle, and Buchak holds that this is its central virtue rather than an embarrassment. It is also, mathematically, a rank-dependent theory: the functional is a Choquet integral with respect to the capacity r∘P, a monotone but non-additive distortion of the probability measure.

**2.** The standard objection is diachronic. Because the theory is non-separable, an agent who chooses prospect A over prospect B at time t₁, and faces the same choice again at t₂ before any uncertainty resolves, may at t₂ prefer B. Hammond's argument then bites: either the agent is dynamically inconsistent, and can be pumped, or she abandons consequentialism. Buchak's response, drawing on McClennen, is to deny the setup. Rational deliberation is global: the agent should choose among the whole gambles actually available to her, and if she foresees her future self's local preferences, the plan she should adopt is the one she would endorse from the start, carried through resolutely rather than renegotiated at each node. Resolute choice, she argues, lets the risk-weighted maximizer keep both consistency and consequentialism while still rejecting the sure-thing principle.

**3.** My claim is that this exchange is being conducted on the wrong terrain, and that noticing why changes what the theory needs. Expected utility theory is separable, so it does not matter how a decision problem is carved up: evaluating a bundle of prospects state by state and then summing, or evaluating the bundle whole, give the same verdict. The unit of deliberation drops out of the theory entirely. Risk-weighted expected utility theory loses that invariance, and therefore it is committed to the existence of a privileged partition: some ways of bundling sub-decisions into a single gamble are the ones over which the risk function is properly applied, and others are not. The theory tells us how to evaluate a gamble once a gamble has been specified; it does not tell us which specifications are eligible.

**4.** Resoluteness is offered as the answer, but it answers a different question. Resoluteness governs an agent's attitude toward a plan she has already formed, and so it presupposes that the agent had a determinate set of competing plans to choose among at the outset. But that set is exactly what the partition determines. Whether the agent at t₁ faces one choice or two consecutive ones, whether the t₂ decision is inside her current deliberation or downstream of it, is not settled by her credences, her utilities, or her risk function; all three are inputs to a gamble, not a specification of which gamble. A defender might respond that the eligible units are simply the maximal acts available at a moment, so the question is closed by construction. But maximality is relative to a description of the agent's option space, and any act can be redescribed as a compound of two acts by splitting the decision nodes it contains. Nor can the agent choose to be resolute rather than sophisticated as a way of fixing her units, since that choice is itself a choice among descriptions of her situation, and evaluating it requires already knowing which description is eligible. Here I think the theory faces a regress rather than a gap, and it is not clear to me that the regress terminates.

**5.** I propose that eligibility is fixed by informational structure rather than by the agent's attitudes. Call a decision node *sealed* relative to a bundle if the agent's epistemic state at that node is, with respect to the uncertainties the bundle concerns, exactly her epistemic state at the bundle's first node: she has received no free information that bears on those uncertainties, and no opportunity to act on such information has arisen. A bundle is an eligible unit of deliberation just in case all of its internal nodes are sealed. The risk function is then applied to bundles and only to bundles so constituted, and it is never applied across a boundary at which relevant information arrives.

**6.** The criterion appears to deliver the results Buchak wants without the machinery of resoluteness. In the sequential-choice cases that generate the pump, the agent chooses twice, both times before resolution, with nothing informative intervening; both nodes are sealed, so they are one unit, evaluated jointly, and no local reversal is available to pump. Where the agent learns the outcome of the first stage before the second choice, the nodes are not sealed, the bundle splits, and the theory may indeed license her to abandon the plan she would have preferred ex ante. That last consequence is not a concession extracted under pressure; it is the theory explaining why the diachronic objection seemed to have force, since the objection's force varies systematically with whether the objector's setup smuggles in free information.

**7.** The criterion has a cost I want to own rather than bury. It implies that two agents with identical credences, identical utility functions, and identical risk functions can face different rational requirements solely in virtue of the informational layout of their situations, and it implies that a rational risk-averse agent on this theory sometimes has reason to avoid or destroy information, not because she fears what it will do to her evidence, but because receiving it changes the units over which she is permitted to deliberate. That is a substantive claim about the value of ignorance, and it is one the theory is not otherwise equipped to make.

4. **The criterion's edge cases.** Where does the sealed-node account break? Consider at least: probabilistic or partial information that bears on the uncertainties without resolving them; information the agent pays to acquire rather than receives for free; imperfect recall, where the agent forgets which node she is at; and cases where the agent herself brings about the information structure, as by choosing a test. Tell me whether any of these are fatal rather than merely awkward.

5. **Novelty and framing.** Is the central claim — that the disagreement over risk attitudes is really a disagreement over units of deliberation — interesting, or is it a repackaging of the familiar dynamic-inconsistency complaint? 6. **Section 7.** Are the two consequences I concede — that rational requirements can vary with informational layout alone, and that the theory licenses avoiding information — genuine costs, or would a defender be better off advertising them as predictions?
