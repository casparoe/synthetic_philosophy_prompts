**Title: Liable, therefore enfranchised — a guilt-based case for votes at 16, votes for residents, and compulsory turnout**

1. Almost every argument for votes at 16 runs on one of two rails. The first is competence: sixteen-year-olds can drive, work, pay tax and be tried as adults, so they can pick a party. The second is affected interests: they'll live longest with the decisions we make now, so they should get a say. Both are shaky. The competence rail proves too little — nobody proposes testing adults, so the bar can't be competence or we'd have to disenfranchise a lot of grown-ups — and the affected-interests rail proves too much, since it would enfranchise tourists, and the unborn, and arguably anyone anywhere affected by our emissions. I want a foundation that isn't about how smart you are or how much you're affected, but about what you're on the hook for.

2. That foundation is in Jaspers' postwar book on the question of German guilt. He splits guilt into four kinds — criminal, political, moral, metaphysical — and the one I care about is political guilt, which is not really about blame at all. It's liability. On his account you are liable for what your state does in your name simply by being a member of it, whether or not you approved, and political guilt is basically inherited: you're born into a state and you pick up its debts the way you'd pick up a loan you never signed for. He thought this applied even to Germans who hated the regime, because the state still acted as their state.

3. From that I want a principle. Call it the liability principle: a person can be held liable for the doings of a collective only if they had, or could have had, some hand in steering it — and, running the other way, anyone who is genuinely liable for a state's doings ought to have a hand in steering it. Liability and voice have to be coextensive. Liability without voice is just being ruled; voice without liability is free-riding on other people's consequences.

4. Now ask who is actually liable. Children are liable in exactly Jaspers' sense: they inherit the national debt, the treaties, the wars, the climate commitments, the border they were born inside. Long-term resident non-citizens are liable too — taxed, governed, conscripted in some countries, deported at will, and answerable to a state that acts in a name they never got to authorise. If liability grounds the vote, both groups are in. Note this isn't the affected-interests rail from paragraph 1: being affected isn't enough, because a German tourist hit by our recession is affected but isn't a member of the enterprise whose doings he's answerable for.

5. The liability view also kills a reform I used to like. There's a proposal, named after the demographer Paul Demeny, where parents cast proxy votes for their children — France nearly adopted something like it in the 1920s, and the German Bundestag voted on versions of it in 2003 and 2008 and rejected both. On my account this gets the vote completely backwards. The vote isn't a share of power that can be handed to whoever's nearest; it's the form your own liability takes. My mum can't discharge my liability by voting for me, any more than she can serve my sentence. So proxy votes for children are not a half-way house to votes at 16 — they're a different and worse thing, and I'd rather enfranchise the actual teenager.

6. Compulsory voting follows too, which is the part I'm least sure of but like the most. If voting is how you answer for what's done in your name, then not voting isn't neutrality, it's declining to answer. Belgium and Australia already compel turnout and it hasn't obviously ruined them. So the liability principle seems to give you the whole package at once: votes at 16, votes for long-term residents, compulsory turnout, no proxy votes.

7. The awkward bit. Liability comes in degrees. I benefit more from my state's doings than someone who's here on a two-year work visa, and a 55-year-old has had forty years to shape things that a 16-year-old has had none. If liability grounds the franchise, shouldn't it ground a weighted franchise — more votes for the more liable? I don't want to say that. One person one vote has to be the floor. But I admit I haven't got an argument for why liability can ground the franchise and then suddenly stop grading.

8. Conclusion: the competence and affected-interest arguments are weak because they're about the voter's properties. Liability is about the voter's position. And position is something a state can actually assign coherently, which is why I think it does more work than people assume.
