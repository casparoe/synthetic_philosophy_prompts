A colleague and I are drafting a joint paper on the wrong kind of reasons problem. She has been the decisive one about cases, and I have kept a record of her rulings. I would like your help predicting what she will say about a case we have not yet reached, and working out what her record already commits her to.

Her verdicts so far, each answering the same question — does the incentive give you a reason to have the attitude?

1. An eccentric will endow the clinic my sister directs if I come to admire a plain saucer of mud on his desk. Ruling: the offer is no reason to admire the mud; it is a reason to want to admire the mud, and to set about acquiring the admiration.
2. The same eccentric threatens to withdraw the endowment unless I fear the harmless spider on the windowsill. Ruling: no reason to fear the spider — its docility counts against fear — but a reason to try to acquire the fear.
3. A joke that is not funny, where laughing at it would delight a dying friend. Ruling: I have a reason to laugh, and she insists that laughing is something one does; the friend's delight is no reason to be amused.
4. A proposition that would make my life much better to believe, with no evidence either way. Ruling: no reason to believe it. Here she hesitates over the acquisition move she used in 1 and 2, remarking that belief cannot be produced on purpose, and she does not say what is left over.
5. A conspicuously admirable person — courageous, witty, generous — whom a tyrant will punish me for admiring. Ruling: the threat is no reason not to admire her; her features remain reasons to admire her; the threat is a reason to keep the admiration to myself and to avoid expressing it.
6. A gift of great benefit to me, given by someone who intended only to purchase my silence. Ruling: the benefit is a reason for gratitude; the giver's motive is a reason to be wary of him, not a reason against the gratitude.
7. Across all of this she holds one line without exception: no arrangement of incentives could make an attitude fitting.

One feature of her language is doing more work than she admits. She always says that the incentive is "no reason" for the attitude, and she has never said whether she means that it is no reason at all, or a genuine reason of the wrong sort.

The new case. The eccentric will make the endowment conditional on my being genuinely and involuntarily frightened by a moth that has settled on my desk, the fright to be confirmed by a physiological monitor. I know the moth is harmless; moths have landed on me a hundred times without alarm; and, as I know from having tried, there is nothing I can do on purpose that reliably produces a startle of that kind. Stipulate that the indirect routes are closed as well — no caffeine, no frightening films beforehand, no piece of self-deception I could undertake with any confidence of success.

What I would like from you:

(a) The candidate accounts that fit verdicts 1 through 7. Consider at least the distinction between reasons that bear on the object of an attitude and reasons that bear on one's having it; the view that an incentive is never a reason for the attitude but only a reason to want it or to bring it about; the view that wrong-kind reasons are genuine reasons that bear on a different question than the attitude's own; and any view keyed to what each attitude is answerable to, or to what the subject can control. State each as a rule rather than as a label, and say which of them, if any, the record already excludes — verdict 6 and the hesitation in verdict 4 seem to me to be doing real constraining work, and I want to know whether I am right about that.

(b) What each surviving account predicts she will say about the moth: whether the offer is a reason to be frightened; if it is not, whether anything normative is left in the case at all, or whether the offer goes wholly inert; and, if it is a reason, whether she would let it be weighed against the moth's harmlessness in a single deliberation or assigned to a separate question from the one the fear itself answers.

(c) Your best guess at her verdict, argued from her earlier rulings rather than from the general plausibility of the accounts. Identify which of the six precedents is the closest analog to the moth case and where the analogy begins to strain.

(d) Whether she can hold the line in verdict 7 while granting — if that is your prediction — that the offer is a genuine reason to be frightened, and what that concession costs her: in particular, whether she needs some clause about the way in which a reason bears on an attitude, or whether she can do without one and still explain why no incentive could make an attitude fitting.

(e) One further case, describable in two or three sentences, that would separate the two accounts you judge most likely to be hers, such that they issue in opposite verdicts on it.
