I am a high school junior, and for the past two years I have interned with the planning department of my town — a municipality of roughly 40,000 people with one commuter-rail station and a largely single-family housing stock. Part of my job has been to sit in on Planning and Zoning Commission hearings and keep a voting log. I have become especially interested in one member, whom I will call Commissioner Ruiz, because her record strikes me as genuinely difficult to reduce to a single slogan. Our Youth Council has been invited to file a written comment on the next item before her, and I would like to predict how she will vote before I commit us to a position.

Here is her record, in the order the matters came up:

1. In favor of an ordinance permitting duplexes, triplexes, fourplexes, and accessory dwelling units by right in every residential zone, capped at two and a half stories, with parking requirements left unchanged.
2. Against a 55-unit, six-story, entirely market-rate apartment building about 400 metres from the station. The site's zoning envelope capped four stories, so the project needed a height variance; it also sought to reduce required parking to 0.6 spaces per unit, and it would have demolished two small older apartment buildings containing eleven existing rental units.
3. In favor of a 240-unit, twelve-story mixed-use tower on the former rail yard in the downtown core, permitted at that height by right under the town's transit-oriented development overlay, with a structured garage at one space per unit, 8 percent of units deed-restricted as affordable, and the largest addition to the commercial tax base in a decade.
4. Against a neighborhood association's petition to downzone a half-mile corridor from two-family to single-family, a change that would have blocked the conversion of a large old house into six small units.
5. Against a 14-unit, entirely deed-restricted affordable townhouse project proposed by a congregation on its own land in a single-family zone. It required a special permit, offered half a parking space per unit, and would have paved over a community garden that had occupied the site for twenty-five years.
6. In favor of a 36-unit, four-story building with 30 percent deed-restricted affordable units, 600 metres from the station, permitted by right, providing 1.2 parking spaces per unit.
7. Against endorsing a state bill that would have prohibited municipalities from maintaining single-family-only zoning. I recorded two reasons from her, and I could not tell which carried the weight: that the decision belonged to the commission rather than the legislature, and that the bill would not itself produce a single below-market unit.

Now the new matter. A regional nonprofit proposes a six-story, 90-unit building that would be permanently affordable, deed-restricted at 60 percent of area median income for ninety-nine years, on a 1.2-acre vacant lot that currently holds a shuttered laundromat and a gravel parking field, with no residential units on it. The site sits in a single-family zone about 1.5 kilometres from the station, outside the transit overlay, where the cap is two and a half stories and the requirement is 1.5 spaces per unit; the project offers 0.25 spaces per unit. The decisive wrinkle is that the developer is invoking our state's affordable-housing density bonus statute, under which a fully affordable project of this size proceeds as of right: local height and parking limits are overridden, and the commission is left with a design review it cannot use to deny the application. The town's own equity map classifies the census tract as low displacement risk and high opportunity; it is among the wealthiest in town and contains almost no rental housing.

What I would like from you:

- At least four distinct decision rules that would account for this record. Each must be stated precisely enough to be testable against the votes, and each must either fit all seven or fit six with a stated account of the exception. Please do not simply offer labels such as pro-housing or anti-housing; I want the operative variable in each rule and the threshold at which it flips.
- Her predicted vote on the new matter under each rule, and then your single best guess, with the reasoning that makes one rule preferable to the others given how votes 2, 3, 5, and 7 interact.
- Which of the seven prior matters is the closest analog to the new one, which is the most misleadingly similar, and exactly which features do the analogical work.
- Two hypothetical future proposals that would discriminate most sharply between the leading rules, and what she would have to vote on each for those rules to come apart. If instead there is a question I could simply put to her before the hearing that would settle it, propose it and say what each possible answer would imply.
- Finally, if her record genuinely underdetermines this case, tell me what principle she would in effect be adopting by voting either way — that is, what she would be committed to endorsing the next time a comparable application arrived.

Please work only from the record above and from general features of land-use politics. I do not want citations, reading lists, or references to real statutes, ordinances, or court decisions, and please do not supply names of towns, developers, or legal authorities beyond what I have given. Roughly 1,000 to 1,200 words would be ideal.
