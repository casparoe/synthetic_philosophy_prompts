So, quick background: I'm American, moved to Munich about five months ago for a job at a mid-size software company, and in the last few weeks I've ended up with a knot in my head that nobody here seems to find knotty at all, which is part of why I'm asking a stranger. First week, I had to go to the Bürgeramt and register my address, and the city wasn't willing to take my word for where I live — my landlord had to sign a form confirming that I'd actually moved into his flat, and there's apparently a fine if you don't do this within two weeks. Third week, I was moaning about the paperwork to a German friend and he told me, cheerfully, that basically anybody who knows my name plus either my birthday or my old address can go to a registry office or use one of those online portals in some states, pay seven or eight euros, and get my current home address, and that nobody will ever tell me it happened. There's a beefed-up version of the same lookup that also returns former names, previous addresses, move-in and move-out dates, marital status and nationality, where you at least have to assert some legitimate interest; and there's a blocking entry you can apply for if you're, say, in a women's shelter or a witness-protection situation. And I read that the simple lookup used to be available to advertisers and address brokers until a public uproar got it changed — which suggests the default answer was once "sure, why not."

Meanwhile this same country, and this same company, treat personal data like a relic in every other context. I've sat through three meetings about a cookie banner. Our HR department will not let a manager keep a spreadsheet of applicants. Our customers get notified about processors, we do impact assessments, everything is fenced in by purpose. And people say all of it proudly descends from a constitutional court ruling about a census in the early eighties that read something like a right to informational self-determination into the basic law — I'm fuzzy on the details, so don't lean on my version of it. A colleague from our Shanghai office made it worse by pointing out that mandatory address registration plus a state that knows everything about everyone is also the skeleton of the system she grew up with, minus the scoring; whereas where I grew up there's no national ID, no duty to tell the state where you sleep, no landlord vouching for your existence, and yet your home address, your mortgage, your voter registration and your relatives' names are all purchasable from a data broker for about twenty bucks. So which of us is the privacy country keeps sliding around depending on what I'm looking at.

Here's what I want: an account of what conception of privacy this specific bundle of practices presupposes, if it presupposes a single one. Reconstruct it — is privacy here control over processing, or the availability of procedure and objection, or something about who is doing the looking (accountable state office versus anonymous corporation), or about legibility to the state being fine as long as it's reciprocal and bounded? Then tell me which rival conceptions the bundle quietly rules out: I suspect it can't accommodate the view that privacy means being hard to find, or the view that it's a right against being known rather than a right about how knowing is governed, but I don't know how to argue that. Then deal with the apparent incoherence head-on: is mandatory registration plus open address lookup plus paranoid corporate consent actually a consistent package, or is it a historical sediment that gets rationalized after the fact, and if it's consistent, what is the premise doing the unifying work? Please be strict about separating what these institutions genuinely cannot run without from what they merely happen to fit nicely with — I assume more than one theory licenses the practice, and I want the underdetermined parts marked. And end with what would actually have to change institutionally if you swapped in the conception I grew up with, or in one where the point is to be unfindable rather than to be in control.

On confidence: I'd rather have a committed reconstruction than a hedge-fest, but tell me plainly where it's most likely wrong, which piece of my description you'd want to check before building on it, and what argument or evidence would change your verdict about what the practice presupposes. I'm going to a Stammtisch on Thursday and would like to be able to say one coherent sentence instead of "in America we do it the other way."
