I convene a small graduate seminar on comparative constitutional theory, and each year the same thing happens in the week on the allocation of authority: the students arrive having read a bit on the United States, Germany, Switzerland and the EU, and they collapse everything onto a single axis running from "centralise" to "decentralise." On that axis every position sorts itself instantly, the debate goes nowhere, and nobody notices that the interesting disagreements are about *who gets to decide whether the centre may step in*, not about how much power the centre ends up with. A colleague of mine tried to fix this last term by drafting a self-diagnostic handout, but her version still smuggled the labels into the questions — she asked students whether they found subsidiarity compelling, which is exactly the thing the instrument is supposed to reveal. I would like to build a better one, and I am hoping you can produce it.

What I want is a decision tree (or, if you think a scored quiz suits the material better, a quiz with a scoring key that maps answer patterns onto positions) which takes a reader who holds only ordinary intuitions about who should decide a public question and lands them on a named position about competence allocation in a federal or quasi-federal system. Please construct it out of the logical space first and locate the actual positions second: state the branch questions, justify briefly why each one is orthogonal to the others, and only then say which view sits at which leaf. No node may contain the words subsidiarity, federalism, sovereignty, or competence, and each node must be phrased as a question a reader could answer without knowing where the answer leads. I would like five to seven nodes, ten to twelve leaves, and the whole thing to fit on two pages of handout. The nodes I have in mind, which you should feel free to revise, merge, or reorder if you think my set is wrong, are: whether authority at the lower level is held as a matter of right or merely as an efficiency default; what the trigger is for the higher level to act — that it can do the job better, that the lower units impose costs on each other, that a floor of rights or living conditions is being undercut, or that a lower unit simply lacks the capacity and needs help from above rather than non-interference; whether the higher level can act directly on individuals and local authorities or only through the governments of the constituent units; who gets the final word on whether the trigger was in fact met — a central court, a chamber representing the constituent units, the lower-level legislatures collectively, or nobody at all, the question being left to political forbearance; and whether the set of people affected by a decision has to match the set of people voting on it.

For the leaves, please make sure the following appear, named as practitioners would name them: dual or layer-cake federalism of the older American kind; American-style conditional-spending federalism constrained by the rule that the centre may not conscript state executives or legislatures; German executive federalism with its dense joint decision-making and its requirement that federal concurrent legislation be shown to be necessary; the Swiss arrangement with its presumption in favour of the cantons and its test that the Confederation take on only tasks exceeding cantonal capacity or needing uniform regulation; the EU's combination of conferred powers, a subsidiarity test that applies only outside exclusive competence, and the mechanism by which national parliaments can raise a reasoned objection; and a version of subsidiarity in the older Catholic-social-teaching sense on which the higher body owes the lower one positive assistance and capacity-building rather than mere restraint. Each leaf should carry one sentence naming the commitment its defenders would least like to own, and one concrete policy illustration. Beyond that, I want three things that handouts of this kind usually fudge: an explicit list of which pairs of leaves your tree cannot really separate, and why; the unoccupied combinations in your space, with a verdict on each as to whether the vacancy reflects incoherence, unattractiveness, or mere neglect (I am particularly curious about the combination in which lower-level authority is held as of right, the centre may act directly on individuals, and no institution has standing to police the trigger); and your view on whether the regress problem — that the principle never says whether the relevant "local" level is the commune, the region, the nation, or a cross-border basin authority — belongs on the tree as a node or on a separate axis entirely.

Two further constraints. First, please thread one synthetic running case through the whole instrument so that each leaf can be shown making a different ruling on it: a river basin spanning four constituent units of a federation, in which the upstream unit's permissive land-use policy causes recurring downstream flooding, the downstream unit with the worst exposure is the poorest and cannot afford the retention works, and one of the four units contains a concentrated linguistic minority that objects to basin-wide building standards on cultural grounds. For that case, each leaf should say who may legislate, who pays, and who may compel the recalcitrant upstream unit. Second, this is a classification exercise and not an argument: I do not want a ranking of the positions, a defence of any leaf, or a concluding paragraph on which arrangement is best. Do not include citations, case names, article numbers, or a reading list — describe each institution in your own words, since my students will not have the primary texts in front of them and I would rather have a self-contained handout.
