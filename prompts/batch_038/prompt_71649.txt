You are a philosopher of biology who has worked on the levels-of-selection debate for many years and who has set, marked, and moderated a great many examination papers in it. Please think carefully and work in the order laid out below: I want your diagnosis of the difficulty before any drafting.

**The course.** I teach an upper-level philosophy of biology course — twenty-four students, mostly final-year undergraduates with a few beginning graduates. They have had a full term of elementary population-genetic modelling: they can follow a one-locus, two-allele calculation, they have met the Price equation in its simplest two-level form, and they know Hamilton's rule. They have also had the standard philosophical framing of the units-of-selection debate, including the distinction between cases in which group structure changes the frequency of an individual-level trait and cases in which groups themselves reproduce and group-level traits are inherited across group generations. I do not want the examination to reward reciting that literature. A student who has never read a page of it should be able to earn full marks; a student who can recite it fluently but cannot handle a concrete case should not.

**The scenario.** I have constructed the following and want the question built on it. Please treat it as given, but check it before you use it.

A hypothetical seed beetle. Each female lays a clutch of ten eggs in a single fallen fruit and dies; the larvae develop inside that fruit, emerge, and mate at random in a common dispersal pool. One diploid locus, alleles A and B; random mating, no dominance, no mutation, no migration, density-independent development.

Larvae carrying A bore a shared exit tunnel used by all ten occupants of the fruit. Payoffs: every larva has a baseline of 2.0 surviving adults' worth; each A larva's tunnelling raises the fruit's total yield by 1.6 larvae, and that increment is shared equally among all ten occupants; each A larva additionally pays a private cost of 0.6.

Because a clutch is laid by a single mated female, fruit composition is fixed by the mother's genotype: a BB mother's fruit contains ten B larvae, an AB mother's contains five A and five B, an AA mother's contains ten A.

In the parental generation A is at frequency p = 0.20 among 1,000 fruit-colonising females, giving 40 AA fruits, 320 AB fruits, and 640 BB fruits. Per-fruit adult payoffs are then: AA fruits, 3.0 per larva, all A; AB fruits, 2.2 per A and 2.8 per B; BB fruits, 2.0 per B. Totals: 4,720 A offspring against 17,280 B offspring, so p′ = 4,720/22,000 ≈ 0.215. A therefore rises in frequency even though, in every fruit that contains both types, B outproduces A by 0.6.

Before drafting anything, verify this arithmetic line by line and confirm that both qualitative claims hold — that A is disfavoured within mixed fruits and favoured in the population as a whole. If you find an inconsistency, repair it with the smallest change possible and tell me what you changed. As a further sanity check, state whether Hamilton's rule is satisfied for an A larva in an AB fruit, given that its nine patch-mates are full siblings, and say whether that formulation and the two-level partition agree about the direction of change.

**What to write.**

1. A short diagnosis, no more than two hundred words: where the real difficulty of this question lies, and what a fluent, well-organised answer can say that is nevertheless wrong.

2. The examination question exactly as students will see it, with the scenario and its parameters stated cleanly, three sub-parts, and a mark allocation summing to 25. It sits on a three-hour paper and should be budgeted at roughly fifty-five minutes. Part (a) should require students to derive the fruit-composition classes and the offspring totals themselves and to locate, in the numbers, both the within-fruit disadvantage and the whole-population advantage; the question should nevertheless state the result p′ ≈ 0.215, so that a slip in arithmetic does not lock a student out of the rest. Part (b) should set two student responses against each other — one saying this is plainly group selection, since fruits with more A outproduce fruits with fewer and that outweighs A's within-fruit disadvantage, the other saying that nothing at all happens at the level of the fruit, since every A larva shares its fruit with nine full siblings, so what is going on is gene-level selection with assortment — and should ask whether these claims conflict, and what each is committed to. Part (c) should be the crux: both the two-level partition of the change in frequency and the Hamilton's-rule inequality are applied to the very same data, and the partition is an algebraic identity; does it follow that the disagreement in (b) is merely verbal? Students must defend an answer and must describe some modification of the beetle case in which the two readings would no longer be two ways of describing the same change. Be precise in the marking scheme about what counts as an acceptable modification here: changing the payoff numbers is not one, since it leaves the two readings tracking the same facts; the modification has to concern how group structure is transmitted across generations.

3. A model answer of about a thousand words, which does the arithmetic explicitly and which states clearly what would have to be true for exactly one of the two readings to be correct.

4. A marking scheme of six to eight must-hit moves, with the marks each carries summing to 25, and one line per move on the characteristic way a competent but not excellent student botches it.

5. A discrimination section: two or three things a first-class answer says that a good answer omits, and a sketch of the most seductive wrong answer — the one that concludes from the fact that both bookkeeping schemes describe the same numbers that the dispute cannot be substantive — together with the precise point at which that inference fails.

6. A brief note on whether fifty-five minutes is realistic for this, and if not, what you would cut.

Two standing constraints. No part of the question, the model answer, or the marking scheme should turn on knowing what any particular biologist or philosopher has argued, and there should be no citations, reading lists, or named-author comparisons anywhere in your response. And please do not pad: if a section can be shorter without losing content, make it shorter.
