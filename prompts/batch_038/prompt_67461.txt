Forty years on a physics bench, and only lately I've got properly into philosophy, where I keep running into Aumann's result that two rational people who share a prior and both know what the other has concluded simply can't agree to disagree. It sits badly with my instincts, which runs like this:

- Two of my old post-docs, same data set, same training, both honest: they announce different estimates for the same parameter and I say something has gone wrong, one of them is fooling himself.
- Me and my brother on the football. We've watched every match, we've argued it for thirty years, we each know the other isn't stupid. That's no scandal at all — different households made us.
- Two doctors from different schools, both shown the same scans and the same bloods, still give different diagnoses. Not fine, in my book, even though their backgrounds differ just as much as mine and my brother's do.
- A believer friend of mine and me on whether there's a god. We've swapped everything we've got over decades and each thinks the other sincere and sharp. Fine.
- A colleague whose numbers I knew were shaped to please a funder: our disagreement was fine, and I was under no duty to split the difference with him.
- And the one I can't settle: someone tells me my priors are as arbitrary as his, that I only got lucky being raised where I was. I grant him the premise entirely and still think I'm the one who's right.

Is there a principle that gets all of these, or close? State it precisely, clause by clause, with the case that motivates each clause; then give me its verdict on one case I haven't mentioned that you think is diagnostic; and be straight with me about which of my verdicts it has to overturn. If a simpler principle buys most of the list by sacrificing one or two, say which trade you'd take. Put the principle itself up front in a sentence or two before you unpack it, and stop at 450 words.
