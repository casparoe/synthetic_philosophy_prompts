I am working through how Rawls's difference principle operates as a criterion of justice, as distinct from the maximin rule the parties in the original position are said to employ, and I have arrived at a case I cannot resolve on my own.

Consider two schemes, S1 and S2, identical in every respect save the internal distribution of primary goods within the least-advantaged representative position. In both, the expectation for that position takes the same index value, and that value exceeds the expectation of the least-advantaged position under any other available scheme. But in S1 the goods are distributed tightly within the group, whereas in S2 they are distributed widely: a substantial minority of its members end up far below the group's expectation, through unemployment, illness, or family breakdown, with no separate provision catching them.

Is the difference principle indifferent between S1 and S2? I want an explanation that keeps the decision rule separate from the principle and identifies the source of the indifference, if indifference it is: does it follow from the principle being framed over expectations of representative positions rather than over individuals, from the index problem, or from constraints on what the parties are permitted to reason with? If the principle is indifferent, how is that to be squared with the fact that maximin is ordinarily presented as an aversion to the worst case rather than to a poor average? I would also like to know whether the social bases of self-respect, the strains of commitment, or the conditions of chain connection and close-knittedness have any purchase here, or whether they are being asked to do work they cannot do.

Please be explicit about which parts of the apparatus carry the weight at each step, and say plainly where you think a Rawlsian would insist the case has been misdescribed rather than answered.
