You are a philosopher of statistics who works across formal epistemology and research methodology. Before committing to a verdict, brainstorm privately the full list of candidate concepts that might already cover what I am about to describe, and only then decide which, if any, fits.

The pattern runs like this. Someone is asked for the probability of a single, unrepeatable event — whether a given regime survives the next five years, whether this patient's graft fails, whether a particular crisis would have escalated had the ambassador not died of a heart attack. A number is produced by locating the event in a class of past cases and reporting a frequency inside that class. When the number is challenged, the class is redrawn: "regimes" becomes "personalist regimes," then "personalist regimes under fiscal strain," then "personalist regimes under fiscal strain whose military had been defeated within the previous decade." Each narrowing is offered as a gain in relevance, and each is supported by a real causal story. The consequence is that some defensible class can be found for almost any outcome, so the original question looks as though it never had a determinate answer; and yet the practice does not change. The numbers are still published, still used to price policies and to settle bets, and disagreements about which class is correct are argued as though they were empirical questions that better data would resolve.

Is there an established term for this? Take the obvious neighbours in turn — the reference class problem, the problem of the single case, external validity, Goodman-style predicate gerrymandering, hypothesising after the results are known — and say for each why it is close and precisely where it falls short. I am especially interested in whether the features that strike me as distinctive (that the classification is discretionary, settled only after the target event is known, and answerable to no evidence one could go and gather) are treated anywhere as a separate pathology, or whether the literature simply folds them into the reference class problem. If nothing fits, say so plainly and coin a term, defining it by boundaries rather than by illustrations alone: specify what makes a narrowing of the class a legitimate refinement instead of the thing I mean, and give one case that sits genuinely on the line.

Finally, does the diagnosis depend on which interpretation of probability one adopts? My hunch is that a subjectivist ought to find nothing troubling here, a frequentist ought to find it fatal, and a propensity theorist ought to land somewhere third — but I may have that backwards, so tell me what each view actually licenses.
