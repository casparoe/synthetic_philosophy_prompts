I started at a tiny criminal-law reform charity three weeks ago and my first real assignment is a submission arguing for decriminalising some consensual adult conduct that lots of people find revolting rather than harmful — I'm not going to say which, it doesn't matter for what I'm asking. At the kickoff my manager said something like "just give them Wolfenden, the argument's already been made" and everybody nodded, me included, and then I went home and realised I couldnt actually tell you what the Wolfenden argument is beyond the general vibe of it. Reading around I keep getting pushed back behind Wolfenden to Bentham, and specifically to that fragment of his on offences against one's self which he wrote in the 1780s and then never published — apparently it only saw print in the 1970s, and the editor found notes in the manuscript showing how nervous he was about the whole thing. What I want is to understand the 1957 moment through Bentham's actual machinery rather than through the harm-principle slogan everyone repeats, because my hunch is he'd have liked the committee's conclusion and hated the reasoning that got them there.

So: take the Wolfenden Report (1957) and the Hart–Devlin arguement it set off, and run them as a Benthamite case study. Here are the facts I'm treating as given, stop me if any are wrong: the committee recommended decriminalising homosexual acts between consenting adults in private; Parliament partly implemented it ten years later; Devlin's 1959 lecture argued that a society is held together by a shared morality which it may defend with the criminal law, and that the measure of what to forbid is the reasonable man's feelings of intolerance, indignation and disgust; Hart replied in the early sixties with something closer to Mill, distinguishing the morality a society happens to hold from a morality that can actually be defended, and attacking Devlin's disintegration claim as an empirical assertion with no evidence behind it. Now do the mapping. Which of Bentham's moves in the unpublished fragment does the Report reproduce, and which does it skip? I'm thinking of his line of attack that the act has no non-consenting victim, that the punishment is a far greater evil than the offence, that criminalisation hands a weapon to blackmailers and informers and multiplies false accusations, and that the secondary mischief — the alarm the offence spreads through the community — is trivial next to the alarm the prosecutions spread. Set those against his four cases in which punishment is misapplied (groundless, inefficacious, unprofitable, needless) and tell me which of the four is doing the real work in 1957 and which ones the committee never touched at all.

Then the harder part, which is the bit I actually need. First: Devlin's disgust, on Bentham's own hedonic accounting, is a real pain felt by real people — so does a straight felicific calculation have to count it, which would hand Devlin half the argument? Bentham's escape is supposed to be that approving or disapproving of an act merely because you feel that way is a different principle altogether, the one he calls sympathy and antipathy, not a utilitarian calculation at all; I want to know whether that move genuinely blocks Devlin or just relabels him, and how much daylight is left between them once you notice Bentham happily counts alarm and unease as harms elsewhere. Second: Wolfenden carves out a private sphere the law has no business entering, and that smells like a side constraint — Bentham had nothing but contempt for natural rights, so is that sphere even available to him, or is he forced to say the recommendation is only contingently correct and reversible the moment the numbers shift? Third: he wrote the argument down and then buried it for the rest of his life, which on his own theory was either a cowardly miscalculation or a perfectly accurate estimate of the consequences of publishing — say which, and what it costs the theory either way. Fourth: is Hart the Benthamite in this fight, or is he smuggling in Mill's liberty principle in a form Bentham would have called question-begging? Be explicit about the places where Bentham's framework simply has nothing to say about a 1950s dispute of that shape, instead of stretching it until it covers everything, and please no reading list at the end — I want your reasoning, not references.
