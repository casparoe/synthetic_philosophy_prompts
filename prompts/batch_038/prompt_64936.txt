I'm mid-draft on a novel and I've hit a wall that turns out to be a philosophy wall rather than a plot wall, so bear with me.

The setup: my narrator Adèle has spent eleven years writing letters to someone she knows only as V. Never met, never seen a photograph, no surname ever exchanged. V is warm, funny, tells her everything. Separately, Adèle is a critic, and for those same eleven years she's built her reputation demolishing the work of a reclusive painter she knows only as Ilse Brandt, whom she considers a fraud and a coward. Same person, obviously. Adèle doesn't know it, and neither does the reader — I'm not running dramatic irony here, I want the reader blindsided at exactly the moment she is.

Then chapter 38. Two things land at once: Adèle finds out V is Brandt, and she finds out that every cruel thing she ever wrote to V *about* Brandt, she wrote to the woman herself. The second hit is way bigger than the first, which surprised me, and it's the part I need help with.

Here's a coinage of mine, use it or bin it: I've been calling the amount of damage a reveal can do the reveal tax, and I'd been assuming the tax is proportional to how much the character didn't already know. But I've now written a scene where the tax is enormous and, on one account of what knowing is, she arguably learns nothing. On the view where a name carries its bearer and no more, whatever Adèle comes to believe at the reveal is something she was already committed to all along — she believed all those things about V, she believed all those things about Brandt, and the identity was true the whole time. So where does the voltage come from? On the other view, the one where the two names differ in their mode of presentation or whatever you call it, the voltage is obvious, but then it looks like I've bought my climax by multiplying entities, and I'm not comfortable that I've explained anything rather than just stuck a label on the thing that needed explaining.

So can you explain the underlying conceptual issue to me properly? Not a survey — I can get a survey. Specifically:

- Why does the puzzle bite at all? I want the actual pressure point, the thing that makes an identity statement between two names feel informative when a thing-is-itself statement doesn't.
- What does the sense-based answer really commit you to, and is it earning its keep? There's a worry I can't shake that the sense is just a name for the puzzle rather than a solution to it. Tell me whether that worry is fair, and what makes one mode of presentation the right one for a given name.
- What does the direct-reference camp say instead, and — this is where I'm most lost — what are the live candidates for the thing that changes in Adèle at the reveal? I gather there are several distinct proposals and I can't tell them apart. I want them separated out and costed.
- Then the nastier cousin: the case where the character ends up holding two beliefs that flatly contradict each other and has no way of noticing, the bilingual one with the city that's pretty in one language and ugly in the other. Why is that supposed to be worse than the ordinary identity case, and does it apply to Adèle? Because there's a version of what she wrote to V that she would never have assented to if she'd seen the sentence taking that shape, and I want to know whether that means she believed it.
- Finally: which of these accounts actually predicts my reveal tax? I want to know whether the size of the emotional hit tracks anything semantic at all, or whether I've been measuring psychology and calling it information.

Two or three sentences of bottom line first — which account you'd bet on for my chapter and why — then walk me through all of the above, then close with a table comparing the accounts. The table should have exactly these columns:

- account — a short name for it
- Adèle's belief state before chapter 38 — one sentence, in the account's own terms
- what literally changes at the reveal
- does she acquire a new proposition — yes / no / not the right question, with a phrase saying why
- what it predicts about the size of the reveal tax
- where it creaks — the cost or the objection you'd raise against it yourself

One row per account, including at minimum the sense-based one, the direct-reference-plus-something-psychological one, and whatever account treats the problem as being about belief reports rather than about beliefs. Add a fourth row if you think there's another serious contender I've missed.

Don't send me off to the literature — I'm not going to read it, I have a deadline. Just explain it to me like I'm smart but haven't taken a philosophy class since I was nineteen.
