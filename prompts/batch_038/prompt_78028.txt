I am preparing a seminar paper for a history-and-philosophy-of-science module in which we are asked to treat a historical author's scattered rulings as a body of case law, and I have chosen Galileo's scriptural hermeneutics — chiefly what he says in the 1613 letter to Benedetto Castelli and the longer 1615 letter to the Grand Duchess Christina. My tutor dislikes the lazy framing in which Galileo simply invented the warfare model of science and religion, and my classmate has gone to the other extreme, insisting that Galileo is effectively a separation-of-magisteria thinker for whom scripture has jurisdiction only over salvation and therefore never competes with natural philosophy at all. I suspect that reading is too clean, because it cannot explain why Galileo keeps insisting that the literal sense stands until demonstration forces us off it, and why in 1615 he is content to hold Copernicanism merely as a hypothesis rather than as a settled truth. What I want to do for the paper is lay out his actual exegetical verdicts as precedents, work out which principles they are jointly consistent with, and then test those principles on a passage he never addressed.

Here is the precedent set as I have it, and please tell me if you think I have got any of it wrong historically, since I am working from memory of the two letters rather than from an open book. P1: the anthropomorphic texts — those that give God hands, feet and eyes, and those in which God repents or is angered — are read non-literally, and without any appeal to demonstration, on the grounds that whoever binds scripture to the letter in natural matters is obliged to swallow these absurdities as well. P2: the cluster of verses saying that the earth is founded and cannot be moved, together with the opening of Ecclesiastes, where the earth abides for ever while the sun rises and sets and hurries back to its place, is treated as speech accommodated to ordinary appearance and to the persuasive purpose of the passage, not as a cosmological assertion; the supporting move is a reductio, that if the sun's rising and setting is literal then the earth's abiding for ever must be literal too, which lands one in impiety. P3: the halted sun of Joshua 10 is not merely dispensable but self-defeating on the very cosmology its defenders hold, since stopping the sun there would have shortened the day rather than lengthened it as the narrative reports; so the passage demands a non-obvious exegesis, and once the earth's motion is demonstrated the miracle can be described in terms consistent with that system, with the further point that a text reporting a miracle cannot be read as a report of ordinary natural mechanism. P4: the Copernican system itself, as of 1615, is not yet demonstrated and is therefore to be held as a hypothesis, but the conditional is explicit — were it demonstrated, the theologians would have to revise their exegesis rather than deny the demonstration. P5: no private individual, and least of all a mathematician, may declare a passage non-literal on his own authority, that being the business of the Church and its councils advised by the Fathers; yet the Fathers' incidental remarks about nature carry no weight in natural philosophy, since they wrote to persuade ordinary people about salvation and did not profess to demonstrate anything. P6: over all of it sits the framework rule that nature and scripture proceed from the same source and cannot genuinely contradict, so every apparent conflict is a defect either in our exegesis or in our physics.

The new case is the firmament of Genesis 1, verses 6 to 8: the vault stretched out in the midst of the waters, dividing the waters below it from the waters above it. It has the following features, which I think make it a sharp test rather than a repeat of P2 and P3. It concerns the physical constitution of the heavens and not salvation. Nothing demonstrative settles it in 1615 — there is no geometrical proof that the celestial region contains neither solid orbs nor water, and Galileo's own positive cosmology is still hypothetical by P4. There is, however, a good deal of powerful but non-demonstrative evidence in circulation: the Medicean stars circling Jupiter, the telescopic moon with its earthlike relief and its borrowed rather than self-generated light, the new star of 1604, and the comet arguments about whether the planetary orbs can be solid and continuous. The Fathers are not unanimous on it, some taking the waters above literally, some reading them as spiritual substances or angels, some allegorising the whole. And unlike P2 and P3, where scripture appeared to resist the new physics, here a further question arises: whether scripture may be enlisted as positive evidence for a cosmology at all, and if so whether that licence survives the framework rule of P6.

What I need from you is this. First, identify the candidate hermeneutical principles that are jointly consistent with all six precedents — my own short list would include a soteriological-domain principle of the kind my classmate favours, a demonstration-gated principle on which the literal sense is the default and only strict demonstration displaces it, an accommodation or appearance-semantics principle on which cosmological verses are never even candidates for physical claims, a miracle-exemption principle, an institutional-deference principle, and a uniformity principle derived from the reductio in P1 and P2 — but tell me if you think some other principle, or a weighted combination, does better work, and say which precedents each principle explains and which it strains or contradicts outright. Second, give me the verdict each surviving principle predicts on the firmament passage, distinguishing carefully between holding the literal sense, abandoning it, treating it as non-binding but not false, and refusing to rule at all pending the Church. Third, tell me which precedent is the closest analog and why, and give me your single best guess about what Galileo would say, with the reasons for preferring that principle over its leading rival, and an honest statement of whether my precedent set genuinely underdetermines the verdict — if it does, say what principle he would be adopting by ruling either way, and what that principle would cost him elsewhere, in particular whether it would undercut his handling of Joshua 10 or his hypothetical stance on Copernicanism. Fourth, name the further case that would best tell the principles apart: from the passages I am considering — the earth hung upon nothing in Job 26, the two great lights with the stars also in Genesis 1 verse 16, the circle of the earth with the heavens stretched as a curtain in Isaiah 40, the sun's circuit from one end of heaven to the other in Psalm 19, and the floodgates of heaven in Genesis 7 and 8 — say which one most sharply separates the leading rivals and how they diverge on it, or propose a better test passage of your own.

Please do not give me sources, a bibliography, or a reading list; this is for a closed-book viva and I need the reasoning, not the references. Write the answer as continuous prose, ending with one sentence that states your recommended verdict in the plainest terms, and then follow it with a single table using exactly these columns, with one row per candidate principle: rule_id, a short label; rule, the principle in one or two sentences as it applies to a biblical passage; precedents_explained, which of P1 to P6 it accounts for and how; precedents_strained, which it fits badly or contradicts; verdict_on_firmament, one of literal retained, literal abandoned, non-binding, or deferred, plus a clause of reasoning; confidence, high, medium or low; and discriminating_case, the passage that most sharply separates this principle from its leading rival, with a clause saying how the two come apart there. Put an asterisk after the rule_id of the principle you judge Galileo actually to be running.
