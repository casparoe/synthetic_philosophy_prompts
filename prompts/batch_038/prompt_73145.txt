I'm a junior, and for the past two years I have hand-lettered the posters for my school's theater department. The question became urgent for me last spring, when I traced an 'a' I had lettered for one poster into a completely different alphabet for another: on its own the tracing still looked like a perfectly good 'a', but in the new setting it looked wrong in a way I could not explain. This year I am doing an independent study on that problem — what makes every 'a' an 'a'? — and my teacher has asked me to bring in a case I have designed rather than a summary of the debate. I would like your help building one to a specification.

I have been reading two kinds of material. On one side are the ordinary philosophical accounts of categories: a mark is an 'a' because it has some set of features, or because it matches a best example, or because it shares overlapping similarities with enough other 'a's while sharing nothing with all of them. On the other side is Douglas Hofstadter's Letter Spirit project, which as I understand it tried to get a program to draw the remaining letters of an alphabet in the style of a handful of sample letters, on the assumption that style belongs to a whole alphabet rather than to any single glyph. Those two pictures seem to disagree about something basic, and I want a scenario that forces the disagreement into the open.

Call the first the resemblance view: whether a mark is an 'a' is settled by that mark taken by itself together with the reader's experience of 'a's. History, setting, and neighboring letters do no work. Call the second the coherence view: a letterform is not self-contained, and part of what makes a glyph the letter it is lies in how it fits with the rest of its alphabet — so that one and the same drawing can be an 'a' in one family and fail to be an 'a' in another, not because the drawing changed but because its neighbors did.

The case I want is a single glyph and two alphabets. Describe the glyph in words precisely enough that I could draw it with a brush pen without seeing a picture — what it does with its bowl, its counter, its aperture, its stem, its terminals, its stroke weight. Then describe two hand-lettered alphabets that differ from each other only in their other letters, covering the four or five letters that actually matter. Shown by itself to ordinary literate readers, the glyph is an unproblematic 'a'. Set inside the first alphabet, it still reads as 'a' without effort. Set inside the second, it is read as some specific rival letter of that alphabet, and that failure has to follow from the geometry of the family rather than being announced. The same physical drawing must appear in both contexts.

Constraints: the setting must be realistic — my posters, a classmate's font, a school newspaper masthead — with no imaginary readers, no unusual perceptual training, no computer models, and no science fiction. The scenario itself should be one paragraph, with a sentence or two per alphabet. Please do not build the case on the difference between the single-story and the double-story 'a', which is the first example every write-up reaches for, and do not build it on an unfamiliar script; I want the engine to be style, not a fact about the history of writing.

After the scenario, I want the demonstration that it works:

- Walk through what each view says about the two placements, and why. The resemblance view should be shown to be unable to tell them apart, not merely to be less tidy about them.
- Identify which feature of the case is doing the work, and tell me what happens if I remove or weaken it — in particular, whether it collapses into a glyph that is simply ambiguous everywhere, which would prove nothing.
- Tell me how a defender of the resemblance view would attack the case. If their best move is to insist that resemblance is always to the letters nearby rather than to 'a's in general, say whether that is still the resemblance view or a surrender to the coherence view.
- Say whether the structure generalizes: does this show something special about letters, or could an equally good case be built for a category whose members do not belong to a system of contrasts, like chairs?

Then a harder second part. Build the mirror case: a mark that would not be called an 'a' at all when shown alone, but that reads immediately and correctly as the 'a' of its alphabet once its family is around it. One paragraph, with the geometry explained rather than asserted, and under the same constraints about realism and about the single-story/double-story shortcut. Tell me which of the two directions was harder to construct and why. If the mirror case cannot be built honestly, say so rather than faking it.

So that you know what counts as cheating: the point of the exercise is to test whether a letter's identity can be a property of a shape in isolation. A scenario that simply stipulates that readers misread the glyph in one context, or that assigns the verdicts by label instead of making them fall out of the two views, does not help me. I also cannot look anything up while I work on this and my teacher does not want a bibliography — I want the design and the reasoning, not references. Finally, if any part of this specification turns out to be unsatisfiable as stated, tell me which constraint has to go and why, rather than quietly dropping it.
