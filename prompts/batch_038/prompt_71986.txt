At a policy seminar on foreign aid, a panelist waved off the case for unconditional cash transfers by reciting the old proverb — give a man a fish and you feed him for a day, teach a man to fish and you feed him for a lifetime — as though it settled whether aid should buy consumption or build capacity. I want to push back, but not with a rival slogan, so I need the sentence taken apart first. Could you lay out the distinct claims it might be making? Among the questions I have in mind: what exactly is being measured by 'feed', and over what horizon, given that 'a day' and 'a lifetime' are being compared rather than added; what the proverb assumes about why someone lacks fish in the first place — missing skill, missing capital, missing access to the water, missing markets, missing security of tenure — and whether the lesson survives if the binding constraint is something other than know-how; what the 'teaching' half presupposes about who does the teaching, at what cost per person taught, and with what success rate; whether the two gifts are contrasted in kind or only in durability, since cash can also buy the net, the boat, or the school fees; and whether the second half is a prediction, a claim about dependence and dignity, or a ranking of aims. For each reading, tell me whether it is a platitude, a testable empirical hypothesis (with a rough sense of what evidence would bear on it), a normative thesis in disguise, or straightforwardly false or question-begging. End by saying which readings, if any, would actually license preferring training programs to cash, and which uses of the proverb in that seminar were doing no work at all. Please give this as a numbered menu I can bring to the next session, not as a continuous essay.
