I am a fourth-year doctoral student in philosophy, and my comprehensive examination includes a comparative epistemology component on which one of my three readers is a Sanskritist who works primarily on Buddhist pramāṇa theory. The reduction of the Nyāya four to the Buddhist two is therefore very much on my mind, and my adviser has asked me to circulate a short statement of where I actually stand before the oral, so that the committee can attack my view rather than my exegesis. What follows is that statement, written as baldly as I can manage it. I would like a consistency audit before it goes out: which of these commitments genuinely pull against one another, which only look as though they do, what the cheapest repair is in each case, and — since I would rather make one large revision than six small ones — which single change would clean up the most.

1. There are exactly four pramāṇas — perception, inference, comparison, and verbal testimony — and none is a species of, or a mere conjunction of, the others. I individuate them by their characteristic output: by the sort of true cognition each produces, not by the causal process that runs to it. The causal story is usually a tangle of contributing conditions, and in any case sources of knowledge ought to be classified by what they deliver to the subject.

2. Perception is a non-erroneous cognition arising when a sense faculty is in contact with an object. What troubles me is that this seems to make the notion incapable of failing: if only the non-erroneous cognitions count as perception, then the claim that perception is reliable looks like a stipulation rather than a discovery, and I have never been able to say what distinguishes it from defining knowledge as true belief and then announcing that knowledge is infallible. I do take perceptual error to be real and to need explaining — the rope taken for a snake, the shell taken for silver — and I accept the standard line that in such a case the silver is something real, only it is not here.

3. Absences are perceived rather than delivered by any special source: looking at the bare table, I am directly aware of the absence of the pot, and I see no need for the Bhāṭṭa's separate pramāṇa of non-apprehension. But I also hold that contact between a faculty and an object is what perception consists in, and an absence does not look like the sort of thing a sense faculty could run into.

4. Testimony is irreducible, and the hearer's warrant does not consist in any belief about the speaker: children, and adults attending to something else, acquire knowledge from being told things constantly, without forming any view whatever about the source, and if such a view were required they would almost never have any knowledge at all. At the same time I think that for a competent adult, entitlement to accept a telling must rest on something like an implicit inference about the speaker's competence and honesty, since otherwise acceptance is a reflex rather than a rational move; and I hold that āptatva, in the cases where we bother to establish it, is established by inference from what we have independently learned about the person.

5. Comparison is a pramāṇa in its own right. The cognition it yields — this is the gavaya, the animal the forester's sentence was about — is a relational awareness of a resemblance; no pervasion licenses it, so it is not inference; the words by themselves did not produce it, so it is not testimony; and I am not persuaded that resemblance as such is an object of the senses, so it is not perception either. Yet I also think the case works only because the hearer has retained the earlier sentence and now recognizes the animal before him as resembling the cow, which means the causal chain plainly runs through a memory and through somebody else's words.

6. Inference depends on pervasion; pervasion is grasped through observation of agreement in presence and in absence; no such observation can ever cover the instances that matter; to establish a pervasion by a further inference would require a pervasion already in hand, and so on without end; and I have no appetite for the theistic repair the tradition has on offer, of an intelligent being who keeps causal relations steady, because I do not want my account of inference to lean on a metaphysics I cannot independently defend. I nonetheless insist that we have a great deal of inferential knowledge and that none of this is a reason to fall back on perception alone.

7. I am an externalist: whether a cognition amounts to knowledge is a matter of how it was produced, not of whether its subject is in a position to vindicate it. I accept the Nyāya view that the truth of a cognition is not apprehended along with the cognition itself but only afterward, typically through the success of the activity it prompts.

Please rank the genuine tensions by severity rather than reporting everything that sits uneasily next to everything else; my suspicion is that two or three of the items above are perfectly stable on inspection, and I want to be told which, and shown the distinction that does the stabilizing. Where a tension is real, say which commitment has to go or be narrowed, what the minimal reformulation looks like, and what I lose by adopting it — including, specifically, whether any of the repairs concedes ground to the two-pramāṇa reduction I am trying to resist, since that is the concession I can least afford to make in front of this committee. I am not after a survey of the schools, a taxonomy of positions, or pointers to literature; I want the audit of this particular set, and a clear answer to the question of which single revision buys the most coherence.
