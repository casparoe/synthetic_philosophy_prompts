Until last month I was the information governance lead at a housing association in the north of England. Reviewing access logs, I found that a maintenance contractor we use had been photographing the interiors of tenants' homes during routine visits — images showing the tenants themselves, their medication, their children, their disability equipment — and uploading them to a commercial database used by tenant-screening and insurance-pricing firms. On my count twenty-six households are affected, and the collection is continuing.

Warning the tenants individually is not an option. The contractor can see its own job queue and would know within a day; it would purge everything and deny the lot. Our director has declined to act, on the grounds that the contract runs until next spring and a dispute would cost more than the arrangement is worth. The Information Commissioner's Office will not open an investigation on the strength of an anonymous tip from somebody who cannot produce a single document. Please assume the procedural routes are exhausted: I have raised it twice in writing, and I am not looking for a list of escalations.

There is one document that would suffice. A single tenant's complete file contains her photographs, her medical information and her tenancy record. Giving it to a journalist would expose the practice and end it. But that tenant already knows about the photography, and she wrote to us in terms that could not have been clearer that she does not want her details passed to anyone; I personally gave her an assurance that we would not.

A colleague in compliance says the decision is arithmetic: one breach of confidence to stop twenty-six. She adds that if I am unwilling to do it myself, I should ask the temporary records clerk — who has access to the file and owes this tenant no personal assurance — to copy it and post it instead. The tenant's privacy is breached either way, she says; the only difference is whose hands are on it, and that difference cannot be what matters. In fact, she argues, the second plan is the better one, because it achieves the same result without my breaking a promise I made.

I cannot argue back. I simply find the second plan worse than the first, and both worse than letting the twenty-six go on being photographed, which I know sounds indefensible. Is my colleague right that what I am feeling is squeamishness dressed as principle? If there is something real in the distinction I keep reaching for, what exactly is it, and does it survive the case she has made against it? And what ought I to do on Monday morning?
