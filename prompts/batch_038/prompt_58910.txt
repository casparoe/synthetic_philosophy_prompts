I'm on the conduct committee for a 60-person residential co-op and we're rewriting the bylaws section on private sexual conduct — the part about what the community can actually sanction, as opposed to just gossip about. I've been reading Bentham on this (the decriminalization argument, plus his stuff about when punishment is groundless, ineffective, too expensive or just needless), and I want to sanity-check whether my gut verdicts add up to anything or are just vibes. Here they are, roughly in the order I thought of them:

1. Two members, both single, both consenting, never told anyone. Somebody finds out and half the house is revolted. Not sanctionable. The revulsion is real but it doesn't get you anywhere.
2. Same facts, except one of them has a partner outside the house who doesn't know. Sanctionable — but I think what's sanctionable is the lying, and the sex is just the place where the lying happened.
3. A member keeps a private journal of fantasies that would make most of us sick. It gets leaked and read aloud at a meeting. Not sanctionable. Nothing was done to anybody.
4. A member who can't stop narrating his sex life to people who didn't ask. Sanctionable — but again the wrong is the volume, not the content, and I want the bylaws to say that in a way that doesn't sound like a distinction without a difference.
5. A senior member (controls the housing list, writes the references) and a junior one. Both adults, both enthusiastic. Sanctionable. And here's where I squirm: if the junior gets an outside reference, moves out, and they're still together and happy twenty years on, I still think something was wrong back then. My harm story doesn't obviously have anywhere to put that.
6. A member who asks us to help enforce a vow of celibacy against themselves — report any lapse, pull their room if they slip. I'd refuse, even though it's it's own life and its own request. Which looks like it contradicts my whole consenting-adults-nobody-else-involved-hands-off line.
7. To catch case 1 we'd have to be watching bedrooms. I think the sanctionability of an act literally shifts depending on what catching it would cost us in privacy and in what it does to whoever does the catching. Some of the committee think that's cowardly relativism.

The principle I keep reaching for: the community may sanction conduct only if it produces a harm to someone other than the actor that would still have happened had nobody ever found out about it. Call it the silence test. Disgust fails it, because disgust needs an audience; the affair with a deceived partner passes, because that partner is being wronged whether or not they ever learn.

So:
- Run 1–7 through it. Which verdicts does it actually deliver, which does it deliver only with a fudge, and which does it flatly contradict?
- I already suspect it wrongly acquits the member who lies about an STI to a new partner, since that harm only materialises on discovery. Does that kill the test, or is there a distinction — harms that are *constituted* by the finding-out versus harms merely *revealed* by it — that saves it? If there is, state it precisely enough that I could put it in bylaws without a lawyer weeping.
- Then give me the principle as you'd finally write it, clause by clause, saying which case motivates each clause, plus its verdicts on three cases I haven't listed.
- Be honest about which of 1–7 it forces me to give up. I'd rather drop one verdict than end up with a principle that has nine seperate escape hatches.

And where Bentham's own categories do better work on 3, 6 and 7 than my silence test does, say so — I'd rather find out now than argue about it in a meeting.
