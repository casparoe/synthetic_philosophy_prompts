A recurring topic on the forums I read is when the Anthropocene began — the Neolithic, the Columbian Exchange, the mid-twentieth century — and for years I enjoyed the dispute without asking what would have to be true for anyone in it to be right. When I finally tried to write that question down, I got an argument whose conclusion I cannot accept.

(1) A boundary in the geologic time scale is discovered rather than stipulated only if there is a fact, independent of the ratifying commission's choice, about where it falls.
(2) The only marker ever put forward for the Anthropocene boundary that is both globally synchronous and physically detectable is the fallout radionuclide signal from mid-century weapons testing.
(3) But the Earth system did not turn over in the mid-1950s. The curves that matter — carbon dioxide, methane, fixed nitrogen, sediment flux, extinction rate — bend gradually across centuries and were already bending well before 1950.
(4) So the sharpness of the proposed marker belongs to the tracer, not to the transition: a near-instantaneous physical signal has been laid down on top of a slow process, and the boundary inherits its crispness from the tracer.
(5) Where the transition is gradual, the location of any boundary on it is fixed by decision; and by (1), whatever is fixed by decision is not discovered.
(6) Hence the Anthropocene boundary is not a discovery. And the reasoning reaches every other boundary that was fixed for workability on a more or less continuous record, which is what picking a golden spike amounts to, so nothing in the time scale is discovered either.
(7) Which suggests the 2024 rejection of the Anthropocene proposal was a moment of unguarded honesty: it was turned down because the interval was too short and the sediment record too thin to work with — criteria about usefulness, not about truth.

So the start-date debate is a verbal dispute, stratigraphy is conventionalist all the way down, and there is no fact of the matter about when we left the Holocene. I don't believe that. I am not a conventionalist about the K–Pg boundary, which I'd happily say is out there whether or not anyone ratifies it, and I don't want to give up (3) and (4), which look to me like plain empirical reporting rather than philosophy.

Which step do you reject, and what does rejecting it cost? I'd also like your view on whether this is a valid argument with one premise that is merely expensive to keep, or whether something slides in the middle — I genuinely can't tell which, so please don't defer to my framing. Assume I know the literature already; I want the diagnosis, not a reading list.
