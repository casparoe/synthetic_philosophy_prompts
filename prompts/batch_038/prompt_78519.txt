I'm a second-year PhD student in history of economic thought and I've been roped into running a workshop session on the socialist calculation debate for a mixed crowd — half of them are econ grad students who think the whole thing was settled by Lange in 1938 and are just being polite to the Austrians, and half are friends of mine on the left who think the "calculation problem" is a libertarian urban legend that nobody with a spreadsheet takes seriously. What I want to hand out is something that sorts people by what they'd actually say about concrete cases, not by whose team they think they're on. Nobody should be able to reach the right leaf by recognising a name.

So: a decision tree. The entry point should be something the whole room can nod at — an advanced economy has tens of millions of heterogeneous capital goods, nobody knows all the relevant local facts, and production has to be coordinated somehow. From there each branch node should be a question phrased so a student can answer it without any idea where it leads, and no node may mention a person, a school, or an -ism. The nodes should be built out of cases rather than out of slogans; please work in at least the following, wherever they fit best: whether you can price a loaf of bread correctly if you've never priced the flour mill that made it; the factory-floor worker who knows the bearing is about to go and has no way to report it except by fiddling a form; a giant retailer that plans internally, in physical units, across thousands of sites and does it rather well; a wartime board that rations and fixes prices for four years and still ships tanks; a solved optimisation programme that spits out shadow prices for every input — are those prices or something else wearing the name; and whether a supercomputer fed enough sensor data changes the answer or merely changes the shape of the question.

Leaves I want to see, at minimum: the view that rational allocation without market-generated factor prices is impossible in principle; the view that the trouble is dispersed, partly tacit knowledge and the absence of a discovery process rather than the arithmetic; trial-and-error market socialism with a central board setting prices by tâtonnement; the later computerised version of that same proposal; planning in labour-time or in physical units instead of money; negotiated coordination between devolved boards and unions rather than either a market or a centre; the mixed "feasible" position that keeps markets for some things and plans others; genuine market socialism with competing worker-owned firms; and at least one leaf that says the debate was never really about calculation but about incentives, exit, and politics. For each leaf give me one sentence on its most awkward commitment, and a tag saying whether it takes the impossibility to be conceptual or merely contingent.

Then two bits of honest bookkeeping. First, tell me which combinations of answers your questions make available but no real position occupies, and for each say whether that cell is empty because the view is incoherent, because it's unattractive, or because nobody has got round to it. Second, flag any pair of leaves your tree cannot actually separate — I'd bet on the two Austrian positions and on the early and late versions of the trial-and-error view — and say what you'd have to add to tell them apart.

Format: start with three sentences saying which single fork does most of the sorting work and which two positions you expect to end up conflating, then the tree itself, then the two bookkeeping sections. British English throughout, and please keep the whole thing under 1,200 words. Naming people on the leaves is fine, that's the point, but don't append a reading list or bibliography — and if you're not sure about a title, a date, or who wrote what, just leave it out rather than guess, I'd much rather have a vaguer tree than an invented one.
