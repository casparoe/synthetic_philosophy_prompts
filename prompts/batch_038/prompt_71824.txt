You are a professional philosopher whose work sits at the intersection of virtue ethics, moral psychology, and the philosophy of public memory, and who reads ancient and medieval biography alongside contemporary ethics. Please think carefully and work through this stepwise rather than answering from first impressions. Before you commit to a reconstruction, spend a short opening section brainstorming: list the candidate theories of character, of our knowledge of character, and of honor that could each make sense of some part of the practice described below, and note which parts of the practice each candidate fails to explain. Only then give your considered reconstruction.

I come at this as a layperson with some reading but no technical vocabulary. I read Plutarch and the saints' lives in school, and I have come to suspect that a certain modern institutional practice is a descendant of both, though I cannot say of which, or whether the two descendants are consistent with one another.

Here is the practice. Universities, hospitals, navies, cities, and foundations routinely attach the names of individuals to buildings, wings, streets, ships, lecture series, and prizes. Two quite different reasons produce these names. Some honorees are celebrated for what they did or wrote. Others are simply donors who purchased the naming right, and nobody supposes their character admirable or imitable; the same plaque format, on the same wall, is in effect sold. Once a name is on the facade, the institution carries it for decades or centuries and treats it as doing something: not merely recording a fact about the past but standing as a present statement by the institution about itself.

Then a second practice has grown up around the first. When research or journalism brings to light serious defects in an honoree's character — racism, cruelty, corruption, predation, often conduct that was legal and unremarkable in its own time — the institution is widely thought to be obliged to remove the name, and after a public process it usually does. Removal is treated as the appropriate response and as something more than cosmetic, even though the honoree has been dead a century and can be neither harmed nor vindicated. At the same time, the practice has features that strike me as hard to reconcile:

- The same institution typically continues to teach, cite, and celebrate the person's work. A school of public policy stops being named after its subject while the department down the hall keeps assigning them.
- Faults that were public knowledge when the name was bestowed, and were simply not thought disqualifying, are treated differently from identical faults discovered later.
- Defects bearing no relation to the achievement being honored — a benefactor of a children's hospital who turns out to have been violent toward his own household — are treated as spoiling the honor rather than as beside the point.
- Where the name is kept, the standard compromise is contextualization: a plaque or a webpage setting out the defects, offered as morally better than either silence or removal.

I am not asking whether renaming is good policy. I am asking what has to be true about character, about our knowledge of it, and about honor for the practice to make sense. In particular:

1. Reconstruct the implicit account of character. Does the practice require that character be a single global thing capable of being summed up, that a life have an overall moral valence, that traits be stable across situations and across decades, and that a person be identified with their character rather than with any of their deeds? Does it require anything like the unity of the virtues?

2. Reconstruct the implicit epistemology. The practice treats a commission reading documents a century or two later as competent to reach a settled verdict on a person's character, and treats that verdict as authoritative enough to alter public standing. What does this presuppose about how character is known, and about the sufficiency of documentary and testimonial evidence? Contrast it with the classical alternative it descends from: Plutarch opens his life of Alexander by announcing that he is writing lives rather than histories, on the ground that a slight thing — a phrase or a jest — often discloses character better than battles in which thousands die and than the greatest sieges. Does the modern practice accept that epistemology, or does it quietly substitute something more forensic and more coarse-grained? And what does it assume about the record surviving well enough to support a verdict?

3. Reconstruct the implicit theory of honor. What kind of act is the bestowal of a name, such that a later act of removal can undo it? What distinguishes honoring a person from commemorating them, from remembering them, and from studying them — and does the practice actually rely on those distinctions, or collapse them? Explain why contextualization is supposed to occupy a morally distinct position between silence and removal rather than being a weaker version of one of them.

4. Test the parts against one another. Is it coherent to hold simultaneously that a person's character can be finally assessed from records, that this assessment retroactively changes what a name meant for the fifty years it was uncontested, that the defects need bear no relation to the honored achievement, and that the same person may nonetheless be taught and cited without embarrassment? Give special attention to the donor/honoree ambiguity: what does the existence of purchased names tell us about whether the institution ever held the honorific reading, and does the honorific reading survive being admitted to share a form with a commercial transaction?

5. Say which rival views the practice rules out. At a minimum, consider: the Plutarchan picture of a life as a mixed thing whose faults are part of the material of moral learning rather than a contamination of it; the hagiographic picture, which is the practice's real ancestor and which solves the problem of faults by omission and by a standard of heroic virtue no ordinary life could meet; the psychological view that conduct is governed more by situations than by global traits; the separation of a work from its author; and any view on which moral appraisal is indexed to the standards available to the agent. For each, state whether the practice is inconsistent with it, merely in tension with it, or untroubled by it.

6. Throughout, separate what the practice strictly presupposes — what would have to be true for it to be rational — from what merely fits it comfortably, since several incompatible theories may license the same behavior. Mark explicitly where the practice is philosophically underdetermined, and where the official justifications given for it (that it is about the institution's values now, that the honoree no longer represents us) are compatible with rival underlying theories.

7. Close with the replacement. Suppose an institution adopted the Plutarchan picture in earnest: lives are mixed, character is disclosed by trifles as readily as by great deeds, and the moral use of a person consists in the whole life including its failures. Describe its naming conventions, its plaques, its ceremonies, and its decisions about removal. Would anything recognizable as the current practice survive, or would the honorific function have to be abandoned altogether in favor of a purely pedagogical one?

Do not include a bibliography, a reading list, or citations; argue from the content of the views rather than from name-dropping, and where you invoke a historical position, characterize it in your own words. Flag where you are uncertain, and say which step in your reconstruction is doing the most work.
