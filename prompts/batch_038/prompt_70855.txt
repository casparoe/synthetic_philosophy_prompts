Our philosophy of education seminar closes its Whitehead unit next week, and I have volunteered to read something aloud at the end of the session rather than submit another paper. The lecture I want to answer is the one on the rhythm of education: the claim that learning moves through romance, precision, and generalization, and — the part I care about most — that these are not three stations passed through once but cycles nested inside cycles, each one reproducing the same subordinate stages while coming out different at the other end, so that rhythm for Whitehead is difference carried through a framework of repetition. The other idea I want the poem to hold is his warning about inert ideas: knowledge received into the mind but never used, tested, or recombined, so that the characteristic failure of precision is a student well stocked with facts she cannot think with.

Please write a pantoum of five quatrains, with the final quatrain taking up the first and third lines of the opening so the circle closes. Set it on learning a language: first hearing it as sound and story, then the grammar and the drills, then at last reading with the apparatus in hand. The form should carry the argument, since in a pantoum every line that returns arrives in a new position and in new company: I want a reader to feel that this recursion is renewal rather than repetition, and to feel the difference between a line that comes back still alive and one that has gone inert — ideally one returned line that first appeared as wonder and reappears later hardened into something recited under examination. Please do not name the three stages or use the term of art for the dead ideas; someone who knows the lecture should recognize the structure in what the lines do, and someone who does not should simply get a good poem about learning a language.
