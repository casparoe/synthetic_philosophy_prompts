I write for the ideas desk of a science magazine and I am reporting a piece on what it actually means to defer to objective chance. Please treat what follows as a request for expert judgment rather than a summary of the literature: for these purposes you are a professional philosopher of probability who has thought hard about the Principal Principle and the proposals meant to repair it, and I want your own considered verdict.

Here is the background as I have it, and you should feel free to correct me where I have it wrong. Lewis's Principal Principle tells a rational agent to set her credence in a proposition equal to its objective chance, provided the rest of her evidence is admissible. The trouble, as I understand it, is that the future course of a chance-governed process can itself bear on the chance hypothesis that governs it: a long run of outcomes that is possible but unlikely looks, once it has happened, like evidence that the chances were not what we took them to be. Let that information in and the principle seems to require an agent to be certain of things the very same principle elsewhere treats as uncertain; bar it and you need an account of admissibility that does real work rather than restating the principle. Lewis himself later reformulated the requirement so that the agent conditionalizes on a complete chance hypothesis instead of treating individual chances as premises, and much of the subsequent discussion has turned on whether that reformulation is enough.

Now consider a world of my own devising, which I want you to use as the test case. In this world there is a decay process whose chance law is not fixed in advance. At any moment the law assigns the chance for the next interval on the basis of the record of decays so far, so that the law is continuously revised by the very history it governs. There is no prior chance hypothesis that stays true through the whole history; which hypothesis is in force at a time depends on what has happened up to that time. Learning the record is therefore learning something about the current law, and the current law is about the future, so there is no clean line between information about the past and information about how the chance-governed process will turn out.

Against that background, please rank the following five candidate deference norms from most to least satisfactory as an account of what deference to objective chance requires, judged in the world just described.

A. Unrestricted alignment. Whenever your total evidence entails that the chance of some proposition is x, your credence in that proposition given that evidence should be x, with no restriction on what else the evidence contains.

B. Admissible-evidence alignment. As in A, but the requirement bites only when the remainder of your evidence is admissible, where admissible evidence is evidence that the relevant chance hypothesis renders irrelevant to the proposition in question: information about the past, or about matters the chance hypothesis screens off.

C. Hypothesis-conditional alignment. Chances are never to be used as premises. Instead, your credence in a proposition, conditional on a complete chance hypothesis together with whatever admissible evidence you have, should equal the chance that hypothesis assigns to the proposition.

D. Thresholded alignment. This one is my invention. The requirement to match credence to chance is switched on only when your evidence settles the chance hypothesis to a high degree. If, given everything you know, one hypothesis carries posterior weight above some threshold close to 1, defer to it; if that weight is spread across hypotheses, ignore chance information altogether and set your credences by ordinary conditionalization on your non-chance evidence.

E. Expected-chance alignment. Also my invention, and deliberately deflationary. Never treat chance information as a special input. Fix an ur-prior over complete chance hypotheses, conditionalize it on your total evidence, and let your credence in a proposition be the posterior expectation of its chance. Deference then falls out of ordinary updating, and the substantive constraint is relocated into the ur-prior, which is required to have the property that conditionalizing on any chance hypothesis together with admissible evidence reproduces the chances that hypothesis posits.

Score each of the five out of ten on two dimensions and give me the numbers in the body of your answer. The first is coherence-safety: whether the norm can ever demand of a single agent a set of credences that is incoherent, or demand certainty about something the same norm elsewhere treats as uncertain. The second is bite: whether the norm genuinely constrains an agent's credences, as opposed to being satisfied automatically or collapsing into ordinary conditionalization. Where the two dimensions pull apart, tell me which you are weighting more heavily and why.

Begin by working through, in a few sentences, how each candidate behaves in the feedback world: which of them still have a usable notion of admissibility there, which are asked to swallow something inconsistent, and which simply stop saying anything interesting. Only then commit to an order, and state that order early enough that I can find it without hunting for it.

One request on form. No headings, no bullet points, no bold, no tables: continuous prose throughout, the way you would write a considered letter to a colleague. And please do not give me a bibliography, a reading list, or citations. I am after your judgment, not your references, and I would rather have a well-argued verdict than a name-check. If you think my description of the background, or of any of the five candidates, is mistaken, say so in prose and rank the corrected version instead.
