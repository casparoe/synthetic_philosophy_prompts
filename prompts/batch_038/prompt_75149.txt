Been going down a rabbit hole on bounded rationality for fun (I work in procurement, so it's sort of job-adjacent) and there's a specific thing I keep running into that I can't find a label for. Not satisficing itself — I've got that one. It's the step above it.

Two cases. (1) I'm hiring. I know I could run a proper structured process: scorecard, work sample, three rounds. I also know building that takes maybe fifteen hours, and I have a rough feel for whether fifteen hours actually buys me a better hire than two informal chats and my gut. So I spend twenty minutes deciding how to decide, settle somewhere in the middle, and get on with it. (2) A friend is house-hunting. Before she looks at a single listing she sets herself a rule: view at most six, stop at the first one that beats the best of the previous ones by some margin. Then she spends an evening wondering whether six is the right number, whether it should be fifteen, and eventually says 'whatever, I'll know when I'm sick of it'.

So the pattern: choosing your decision procedure is itself a decision, and that decision could be made by a better or worse procedure, and obviously there's a regress. What actually happens is that people terminate it at a point that looks arbitrary — it drifts with fatigue, deadline pressure, mood — and then treat everything below that line as settled, without ever noticing they cut it off. What I want a name for is that termination, and the fact that it goes unexamined. Not the heuristic, not the threshhold, but the point where you stop asking whether your method for choosing is good enough.

Nearest things I've hit on, and why none fit exactly: aspiration levels (those govern when to stop looking at options, not when to stop improving your rule for looking); expected value of information / value of computation (right shape, but they treat the top level as something you can actually compute, whereas the whole issue is that the computation up there is itself too expensive to do properly, so it gets skipped rather than solved); optimal stopping (options again). And there's the old objection that satisficing is just optimising in disguise, since you have to optimise to pick your threshold — is what I'm describing simply that objection seen from the inside, or a distinct thing?

So: is there an established concept for this, and if there are several competing ones, which fits best and what does each get wrong about it? If nothing fits, coin something — definition, and where the edges are. What counts as a case and what doesn't: does it cover just picking a threshold on the hoof? choosing a career? an AI system deciding how much compute to spend on planning? I'm after the carving, not a bare label, and definately not a reading list.

British English please, and jargon is fine as long as you define it the first time round.
