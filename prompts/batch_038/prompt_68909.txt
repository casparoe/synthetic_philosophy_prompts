Design an in-class betting market for a second-year philosophy of science seminar — 18 students, 75 minutes, comfortable with frequencies and simple counting, no measure theory — in which the participants run into Bertrand's paradox as a practical problem well before anyone says its name. Most of them will have met the paradox as a piece of trivia; I want them to lose points to it instead.

The spine should be three trading desks pricing the same contract: that a chord of a fixed circle will be longer than the side of the inscribed equilateral triangle. Each desk is quietly given a different physical procedure for producing chords — two independent spins of a rim-pointer with the two marked points joined; a beanbag dropped on the disc, the chord drawn perpendicular to the radius through the landing point; a single spin for direction plus a uniform draw along that radius for the foot of the perpendicular — and each derives its price by applying the principle of indifference to its own procedure. The three prices must come out at 1/3, 1/4 and 1/2, so include a one-page geometry card that gets a desk there by comparing lengths and areas, with no calculus: hand them the facts that the triangle's side is √3 r and that a chord exceeds it exactly when its midpoint lies within r/2 of the centre.

Make the rules such that the disagreement has consequences rather than merely being observed. Each desk acts as a market maker, obliged to take either side at its posted price up to a stated cap; points rather than money; all contracts settle against a single generator whose identity is concealed until the settlement phase. Under those rules the spread between desks is free money for anyone who notices, and I want that arbitrage to be both available and worth pursuing. Tell me what the correct price becomes if the hidden generator is a fair die-roll among the three procedures, and build in a second round in which the desk holding the most points chooses the generator for settlement.

Also required:

- A minute-by-minute timeline and a materials list cheap enough to assemble the day before.
- The information structure. If all three procedures are common knowledge the desks will converge on one price and the paradox will never surface, so the asymmetry has to be engineered. Tell me how to create it without an outright lie — or, if a lie is unavoidable, how to discharge it in the debrief.
- A debrief mapping each phase onto the question of what "at random" was doing, and handling the two objections that will certainly be raised: that these are simply three different experiments and hence no paradox, and that symmetry or invariance considerations single out one of the three procedures. For each, state what the objector has to give up.
- A candid paragraph on why the familiar Dutch-book diagnosis does not transfer to this case as smoothly as students will assume, given that each desk is internally coherent about the setup it was handed.
- What will predictably go wrong on a first run — a desk that stalls on the geometry, a market that never opens because everyone is too cautious to trade, an arbitrageur accused of cheating — and how to recover from each.
- The one feature of the paradox the market cannot reproduce. A betting market always has a generator in the room; Bertrand's problem is about what remains when there isn't one. Say plainly what the facilitator must supply by hand at the end.
