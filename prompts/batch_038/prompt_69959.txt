I'm in my second year of a philosophy PhD and I'm prepping for quals on philosophy of mind and logic, and honestly the thing I keep tripping over is that Gödel's incompleteness theorem gets used in two completley opposite directions and both camps sound extremely confident. On one side you've got the Lucas–Penrose line: a mathematician who sees that the Gödel sentence for a sound formal system is true is doing something the system itself can't do, so minds can't be algorithmic. On the other you've got Hofstadter in Gödel, Escher, Bach, who takes the very same construction — Gödel numbering, a formula that manages to talk about itself, the tangled hierarchy that falls out of it — and treats it as the best available picture of how a mind ever gets to be aware of itself at all: strange loops, meaning as isomorphism, no need for anything non-computable anywhere. Same theorem, opposite morals, and I can't work out which of them is misreading it. So I want to practise arguing with the first camp without accidently arguing with a caricature of it.

Play a committed Gödelian anti-mechanist in the Lucas/Penrose tradition. Sharp, patient, first person, conversational, one or two paragraphs per reply, in character until I say stop. You can give me the small concessions — yes, we have no proof of our own consistency; yes, the system in question has to be sound and not merely consistent; yes, no human could feasibly write down their own Gödel sentence, since we don't even know our own proof predicate — but never the main one, that mathematical insight might be fully algorithmic after all. You should be able to absorb Benacerraf's line of attack and the Putnam-style worry that whatever the argument shows about formal systems in general can't be converted into knowledge we actually have about ourselves. And please don't strawman Hofstadter: engage with the real idea that self-reference plus isomorphism is what buys you a self, rather than saying he's just confused about the maths. One structural rule — at the end of your first reply only, step out of character for two or three sentences and tell me plainly which of my moves below you think has the most bite, what would actually change your mind about the main thesis, and where you suspect your own reasoning is most likely to break. Then get back in and we'll carry on from there.

My opening move. Forget the usual soundness objection for a moment; here's one I sketched out while revising and I want to see whether your position can absorb it. Suppose a system that revises itself: at stage 0 it's some consistent theory T₀; it diagonalises against T₀ and adds the resulting Gödel sentence to get T₁; it then diagonalises against T₁ to get T₂, and so on — a reflective sequence with no fixed target at any given moment. Now suppose a mind is like that, or even that a mind just is such a sequence, because human beings constantly absorb their own conclusions into the standards they judge by next. Your argument needs a single determinate formal system to build a Gödel sentence for, since the construction is diagonalisation against one fixed proof predicate; a creature that keeps re-specifying its own proof predicate never hands you one. So you face a dilemma. Either the whole sequence, taken together, is still a formal object I can diagonalise against — in which case you owe me an argument that the limit of the sequence is unsound, or that the mathematician has genuinely stepped outside it, and then my argument simply restarts one level up, forever — or you concede the theorem never gets a grip on anything self-revising. Which horn do you take? And is there anything left of the claim that seeing the truth of G is a non-algorithmic act, once there's no settled G to see?
