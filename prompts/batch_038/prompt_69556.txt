I'm the sole staff person supporting the standards committee of a regional federation of worker cooperatives — thirty-eight member co-ops across food production, light manufacturing, software, and home care. Membership is not decorative: it carries access to a revolving loan fund, a shared certification mark that members put on retail packaging, and preferential purchasing from other members. Two or three times a month I get a file that turns, whether or not anyone says so in those terms, on a Marxian question — is this arrangement extracting surplus from someone who has no claim on it, and does the work itself deform the people doing it. At present I have no framework, and the decisions are made by seven elected members with mixed schooling, no taste for theory, and about as much patience for abstraction as a zoning board. I get roughly two hours per file. I took one undergraduate course that covered the theory of surplus value and the early writings on alienation, and I would like to build the procedure out of that material, though I'm aware Marx is not obviously in the business of handing anyone a decision rule.

What I want is a procedure I can actually run: the questions to ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, which of them are answerable from a co-op's books and governance documents and which are not answerable from anything I can obtain, where the procedure terminates in a refusal, and where it stops and hands the question back to the committee because the remaining issue is one of values rather than facts. It has to keep the two axes distinct rather than letting them collapse into a single score of good-or-bad workplace: the appropriation of surplus (who produces, who claims, under what property relations, and whether the claim rests on ownership rather than on work), and the character of the activity itself (the four moments of alienation in the 1844 manuscripts — from the product, from the act of producing, from species-being, and from other human beings). For each of those four, tell me plainly whether a non-specialist reading a file can operationalize it or whether it cannot be operationalized at all, and don't hand me a checkbox I'll be unable to fill in honestly. The procedure must also confront the feature of Marx's account that makes my current practice look confused: his claim that exploitation occurs through the free exchange of equivalents and does not depend on the wage being below market, on coercion, or on anyone's intentions or bad faith. Much of what my committee currently tests — pay levels, consent, whether workers are better off than their alternatives, the existence of a grievance channel — may be beside the point, and I need to be told what to do when a file shows non-member workers paid above the local market, satisfied with the arrangement, and freely consenting to it. Where the distinction between labor and labor power, or between absolute and relative surplus value, buys me something observable, keep it; where it is load-bearing in the theory but invisible from inside a co-op's file, say so and move on. Address how far the procedure reaches beyond the member's own walls — the co-op that is internally impeccable but buys its inputs from firms running ordinary wage labor — and use the formal versus real subsumption distinction only if it actually generates a different verdict somewhere. Finally, be explicit at the step where it happens, not in a closing caveat, about anywhere Marx's account does not supply a decision rule and you have had to import one: for instance the analytic-Marxist move to define exploitation in terms of unequal ownership of productive assets and a counterfactual test about what a coalition could withdraw with, or a domination-based test about who is subject to whose arbitrary will. Tell me which distinctions you judged indispensable to the translation and which you dropped, what the dropped ones were doing in the theory, and whether the imports change any verdicts.

Then run the procedure on three files I have in front of me. First: a bakery with twelve member-owners, one member one vote, pay held within a two-to-one band, surplus distributed in proportion to hours worked, and six non-member seasonal workers paid above the local living wage with no realistic path to membership, because the buy-in is five thousand dollars and the season runs four months. Second: a software co-op that is fully worker-owned and governed but in which a mission-aligned venture fund holds three million dollars of preferred stock, a board seat, and a liquidation preference, and whose product is a scheduling and location-monitoring platform sold to logistics firms. Third: a member co-op with impeccable internal democracy that voted last year to contract out its night-shift cleaning to an outside non-union firm, after which member distributions rose by twelve percent. I expect the third to come out badly and I want it to: if the procedure gives a clean pass to the file my committee is proudest of, it is tracking the wrong thing. Close by telling me where the procedure will misfire — which files it will classify wrongly and why, what a co-op could do to game it while satisfying every step, which inputs I simply will not be able to obtain — and what it cannot do even when it is run competently and in good faith.
