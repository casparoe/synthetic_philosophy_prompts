I am a fourth-year doctoral student in philosophy of mind, and I have been asked to open an interdisciplinary workshop on temporal consciousness with something other than the usual slide of a timeline. I would like a pantoum about the color phi phenomenon in which the form carries the argument rather than decorating it.

For the members of the room who have not met the illusion: two small lights of different colors — let us say a red and a green — are flashed a few degrees apart in the visual field, less than a second apart in time. At the right spacings and intervals the observer does not report two flashes. They report one thing crossing the gap, and changing color somewhere in the middle of a path that no light ever traveled. What makes this a philosophical problem rather than a party trick is the timing of the report: the subject locates the change of color before the second light came on, that is, before anything in the world could have informed them of the change. The information seems to arrive after the experience it is supposed to account for. (The effect was first measured in the mid-1970s by two psychologists working on a question a philosopher had posed about what a change of color would do to apparent motion.)

Now the two hypotheses I need the poem to hold in suspension. Call the first Orwellian: the subject really did see the red light, and only afterward, once the green one has arrived, the record is quietly rewritten in memory — the past edited to suit the present, after the manner of the ministry that corrects yesterday's newspapers. Call the second Stalinesque: nothing is rewritten, because the reconciliation is finished before the performance begins; the verdict is settled backstage, and what steps into consciousness is the completed trial, with the mid-flight change of color in it from the outset. Here is the twist that matters, and it should be enacted rather than announced: the two accounts can agree about every neural event, about exactly where and when the erroneous content enters the causal pathway. They differ only about whether that location lies on the near side or the far side of experience. And subjects cannot tell them apart, because an experience that was misbegotten and an experience that was immediately misremembered feel precisely alike from the inside.

The formal commission, then. A pantoum of four or five quatrains, in which the second and fourth lines of each stanza return as the first and third of the next. In a pantoum those returns are traditionally verbatim, and the repetition is what makes the form the right instrument for this material: it is a machine for handing the reader a line they have already read and letting them discover that what came afterward has altered its meaning. I want the choice described above to be made visible in your handling of the returns. Either revise them slightly as they come back — a pronoun, a tense, a single modifier — so that the reader, like the subject, reports having seen the change all along; or leave them letter-for-letter identical and let the intervening quatrains do all the falsifying, so that the line was never anything but what it now says. Choose one, but choose so quietly that a reader who has just finished the poem cannot say which you did, and would not be able to say after a second reading either. That inescapability is the point.

Three further constraints, offered in the spirit of the workshop rather than as contest rules. The two lights should be something other than laboratory furniture: allow the red and the green to be, for at least one stanza, an experience rather than a pair of stimuli. Do not resolve the puzzle in the closing quatrain, and withhold the consolation that consciousness is therefore an illusion or that the theater was empty all along; the pantoum's habit of bringing back the opening lines at the end is welcome, a summary is not. And keep the words Orwellian, Stalinesque, phi, Cartesian theater, and qualia out of the verse itself — the distinction should be legible in what the poem does, not advertised in its vocabulary.

Roughly eight to ten syllables per line will do, and I do not require a rhyme scheme; if you rhyme, let the rhyme be one of the things the repetition renders ambiguous. Someone who works on this literature should finish the poem knowing exactly which puzzle it addresses and which side of the equivalence it has made invisible. Someone who has never heard of the illusion should simply have a good poem about a moving light that changes color before anything tells it to.
