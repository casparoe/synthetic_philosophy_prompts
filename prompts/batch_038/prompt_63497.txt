For fifteen years I have run trained sensory panels: screening candidates on detection thresholds, building physical reference standards for aroma and mouthfeel descriptors, running blinded and replicated sessions in balanced presentation order, tracking inter-panelist agreement, and keeping analytical tasks (what is present, at what intensity) strictly separate from hedonic ones (how much you like it), since collapsing the two is the classic way to ruin a panel. Restate Hume's case in Of the Standard of Taste in that vocabulary as faithfully as you can — the borrowed story of Sancho's two kinsmen, whose disparaged verdicts on a hogshead are vindicated when it is drained and the cause of the fault turns up in it; the five qualifications he demands of a true judge; and the appeal to the joint verdict of such judges, confirmed across the ages, as the standard itself. Then give me the residue report, claim by claim. Which of his moves translate cleanly into screening, training, reference standards, blinding, replication, and agreement statistics? Which translate only by distorting what those procedures are for in my field — for example, by treating agreement as a mark of reliability when here it is largely a training target to be manufactured? And which cannot be expressed at all in a methodology that assumes liking has no right answer and therefore never asks whose liking counts? Finally, test a suspicion of mine: that all five of Hume's qualifications are screening criteria for the analytical channel, while his conclusion is a claim about the hedonic channel, so that the standard of taste works only by a covert shift between them. Tell me plainly whether the translation bears that out or shows my reading is too neat, and if it is too neat, name the part of the argument that survives the shift.
