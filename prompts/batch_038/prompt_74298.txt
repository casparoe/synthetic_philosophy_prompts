Nearly every applied Bayesian analysis I referee reports that a flat, or otherwise non-informative, prior was adopted for the parameter of interest, and offers the same justification: the conclusions are thereby driven by the data rather than by subjective assumptions. Two features of the practice puzzle me. First, flatness is only ever claimed in the parameterization the authors happen to use — a uniform prior on a variance is not uniform on the standard deviation, on its reciprocal, or on its logarithm — and no argument is ever offered that this is the scale on which one ought to be ignorant. Second, the same authors supply sensitivity analyses showing that their conclusions shift when the prior changes, which seems to concede that the prior was doing substantive work all along. A colleague suggested that the first puzzle is a nineteenth-century paradox about random chords of a circle in disguise; I cannot see the connection.

What account of evidence, probability, and ignorance does this practice presuppose? Reconstruct it as explicitly as you can: what would have to be true of prior probabilities — and of the parameters they are placed on — for flat-means-neutral to be more than a slogan? Then say which rival accounts the practice rules out (a prior as one agent's betting commitment, a prior as an instrument for good long-run decisions, a prior as whatever invariance under reparameterization demands), whether the practice strictly requires your reconstruction or is equally licensed by several views, and where my two puzzles amount to genuine incoherence rather than mere inelegance. Close with what such a methods section would have to say instead if the field gave up the idea that any prior can be neutral.
