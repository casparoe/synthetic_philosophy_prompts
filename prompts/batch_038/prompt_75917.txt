so last night i had two friends over and what started as a conversation about whether my cousin should try for a third kid turned into like a three hour argument between them, and honestly I lost the thread somewhere around the second glass of wine and want someone sober to go through it with me. Neither of them is a philosopher exactly — Maya does data stuff for a climate nonprofit, Owen teaches high school history — but they're both sharp and neither one was being a pushover. The setup was that my cousin and his wife are stretched thin already with two kids, money is tight, but they clearly want a third and would obviously love the kid. Maya said that matters less than people think, and that if the third kid would have a good life then having her is, straightforwardly, one of the better things they could do with their lives. Here's roughly how it went, in order:

(1) Maya: on basically every reasonable way of adding up wellbeing, a third happy person is a big plus. People treat "they can barely afford two" as decisive, but the kid wouldn't be worse off for never existing, so the only real question is whether her life would be good, and everyone agrees it would be.

(2) Owen: that's backwards. There's nobody it's better for. You can't benefit someone by creating them — before they exist there's no subject to benefit. So a happy extra life isn't a gain to anyone, and "the world is better with more happy people in it" is just a way of sneaking in a number and calling it value.

(3) Maya: fine, accept your rule and see where it lands. Compare a world with ten billion people whose lives are rich and full and wonderful to a world with a thousand times as many people, all of them happy, no suffering, no injustice, but each life just barely worth continuing. If adding happy people never makes things better, the second world isn't better than the first. But it also can't be worse, because nothing is worse for anyone. So you're stuck saying they're on a par — and then a tiny extra nudge of happiness for anyone in the huge world makes it strictly better. You end up committed to saying the enormous barely-worth-living world is the better one.

(4) Owen: I know, I've heard this one, and I think it's the least interesting move in the whole debate. "Repugnant" just means it clashes with the intuitions of people who already think population is fixed. If my principles say something that sounds weird when applied to ten trillion people, that's evidence about my principles, not about ten trillion people. I'd rather bite that bullet than yours.

(5) Maya: then bite this one instead. A woman is pregnant and her doctor says: if you keep going with this pregnancy the child will have a significant but manageable disability; if you wait a few months and conceive again, that child — a different child — will be healthy. Everyone agrees she has a strong reason to wait. But on your view there's no reason at all, because it isn't better for either possible child, and you've said there's nothing impersonally better about more or happier people. Your view can't even get the easy cases right.

(6) Owen: it can, you're just insisting that the only kind of reason that counts is "the outcome contains more value." Choosing the healthier child is what a loving, responsible parent does, and it's a choice between two possible children, not a ranking of worlds. I don't need impersonal value to explain why I admire the woman who waits.

(7) Maya: so your reasons are just... tastes you approve of. And notice you've quietly changed sides — in (2) the whole objection was that you can't have value floating around unattached to a person, but now you've got duties to a child who doesn't exist and never will unless she chooses it. Who exactly is she owing that to?

(8) Owen: the child she'll have if she waits, who is right now nobody. That's the point — your framework can't even name her, and mine at least takes seriously that parents are choosing between real possible futures rather than adding to a total. Also, if a happy extra life really is a big impersonal plus, you're committed to saying my cousin should have the third kid even if it means the two existing kids get noticeably less of everything, as long as the arithmetic comes out ahead. Are you actually willing to say that at the dinner table?

(9) Maya: yes, reluctantly, and I think anyone who says no is just privileging the people who already exist. The existing kids aren't entitled to a slice of the world's resources that would go further if it made another whole happy life. But I'll grant the numbers have to actually work out, which in this case they probably don't.

We never got anywhere and I stopped being able to tell who was winning around move 5. Can you go through (1) to (9) one at a time and rate each move — clearly right, defensible, weak, clearly wrong — and say why? Then tell me who's actually ahead on the merits, whether (3) and (4) are even addressing the same thing or just trading intuitions at each other, where Owen is equivocating between "not better for anyone" and "not better, period," and what neither of them brought up that might break the deadlock. Last thing: if each of them had to give up exactly one move to get to a position they could both live with, which single concession should each make, and what does it cost them?
