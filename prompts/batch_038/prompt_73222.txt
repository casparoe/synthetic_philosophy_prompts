I am coordinating a small working group on the concepts of health and disease, and the two senior members — one a philosopher who defends a Boorse-style biostatistical account on which "disease" is a value-free notion of statistically subnormal functional performance relative to an age-, sex-, and population-indexed reference class, and the other a clinician-philosopher working in the Canguilhemian and Nordenfeltian line on which no state is pathological except relative to the living being's own capacity to institute norms or to reach its vital goals — have now spent two years trading the same thought experiments at each other without ever agreeing on what would settle anything. A regional payer's evidence-review panel has asked them, in three weeks, for a joint document they can both put their names to, because the panel has to make coverage and labeling decisions and is tired of being told that the answer depends on which philosopher it consults. Please draft that document: the joint statement of a good-faith adversarial collaboration between a naturalist about disease and a normativist about disease. I want the claims both can sign — about what the two accounts actually have in common as descriptions of the facts (that traits are distributed statistically, that organisms have parts organized in characteristic ways, that some states frustrate goals and shorten lives, that clinicians must decide things under uncertainty), about what each side is really claiming when it says the concept of disease is or is not value-laden, and about the division of labor between the concepts of disease, illness, disorder, and sickness; the residual disagreements stated precisely, each phrased so that the advocate of that very position would accept the wording as a fair statement of their own view rather than a paraphrase chosen by the opponent; the cruxes, meaning the sub-questions such that settling one would actually move at least one side; and, for each crux, the kind of argument, case, or empirical finding both would accept as bearing on it, with each side's prediction written down in advance.

To keep the collaboration from floating free, anchor it in two stipulated cases, and please treat the physiology as given rather than correcting it. Case A: in a fictional population, a slow remodeling of the arterial wall has, over three generations, become the statistical mode for the relevant reference class, is entirely asymptomatic, does not impair performance on any functional test, and yet is reliably associated with substantially shortened life expectancy and with late-life events that patients and clinicians alike regard as calamitous. Case B: a congenital trait found in well under two percent of the population causes measurably slower clearance of a certain metabolite and a correspondingly poor score on one standardized performance measure, but its bearer reports a full and self-directed life, meets every goal she sets for herself, has no symptoms, no reduced life expectancy, and occupies a social and occupational environment in which the trait carries no disadvantage. For each case, record what each camp's account classifies it as, what it costs that camp to accept the classification, and what revision each side is prepared to make to its own theory rather than bite the bullet. Note explicitly that a Wakefield-style hybrid is off the table as a negotiated compromise: each of the two believes the hybrid concedes the central point to the other side, and the document should say why, in each camp's own terms.

Format the output in five parts: a numbered section of agreed claims; a disagreements table with columns for the topic, the naturalist's formulation, the normativist's formulation, and a judgment of whether the disagreement is substantive or merely a dispute about which word carries the evaluative load; a section on cruxes with pre-registered predictions; a short joint operational rule the payer's panel could apply without first resolving the metaphysics, together with an explicit list of which of the panel's verdicts under that rule would come out differently if one camp turned out to be right — this is the part I most want, since it is the only thing that makes the collaboration falsifiable rather than diplomatic; and a closing paragraph identifying which portions of the two-year argument were verbal, and what the parties should have said instead of what they did say. Do not include any citations, bibliography, reading list, or quotation from a text, and do not name specific books or articles; where you need to gesture at a theory, describe it. Both parties should come across as equally sharp, and neither section of the table should be a strawman. Keep the whole document under about 1,500 words.
