Three months ago I was moved, without any consultation, onto the incident reconstruction team at the English water company where I work, and I have spent every day since reading minutes, memos and shift handovers in an attempt to establish what the organisation decided. In the spring, four people in one of our supply areas died in a gastrointestinal outbreak traced back to the treatment works; the regulator has referred the matter to the Crown Prosecution Service, and my reading of the Corporate Manslaughter and Corporate Homicide Act 2007 is that the prosecution need not find a single guilty mind at the top but must show a gross breach of a relevant duty of care arising substantially from the way the company's activities were managed and organised by its senior management. I am not a lawyer and this is a professional predicament rather than a legal one, but it has left me unable to stop thinking about a question I last encountered as an undergraduate: under what conditions does a collection of people amount to an agent that can intend something, as opposed to a collection of people who each intend something, severally, that happens to add up the same way.

I want a short story about this, of roughly two thousand to two thousand five hundred words, because the abstractions are precisely what make the subject unusable in conversation, and I would like something I can circulate to colleagues in a session on responsibility and record-keeping without it feeling like a paper.

The scenario I would like you to build is as follows. A fictional English water company (please invent the company, the works, the reservoir and the town; nothing should be identifiable with a real undertaking) is preparing its response to a charge of corporate manslaughter. The narrator is a governance analyst, not a lawyer, whose job is to assemble the decision record and produce a chronology showing who knew what, when, and on whose authority anything was done. Partway through, she discovers a standing document — an internal instruction of some kind about what to do when raw-water quality deteriorates outside the tested season — that has been re-issued every year for eleven years, each time countersigned by whoever currently holds the post, so that no two signatories ever held it together and nobody alive signed it twice. It has acquired a nickname among the operations staff. She discovers, near the end, that she countersigned it in her ninth year at the company, when she was twenty-six, and did not know what she was joining.

The story needs to dramatise, without ever naming or explaining, each of the following. First, the gap between a room full of people who all intend the same outcome and a group that intends it jointly: at least one meeting should be shown where the participants are responsive to one another, adjust to one another's contributions and support one another's efforts, and a second where they do all three things for entirely separate private reasons, and the reader should be able to feel the difference without being told it exists. Second, the idea that joint commitment, once entered, generates obligations and entitlements that no party can dissolve alone, including the standing to rebuke: I would like a scene in which a character rebukes someone for letting the group down and neither of them can afterwards say where her right to do that came from. Third, the aggregation problem — the minutes should show a board endorsing, by majority, each of three factual premises about the works, and then endorsing the operational conclusion that follows from them, although not one director present believed all three; the prosecution treats that as the company's own judgement, the defence treats it as arithmetic, and somebody in the story has to decide which. Fourth, the contrary insistence that whatever intentionality a group has must live in individual heads: give this view a character who holds it, and let her be right about at least one important thing, so that she is not a strawman.

Please make the defence's best argument genuinely strong, and include one scene in which a conscientious person doing exactly what the procedures require thereby becomes a link in the chain that kills someone. The ending should not resolve whether a group agent existed or whether anyone intended anything; the verdict the reader reaches should depend on which account of joint action they arrived with.

Some constraints on execution. No philosopher may be named anywhere in the story, and no sentence of the fiction may use the technical vocabulary of this literature; the machinery has to be carried by documents, rota sheets, meetings, silences, and what people say to one another in corridors. Please include at least one genuinely comic beat drawn from the absurdity of committee life, so that this does not read as a parable. Keep the prose restrained and concrete, in British English.

After the story, and clearly separated from it, add a note of about two hundred words, written for the same colleagues, saying where the story's structure in fact lands on the question of whether the company was an agent, and setting out what a reader who holds the opposite view would have to change about the events — as opposed to their description of them — to reach the other verdict. That note, and only that note, may use the technical terms.
