My father drank through most of my childhood and was very rarely there. Last month he told me, quite directly, that I have been neglecting my own children in something like the way he neglected me. I believe what he said is true, and I also cannot stand that he was the one to say it. A friend of mine, who listens to a great many debate programs and thinks of himself as a clear thinker, has a stock reply to anyone in my position: refusing criticism from a hypocrite, he says, is like refusing a doctor's advice because the doctor smokes. Either the diagnosis is right or it is not, and the habits of the person delivering it have nothing whatever to do with that; treating it otherwise is just an elegant way of dodging an unwelcome truth. From this he concludes that all talk of standing, or of someone having no right to blame you, is muddled — there is only the question whether the claim is correct, and the critic's own conduct is beside the point.

I would like the analogy audited rather than either accepted or waved away. I have no training in philosophy and no access to the literature, so please work entirely from your own reasoning and do not send me off to read anything in particular. Give me exactly five numbered items, one or two sentences each, with no introduction and no conclusion:

1. The correspondence, item by item: what in my situation plays the doctor, the patient, the diagnosis, the cigarettes, and the act of taking the advice.
2. The places where that mapping fails that genuinely matter for the conclusion my friend draws from it.
3. The places where it fails that look damaging but are really beside the point, so that I can stop pressing them.
4. What the analogy quietly assumes about what blaming is — what kind of thing it would have to be for the doctor comparison to fit, and whether it is that kind of thing.
5. Whether his conclusion survives the audit, and, if it does not, what comparison would do better justice to a case like mine.
