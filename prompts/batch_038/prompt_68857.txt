I run a Wednesday night arguing club at our community center — about twelve of us, no academics, two nurses, some retirees, my brother in law. This year I started handing out a written challenge each month with a $20 prize, and I want to actually grade them instead of letting people vote for whoever talks loudest.

The topic I want: compromise between people whose values genuinely clash. Its my own hunch that a compromise is basically a trade, and trades can leave both sides better off — I care a ton about the zoning rule and almost nothing about the oak tree, you're the reverse, we swap and we both win. But something seems different when whats on the table is a principle rather than a tree, and I can't put my finger on it. Two things I keep circling: the people a principle protects usually aren't at the negotiating table, so the two traders might just be spending someone else's moral credit; and someone who thinks an act is flat out forbidden doesn't seem to have any price at all, so what is actually being traded in that case?

Write me one essay question a person can handwrite in 45 minutes that they can't pass with "compromise is hard but we all have to give a little." It should force them to say precisely when a moral trade creates value and when it just shoves the loss onto whoever wasn't invited. Then give me a model answer of about 600 words, pitched at what a sharp amateur could plausibly reach in that time rather than a journal article, plus a marking scheme out of 20 naming five things a winning answer has to do, the marks for each, and one line per item on what a decent-but-losing answer gets wrong or skips. Please don't build any of it on philosopher names or book titles — I've never read them and I wouldn't be able to check anything.
