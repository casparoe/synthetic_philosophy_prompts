I teach a third-year value theory seminar at a UK university: sixteen students, a single 100-minute session, a room with movable chairs, a whiteboard, and a printing budget but no other materials. The session opens a fortnight-long block on hard choices, and I have deliberately withheld the reading: the students arrive without the vocabulary of incommensurability, parity, or the failure of the trichotomy, and I want them to meet the phenomenon before they meet the labels.

What I need is a designed activity in which the class, making decisions that actually bind us, runs into the breakdown of commensurability structurally rather than as a moving story. I have given up on staging transplant-and-lifeboat cases: these students have rehearsed intuitions about them, and they file such cases under tragedy rather than under incomparability. So the object of choice must be something they genuinely control. My standing commitment is that whatever the activity yields, I will honour it. Concretely, I want the three alternatives under consideration to be three substantively different ways of running the final four sessions of the module — differing in what is read, in how each session is structured, and in what is assessed — with the class's collective verdict determining which we adopt. Design the three alternatives yourself, but each must be best along a different dimension, and you must explain to me why a well-informed, rational student could defensibly go either way on each of the three pairwise comparisons without being muddled or evasive.

The activity must hit the following beats, in this order, and I need each one specified precisely enough that I could hand it to a colleague and have it run identically:

1. Private written pairwise verdicts, collected before any discussion, so that we have a recorded class profile on the board rather than a mood.
2. An aggregation rule that I apply mechanically and without discretion, with a fixed agenda order — and then a second run with the agenda reversed, so that the instability is visible as a fact about the record and not as an artefact of my chairing.
3. A small-improvement stage: one alternative is strictly and really improved by something trivial but welcome to them (fewer pages, a short extension on the summative work, a choice of seminar time). Before we take the new verdicts, you must state in advance what the pairwise verdicts *should* become if the class's earlier answers were tracking a single common scale, so that when the actual answers fail to move that way, the discrepancy is on the board in my handwriting rather than in an argument from me.
4. A stage in which each student must say, for one comparison they have already made, what would have to change for their verdict to reverse — and discovers that they cannot name an exchange rate.

Constraints: no points, no tokens, no notional money; no opening lecture; I act only as facilitator and must not signal a preferred outcome at any stage; and none of the technical terminology may be voiced by me until the final fifteen minutes.

Deliver, then: the three alternatives in the exact wording I will print on the cards; a run sheet with timings and the verbatim wording of every prompt I put to the room; the debrief as a sequence of questions, each mapped onto the moment in play it is meant to retrieve, and drawing the distinctions between better, worse and equally good, the trichotomy, the small-improvement test, and parity as against both vagueness and mere incomparability — concepts only, please, and no reading list, since I will supply the texts myself.

I also want the unflattering parts. Give me the failure modes I should expect on a first run, ranked by how likely they are to wreck the session, each with a handling: the student who announces that everything is comparable and refuses to play, the class that invents a compromise fourth alternative and collapses the trilemma, the group whose verdicts come out perfectly transitive so that nothing interesting happens, and the risk that the exercise teaches the wrong lesson entirely — that incommensurability is simply indecision, or that when values clash anything goes. Include a scaled-down version for eight students, and a version that survives a seminar in which two or three students dominate the discussion.

Finish with a frank statement of what this activity cannot reproduce and what I must therefore supply in my own person: I already suspect it cannot make them feel the irreversibility of a hard choice, since the module continues either way, and that it cannot distinguish parity from vagueness. Tell me where else I am fooling myself.

Open with your bottom line: one sentence naming the single moment in the run of play at which the phenomenon actually lands for the students, and the feature of the design that produces it. Then give everything else. No preamble, and no restating of my request.
