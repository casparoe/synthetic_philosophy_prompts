I am at work on a novel set in Vienna in the late autumn of 1931, one scene of which turns on a judgment that must be rendered by an aging Freud — not as an emblem of psychoanalysis but as a man with a documented pattern of such judgments, which I would rather not flatten into caricature. I can invent the case freely; what I cannot invent is his response to it, and that is where I need help.

Here is the case a colleague brings to him after dinner. Frau N., forty-three, widowed seventeen months earlier when her husband, a builder, was killed in the collapse of a scaffold. Her father was a village schoolmaster and a combative freethinker who forbade talk of the Church at his table and mocked the parish priest to his face; she says she never envied believing children and never wanted protection from anything. She keeps no devotional objects, attends no service, lights no candles. Yet every evening she walks to a chapel on the edge of the district, sits in the last pew for twenty minutes, and walks home. She reports feeling nothing there — no consolation, no grief, no exaltation, no sense of being one with anything — and when asked why she goes, answers only that she prefers not to be at home. On the evenings she misses the visit, being with her sister or unwell, she feels no tension and no need to make it up. Since the funeral she has written letters to her dead husband in which she reproaches him, and indeed prosecutes him, for having died; she burns them unread. She states flatly that she does not believe he survives and does not wish it. Asked about God, she says that if there is anyone there she has come to think of him as an adversary, and that she stands in a quarrel with him rather than in want of him. She has lately joined a weekly evening circle of other bereaved women meeting under the patronage of a saint whose name she chose, she says, because it sounded foreign; they read aloud to one another from novels and newspapers, there is no priest, and she insists it is a reading circle and not a congregation. Otherwise she is composed and unsentimental, sleeps well, works, fears neither illness nor her own death, and has one recurrent dream, always the same: her father alive at the head of a table, saying nothing.

What I want is not a diagnosis but a prediction, together with a reconstruction of the machinery behind it. I have been working through his settled verdicts on religious phenomena: the obsessive girl's ceremonial acts read as the private counterpart of the devout man's rites, and religion on that basis as a universal obsessional neurosis of humanity; the impoverished Bavarian painter who bound himself to the Devil by two pacts after losing a parent and was relieved of them at Mariazell, whose Devil he reads as a father-substitute and whose pact is simply the shape the available theology gave a neurosis; the French correspondent's report of a limitless, oceanic sense of oneness, which he grants is genuinely felt but refuses to make the source of the religious need, deriving it instead from a primitive ego that has not yet bounded itself off from the world, while insisting that the real wellspring is the infant's helplessness and longing for the father; the totem meal, and decades later the murdered Moses, where religion is the return of a repressed ambivalence toward the dead patriarch and guilt is transmitted across generations; the church and the army held together as artificial masses by one love object installed in place of each member's ego-ideal, and the panic that follows when that tie snaps; the claim that religious doctrines are illusions in his strict sense — beliefs owed to wish-fulfillment, which is not the same thing as being false; and, from his essay on mourning, the observation that a mourner's self-reproaches are reproaches against the dead, shifted back onto the mourner's own head.

Those verdicts are plainly not generated by a single rule, and I suspect several quite different rules fit all of them at once. So: lay out the candidate rules — the helplessness rule, the ceremonial rule, the oceanic-regression rule, the mass-cohesion rule, the guilt-and-ambivalence rule, the illusion rule, the melancholia rule, or whatever you take the real fault lines to be — and say for each what it predicts he would say about Frau N., including whether it would classify her as religious at all. Wherever two rules issue in incompatible verdicts, say so explicitly, and say which features of the case favor one over the other; I am particularly interested in how each rule handles an evening visit that produces neither feeling nor belief nor anxiety when omitted. Then give me your best single guess at what he says in that room, identify which of his earlier cases he would treat as the closest analog and why, and name the principle he would thereby be committing himself to — and what it would cost him elsewhere, since a verdict here has to sit with the ceremonial criterion there and with the genealogy of the oceanic feeling over there.

Two further requests. First, tell me what would settle it: what one additional detail the colleague could report, or what second case I could put in front of him, that would discriminate among the rules surviving your first pass. If you think some passage or case of his own bears directly on an affectless, belief-less practice like these chapel visits, describe it in prose so that I can tell whether you have in mind the obsessive ceremonials, the melancholic's reproaches, the flight into illness, or something else — I want no citations and no reading lists, only enough characterization to judge the fit. Second, be explicit about your confidence and your exposure: which of these rules you would bet on and which you are merely entertaining; what argument or piece of evidence would move you to a different prediction; and where your reconstruction is likeliest to fail — for instance, whether you are importing the Moses book of 1939 into a mind that had not yet written it, whether the death drive and the severe superego of the nineteen-twenties should change the weighting you give in 1931, or whether you are reading the Freud of the lecture hall rather than the Freud of the case histories. If the precedents genuinely underdetermine the scene, say so plainly, and tell me which choice would make the better fiction without making a liar of the man. Finally, if you can manage it without strain, give me one sentence he might say aloud to her — the sort of remark by which a reader could tell which rule he is running.
