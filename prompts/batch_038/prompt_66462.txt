I am in the middle of an epistemology seminar whose current unit is formal models of belief, and last week's session on logical omniscience ended with an argument between two of my classmates that the instructor let run to the end of the period without settling. I would like a careful, move-by-move adjudication. I will try to report the exchange neutrally; neither position is mine.

**Setup, which both parties granted.** Dana is a competent mathematician. She assigns credence roughly 0.999 to the axioms of a standard arithmetic, and, for the sake of argument, both agree those axioms settle a particular decidable question — say, whether a given two-hundred-digit number is prime. Dana has no view about the number; she says she would put it near fifty-fifty. Both also agree she is not careless, confused, or otherwise failing at anything.

**Move 1 (Aaron).** Dana's credences are flatly incoherent, and not mildly so. Coherence requires credence to be preserved under entailment; her 0.999 in the axioms alongside 0.5 in a consequence is a package she would accept bets on in a combination that guarantees her a loss. Hintikka's label names a defect in the model, not a permanent fact about us, and any framework that scores her as rational is measuring the wrong thing.

**Move 2 (Bea).** That is a reductio of the model, not a verdict on Dana. A norm no finite agent can satisfy even in principle is not a norm but a description of a limit. The repair is to fine-grain the content: Dana bears her attitude to the axioms under one presentation and has never entertained the primality claim under its own. If contents are individuated the way standard possible-worlds semantics individuates them, then logically equivalent claims are literally the same content — and that assumption is what generates the paradox.

**Move 3 (Aaron).** Guises relocate the problem and break the machinery. Once contents are individuated that finely, coherence ceases to be a constraint relating distinct attitudes, so the probability calculus stops disciplining anything: you get a bloated inventory of unrelated states, each trivially coherent. And note the empirical point — tell Dana that the axioms entail the answer and she updates immediately. She is not immune to the norm; she has not yet discharged it. That looks like an unexplored consequence, not a different species of attitude.

**Move 4 (Bea).** Grant the updating. It shows she endorses the norm, not that she now violates coherence; and your account still owes a description of what she violated before being told — something she could not have been guided by. More seriously, your ideal cannot discriminate: on it, Dana, a first-year student, and a thermostat all sit at the same maximal distance from coherence. A graded alternative — coherence relative to the consequences an agent can actually derive, within some bound on effort — restores the distinctions we care about.

**Move 5 (Aaron).** The graded alternative collapses on its parameter. Which bound? There is no non-arbitrary specification; it shifts with time of day, with whether someone hands her a lemma, with whether she has a laptop in her bag. So the norm becomes hostage to hardware and circumstance: a faster reasoner is permitted to be less coherent, and no one can ever be criticized for failing to think harder. Worse, your own case turns on Dana being one proof away. If the only difference between her and someone who has written the proof down is unexpended effort, the difference is one of degree, not of kind. Keep closure as the norm of coherence and treat her violation as excused: incoherent, but not blameworthy.

**Move 6 (Bea).** Then the dispute is empty — you have conceded the criticism is soft, and soft criticism directed at an unreachable target is not criticism but a description of finitude. "Try harder" functions as a norm only where the target is reachable. And your soft-criticism view must handle the undecidable variant: substitute whether a particular program halts. There is no proof for Dana to find, so no expenditure of effort closes the gap, yet you presumably will not call her 0.5 incoherent there. Treat the two cases differently and you have already adopted something like my resource-relative norm; treat them alike and you are calling every finite agent, including yourself mid-sentence, massively irrational.

**What I would like from you:**

(a) A rating for each of moves 1 through 6 — clearly right, partly right, or clearly mistaken — with a sentence or two of justification per move, flagging explicitly wherever a move begs the question against the other side.

(b) A verdict on the two constructive proposals on the table, fine-grained or awareness-relative contents versus coherence relative to a derivational bound: which, if either, survives the objection the other raised, and whether there is a third option neither of them named.

(c) A view on move 5's "incoherent but excused" distinction — whether it preserves Aaron's discriminations or quietly concedes Bea's point.

(d) Whatever both are missing. Two candidates I noticed but could not press in seminar: neither one examined whether the Dutch-book charge in move 1 is even available at this distance from the coherence assumptions it depends on, and neither said how the diagnosis would change if the subject were a research group holding the 0.999 collectively rather than a single mathematician.

Please evaluate the arguments as actually made rather than by appeal to authority; I am not looking for attributions or a reading list, only an assessment of the reasoning.
