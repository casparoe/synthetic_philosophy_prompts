I teach a first-year graduate seminar in formal epistemology and decision theory — sixteen students, one eighty-minute session in a room with a whiteboard and no computers. They have had expected utility, Bayes, and conditionalization, and I showed them the Ellsberg urns in lecture last week without resolving them. The topic of this session is the result, usually credited to I. J. Good, that says roughly the following: for an agent who maximizes expected utility, updates by conditionalizing on whatever she learns, faces a fixed set of available acts, and whose utility function is not itself disturbed by the learning, freely available information is never expected to make her worse off — and it is strictly valuable exactly when it could change her choice. I want a classroom game in which the students run into that claim from the inside, and then run into its limits, without my stating it. In particular I want the design to be what you might call premise surgery: a baseline round in which every condition above holds and the students who look end up ahead, one round in which the information cannot change any decision because one act dominates, and then several further rounds, each of which is engineered to break exactly one of the conditions — so that in each of them declining free information turns out to be the rational play, and the students discover this from the scoreboard rather than from me. They should not be told in advance which round is which, or that the rounds differ in this systematic way at all.

Please give me the full apparatus: the physical setup and a materials list that costs nothing beyond paper and envelopes or cards; an exact payoff scheme in points rather than money, with a conversion at the end that creates real but small competitive stakes; the mechanism by which a student declines or accepts an information offer, including whether declines are public and whether a student can decline strategically in order to mislead others; the timing of each round to the minute, including a running scoreboard that the students fill in themselves; and a debrief that walks backwards from the scoreboard to a statement of the theorem that the students should be able to articulate before they hear its name. For each non-baseline round, tell me which condition it is designed to break and what feature of the round does the breaking — and be explicit if you think one of my suggested failure modes is a poor fit and something else should take its place.

Two things I especially want you to be candid about. First, what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this: the ambiguity-neutral students who spoil the anomaly by cheerfully looking, the students who reverse-engineer the round order and start playing the design instead of the game, refusals that come from suspicion of the instructor rather than from any risk attitude, scorekeeping errors, and the split between students who care about points and students who do not. Tell me how to detect each in the room and what to do about it. Second, where does the activity fall short of the idea it is meant to convey — in particular, the fact that I am handing these students a utility function through the scoring rule, so that a preference for not looking may be a preference about the game rather than a genuine attitude to risk, and the fact that at least one of the conditions cannot be put under real pressure with sixteen people and paper cards and must be supplied by argument in the debrief. Say plainly which condition that is and what I should say to cover for it. If the design holds together for eight students instead of sixteen, note the minimal version as well.
