I am preparing a graduate seminar session on the equality-of-what question — the dispute between resourcism, welfarism, and the capability approach over which currency distributive justice should equalize — and rather than run it off thought experiments I want to hang it on an institutional episode: the construction and successive revision of the United States poverty measure. Please treat the episode as a case study of the theory and run the analysis in both directions, theory illuminating case and case testing theory.

To keep the exercise independent of either of us recalling details, here is the record I will assume. Act one: in the mid-1960s Mollie Orshansky, an economist at the Social Security Administration, built the thresholds that became the Official Poverty Measure by taking the cost of the Agriculture Department's Economy Food Plan — the cheapest of several plans designed to meet minimum nutritional requirements — and multiplying it by three, the multiplier being derived from a 1955 household survey finding that families of three or more spent roughly a third of their after-tax money income on food. The thresholds were fixed in dollars, varied by family size and composition, and thereafter updated only by a general consumer price index. They measure pre-tax cash income: they exclude in-kind benefits such as food assistance and housing subsidies, ignore taxes and refundable credits, make no adjustment for regional differences in housing cost, and subtract nothing for medical, childcare, or commuting expenses. Adopted for statistical use in 1969, these thresholds still govern eligibility for a large body of federal programs. Act two: a 1995 National Academies panel recommended a redesign, and from 2011 the Census Bureau and the Bureau of Labor Statistics published the Supplemental Poverty Measure alongside the old one, as a statistical supplement with no role in determining eligibility. Its threshold is anchored not to a diet but to expenditure — the thirty-third percentile of what two-adult, two-child households actually spend on food, clothing, shelter, and utilities over five years of survey data, multiplied by 1.2 to cover other needs, then adjusted for family size and composition and for housing costs across metropolitan areas. On the household side it counts cash income plus near-cash benefits and refundable credits, minus taxes paid, and then subtracts out-of-pocket medical spending, work-related expenses including childcare, and child support paid to another household. Act three: in 2019 the executive branch opened a rulemaking on whether to keep updating the thresholds with the standard consumer price index or to switch to chained CPI-U, which registers consumer substitution away from goods whose relative prices have risen, or to a further variant calibrated to the spending patterns of lower-income households; after an unusually large volume of public comment the change was not adopted, and the press covered the dispute almost entirely as a fight about the politics of the poverty line.

First, a parameter-by-parameter mapping. Treat any poverty metric as five separable commitments — the anchor of the threshold, the indexation rule, the definition of household resources, the equivalence scale, and the geographic adjustment — and say for each which of the three currencies it operationalizes, where it is indifferent among them, and where it commits to something none of the three recognizes. My suspicion is that these accounts are usually deployed as though they were rival answers about a whole measure, when in fact any implemented measure mixes them: a metric can be resourcist in its anchor and capability-oriented in its resource definition. Test that suspicion, and tell me where the Official and Supplemental measures are internally inconsistent under this decomposition, as opposed to merely different.

Second, three puzzles I cannot resolve on my own. Is the subtraction of out-of-pocket medical spending doing capability work or welfare work? It looks like a conversion correction — the same income yields fewer functionings for a sick person — but the amount subtracted tracks a household's realized losses, which smells like an attempt to measure achieved well-being rather than real freedom. Tell me whether the two come apart here and which one the arithmetic is actually tracking. Is the expenditure-percentile anchor a covertly relative standard, one that makes the line rise with general prosperity and thereby answers the currency question with something like equal capacity to consume as the bottom third of one's contemporaries? If so, is that a defect or the correct view? And is Orshansky's multiplier itself a conversion argument smuggled inside a resource measure, since the factor of three was inferred from how income was in fact translated into food rather than from the cost of any bundle?

Third, the reverse direction: what does the episode do to the capability approach? The Supplemental measure was implemented as an accounting exercise with no explicit appeal to functionings or to the distinction between means and ends — does that show the capability critique was doing genuine explanatory work in the redesign, or that it was fitted on afterward to a set of technical choices made for other reasons? Be precise about where the theory is silent: it can say that income is a poor proxy for advantage, but it does not by itself select a ledger, and neither the thirty-third percentile nor the factor of 1.2 is derivable from any of the three accounts. Identify which design choices the theory can discipline and which are irreducible convention, and do not make the theory speak where it does not.

Fourth, Act three on its own: which currency was the indexation dispute actually about? Is chained CPI-U a welfarist move, because it credits substitution as a way of preserving satisfaction, or a resourcist one, because it still measures the changing cost of a bundle? And what does it show about the theory that a normative argument about the currency of justice was conducted for months entirely in the technical vocabulary of price indices, with the normative content never surfaced?

Three constraints. Do not give me a bibliography, a reading list, or citations; I want the argument, not the sourcing. If any of the facts above is materially wrong, say so in one line and proceed with the corrected version rather than abandoning the analysis. And close with the single place where you think this episode genuinely strains, embarrasses, or fails to test one of the three accounts — or tell me plainly that it does none of these, and why not.
