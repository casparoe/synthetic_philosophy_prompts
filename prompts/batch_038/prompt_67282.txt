Okay so this is a slightly weird ask. I'm a novelist, three books out, and I've got nine months of funded time at a residency starting in the fall — room, board, a stipend, no teaching — but the fellowship office wants a "research statement" to go with the book, meaning an actual question-driven plan rather than a synopsis or a theme. The book is about a man in his sixties who was genuinely dangerous when he was twenty-four, and who has spent forty years building an architecture around himself so the thing never gets a chance to happen: the job, the small town, the routines, the distance from his brother, never more than one drink, the wife he picked precisely because she would never corner him. That much is easy to write. What I cant figure out, and what I want the nine months for, is a distinction I keep needing and don't have.

Here's what I've noticed. The mask he built is maintained by the disposition it masks. He stays away from his brother because he's afraid of what he'd do; staying away means the fear never gets tested; the fear never getting tested is the only reason the disposition never gets worn down or revised. So the arrangement is self-sealing — the loop feeds itself, and it would keep feeding itself long after the circumstances that made it necessary have gone. My brother-in-law is a philosophy grad student and he pointed me at the literature on dispositions, the fink and mask cases where the object fails to manifest because something interferes, plus the antidote and mimicker variants and Lewis's patch to the simple conditional analysis. But as far as I can tell, in every one of those cases the interference comes from outside — a sorcerer, packing material, an electro-fink — and the whole point is that the disposition is still there underneath, intact, waiting. Nothing he gave me seems to handle a mask the bearer installed on purpose, forgot they installed, and which is now the only thing keeping the disposition alive rather than merely hidden. And there's a further wrinkle I need for the ending: by page 300 the brother is dead, the town has changed, and there is literally no circumstance left that would trigger anything. Is the disposition still there? Is "he's still violent" true, false, or just badly posed at that point? There's also that move someone makes where disposition talk is interest-relative, so what's true depends on what the ascriber cares about — I suspect that view dissolves my question entirely, which would be bad news for the novel, so I want it engaged head-on instead of waved away.

So: write me the statement, call it two pages. I need the question stated precisely enough that it could come out either way — right now I honestly can't tell if I'm asking one hard question or three soft ones, and I need you to say which and cut what doesn't belong. Then why the existing positions don't settle it: the conditional analyses and their patches, the causal-grounding accounts, the interest-relative response, and anything else that bears on a mask that sustains itself. Then the method — what I'd actually be doing month by month for nine months while also drafting the book three days a week, so the plan has to survive contact with a real schedule, and I want you to say what gets cut first if I fall behind. Then what a result looks like: I want something usable, ideally a set of conditions or a test a reader could apply to the text, so that the difference between "sealed and still there" and "burned out thirty years ago and nobody noticed" is something the book can dramatize rather than assert. And be honest about failure — tell me what would count as having wasted the nine months, including the possibility that the whole distinction is just a way of talking and I should rebuild the novel around something else. Don't bother with citations or a bibliography; my brother-in-law and I will fill those in. I want the argument structure, not the references.
