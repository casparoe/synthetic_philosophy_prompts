I'd like a joke about commitment races, and I want to set out the structure I have in mind first, so that the piece can be judged against something more specific than the phrase itself.

A commitment race arises where agents are able to bind their own future behaviour in ways that other players can observe: they might rewrite their own decision procedures, sign instruments with genuine enforcement behind them, or otherwise burn the bridge back to flexibility. Once such a commitment is in place and known, it changes the game everyone else is then playing, usually to the committer's benefit, since a party that visibly cannot back down gets to fix the terms. It follows that each player has a reason to commit before the others do, while the advantage of committing first largely evaporates once everyone has committed. What results is a race: sustained pressure to bind yourself early, before you have thought hard about what you are binding yourself to, in a game whose payoffs are still being settled by whatever the other players are about to commit to next.

Requirements:

- The punchline should turn on some feature of the race rather than on the vocabulary. Suitable raw materials include the incentive to commit ahead of deliberation, the decay of first-mover advantage once a commitment becomes common knowledge, the fact that a commitment only binds if it can be seen, the way a precommitment to retaliate obliges you to carry out a retaliation that has by then ceased to serve you, and the paradox that the player constitutionally incapable of committing may come out best. Choose one and build the joke on it.
- Avoid the obvious verbal shortcuts: no puns on committing to a relationship, a crime, or an institution; no "commitment issues"; and nothing whose humour would survive being retold with the word "commitment" swapped for "decision".
- Any form is acceptable — a single line, a brief exchange, a short scenario with a straight man — provided it is self-contained and lands without a footnote.
- It need not be intelligible to someone who has never encountered game theory, but it must not depend on the reader having encountered any particular author or result either.
