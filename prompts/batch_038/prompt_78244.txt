I cover science and ideas for a weekly magazine, and for the past year I have been reporting on a narrow question that keeps expanding: how we decide which animals count. The machinery is visible once you look for it. Welfare law protects some species and not others; regulators argue about whether an octopus or a shrimp belongs inside the protected category; cognition researchers publish a finding and it is immediately recruited to move the line a few inches. What has struck me, trying to write this up, is how much of the line-drawing sounds like narrative judgment wearing the clothes of empirical finding — which is why I want to build the essay around a novel rather than a case study.

The novel is Yann Martel's *Life of Pi*, and the philosophy is Robert Nozick's treatment of animals in *Anarchy, State, and Utopia* — in particular the two-tier proposal by which he tries to keep inviolability for persons without simply writing animals out of ethics altogether. As I understand it, he runs two moral regimes side by side: a broadly utilitarian one for animals, on which their happiness counts and ought to be promoted, constrained against the most brutal forms of treatment, and a strictly Kantian one for people, who may never be used merely as a resource for someone else's greater good. The rights of persons rest, on his account, on capacities animals are said to lack: conscious self-government, the ability to shape a life around a long-term plan, regulation by principles one has accepted, and the sort of reciprocal limit-setting that makes side constraints intelligible. He also concedes that the boundary is awkward, since human beings are animals too. Please begin by stating the view in your own terms, accurately and briefly, and by telling me whether the two tiers amount to a genuine compromise or to a way of having it both ways.

Then I want the reading. The obvious move — a boy and a tiger in a lifeboat, read through animal ethics — is available to anyone, so I would rather you work through the parts of the book that make the Nozickian frame genuinely uncomfortable:

- The early zoo chapters, where the narrator argues that a captive animal with territory, routine, adequate food and no predators is not obviously worse off than a wild one. Is that Nozick's utilitarianism for animals in practice, and does the novel endorse it, ironize it, or do both at once? What becomes of that endorsement when the same family loads its animals onto a freighter bound for North American buyers and the ship goes down?
- The lifeboat as a zoo under management: Pi establishes dominance over Richard Parker through territory, feeding schedule and conditioned seasickness, and says the tiger's presence is what kept him alive. Under Nozick's framework, is Pi promoting the tiger's welfare, or using him as a resource for a human good? Does the book seem to know which of these it is describing?
- Killing in order to feed: fish and turtles killed to eat and to feed the tiger, the first killing costing Pi something the later ones do not; the hyena eating the zebra alive and killing the orangutan who arrives on a raft of bananas. Does the novel treat predation among animals as morally different from killing by humans, and can Nozick's view preserve that difference, or does a total-happiness calculus for animals flatten it?
- The floating island that shelters and nourishes both of them and quietly consumes whatever sleeps in its trees at night. What is that doing in an argument about welfare: a naturalistic theodicy, or a counterexample to the idea that an animal's good can be read off its habitat?
- The ending, where Richard Parker walks into the jungle without looking back and Pi is undone by the absence of a proper farewell. Does the book imply that the relation it cares about is reciprocal — and if so, does that undercut the claim that animals lack the capacities on which inviolability is supposed to rest?

I also want the honest-critic part done properly. If the animals of the first story map onto the humans of the second, and the two shipping officials are invited to choose between them, is the novel about animals at all, or is it using them as an allegorical device that denies them precisely the independent standing I want to grant them? Confront that. And say where Martel is simply not thinking in Nozick's idiom — Pi's world is built out of sacredness, wonder and the choice of the better story, not rights and constraints — so that I don't force an analytic frame onto a book that thinks differently. Finish by telling me what the novel can do for this debate that an argument cannot: whether fiction changes how we hold the capacity criterion, or only dramatizes how we already hold it.

Practical constraints. About 1,800 to 2,500 words. No verbatim quotation from the novel — I will pull the lines from my own copy, so scene description and paraphrase are what I need. No bibliography, no reading list, no citations to secondary literature and no invented page numbers; if you are unsure of a plot detail or of how Nozick puts something, flag it rather than smoothing it over. Mark the two or three paragraphs that would work in print with minimal surgery, and tell me plainly where you think my conceit is weaker than I assume it is.
