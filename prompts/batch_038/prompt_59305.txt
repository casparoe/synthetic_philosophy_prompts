For my categorization seminar I've been building a little program that generates whole alphabets in invented handwriting styles, and one pattern keeps biting me that I don't have a word for.

If I distort a single letter — say I round the bowl of a single-story 'a' until it's nearly a circle and shrink its stem to a nub — it stops reading as an 'a' and starts reading as an 'o'. But if I apply that same distortion to every letter at once, so the whole family gets rounder and losser together, nothing loses its letterhood: my round 'a' is still an 'a', and it still doesn't collide with my round 'o', because the 'o' got rounder too and lost its stem. Same transformation, opposite verdicts, depending on whether it hits one member or the whole set. It's like a letter isn't its features but its distances to its neighbours, and those distances can slide around freely as long as everything slides together.

So: is there an established concept for a distortion that destroys a category member in isolation but is harmless — or is even what people mean by a consistent "style" — when applied globally? I know it's adjacent to family resemblance, to prototype shift, and to whatever Goodman-ish worries there are about which physical marks count as the same character, but none of those is the thing itself. Give me the nearest candidates, exactly where each one fails to cover it, and if nothing does, coin something and tell me what a boundary case looks like (does it still hold when a global distortion makes two letters actually merge?). Don't just hand me a label — I want the carving.
