I sit on a municipal committee drafting guidance for long-stay visitors, and at our last meeting a colleague ended a disagreement by invoking the proverb "when in Rome, do as the Romans do." The disagreement concerned a draft line telling visiting researchers that they ought to tip restaurant staff at the local rate, join queues in the local fashion, and not cross against the light even when the street is empty. I argued that a settled custom is not thereby a duty, and that some local customs — tipping above all — look less like shared norms than like ways of shifting an employer's costs onto guests. He replied that I had missed what the proverb actually asserts. I would rather have it unpacked than re-litigated: lay out, as a menu, the different claims the sentence could be making — from mere prudential advice about not standing out, through claims about coordination and the expectations one arouses in others, to claims about the respect owed a host community, to something closer to a metaethical thesis on which local practice settles what is right — and for each say whether it is plausible and whether it would genuinely oblige a visitor in each of my three cases. Mark where the readings diverge between tipping, queueing, and an empty intersection; say what the proverb can require when "the Romans" disagree among themselves; and identify any reading on which it fails precisely because the local practice is contested or objectionable.
