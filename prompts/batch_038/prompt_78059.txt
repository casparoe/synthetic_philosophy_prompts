I've been going back and forth with my brother about university funding — I did my degree in England, he did his in Germany — and instead of arguing about slogans I started writing down my gut verdicts on specific designs. Now I can't tell what principle, if any, they add up to. Here they are:

1. No tuition at all, paid out of general taxation. A plumber's kid who left school at 16 pays for a surgeon's kid who becomes a corporate lawyer. Verdict: unjust, even though the system overall redistributes plenty. The transfer runs the wrong way inside this particular program.

2. Same thing, except every graduate pays a surcharge of ~3% of income above a threshold for twenty years, and that funds the system. Verdict: clearly just, not merely better. The people who get the credential pay for it, spread over what it lets them earn.

3. Tuition at cost, plus means-tested grants for low-income families, same total public spend as 2. Verdict: I keep wanting to like this one because it's the most precisely targeted of the three and I don't. On every distributional metric it beats 2 and I still think 2 is the better design. My reasons are mushy — take-up failure, the kid has to know to apply, and the cleaner/teacher/care-worker family gets nothing — but none of them feel like principles.

4. Public loans with interest indexed above inflation, the rate rising with the borrower's income. Verdict: bad, and bad for a specific reason — the state has stopped insuring and started making a margin, i.e. it's charging people for the very risk it's uniquely placed to pool.

5. Income-contingent loan at the government's own borrowing cost, fixed % of income above a threshold, balance wiped after thirty years. Verdict: best of the lot.

6. But under 5, the teacher and the nurse pay for thirty years and get wiped at the end, the dropout who never earned much is wiped early, and the banker clears the whole thing in eight years and then pays nothing more — so per unit borrowed the highest earner pays least. Verdict: 5 is still the best option, and it's still unjust in a way I can't articulate, and not unjust enough to make me prefer 3 or 4.

7. Free tuition at a hyper-selective place with a huge endowment per student, vs. free tuition at an open-access college where half the students don't finish. Same slogan, same money. Verdict: the second is unambiguously good; the first is fine but I'd never campaign for it, mostly because I suspect it lands on people who'd have paid anyway.

8. A for-profit college charging $30k for a certificate with weak labor-market value, funded by public loans. Verdict: bad. But I also refuse both obvious fixes — restricting loan eligibility punishes honest students at cheap colleges, and tying eligibility to graduate earnings means the state ends up running the curriculum of the whole sector by remote control. So I'm condemning the thing and vetoing every instrument that would change it.

My tentative principle, such as it is: what's being distributed isn't the cost of education, it's the variance of the earnings risk attached to getting one. The state should be risk-bearer of last resort because it's the only agent that can pool across cohorts and can't go bust, and it should charge roughly what it costs it to carry that risk — the risk-free rate — with anything above that being profit off the people it's supposed to be insuring. Who benefits, who can pay, and who deserves are all downstream of that.

Problem is I think this fails on my own cases. It says nothing against 1, since there's no loan and no risk-bearing at all, yet I think 1 is unjust. It blesses 3 outright if the loans in 3 are priced at cost with forgiveness, and I reject 3. It's silent on 8. And it can't see the injustice in 6. There's also a feasibility clause I keep smuggling in: I like 2 partly because it looks universal and builds a constituency of graduates all paying the same surcharge, whereas 3 gets gutted at the first budget squeeze — roughly the thing Korpi and Palme argued about welfare states generally, that the more you target, the less you end up redistributing. I don't know if that's a genuine clause of the principle or an excuse I reach for after the fact.

Also name a case that would most sharply discriminate between your candidates. And be blunt about which of my verdicts have to go, especially whether 2 and 3 can be saved together.
