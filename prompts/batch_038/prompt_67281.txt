I cover science and ideas for a British broadsheet, and for the better part of a decade I have been filing on a single question: which animals are conscious, and how anyone is supposed to know. Over that period the argument has acquired an odd shape, and I would like help diagnosing it, because I am no longer sure what kind of disagreement it is.

Let me describe the two camps as I have come to know them, using labels of my own rather than theirs.

The first — call them the indicator camp — treats sentience as a cluster property, evidenced from many independent directions at once: nociceptors and endogenous opioids; withdrawal that cannot be dismissed as reflex because it is traded off against hunger or a mating opportunity; protective tending of an injured body part; conditioned aversion that survives a change of context; play; novel problem-solving that looks as though the animal is representing the state of its own body rather than the reward. Each indicator is weak in isolation. Together, on their account, they license an inference to the best explanation: this system very probably has experience. Their method is cumulative and their conclusions are deliberately hedged — likely, plausible, cannot be ruled out — with a policy corollary that where the evidence is suggestive and the stakes are high, precaution should decide the case.

The second — call them the mechanism camp — thinks the whole edifice is evidentially hollow. Behavioural and pharmacological indicators, they say, are precisely what one would expect of a sophisticated unconscious control system; nociception is not pain, sensitisation is not suffering, and a list of correlates is not a criterion. What is needed is a theory saying which physical processes generate experience and which do not — a global-workspace, recurrent-processing, higher-order-thought, integrated-information or predictive-processing account — after which the question for any animal becomes narrow and tractable: does this nervous system implement the relevant mechanism? Their conclusions are bolder in both directions, admitting some invertebrates that the first camp finds marginal and excluding many organisms the first camp would protect.

Now the puzzle. Each side concedes the other's data. The indicator camp agrees that nociception alone proves nothing; the mechanism camp agrees that the behavioural findings are real. They commission the same experiments, read the same results, and end up further apart than when they started. Both also accept that the question is decision-relevant — the Animal Welfare (Sentience) Act 2022 drew a legal line taking in all vertebrates together with cephalopods and decapod crustaceans, on the back of a commissioned scientific review, and every subsequent regulatory fight reopens the science — and both insist that this relevance ought to sharpen the epistemics rather than distort it.

My working hypothesis, offered as a hypothesis, is that the stall has nothing to do with evidence and everything to do with the logical grammar of the target property. The indicator camp treats sentience as a threshold concept: a binary fact about a creature, approached asymptotically by accumulating graded evidence. The mechanism camp treats it as a profile: something that comes in kinds and dimensions rather than amounts, so that no finite list of indicators can ever be summed into a verdict, and the only intelligible question is which processes realise which kind of experience. If that is right, the two camps are not disagreeing about animals at all; they are answering different questions under one word, and every experiment gets scored against two incommensurable standards. A rival hypothesis I would like taken equally seriously is less flattering: that the disagreement concerns priors over evolutionary convergence — how widely one expects a functional problem such as flexible harm-avoidance to have been solved by experience rather than by cleverness alone — and that neither side can justify its prior, so the argument is bedrock all the way down. A third possibility is that the policy frame has quietly displaced the scientific one, and that what looks like a dispute about evidence is in fact a dispute about who carries the burden of proof once a verdict has consequences.

So: which is it? Diagnose the structure of the disagreement, working from what each side actually claims rather than from my labels, and tell me whether it is verbal, substantive, methodological, or simply two questions sharing a name. Then tell me what, if anything, would count as progress — a distinction that dissolves the impasse, an experiment that both camps would agree in advance to be decisive, a formal result such as an agreed measurement scale whose axioms both would accept, or a change of question — or make the case that no progress should be expected, and say why. Please do not send me a reading list or citations; I know the literature, and what I need is argument. I am drafting a long read, so something in the region of twelve hundred words of reasoning rather than a survey would suit, and I would like you to finish by identifying which part of your own diagnosis you consider most likely to be wrong.
