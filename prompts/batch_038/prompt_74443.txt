So, in three weeks I'm somehow on the hook for a 20-minute slot plus about ten minutes of Q&A at my lab's internal alignment offsite — the one where everyone goes to a hotel for two days and the wifi is bad. I'm a second-year PhD student attached to the post-training team and I've never presented to this group before. My talk argues something a bit unfashionable: that process supervision and outcome supervision are not two seperate paradigms, and that the empirical case for step-level reward models is mostly a credit-assignment result wearing a conceptual costume. My three moves are roughly these. (1) In nearly every PRM people actually train now, the step label is an estimate of "if you continue from this prefix, how likely are you to reach a correct final answer" — an outcome quantity, just estimated by rollout instead of by a person; and even the human step labels in that 800k-label dataset everyone cites look to me like the annotator doing the rollout in their head, so the target being learned is outcome-defined either way. (2) That makes a PRM a value function over prefixes with terminal success as the reward, which means dense step feedback beats sparse outcome feedback for the same reason it always has in RL — variance in credit assignment — and not because we've learned anything about what reward is supposed to be a function of. (3) So the real design axes are label noise (rollout count), whether you bootstrap or not, and how much surface you leave for the policy to exploit your reward model's mistakes: bias-variance and reward-hacking knobs, not a paradigm. And here's the part I'm most nervous about — if this is really just the Monte Carlo versus TD distinction, we should have inherited that literature's pathologies, bootstrapped estimates compounding their own bias, instability under function approximation, the whole deadly-triad story, and I want to claim that some of the field's surprise about PRMs being confidently wrong or getting gamed by plausible-looking steps is a rediscovery rather than a discovery.

The audience is the problem, which is why I'm prepping. The room will be maybe 25 people: mostly empirical post-training and RL folks who ship reward models and think in ablations and compute-matched comparisons; two or three theory-leaning people who want guarantees and won't accept a hand-wave; one senior person who did classical value-function work for a decade before LLMs and who I suspect will enjoy this far too much; and a couple of PRM die-hards whose papers I'd be implicitly calling confused. Nobody in that room does philosophy of anything. So please don't give me the internet-flavored objections — no mesa-optimizers, no wireheading, no essay about whether reward is a relation. Give me the eight or so things these specific people will actually say, ordered by how much damage each would do to the talk, each phrased the way that kind of person phrases it (including the mildly condescending framing from the die-hards, who I expect will open with "did you control for compute"). For each one, give me a reply I can deliver standing up in under two minutes, plus a one-line note on where that reply is soft — I'd rather find the hole myself than have someone else find it in front of everyone. I also want: what to concede in the first five minutes so the talk doesn't die on a technicality; the single reply that absolutely has to land for move (3) to survive; which objections I can kill with one slide instead of verbally; and the question I'm praying nobody asks. My guess is that it's whether human step labels really are just mental rollouts, since I have zero data on that, but tell me if there's something worse lurking.

One thing I've been warned about: at least one person will say the distinction is definitional and not empirical — process reward is a function of the prefix, outcome reward is a function of the whole trajectory, that's a difference in the argument of the function, full stop — and I honestly don't know how to answer that without sounding like I'm just relabelling things.
