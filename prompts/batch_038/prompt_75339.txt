Ok so I've been chewing on the tickle defense — the idea that in Newcomb-type problems the agent always has some introspectible upstream mental state (a leaning, an urge, a "tickle") that the predictor is really keying onto, so that once the agent conditions on her own tickle, evidential and causal decision theory stop diverging and pick the same act. If that generalizes, then most of the CDT/EDT war is fought over problems no human ever actually faces, which feels like a big deal and also like a dodge.

Here's the proposition I want a number on. Call it P:

P = in every realistic Death in Damascus case, an EDT agent and a CDT agent end up performing the same act, because there is some antecedent state of the agent that screens off the correlation between her choice and where Death will be.

(For the setup: you run into Death in the Damascus market, he mentions he has an appointment with you tomorrow in Aleppo, you buy a ticket to Aleppo, and he's standing there when you arrive.)

Give me your actual credence in P. Not "it depends on your theory of conditionals" — a number, plus the decomposition. I want the sub-questions broken out with a rough probability and a line of justification each, roughly along these lines, though add or swap terms if you think mine are the wrong cuts:

- how likely is it that there even IS such a screening-off state here, as opposed to the predictor keying directly on the decision itself;
- how much rides on whether we let the agent randomize over Aleppo / staying / somewhere third, and on whether a randomized choice counts as having a visible tickle at all;
- whether Death's foreknowledge is better modeled as an extremely reliable predictor of a physical process (in which case the counterfactuals behave normally and the two theories may just coincide) or as something that makes the counterfactuals go weird;
- how much work "realistic" is smuggling in — i.e. the probability that the cases where CDT and EDT genuinely diverge are exactly the ones with no plausible tickle.

Then combine them however you like into one figure and show the arithmetic. Tell me which single term P is most sensitive to, and what sort of argument or thought experiment would move it the most.

One more thing: rerun the number for a version where Death is only 92% accurate about where you'll end up instead of being infallible, and say whether the drop from infallible to merely very good makes P go up or down and why.

My friend puts P at about 0.75 and I'm at about 0.25. Where do you think thier disagreement actually lives — which of the terms above is the one we'd have to settle first before anything else mattered?
