Background first, because I think the details matter for where this position actually bites. I run a small data-comms shop inside a county public health agency — four of us, we make the charts that end up in press releases, clinic waiting rooms, and the occasional slide a commissioner reads for eleven seconds. A couple of years ago someone taped one of those Vienna museum lines to the wall above the printer: "To remember simplified pictures is better than to forget accurate figures." I went down the rabbit hole from there and never fully came back out — the Gesellschafts- und Wirtschaftsmuseum in Red Vienna, Arntz's woodcuts, Marie Reidemeister doing the "transformation" from messy source data to a self-explanatory chart, the Mundaneum side-venture, the Moscow Izostat institute doing Five-Year-Plan graphics, the exile years in The Hague and then the Oxford institute. What I came out with is a position I've been pushing on my coworkers and getting mild pushback on, and I want it stress-tested properly rather than the polite version.

Here it is. A well-designed picture language isn't just a popularization layer you put on top of real social-scientific content — it's an epistemic improvement on prose for that content, for two reasons. First, pictorial representation is systematically less ideologically and nationally parochial than verbal language: a sentence smuggles in a whole metaphysical and political history through its vocabulary, while a repeated unit symbol mostly doesn't. Second, the formal discipline of the medium is itself an honesty device — you can't fudge a serial repetition, and there's no pictorial equivalent of the weasel subordinate clause ("arguably," "in the context of," "experts note that") — so a picture language forces you to make your claim legible, and legible claims are the ones you can actually be caught out on. And a corollary I also hold: every apparent failure of the project — cross-cultural misreading of icons, the fact that you can't picture a negation or a conditional or a counterfactual, the fact that the same technique was used to sell Soviet production targets — is a failure of implementation or of scope, not of the idea. I'm aware that corollary has the shape of a motte-and-bailey, and I also know I'm partly reading Neurath secondhand, so if you think I've inflated him into something stronger than he said, say where.

So: what would count against this? I want the map of its exposure, ranked from fatal to merely awkward. Concretely — which empirical results would be real evidence against it rather than noise (systematic cross-cultural comprehension failures that no amount of redesign fixes; findings that repeated-unit pictograms underperform plain bar charts on the comparison tasks that matter; the observation that the transformation step is exactly where all the judgment and ideology gets concentrated, so the medium relocates the problem rather than dissolving it)? What does my position predict that its rivals don't, such that the rivals could be caught out too — I'm thinking of the strong theory-ladenness line, on which no representational medium is neutral, and the deflationary design line, on which the medium doesn't matter and good practice is good practice? Is the self-undermining worry real: if pictures unite and words divide, then the claim that pictures are neutral is itself a sentence, in a verbal language, defending a program that was explicitly a socialist planning program? Is the can't-express-negation-or-modality limitation fatal, or does the correct reply just collapse into "fine, it's a helping language, not a replacement," which sounds like retreat to the motte? Does the historical record of actual use — municipal visual education, Izostat, the American tuberculosis campaigns, warning signage, emoji — tell for or against, and does the view need a case where a picture campaign changed belief or behavior where prose demonstrably failed? And which of these would Neurath's own defenders concede as counting against them, versus dismiss? Finally, give me an honest verdict on the corollary: is "any failure is bad implementation" unfalsifiable, and if it is, is that a problem for a view of this kind or is it just the normal structure of an engineering program (nobody thinks a collapsed bridge refutes structural theory)? Don't hedge into mush — if the answer is "this is well insulated and that's fine" or "this is dead," say which. And then tell me what would change your own mind: which of your arguments above you think is weakest, where you're most likely to be wrong, and whether you'd downgrade anything after actually handling the primary material. I'm not asking for citations or a reading list, I'll chase sources myself — just the reasoning. American English, please.
