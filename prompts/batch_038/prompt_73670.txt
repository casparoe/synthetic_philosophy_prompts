I'm applying for a ten-week summer research grant from my own department — small money, mostly so that I dont have to work in a bar — and I need a two-page proposal on a question about transitivity. Here's the half-formed idea I want made fundable.

The usual spectrum set-up: one person enduring something agonising over a long stretch, versus an enormous number of people each enduring something trivially unpleasant. You can walk from one end of the series to the other in steps each of which looks like a change for the better, while the two ends look like the reverse of that. Standard responses are that the steps are too small to detect, that the relation is vague, that some pairs are on a par rather than comparable, or that the intransitivity is genuine and the money-pump objection can be absorbed. My hunch is that all of them share a hidden assumption: that there is one relation, 'better than', which has to rank every pair in the spectrum, so that intransitivity can only be a fact about that relation. But suppose the ranking actually depends on a weighting parameter — how much duration you trade against intensity, say — and the series only looks intransitive because we are aggregating orderings taken at different parameter values without noticing that we are doing it. That would make it a Condorcet-cycle style artefact rather than a feature of value.

So the question: can that diagnosis be made to work, and if it can, what is left of the intransitivity debate?

Turn that into a proposal containing:

- the question stated precisely enough that a reader could tell whether I had answered it, rather than something that just sounds like a question;
- why the existing positions don't already settle it, including how I'd handle the obvious objection that parameter-indexing merely relocates the problem to the choice of parameter, and to whether there is any fact about which parameter value is the right one;
- the method, week by week — what I would actually be doing, given that I can't run experiments and I don't want this to turn into a survey of other people's views;
- what a result would look like: what I'd have to establish for the diagnosis to count as successful, and what a partial success would be;
- what would count as the project failing, stated in a way that isn't just 'I didn't finish';
- an honest read on scope. Ten weeks, one person, essentially no supervisor input, and I'm not a formal epistemologist.

Skip the reference list, I'll put that together myself — describing positions and arguments without naming names is completely fine.

If you reckon the parameter-indexing idea doesn't survive contact with the real cases, say so plainly, and say what would have to be true of it for you to change your mind — I'd much rather hear that now than in week nine.
