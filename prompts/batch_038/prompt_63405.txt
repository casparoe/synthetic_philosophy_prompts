I have no academic training in history or philosophy — I left school at sixteen and work in logistics — but I have been reading on my own about why some societies conquered others, and I have run into a passage I cannot evaluate. It comes from a popular history book I picked up at an airport, in a chapter about whether climate, disease, and volcanoes can be treated as actors in history. The paragraph runs, more or less in full:

"For most of the nineteenth century, geographers were hard determinists: they held that climate determines the character of peoples, and therefore the fate of nations. That view was not merely wrong but wicked, since it supplied a scientific-looking excuse for empire, and it was abandoned by the discipline in the early twentieth century in favor of possibilism, the doctrine that the environment only sets outer limits within which culture freely chooses. Possibilism settled the question. The recent fashion for books that explain world history by domesticable crops, the orientation of continental landmasses, and epidemic disease is determinism returned in more respectable clothes. Their authors protest that they are only soft determinists — that geography shaped rather than dictated events — but this is a distinction without a difference: if a factor reliably predicts which societies became rich and which were conquered, it is doing the determining, whatever it is called. Once we see this, we can also see why the honest conclusion is that germs, monsoons, and eruptions belong in the background of a historical narrative, as setting rather than as cause, and that agency in history belongs only to human beings, since only human beings can act for reasons."

I am inclined to think the last sentence is wrong and that the paragraph is unfair somewhere earlier, but I cannot locate where, and I would rather not simply take the author's word for the history of his own discipline. Could you work through it carefully? Specifically:

1. Which of its claims about determinism, about possibilism, and about the current popularity of geographic explanation are accurate, which are common but rough simplifications, and which are wrong in a way that actually changes the argument?

2. The paragraph slides between determines, shapes, dictates, and reliably predicts. Are these interchangeable? If not, which of the substitutions is load-bearing, and what does each one hide?

3. Is the move from this view was used to justify empire to this view is false a legitimate one? And is the mirror-image move — treating the modern authors' evidently anti-racist intentions as a point in their favor — legitimate either?

4. It borrows hard and soft determinism from the free-will debate. Does the borrowing carry over cleanly, or does soft determinism mean something there that makes the label misfire when applied to these historians?

5. It seems to me that explaining why an epidemic devastated a particular population in a particular decade, and explaining why some continents had guns and steel centuries later, are not the same task — and that the paragraph treats them as rivals for a single slot. Am I right, and does it matter for whether the conclusion holds?

6. Does the conclusion — that non-human factors can only be setting and never cause, and that agency belongs exclusively to human beings — follow from anything true in the paragraph? I keep feeling that caused a great deal and was an agent are being run together, and I would like to know whether an epidemic or an eruption can be a genuine cause of a historical outcome without being an agent, and what a historian gives up by refusing to say so.

I am not looking for a reading list or for names of books to go and buy; I would rather understand the reasoning than be told what to read next.
