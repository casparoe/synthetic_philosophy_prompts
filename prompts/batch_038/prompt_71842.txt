I'm in a reading group on machine consciousness — mostly grad students, one of us actually does RL for a living — and last week we ended up arguing about the AI welfare question, specifically whether a reinforcement-learning agent's reward signal is the thing that carries valence. Half the group kept saying stuff like "if reward is negative the agent is suffering," and my friend who does RL said that's naive, because reward is a design choice: you can shift it by whatever constant you like and nothing about the agent changes. That exchange is the seed of what I want you to build. I'm not looking for an essay on the debate, I'm looking for a case engineered to a spec.

Here's the spec. Two agents, same MDP, same architecture, same training procedure, same everything an outside observer could ever measure — same policy, same value *differences*, same advantage function, same learning dynamics — except one has its reward shifted up by a large positive constant and the other shifted down by a large negative one. So on a reward-magnitude view of valence, one of them is in permanent agony and the other permanently elated, while on any view that ties valence to reward prediction error, or to what the reward function reinforces, they are alike in every respect there is to be alike in. Constraints: it has to be a real trainable setup, not sci-fi, so give me the environment concretely enough that I can see it's a thing you could actually run — a gridworld or a simple control task is fine, and a few lines of pseudocode would help. Keep the scenario itself to one or two paragraphs, and no anthropomorphic vocabulary anywhere in the description of the case: no "feels," no "wants," no "notices," no "experiences." And don't smuggle in the verdict — the difference between the two agents has to be a genuine feature of the formal description rather than a stipulated inner life, because the whole point is to find out whether welfare facts about an agent can outrun everything that's functionally nailed down. If they can't, then alot of the reward-as-valence talk in this debate is doing no work whatsoever, and I want the case to make that vivid either way.

Then do the demonstration. Name the accounts you're testing — reward magnitude as valence, TD error / prediction error as valence, a dispositional account on which valence just is what the reward signal reinforces, plus any others you think belong — and for each one give the verdict and the reason for it in terms of the actual math of your case, not vibes. After that, be honest about which feature is load-bearing. My understanding, which you should check rather than take on faith, is that a constant per-step offset leaves the optimal policy untouched in a discounted infinite-horizon task, and that it's really a special case of potential-based shaping with the potential held constant. But I also think the invariance breaks in variable-length episodic tasks, where a constant offset changes how much the agent cares about terminating early — so tell me whether the case survives that, what the verdicts look like if I swap in a variable-length environment, and whether that repair saves the thought experiment or quietly turns it into a different one. Finish with the strongest attack from the losing side: someone who insists the negatively-offset agent really is worse off should get a real argument, including a straight answer to the objection that a difference with zero functional footprint isn't a difference in anything at all. And throughout, please keep settled things separate from things people who know this literature genuinely disagree about — the invariance results are one matter, whether reward has any connection to felt valence at all is another, and I dont want a contested position handed to me as the consensus of the field. If you think my framing is off, that the two views I've named aren't the live options, say so and tell me what the live ones are.
