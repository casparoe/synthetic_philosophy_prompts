Had a three hour argument with a friend last night about moral trade and we got absolutley nowhere, and I'm still not sure whether she's right or I'm just digging in. Quick version of the idea I was defending: people who hold genuinely different moral views can trade across the difference — you back off yours and back mine, I do the same for you — and that this is often a good idea rather than a sellout. Her view is that it's either incoherent, because there's no common scale to trade on, or corrupting, because it turns things that shouldn't have prices into prices. Probably both, she says.

Write me a dialogue between the two positions. Put it on a small grantmaking board, last two decisions of the year, two members, and make the trade concrete rather than abstract:

- One member is a straight welfarist. Her proposal is a conditional cash transfer that pays people small amounts to complete a vaccination schedule; on welfare numbers it's by far the better buy. The other member thinks paying people to do what they already have a duty to do corrupts the practice, and opposes it on principle.
- The other member's proposal funds something with basically no measurable welfare return — keeping a dying regional dialect alive, with a handful of speakers left. The welfarist thinks it's sentimental waste.
- Then one of them floats the trade: I'll vote for yours if you vote for mine. Both grants pass. Neither would have voted for the other's on its own merits, and both know that perfectly well.

Things I want them to actually get into, because these are where we kept circling:

1. Whether the trade needs a shared metric of moral value at all. I kept saying it doesn't — each side gets an improvement measured in its own terms, like any ordinary swap. She kept saying that move already smuggles in a second-order value theory, namely count each party's own satisfaction as equally weighty, which is exactly what a non-welfarist should refuse. I never had a clean answer to that.
2. Whether accepting the trade shows the principled member never really held her view. If it can be traded for a grant, wasn't it just a preference with a price the whole time? What can she say back that isn't special pleading?
3. Complicity and expression. Voting for something you believe is wrong, in exchange for something you believe is right, feels different from abstaining or from losing the vote. Is that difference real, or is it squeamishness? And does it matter that the other side's wrong is going to happen anyway without your vote, so you're not making it happen so much as letting it happen?
4. Whether the welfarist has the symmetric problem: is trading away your view about what's most efficient, for a symbolic good, any cleaner than the other way round?

Make them both sharp. Neither one gets to be the strawman, and I'd rather see my side take real hits than see my side win. They should respond to what the other just actually said — no alternating prepared speeches. Someone can concede a point, someone can shift ground, but dont land them in a neat agreement at the end; sharpen and locate the disagreement instead and let it stay open.

Roughly 900 to 1200 words. Start straight in with the dialogue: no preface, no title, no setup paragraph explaining what moral trade is. No references or names of papers, I'm not chasing anything down, I want the reasoning.

Then after the dialogue, clearly separated, a short note: which of the moves in there are more or less common ground among people who work on this, and which are genuinely disputed or rest on one party's contested assumptions being treated as settled. I want to come away knowing where the debate actually stands rather than thinking one side has it sewn up. Also flag anything you fudged or anywhere you leaned on a debatable reading of the ideas.

Oh, and write it in British English.
