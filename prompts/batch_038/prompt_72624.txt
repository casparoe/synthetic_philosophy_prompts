Two friends of mine — Nora, who is finishing a physics PhD, and Dev, who reads philosophy of science for pleasure — had a long argument at a dinner I hosted about whether the Landauer–Bennett resolution of Maxwell's demon actually resolves anything. I followed the first two exchanges and then lost the thread. Afterwards they asked me to referee and I had nothing useful to offer. Here is the argument as they made it, in order.

(1) Nora: Szilard's one-molecule engine puts the problem cleanly. A single molecule in a box, a partition inserted, the demon measures which side the molecule is on, and the molecule does kT ln 2 of work expanding isothermally against the partition. One bit of information has been turned into work. Brillouin's answer was that the measurement itself must cost at least that much, so nothing is gained; Bennett showed this was the wrong place to look — a measurement can be carried out reversibly, at no thermodynamic cost, provided the demon ends up correlated with the molecule. The cost is not in looking. It is in forgetting: to run the cycle again the demon must return its memory to a standard blank state, and erasing one bit dissipates at least kT ln 2 into the bath. The books balance exactly, one bit for one bit, and the second law survives with information acknowledged as a physical quantity.

(2) Dev: Then the resolution assumes the demon is forgetful, and that assumption is the whole argument hiding in a premise. Nothing forces erasure. Give the demon a long blank tape instead of a single register: it measures, writes the answer into the next unused cell, and moves on. It never overwrites, so it never erases, so it never pays. With a tape long enough it extracts kT ln 2 per cycle indefinitely. Your balance sheet closes only for a demon with finite memory, and finite memory is a stipulation, not a theorem.

(3) Nora: The tape has to start out blank, and a blank state is a low-entropy state. Your demon is not manufacturing work out of information; it is spending a stockpile. And the stockpile is not really infinite — an actually infinite register of reliably distinguishable physical cells is not something a universe can supply: it needs unbounded volume and unbounded energy to keep the cells distinct against thermal noise. What you have described is not a perpetual motion machine but a machine whose fuel tank you have hidden offstage.

(4) Dev: Fine — but notice what you just conceded. Your answer to my objection is that the universe does not contain infinitely many good memory cells. That is a claim about cosmology and the finitude of matter, not a claim about information. If the second law is safe only because memory runs out, then Landauer's principle is not what is doing the saving, and the slogan that information is physical is overselling: the demon fails for the same mundane reason every other engine fails, because the available free energy is finite.

(5) Nora: That is not a concession, it is the conclusion. Show that the demon consumes a low-entropy memory resource, and that consuming exactly one bit of it buys exactly kT ln 2 of work, and you have established that memory states and thermodynamic entropy are denominated in the same currency and trade at a fixed exchange rate. That is a substantive, quantitative claim, and it has been tested — feedback-controlled experiments have driven a system up a potential by acquiring information, with the cost accounted for bit by bit. The finitude of the tape is not doing the work; the exchange rate is.

(6) Dev: Then the exchange rate is doing work only for demons that reset. For a demon that never resets, your principle says nothing, and what is left is my point: the paradox was never in danger of being resolved by the physics of information, because there was never a workable demon to begin with. Any real measuring device is warm, noisy, and powered, and would fail long before any of this accounting came into play.

Please adjudicate the exchange move by move. For each of the six numbered moves, say whether it is clearly wrong, partly right, or clearly right — and where a move bundles two separable claims, rate them separately — giving two or three sentences of justification grounded in the physics and in the logic of the argument rather than in who is the better speaker. Then settle the two questions I could not: first, does Dev's non-erasing demon genuinely defeat Nora's resolution, or does Nora have an answer that does not depend on the memory being finite; second, is Dev right that the second law's safety turns out to be a mundane claim about finitude rather than a claim about information? Finish by naming any consideration that neither of them raised which would change how the dispute should be scored. End your response with a single line beginning "Verdict:" followed by one sentence naming who is more right overall and the respect in which the other is right.
