Two people in my philosophy-of-science reading group got into it last night and I couldn't tell who was winning, so I wrote down their moves as best I could afterwards. Background: we'd been on Carnap's principle of tolerance, and it turned into a row about whether his conventionalism actually has anything to say about planning an economy.

(1) Alice: tolerance means no framework is true or false — you adopt whichever is most expedient and then ask your internal questions inside it. So for Carnap the choice between a market order and a centrally planned one is just a framework choice, a matter of practical expediency rather then truth. His conventionalism isn't hostile to ambitious planning, it's built for it: settle the form of description by decision, then plan inside it. Her example was Neurath's in-kind accounting — drop money prices and reckon everything in tonnes of coal and bushels of wheat — which she said is exactly the sort of thing Carnap would treat as switching to a new linguistic form, not as discovering a fact about the world.

(2) Ben: that conflates a language with an institution. An economy isn't a framework in Carnap's sense. Internal questions get settled inside the framework; whether to direct production by decree isn't an internal question of any framework, it's a political decision, so tolerance is simply silent on planning.

(3) Alice: but external questions are precisely questions about which framework to adopt, and Carnap treats those as practical matters decided by expediency, not by proof. A whole community can resolve to adopt one and then work inside it. Ben's objection just restates the point in sceptical clothing.

(4) Ben: grant that, and Carnap can't be recruited *for* planning either. On his own account the framework choice is something we can only advise about, never demonstrate. So Alice's reading makes tolerance congenial to planning by making it congenial to anything — including a form of description imposed from above by a regime. If you can't say the person who chose differently is mistaken, you can't say the planner is right either.

(5) Alice: politically empty isn't politically neutral. The content of tolerance is that your opponent who picks another framework is not in error, and that removes the main licence for settling framework disputes by force. That's a substantive anti-authoritarian commitment, which is what I meant by saying his view has political teeth.

(6) Ben: that's a different argument from the one in (1), and it doesn't follow from anything Carnap holds — if there's no truth of the matter, don't-coerce isn't implied, it's just a preference Carnap happened to share with his milieu.

Where I got lost was around (4) and (6). Could you go through moves (1) to (6) in order, rate each on a five-point scale from clearly mistaken to clearly right with a sentence or two of justification per move, then say who came out ahead overall and what both of them are missing? Lead with your bottom line in the very first sentence and then give the reasoning — no preamble and no restating the question back at me.
