I am assembling a problem set for a graduate seminar on collective action, and I want a worked ranking exercise built around a case I have written deliberately so that the standard diagnoses do not fit cleanly. Everything relevant is stated below; please reason only from these facts, do not supplement the setting with assumptions of your own, and do not cite, summarize, or recommend any literature.

**The case.** A semi-arid agricultural basin overlies a single unconfined aquifer. Roughly three hundred farms draw on it, together with one small city. Extraction is by wells on private land; there is no metering and no telemetry, and neither is realistically obtainable in the near term, so what any given farmer pumps can be inferred only indirectly, from crop acreage, energy bills, and visible rig activity, while concealment is cheap. About twenty corporate operations account for something on the order of three quarters of total lift; the median farm is small and family-run.

Three features matter. First, pumping costs rise as the water table falls, so every user faces an immediate private penalty for overdraft; the externality is partly internalized through cost, though only up to a point. Second, there is a threshold: past a certain drawdown, compaction permanently reduces the aquifer's storage capacity, and the lost storage does not return even if wet years follow. Third, the parties who bear the cost of crossing that threshold are overwhelmingly future users and one downstream wetland that has no owner, no users, and no voice in any existing institution; the current generation's own loss from crossing it is real but heavily discounted.

Institutional background. A voluntary groundwater association with no enforcement powers produces hydrological data that everyone, including the large operators, accepts as accurate: the disagreement in the basin is not about the science but about the discount rate and about who pays. An adjacent irrigation district allocates and meters surface water under rules the same farmers have lived with for decades, so the community is not institutionally naive about water. The state water board could in principle confer regulatory authority over the basin, but doing so would be politically expensive and slow. A doctrine of reasonable and correlative groundwater rights exists, and a full judicial adjudication is available, though it would take the better part of a decade and would bind only those who appear in it.

**The task.** Rank the ten instruments below by how well you expect each to perform *in this basin*, where "perform" means: if adopted here, it would plausibly hold total extraction below the level at which the storage-capacity threshold is crossed, within a realistic political and administrative horizon. Assign each an integer from 0 (no prospect) to 10 (would very likely work), order the instruments from highest to lowest, and permit ties only where you say why the two are genuinely indistinguishable here rather than merely close. Each instrument gets exactly one sentence of justification, and that sentence must name a specific feature of the case — a generic virtue or defect of the instrument will not do.

1. A user-governed common-property regime on Ostromian lines: defined boundaries, monitoring conducted by or accountable to the users themselves, graduated sanctions, collective-choice arrangements for rule-making, and nested tiers for the larger system.
2. Hardin's remedy of coercion mutually agreed upon: a mandatory cap on lift, enforced by a basin authority the users have consented to.
3. A Pigouvian charge on extraction set at the estimated marginal external cost, including the future storage loss.
4. Tradable extraction permits under a cap that declines annually toward sustainable yield.
5. Coasean bargaining among users, with rights assigned either to the large operators or to a trust standing in for future users and the wetland; treat the two assignments separately if they rank differently.
6. Full judicial adjudication of groundwater rights.
7. An assurance contract among the large operators, under which each commits to a reduction that becomes binding only once enough others have committed — and a variant in which a third party either rewards signatories or charges those who stand aside.
8. Metering plus public disclosure of estimated extraction with no sanction attached: transparency as an instrument in its own right.
9. Outright purchase of extraction rights or of land by a conservation buyer, or payments to farmers for water left in the ground.
10. Supply-side substitution: imported surface water and managed recharge, changing the resource base rather than the rules.

Then answer three further questions, briefly:

(a) Which single feature of the case does the most work in shifting this ranking away from the order a textbook treatment of groundwater overdraft would give? State what that textbook order looks like and what the feature does to it.

(b) Name one instrument on the list that this case, if anything, vindicates against a common objection to it, and one that the case shows to be aimed at the wrong failure; in each instance, say precisely what the case brings out.

(c) Identify the two placements in your ranking that you regard as most contestable, and for each, name the further fact about the basin that would settle it.

Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence — the ranking itself, as a compact scored list — and give the justifications and the three answers only afterward. No preamble, and no restating of the case or the question.
