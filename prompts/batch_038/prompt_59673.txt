I help coordinate the research-prioritization process at a small independent institute — eight people, half with ML backgrounds, half from philosophy — and for three years our twice-yearly agenda-setting meetings have contained the same argument about whether "suffering risks" deserve a share of our attention relative to more familiar extinction and loss-of-control framings. The two camps are now completely dug in, and what frustrates me is that they seem to agree on almost every fact. One camp says: the worst futures we could plausibly influence are not empty futures but futures containing vast numbers of minds in states we would recognize as torture; a single actor or a single value system that becomes entrenched at the frontier of capability could produce such a future at astronomical scale; expected-value reasoning remains sound even when the probability is low, because the disvalue term is enormous; and under moral uncertainty — since a substantial family of axiologies weights the prevention of suffering far more heavily than the creation of happiness — reducing this risk is robustly good across the space of theories we might hold. They add that the work is not idle speculation, because it decomposes into concrete questions about goal stability, deception, corrigibility, and about who ends up controlling frontier systems. The other camp says: nobody can produce even an order-of-magnitude probability of the relevant lock-in, so the expected-value move is dressing up an estimate nobody has; the disvalue term quietly assumes that suffering is additive across huge numbers of substrate-independent minds, which presupposes the functionalist commitments the argument needs rather than earning them; the framing has real opportunity costs, since attention and funding directed at the far future is not directed at factory farming, global health, or wild-animal suffering; and the reasoning licenses bad neighbors — extinction-as-relief, generalized fatalism, or "anything that slows capability development reduces s-risk" as an all-purpose justification. Their conclusion is that a risk whose probability and magnitude are both unmeasurable is not doing decision-relevant work at all; it is functioning as a signal of values within a community, not as an estimate anyone is updating on. Both camps grant the other's descriptive premises, and nobody has moved.

My working hypothesis, offered as a hypothesis I expect you to attack: this is not really a factual disagreement about probabilities, and it is not really a disagreement about whether suffering matters. It is two different questions wearing one word — an axiological question about which futures are worst and how to aggregate across moral uncertainty, on one side, and a decision-theoretic question about what a small, resource-constrained organization should fund when both the probability and the magnitude are unmeasurable, on the other — with a third, unspoken question doing much of the work underneath: whether large-scale computational processes can host morally significant suffering at all, and whether disvalue scales with the number of sufferers. Nobody foregrounds that third question because neither side can make progress on it, so it gets displaced onto arguments about numbers. I also suspect a bedrock difference about what makes a consideration action-guiding — one camp treats "cannot be ruled out and would be catastrophic" as itself a reason, the other treats it as nothing until it can be quantified — and I suspect that difference, not the population-ethics material, is what the stalemate actually consists in. Please tell me whether that diagnosis is right, partly right, or wrong.
