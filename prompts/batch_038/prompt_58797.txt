so I'm getting married in September and my fiancée Dana and I have this thing where we send each other dumb philosophy stuff at 1am instead of actually sleeping, and last week it was Kavka's toxin puzzle — eccentric billionaire, vial of something that'll make you horribly sick for a day but won't kill you, and a million dollars wired to you if at midnight tonight you intend to drink it tomorrow afternoon, with the catch that the money lands in your account hours before the drinking time and you're completely free to change your mind. We got way too into it, mostly because its uncomfortably close to what vows are: a present intention to do something that future-you, once the payoff for having intended it is already banked, may have no reason whatsoever to actually do. Dana's brother is giving a speech at our engagement party next month and asked if I had anything to hand him, and I thought this would be the funny thing to hand him.

Can you write a parody of the applicant handbook that gets mailed out by the foundation running the toxin program — call it 750-850 words, deadpan bureaucratic voice, numbered sections with little headers? I want it to be the document they send to the thousands of people who email every year certain they've cracked it. So there should be a section on what exactly is being purchased (an intention, held at a specified time, independently verified — not a prediction, not a resolution, not a heartfelt desire, not "I'll definitely try," and emphatically not the drinking), a shorter section on what is not being purchased, and then the centerpiece: a "Loophole Desk Rejection Log," six to eight entries, each one an applicant's actual proposal followed by the Desk's one-sentence reason for rejecting it. The proposals should be the moves people really make — hypnosis, a smart contract that dumps your savings into a cause you despise, hiring a very large man to break your legs if you back out, intending to intend, having your spouse hold the intention on your behalf, an experimental pill that makes intentions irreversible, retaining a well-known philosopher to talk you into it at 11:45pm. The jokes have to come from the Desk being right: the reader should be able to see that each rejection tracks a genuine problem — that bolting on a penalty changes what you have reason to do and therefore changes which case you're in, that intentions can't be held by proxy, that a confident prediction isn't an intention, that "intending to intend" either regresses or just collapses into the thing itself, that the deadline has passed while you were arranging all this. No winking, no sentence like "and so we see the paradox of rational commitment," no glossary or explainer bolted on the end. It should read like a document with no idea it's funny.

Two more things I'd love worked in. First, some boilerplate about the foundation's own side of the transaction that reveals, entirely without comment, that the foundation faces no puzzle at all — endowment rules, reputation, ninety years of payouts, an audits department — and just let that asymmetry sit there next to the applicant's problem. Second, one approved-application testimonial, from someone who actually collected, where the reason they succeeded is a character trait rather than cleverness: a person constitutionally incapable of reconsidering, who has never once changed their mind about anything, and whose family is plainly not thrilled about it. Keep it readable for party guests who've never heard of any of this — Dana's uncle is a dentist and he still has to laugh — but precise enough that the two people in my reading group who do decision theory for a living respect it. Bonus points for a line somewhere in the front matter about the program's original purpose having something to do with deterrence, i.e. whether a threat you won't later want to carry out can ever be credible, since that's roughly where the whole thing came from, and for a footer clause telling successful applicants that, the intention having been formed, further deliberation about whether to drink has been discontinued as a matter of policy.
