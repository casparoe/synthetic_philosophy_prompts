A friend and I argued for an hour last night about Parfit's hitchhiker: the stranded traveller who is offered a lift only if he promises to send payment afterwards, and of whom Parfit says it would be rational to be someone who keeps that promise — even though, once he is safely home, paying serves no interest of his own and no one would ever know whether he did.

My friend's line was that nothing exotic is happening here: the case is just Ulysses having himself lashed to the mast. Trustworthiness, on his telling, is a precommitment device, so Parfit is not showing that irrationality can be rational; he is showing ordinary instrumental rationality stretched across time, and act-based accounts of what one has most reason to do need no revision.

Please audit that analogy for me. Set out the correspondence explicitly — what plays Ulysses, the mast, the ropes, the Sirens, the crew who might untie him — and then separate the disanalogies that actually bear on my friend's conclusion from the ones that are merely decorative. Two I would especially like pressed: that the mast does its work precisely because nothing depends on the Sirens noticing it, whereas the driver's offer depends entirely on his being able to see the ropes; and that Ulysses' binding leaves his future act the very one his present self most wants, which is exactly what is in question here. If the analogy cannot carry the weight he puts on it, tell me what a better one would be, or whether the case simply resists being analogised.

Finish by saying how confident you are in each verdict — which you would defend as settled and which are conjecture — and single out the one point in the whole analysis you are least sure of.
