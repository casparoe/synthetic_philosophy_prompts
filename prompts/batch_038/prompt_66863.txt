I'm a fourth-year PhD student in philosophy serving as the elected graduate member of my university's Extenuating Circumstances Panel. The funding regulation we apply provides that a doctoral student 'whose ability to complete on schedule has been significantly impaired by circumstances beyond their control' may receive one further year of stipend. There is no further definition, and our practice has been case-by-case.

Next week's agenda includes a student whose fieldwork archive was closed to them for eleven weeks by a permit denial, forcing a rewritten chapter. Two years ago we granted a comparable claim involving a nine-week closure; last year we denied a thirteen-week one, though that turned on the student having an alternative source. Appeals at ten, twelve, and fifteen weeks are queued behind this one, and whatever we do will be cited in all of them.

The panel is split in a way that goes deeper than the case. One member proposes we adopt a bright line prospectively — nothing shorter than twelve weeks counts — on the ground that a rule announced in advance is fairer than the untracked drift of our decisions. Another insists that any line is arbitrary in a way the panel should not conceal: a student at eleven weeks differs in no relevant respect from one at twelve, and passing off a stipulation as a finding misdescribes what we are doing. Underneath this is a disagreement about what our task is. The first thinks there is a determinate answer as to whether eleven weeks is 'significant' and that our difficulty is ignorance of it; the second thinks there is no such answer until we make one, and that saying so plainly is the only defensible posture.

Please draft the memo I'll circulate before the meeting. It should recommend what we do in the eleven-week case and whether we adopt a prospective threshold, with a time frame for each. It should say how the written reasons ought to characterize that threshold if we adopt one — as tracking a boundary already in the regulation, or as supplying one — and explain what practically turns on the choice, given that the appeals tribunal reviews our construction of the regulation with deference but reviews an exercise of discretion only for reasonableness. And it should take the arbitrariness objection as seriously as it takes the student who will end up on the wrong side of any line we draw.
