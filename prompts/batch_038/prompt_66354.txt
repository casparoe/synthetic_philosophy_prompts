Last night a friend and I spent three hours on a disagreement about bluntness and indirection — the Dutch default of stating unwelcome assessments outright versus the Japanese default of conveying them obliquely — and by the end we were plainly talking past each other. I want a clean reconstruction from someone with no stake in the argument, and I want it made as strongly as it can be made.

Her position, in brief: truthfulness is the fundamental norm governing address, and directness and indirection differ in how much literal falsehood their conventions license. A community whose conventions routinely license false reassurance is therefore morally worse off than one whose conventions require unvarnished statement, and the fact that insiders decode the falsehood perfectly is beside the point, because the speaker has still used the machinery of communication to produce in his hearer a state he does not share. She regards my talk about who bears the cost as a consequentialist redescription that has nothing whatever to say about the distinctive wrong of lying.

My position, which is the one you should argue for: the morally significant difference between the two styles is not located in the truth-value of what is said, but in what the speaker leaves the hearer holding. A blunt statement of an unwelcome judgment creates a claimable record — the hearer knows where she stands, can act on it, can quote it back, can demand later that the speaker stand behind it. Conventional indirection does the opposite even when it is decoded flawlessly: the hearer receives the message but acquires no commitment she can hold the speaker to, because the speaker has retained the standing option to deny that anything of the sort was said. On this view the cost that indirection shifts is not primarily emotional pain but accountability, and deniability is a hedge whose payoff accrues to the speaker while its premium is paid by the hearer. If that is right, the familiar debate over white lies is badly framed, since it assumes the operative question is whether a false proposition was asserted.

Please do the following.

1. Set the argument out as numbered premises running to an explicit conclusion, and mark the one premise you take to be load-bearing — the premise that, if it fails, takes the argument with it. Explain why it is the load-bearing one and what would have to be true for it to hold.

2. Answer her two objections. First: a successfully decoded lie still subverts the hearer's capacity to form her beliefs out of what the speaker actually takes to be the case, so the wrong survives the absence of any false belief. Second: the accountability story proves too much — it appears to rank a tactful, well-decoded private exchange as worse than an accurate but gratuitously public one, and it gives no way to distinguish a norm from the way that norm happens to be experienced inside the community that holds it. Steelman each before answering; do not caricature either.

3. Apply the view to two cases. In the first, at a departmental reception, a chair tells a junior colleague accurately, unprompted, and within earshot of two others, that her manuscript is not publishable as it stands and that he would not support her file. In the second, the same assessment is delivered in a private exchange by way of a literally false but conventionally recognizable remark that the manuscript shows real promise and may yet find its readership; the hearer is fully fluent in the convention, hears the refusal unmistakably, and ends up with no false belief. Say which of the two is the more serious failure on the view you have just argued for, and say precisely what that ranking depends on. I suspect it turns on who else is in the room and on the hearer's fluency; if your argument implies that the ranking flips when the hearer in the second case is a visitor who does not share the convention, say so, and say whether the view can absorb the flip or whether the flip is a genuine problem for it.

Two constraints on the write-up. First, no citations, no reading list, no named authorities, and no invented experimental findings; where a step needs an empirical assumption about how people actually decode oblique speech, flag it as an assumption rather than dressing it as evidence. Second, treat the Dutch and Japanese labels strictly as convenient shorthand and put no weight on them: the argument should go through for any pair of communities, one whose conventions license unvarnished statement and one whose conventions license deniable obliqueness. Close by listing which features of the two cases you relied on and which you did not.

If, having made the argument, you have a reservation about it, put that reservation in a short postscript addressed to me rather than letting it soften the argument itself.
