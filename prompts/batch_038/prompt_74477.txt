Last night a friend and I argued until two in the morning about the two coffee tasters in Dennett's paper on quining qualia, and neither of us moved, but the argument has acquired an absurdly practical urgency for me, so I want help with a piece of writing rather than with the metaphysics directly.

The occasion. On Saturday I am giving a speech at the retirement dinner of a master roaster: forty-one years at the same specialty coffee company, the last twenty of them running its cupping panel. She is not a philosopher and has no patience for philosophy. She has already told the organizers that she will not sit through any speech describing her as gifted or as having a palate like no one else's, because she believes what she actually had was four decades of paying attention and remembering, plus the rare luck of being paid for it. Ninety people will be in the room: her former cuppers and a few food chemists; her family, including two adult children who have always found her profession faintly ridiculous; company management, which will be embarrassed by anything abstract; and, because her brother is one, two or three working philosophers of mind who will be listening for precisely what I am about to do.

My position, which last night did not dent, is that qualia are an incoherent posit — that there is no further fact about what it is like to taste her coffee over and above the discriminations she can make, the reports she is disposed to give, and the long history of adjustments her sensitivities have undergone. My friend's position is that this changes the subject; that when she says a certain roast tastes nothing like it did in the nineties while insisting her standards have not shifted, there is a residue functional description leaves behind; and, worse, that pressing her the way Dennett presses his tasters is a way of winning by refusing to lose.

The difficulty I need solved. I want the speech affectionate, funny, and accurate; I do not want it to be a covert refutation of her self-understanding, and I do not want it to flatter her with vocabulary I believe to be false. So it must praise her for exactly what I take her to have — an enormous, trained, historically layered capacity to discriminate, and to be right about those discriminations — without ever slipping into the register of a private inner show to which only she has access and the rest of us can merely gesture. That constraint should be load-bearing rather than decorative: the metaphysics should determine which sentences are available at all, so the praise comes out more precise and therefore more generous, not deflated. The test is that she can sit through it feeling celebrated entirely on her own terms, while her brother can verify that not one line commits me to qualia. Two hard rules follow from this: the words qualia and consciousness may not appear, and the speech may not contain any consolation or compliment that depends on her having had experiences I would refuse to count as facts.

One further complication, which I want used rather than avoided. Her career contains a coffee-taster-shaped episode she has told publicly, and which made her reputation for honesty: for years she maintained that a particular origin had changed underneath her, and eventually withdrew the claim, concluding that her own standards had moved and the coffee had not. The speech should carry that episode, but it must not convert her story into evidence for my thesis in front of a room that loves her. It has to work whether or not anyone thinks the story shows anything at all about qualia — legible to her colleagues as an anecdote about integrity, legible to the philosophers as the crux of the argument, and neither as a gotcha.

Please write the speech at roughly eight hundred words, about six minutes spoken, with no jargon and nothing that requires a footnote, ending on her rather than on the argument.

Then append an afterword of about two hundred words addressed to me rather than to the room. Identify where the speech takes liberties: which turns of phrase quietly presuppose my side of last night's argument instead of earning it; where it leans on a reading of the coffee-taster case that a realist about qualia would dismiss as question-begging; and where its strategy of praising discriminations rather than experiences would collapse if she were to say, in the room, that she knows perfectly well what she means by how it tasted. Finally, state the single consideration that, if my friend pressed it properly, would most likely force me to abandon the view the speech is written from. Be blunt about that last item — I would rather hear the strongest case against me derived from the architecture of my own speech than be handed something that flatters both of us.
