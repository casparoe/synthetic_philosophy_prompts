Here's the setup I want, which is a bit of a twist on the usual Mises/Hayek vs. Lange/Lerner staging:

It's the present day. A national health service has handed a team of economists and ML people control of its whole medical supply chain — roughly 40,000 line items, from paracetamol to MRI contrast agent to the cold-chain stuff. The team has built an internal allocation system, call it the Optimizer, that does two things: it forecasts demand at clinic level by ingesting everything the system already produces (prescription records, fridge temperature sensors, nurses' free-text shift notes, stockroom scan logs, ward occupancy), and it posts an internal shadow price for every line item, revised daily, with a rule that pushes prices up when a facility draws down more than forecast and down when stock sits. Managers at depots are told to treat those posted numbers as given and minimize cost subject to them. Effectively a tâtonnement run by software instead of by a planning board with a pencil.

Two characters, both extremely competent:

- The lead economist on the Optimizer project. Not a zealot. She thinks the old calculation debate was settled in a lopsided way that both sides misremember, that the real objection from the Austrian side was about dispersed local knowledge, and that her system simply *eats* local knowledge — the tacit stuff a ward nurse knows but never says out loud now shows up in the sensor and notes data. She's read enough Lange and Lerner to be embarrassed by how crude their models were, and she'll say so.
- A visiting comparative institutionalist in the Hayekian line, but a smart one: no "planning always fails" slogans, no Ayn Rand register. He should concede early and genuinely that a price vector for capital goods can in principle be computed, that input-output accounting works, and that the biggest firms on earth are internally planned with administratively set transfer prices. His job is then to relocate the argument somewhere the concession doesn't kill it.

Make the relocation happen on the page — that's the intellectual engine of the piece. He gives up ground, and she thinks she's won, and then he moves to what actually can't be replicated: who discovers the option nobody specified, who holds the loss when the discovery turns out wrong, and the absence of a residual claimant. Have them work one concrete case together step by step, both narrating what their own system would do: an unexpected global shortage of one input hits, and there are three plausible substitutes, one of which requires re-certifying a device and one of which nobody in either camp has thought of. Let them disagree about the transcript of that episode rather than about slogans.

Ground rules:

- Real back-and-forth. Each of them has to pick up the other's actual last point, not a paraphrase of it, and at least twice one of them should say something that visibly forces the other to revise rather than restate.
- No strawmen, no side gets the funnier lines or the better closing sentence.
- Concrete texture: numbers, a named drug, a queue, a specific clinic. I don't want the dialogue to float at the level of "but knowledge is dispersed!"
- They should end at a genuine stalemate, and the stalemate should be about what counts as an error: one of them holds that errors are logged deviations from the allocation that would have been optimal, the other holds that the only errors that matter leave no trace in the data at all, because the road not taken was never a line item. Land that, but let a character articulate it, don't narrate it from outside.
- No footnotes, no bibliography, no title-and-date name-dropping of papers — just the ideas in their mouths.
- Around 1500–1900 words for the dialogue itself. Give them names and slightly different speech rhythms.
