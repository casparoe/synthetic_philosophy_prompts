I've been going to a Tuesday-night Stoicism reading group for about six months — half of us are there for the ancient texts, half are there because they downloaded some mood-tracking app and want the philosophy underneath it. Last week we hit the dichotomy of control, which is basically the whole engine of the modern revival: Epictetus's opening split between what's up to us (opinion, impulse, desire, aversion) and what isn't (the body, property, reputation, office), plus the claim that if you mistake one for the other you'll be miserable and unfree. I went home and tried to write out why I think that split, taken at face value, destroys the ethics it's supposed to support. The problem is it proves way more than I can believe, and nobody in the group could tell me where it breaks. Two of them said I wasn't being a Stoic properly and left it at that, which is not an answer.

Here's what I've got. (1) On the Stoic view the only good is virtue — excellent rational agency — and the only bad thing is vice; everything else, including health, money, reputation, and other people's welfare, is an indifferent. (2) Nothing is worth pursuing except what is good, since if a thing isn't good, getting it can't make your life go better. (3) So no indifferent is worth pursuing, and in particular the welfare of other people isn't worth pursuing. (4) Either the Stoic accepts (3), in which case the system has no room for other-regarding reasons at all and is really a self-improvement program with a metaphysics bolted on, or she denies (3), in which case she's abandoned (1)-(2). (5) The usual escape — prefered indifferents, things with "value" that still aren't good — looks purely verbal to me: if the only thing that makes a life go well is goodness, and health is merely valuable rather than good, then choosing health over sickness can't be part of what a good life consists in, so the choosing is either arbitrary or it's smuggling in exactly the outcome-concern that (1) forbids. (6) Worse, indifference to outcomes erases differences between lives. A doctor who spends forty years curing disease and a man who spends forty years on a couch are equally good people on this view as long as both act virtuously — and if that's true, nothing about what you actually do matters, which is the lazy argument all over again. Someone said Chrysippus had a reply involving co-fatedness, but that's about fate, not about value, and I don't see how it touches (6). (7) So the dichotomy of control leaves Stoicism either selfish (from 3-4) or inert (from 6), or both.

Which step is the first one that goes wrong, and why did it look fine? I'd rather keep (1) and (2) than give up (6), so if you're going to make me pay, tell me what I'm paying. If the diagnosis is that the Stoics run two kinds of value in parallel — worth-selecting versus good-making — then say what does the actual work in that distinction, because that's precisely what my step (5) is denying has any content, and appealing to oikeiôsis or to the reserve clause looks to me like relabeling the problem rather than solving it. And separately: does the modern move to a trichotomy of control (things partly up to us — your effort at a match, your kid's grades, whether your book gets published) help anywhere in this argument, or does it just relocate the same tension one level down? I don't want a reading list, I want the diagnosis.
