so our bioethics seminar spent the last month on embryo selection and enhancement, and the prof finally made each of us write down where we actually stand, in public, so the rest of the group could take a swing at it. Mine is below. I've done the reading — Parfit on the non-identity problem, Savulescu's procreative beneficence argument, the expressivist reply from the disability side, Sandel on giftedness — so please don't give me a tour of the debate or a summary of who holds what. I want an audit of this specific package, argued from the structure of the views rather than from authority.

Here's what I hold:

1. When you can foresee that one of the children you might have would have a clearly better life than another one you might have instead, having the worse one isn't a merely suboptimal choice, it's a wrong one. I mean obligation, not preference. I'm annoyed at how often this gets softened into "well, it'd be nice if."
2. But I want wrongness kept anchored to people. Something is wrong in procreation only if it's bad for somebody — there has to be an individual for whom things go worse. I get queasy at impersonal talk about making the world contain more value; value doesn't float free of the beings it's value for.
3. The case I actually care about: a couple doing preimplantation testing has two embryos, both expected to have thoroughly good lives — no disease, no impairment, decent everything — but one is likely to land noticeably higher on the cognitive endowment scale. I think they should transfer the first, and that transferring the second is a real moral failure, not a lifestyle option. I also think nobody gets wronged in either choice, since without the second choice there's no second child at all. I'm comfortable with all of that at once.
4. No state involvement in the direction of getting people to select. No mandates, no incentives that function as mandates, no public scoring of traits. Same goes for bans on private selection. Coercion is off the table from both directions and I don't see that as being in any tension with (1), tho half the seminar thinks it obviously is.
5. The expressivist objection doesn't land on me. Selecting against deafness or against trisomy 21 doesn't harm any existing deaf or disabled person, and I don't think morality should be in the business of tracking what messages an act sends. A message isn't a mechanism.
6. Ditto for the drift / arms-race / giftedness cluster. If lots of parents converge on the same handful of traits, that's just a pile of individual decisions each of which was fine, and I don't see how a pile of fine decisions turns into an unfine one. Nothing集体 is happening that any individual is doing.
7. I don't think the therapy/enhancement line is morally basic — there's nothing sacred about species-typical functioning as such. But I do reliably feel that steering clear of a serious disease is a different moral matter from buying a few extra IQ points, and I trust that feeling. People tell me I can't have it both ways here.

Which of these genuinely pull against each other? Rank the tensions worst-first, and for each one say what specifically would have to be given up or reworded, and what the repair turns me into — I want to know which kind of theorist I'd end up being, not just that I'd be consistent. At least one of these is going to look like a flat contradiction and isn't, maybe two, so please seperate those out and explain why they survive scrutiny instead of flagging the whole list as incoherent. One friend says I'm a utilitarian who's embarrassed about it, another says I'm a eugenicist who's embarrassed about the word; I don't think either is right but I can't yet say why, and if the audit implies one of them is basically right, tell me. Finally: if the cheapest overall repair isn't the same as the repair for the single worst tension, say which is which and what I'd be giving up by taking the cheap route.
