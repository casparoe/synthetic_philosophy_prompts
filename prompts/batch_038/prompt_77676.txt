I sit on an internal forecasting group that has to produce a written view on how quickly a transformative AI system could go from "roughly as capable as our current best models at economically valuable cognitive work" to "substantially better at essentially all such work." One senior colleague has anchored the group on an epidemiological framing, and it has become the shared vocabulary of our drafts. His argument runs roughly as follows.

Treat each capability improvement as an infection and each system, lab, dataset, or downstream deployment as a susceptible host. Define an R for the process: the number of further substantive improvements that one improvement reliably occasions, either by producing better successor systems or by spreading into new deployments that then generate improvements of their own. If R exceeds 1, the number of capable systems grows exponentially and there is a takeoff; if R falls below 1, the cascade fizzles out, exactly as a introduced pathogen dies out without an epidemic. Crucially, he says, R is never permanently above 1, because the process depletes its own susceptibles: easy wins get taken, high-quality data and cheap compute get used up, and the pool of systems that can still be improved shrinks. That depletion plays the role of herd immunity. So even granting recursive self-improvement entirely, the trajectory is a saturating S-curve, not a blow-up, and the group should write up takeoff as gradual and self-limiting — which means our resources belong in monitoring, adaptation, and institutional resilience rather than in trying to design brakes that would have to work on a timescale of days. He adds that the fast-versus-slow debate is therefore not a philosophical dispute at all but a parameter-estimation problem: we are all agreed on the model, we just disagree about the value of R.

As a second, independent support he cites the 1960 Science article by von Foerster and colleagues that fitted world population to a hyperbola of the form N proportional to 1/(t₀ − t) and thereby assigned a definite calendar date, only a few decades out, to a divergence in human population. The fit to the historical data was good, the extrapolation was absurd, and the date passed without anything resembling a singularity. His lesson: hyperbolic extrapolations of a positive-feedback process are a known statistical trap, and intelligence-explosion arguments fall into it.

I want a full audit of the epidemic analogy before our next draft, and I would like it to be adversarial toward my colleague rather than diplomatic.

Please give me:

1. An explicit correspondence table for the mapping — pathogen, host, susceptible population, infection event, transmission rate, recovery or removal, the reproduction number, the depletion term, superspreader events, and the eventual plateau — saying for each entry what in the AI-development process it is supposed to correspond to, and marking each row as doing real work, doing cosmetic work, or having no coherent counterpart.

2. The disanalogies, separated into two piles: those that actually bear on the conclusion he wants (gradual, saturating, self-limiting, and a mere matter of estimating a parameter) and those that are intellectually interesting but leave the conclusion untouched. For the ones in the first pile, be precise about the mechanism by which they defeat it. In particular I want you to address: whether the quantity that sets the growth rate in the source domain is fixed while the state variable grows, and whether that is also true of the target; whether the saturating term in an epidemic model caps the level of the variable or the rate of its change, and which of those the AI case would need; whether the resource that produces saturation can be replenished or expanded by the very process that consumes it; whether there is a single well-mixed population of hosts at all, or whether the relevant feedback loop runs inside one system rather than between systems; and what becomes of the claim that stochastic fade-out is available as a safety margin when the number of independent improvement events is small rather than large.

3. An assessment of what the analogy smuggles in beyond its stated content — assumptions my colleague has not argued for and may not even notice he is making, including anything about the exogeneity of the process, the homogeneity of the units being counted, the existence of a fixed target variable, and the framing of the whole question as estimation rather than model choice.

4. A verdict on the doomsday-equation argument considered on its own: does the failure of a hyperbolic population extrapolation license skepticism about finite-time blow-up in a capability model, or are the two cases disanalogous in a way that matters? Say what the von Foerster episode does and does not establish.

5. A better source domain. If the epidemic picture is the wrong mechanism, name what you would use instead — from chemistry, demography, engineering, economics, or elsewhere — sketch that mapping, and state honestly whether your preferred analogy still delivers gradualism or whether it is merely a different way of failing to deliver it.

6. Finally, tell me what our group should write. If the analogy cannot carry the conclusion, is gradual takeoff still the right default on independent grounds, or was the analogy the only thing supporting it?
