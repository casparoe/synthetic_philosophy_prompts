Retired from a soil microbiology lab about three years back and since then I've fallen down the decision theory rabbit hole, mostly by way of Hofstadter's superrationality in Metamagical Themas. What I want now isn't an essay, it's a sorting instrument: a decision tree that starts at "you're in a one-shot prisoner's dilemma, you'll never meet this person again, and they've read the same books you have" and walks you down to a named position about what to do and, more to the point, on what grounds.

Build it out of the logical space first, not out of a list of names. I want to see the axes laid bare — the questions that actually cut the space up — and then the views dropped in wherever they land. Somewhere around six to eight nodes, eight to twelve leaves.

The nodes I care about, in whatever order you think works:

- Are the two decisions causally hooked together, or merely correlated because the two of you were built the same way? I want a node that separates "my choosing this makes them choose that" from "my choosing this is evidence about what they'll choose" from "we're just similar and that's the whole story."
- Does it matter who's on the other side — a literal copy of me, a stranger with the same training, or a stranger with no particular reason to think like me? Hofstadter's argument seemed to lean on the symmetry and I never got clear on what exactly collapses when you lose it.
- Is "what if everybody did that" allowed into a one-shot calculation at all, and if so, in as what — a moral consideration, a probability, or something else entirely?
- Can two people count as one agent, so that "what should we do" gets asked before "what should I do"?
- Do you choose an act, or do you choose a procedure for choosing acts? Im pretty sure the AI people made a big deal of this and I still can't tell whether it's the same move as superrationality wearing a different hat or a genuinely different move.

Phrase every node as a situation an ordinary person could answer without having read any of this literature, with the technical claim in brackets after it, never inside the question. I spent forty years designing assays; a question that leaks its answer isn't a question.

Leaves I want on the tree: plain expected-utility defection, Hofstadter-style superrationality, evidential decision theory, causal decision theory, Kantian cooperation by way of the universal law, the team-reasoning or "we-mode" view, and whatever the pick-the-algorithm view is properly called. Add any others that belong. Then tell me which cells of the space come out empty and why — is anyone actually defending the combination where the choices really are causally linked and you still defect? Where there's no correlation of any kind and you still cooperate? For each empty cell, say whether it's incoherent, merely unappealing, or just unexplored.

And flag honestly the pairs of leaves your tree can't seperate. My suspicion is that superrationality and evidential decision theory give identical answers in the symmetric case, and that Kant and team reasoning may too. If so, say so plainly instead of manufacturing a question that pretends to tell them apart. Where two positions reach the same verdict in the prisoner's dilemma for different reasons, note at the leaf the case that would split them, even if it's a different game.

Two last things. Where your nodes rest on a contested reading — say, on treating superrationality as a real competitor to the evidential/causal distinction rather than a popular gloss on one side of it — say so at the node rather than smoothing it over. And keep straight for me what's settled here versus what people who do this for a living are still fighting about; I've read enough breezy popular accounts to be suspicious of confidence in either direction. Don't let me come away thinking superrationality is accepted game theory if it isn't, and don't let me think it's nonsense if it isn't that either. If there is a majority view about the one-shot case among decision theorists, say what it is and how sure you are that there is one.
