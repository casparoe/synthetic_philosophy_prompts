Second year cog sci phd here, TAing a grad/advanced-undergrad seminar on the philosophy of perception — 16 people, one 90-minute block. We're doing cognitive penetrability next week and I'd much rather actually run something in the room than talk about it.

Here's the thing I want them to feel. Almost every result that gets cited as showing perception is cognitively penetrated turns on a verbal judgment: pick the chip that matches what you saw, say how orange the object looked, rate the steepness of the hill, estimate the distance. And basically the whole pushback on that literature says the effect is landing downstream of experience — the percept itself is untouched, what shifted was the response criterion, or the concept they grabbed when reporting, or plain demand characteristics because they guessed the hypothesis half way through. So the genuinely interesting question isn't whether cognition affects perception, it's whether any measurement route could tell you experience-shifting apart from report-shifting. I want them to run into that wall themselves, in the room, before anyone says the word firewall or gets handed the reading.

So: design the session for me. I'm fine with a three- or four-arm thing where each arm is a different measurement route on the same stimulus set — something like naive verbal report, a non-verbal match, a speeded/indirect measure that doesn't give them time to reason about it, and one arm where the hypothesis is announced out loud beforehand. Use whatever arms you think actually make the point, but the point has to come from the numbers landing in different places, not from a lecture.

Constraints: 16 people, one 90-minute block, a colour laser printer, index cards, a phone stopwatch. No lab equipment, no budget for real incentives (though if there's a cheap way to make one arm feel like it has stakes, tell me). I'll prep the stimuli myself, so tell me exactly what they need to look like. What I had in mind is a die-cut silhouette of a tomato, relit so it reads somewhere between red and orangey-brown, paired with a physically matched control of the same luminance and saturation but a different shape; I'd print maybe ten pairs. Tell me if that's a bad idea and what to use instead — I have no idea whether the shape manipulation will actually carry any effect at n=16.

What I need from you:

- the full protocol: roles, minute-by-minute timing, and the exact words for each handoff between arms, because I've run activities like this before and the wording of the handoff is where it allways leaks
- the sheet the subjects fill in, plus how the tallying works so we get real numbers on the board inside ten minutes
- a debrief that maps each arm's result onto the specific methodological worry it was supposed to expose, and says what the differences between arms do and don't license
- an honest list of what will go wrong the first time I run this. I'm already assuming: n is laughably small, a third of the class knows the literature and will perform the expected answer, and somebody will say "well I knew what I was supposed to see" out loud, which nukes the naive arm for everyone
- and most importantly, be straight with me about the ceiling on this. Which feature of the debate can't be reproduced by a paper-and-pencil classroom exercise no matter how I arrange it, and what do I have to supply verbally to paper over that gap? My worry is that the whole thing ends up demonstrating "people's verbal reports shift when you suggest stuff" and never gets within a mile of the perceptual question — in fact their own debrief will tell them exactly that, since it's the same critique. Tell me how real that worry is, how much of it the arm structure can defuse, and whether I should just accept that the activity is really about the epistemology of the measurement rather than about perception at all.
