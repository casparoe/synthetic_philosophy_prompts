ok so this is for my school's philosophy club and I've backed myself into a corner. Please tell me where the argument breaks, I've been staring at it for a week.

Background. We were talking about Dennett's idea that the self is a center of narrative gravity. My understanding (from a podcast and a couple of borrowed book chapters, so possibly already wrong) is that the self isn't a thing sitting in your head, it's an abstraction, the same sort of item as the center of gravity of an object. That comparison felt vivid to me because of a physics lab: we found the center of mass of a cardboard boomerang shape by hanging it from a pin with a plumb line, marking the line, hanging it from a different pin, marking that line, and the intersection was out in empty air. Not in the cardboard at all. Nothing there. But it's still exactly where the thing balances and rotates around when you throw it.

So I tried writing out what I think follows, and it lands somewhere I don't want it to.

(1) If something is a posit or a fiction rather than a thing, then there's no fact of the matter about it: you can't be right or wrong about it, and nothing it does is really done.
(2) Selves are posits of exactly this kind — that's the whole content of the center-of-narrative-gravity comparison, otherwise the comparison is just decoration.
(3) Centers of gravity are fictions in this sense. My boomerang's center of mass is literally nowhere in the boomerang; it's bookkeeping that saves us from tracking a billion molecules.
(4) So there is no fact of the matter about me. Nothing I do is really done by me, my autobiography has no more claim to be true than a horoscope, and I names a convenience.
(5) Blame, praise, promising and punishing all require a real agent who really did the thing. Call it the somebody-has-to-be-home condition.
(6) So no one is ever really responsible for anything. And by (4) this argument isn't anybody's argument, and can't be better reasoning than any other noise, which is a weird thing to conclude after six steps of careful reasoning.
(7) But (6) is unacceptable. I still think it's worse to punish someone for something they didn't do than to not punish them, and that thought seems to need (5).

Which step do I reject, and what do I pay for rejecting it? I want to keep (2) most of all, because dropping it puts me back at the self is a soul or a thing in the brain, which is my club opponent's position and which I don't believe. I also don't want to drop (5), because then responsibility seems to just float around unattached to anybody. I can see that keeping both is what generates the disaster, so I need to know the actual price.
