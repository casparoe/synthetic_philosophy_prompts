I work in freight logistics and spend my days reading carriers' invoices and arguing about whose cost a delay really was, so I have no academic training in history at all — but for the last two years I've been working through the argument about the British Raj on my own, from the side that says Britain gave India railways, a unified administration, a census, the telegraph, English-medium education and the machinery of a modern state, and from the side that says it gave India the famines of the 1870s and the 1890s, the Bengal famine of 1943, Amritsar in 1919, the collapse of handloom textile production, indentured labour shipped to other colonies, and a continuous drain of revenue out of the country. What has struck me, in a way I don't think either side quite admits, is that the debate is conducted as though it were an accounting question: both sides present items, both sides insist their items are the ones that belong on the statement, and both sides behave as though the two columns could eventually be netted into a single figure.

Write me a parody of an independent auditor's report on exactly that premise. The fictional client is the India Account, the period under audit is 1757 to 1947, and the report is addressed to the members of an invented Permanent Tribunal for the Settlement of Closed Empires. Between 900 and 1,200 words, in the deadpan register of real audit prose — an opinion paragraph, a basis-for-opinion paragraph, and then numbered notes to the statements.

The humour has to come from the accounting being done correctly, not from being wrong. Please build the piece out of these difficulties, which are the ones I keep running into when I try to do the netting myself: whether an entity that ceased to exist in 1947 can be audited on a going-concern basis at all, or whether the whole thing has to be restated on a liquidation basis; a related-party disclosure problem, since the party that prepared the books was also the principal beneficiary of the transactions in them; an impairment test on the railway network, whose carrying value depends entirely on whether it is classified as infrastructure or as extraction equipment, and which was in any case built under a guarantee that underwrote private shareholders' returns out of Indian revenues; a measurement-basis note on the fact that the books were kept in sterling while the operations ran in rupees, at an exchange rate the entity fixed for itself; a discount-rate note asking whose time preference is being applied to a claim that compounds across two centuries; a counterfactual restatement of the comparative periods, and what happens to the assurance level when the comparator is a country that was never allowed to exist; and a materiality threshold, which is where I want the piece to hurt, because materiality is normally set as a percentage of something and there is nothing in this ledger that can serve as the base. End with an opinion, and let the choice among an unmodified, qualified, adverse, or disclaimed opinion carry the entire moral weight of the piece without a single adjective of outrage anywhere in it.

The target is the ledger method itself — the idea that this can be settled by balancing columns — and not any living historian. Please don't caricature either side's best-known advocate and don't invent thinly disguised stand-ins for them; let both sides' items appear as serious line entries that the framework then fails to accommodate.

After the piece, add a note of two or three plain paragraphs telling me where you took liberties: which line items correspond to claims historians actually make and which are invented for the joke, which classification decisions quietly favour one side, and whether the satire ends up endorsing a verdict it pretends not to reach. And if you think my premise is confused — if you think a ledger form trivialises famine no matter how carefully it's handled, or that the piece can't stay deadpan without becoming an argument that empires are just bad bookkeeping — say that plainly and argue with me instead of producing something you think is indefensible. I would much rather be contradicted than politely indulged.
