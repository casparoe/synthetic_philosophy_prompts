Here is an argument I reconstructed after reading about fine-tuning, and I have come to distrust it because it seems to demolish inferences I rely on professionally.

(1) To determine whether an observation supports a hypothesis, you compare the probability of your total evidence given that hypothesis with its probability given the rival. Total evidence means everything you actually know, including that you are looking at *this* particular system rather than merely that some system somewhere has the relevant property.

(2) Casino case: you arrive at a roulette table and watch a spin come up double sixes. It is tempting to conclude that there must have been a great many earlier spins tonight, which is why you happened to arrive for this one. That conclusion is unwarranted: however many spins preceded yours, the chance that the spin you watched landed on double sixes is unchanged.

(3) The multiverse inference has exactly the same shape. You find that the constants of *this* universe fall inside the life-permitting range, and you infer that there must be a vast ensemble of universes with assorted constants, which is why this one came out right.

(4) But other universes are causally and probabilistically irrelevant to ours, so the chance that this universe's constants are life-permitting is the same whether there are a billion others or none.

(5) Therefore fine-tuning gives no support whatever to the multiverse hypothesis. A designer who wanted observers, by contrast, would have aimed at this universe specifically, so fine-tuning does support design. The evidence favors design over the multiverse, not the reverse.

(6) Yet (4) states a perfectly general principle: unobserved members of a large population do not change the probability of a property in the particular member you are examining.

(7) Apply it consistently. That the one planet you can resolve sits in its star's habitable zone gives no support to the claim that the system has many planets. That one user in my logs tripped a fault whose per-session probability is roughly one in ten million gives no support to the claim that we serve an enormous number of sessions.

(8) Both of those inferences are unobjectionable, and the second one is bread and butter for me: rare-event counts are how I estimate traffic volume.

(9) So something above must go, and I cannot tell what.
