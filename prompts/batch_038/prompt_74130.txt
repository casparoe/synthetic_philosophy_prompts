I am a novelist, some way into a fourth book, and I have arrived at a question about my craft that I suspect is a disguised version of a question in metaphysics. I should like you to perform the translation deliberately and item by item, and then to tell me what did not survive it.

My vocabulary, which I know well, is that of narrative construction: scene, setting, stage, blocking, props, point of view, offstage, continuity, the world-bible. Since my second novel I have kept a working rule — no room is described until someone is standing in it, no street is named until it has been walked down. Yet I also keep a world-bible, as most of us do, in which I have specified a good deal no character will ever encounter: the full layout of a market square that appears once in the book and from a single angle; a frost that killed an orchard in a year preceding the story; a bridge whose far bank is never crossed. My editor has raised one objection to the manuscript that I cannot answer. In a draft chapter I spent two pages on an empty landscape at dawn — valley, river, fence line, no observer, no consciousness anywhere in it — and every reader who saw it reported the same thing: that it felt dead, that nothing was happening, that they did not know where to stand. I have never been able to determine whether that is a fact about my prose or a fact about description itself, whether a scene containing no perceiver is a scene at all.

Meanwhile I have been reading, in a layman's way, about Newton's bucket and the quarrel it opened. As I understand the two sides: substantivalism holds that space, or spacetime, is a thing in its own right, a stage that would remain were the play cancelled; relationism holds that space is nothing over and above the spatial relations among material things, so that to speak of the space between two objects is only to speak of those objects. Newton's argument turns on the water in a spinning bucket climbing the sides and forming a concave surface: at the instant when the relative motion between bucket and water is greatest the surface is still flat, and it becomes concave later, once the water is at rest with respect to the bucket — so what the shape tracks cannot be motion relative to nearby bodies. Leibniz's reply, as I gather, is that space is an order of coexisting things and that talk of a place with nothing in it has no content. Mach's move is that the water's surface is fixed not by the stage but by rotation relative to the whole mass of the universe, the distant matter doing the work. Newton's own companion case sharpens the difficulty: two globes joined by a cord, revolving about a common centre in an otherwise empty universe, where the tension in the cord is supposed to betray their rotation although there is nothing else to rotate relative to. And in its modern form the dispute appears to have become the hole argument: a spacetime theory seems to permit two models that agree on every event and every relation among events yet differ over which points of the underlying manifold host them, a duplication that makes no observable difference and, on some accounts, costs the theory its determinism.

Here is what I want. Restate that debate in my vocabulary as faithfully as you can, treating the two positions as rival doctrines about a world-bible. Take them in turn: substantivalism; relationism; the bucket; the globes and the cord; Mach's proposal; the hole argument; and, if you can manage it, the claim that the difference between the two models is a difference to which nothing corresponds. For each, state plainly whether it translates cleanly into talk of scenes, stages, props and unwitnessed rooms; whether it translates only by distortion, and what precisely the distortion is; or whether it finds no expression in my vocabulary at all. Then give me the residue report, which is the part I actually care about. Where a piece of the metaphysics cannot be said in the language of narrative craft, is that because the craft language lacks something — because a novel has no way of holding a setting nobody experiences — or because the piece in question was never as clear as it sounded?

I should also tell you the hypothesis I have been carrying, so that you may attack it rather than humour it. It seems to me that this quarrel is an argument about narration conducted in the language of ontology: the substantivalist is one who insists that a description can be true of a scene in which there is no describer, while the relationist insists that every fact about a setting is a fact about what occupies it, which is to say a fact available to somebody standing somewhere. On that reading my dead chapter is not badly written but is an attempt to do something the form may forbid. Tell me whether the hypothesis survives contact with the actual arguments — in particular whether it can make sense of the globes and the cord, where the tension looks like a purely relational fact and yet seems to require something more than relations among the globes — or whether I am merely projecting a novelist's preoccupations onto a dispute about physics.

Two practical constraints. I have no library and no appetite for a bibliography: no reading lists, no citations, no titles or dates you are not certain of. Where you refer to Newton, Leibniz or Mach, paraphrase rather than quote; I would much rather have an honest rendering than a remembered line I cannot check. And be severe about where the mapping is load-bearing and where it is decorative. An analogy that flatters my manuscript while doing no explanatory work is worth less to me than no analogy at all.
