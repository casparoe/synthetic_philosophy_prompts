I spent thirty-four years at a national laboratory computing solutions of partial differential equations, and since retiring I have been reading philosophy and foundations with more time than discipline. The dispute I keep returning to is the quarrel between classical and constructive mathematics, which has run more or less continuously since Brouwer's revolt a little over a century ago. What strikes me as strange is not that it remains unresolved but that nobody appears to expect it to be resolved, and that both sides have largely given up trying to persuade the other. Working mathematicians employ excluded middle without comment and treat constructivism as an eccentric hobby; constructivists produce their analysis and their algebra and mostly do not argue with anyone. A good deal of careful philosophy has nevertheless been written on the subject, including an influential attempt to recast the whole matter as a dispute about the meanings of the logical constants, on the model of the realism debate in other areas of discourse. That reframing does not seem to have been adopted by the people who actually do mathematics, and I cannot tell whether that is because it failed or because it was beside the point.

Three things I have picked up puzzle me, and I cannot resolve them on my own. First, as I understand it, Peano arithmetic is Π⁰₂-conservative over Heyting arithmetic: every statement of the form "for every n there is an m such that" some checkable condition on n and m holds, which classical arithmetic proves, is already provable intuitionistically; the two systems diverge only above that level, which is roughly where the quantifier structure of convergence and limit claims lives. What I find remarkable is that each camp treats this theorem as vindicating itself. To the constructivist it shows that classical logic, applied to concrete numerical questions, delivers nothing the constructive framework could not deliver, so the extra machinery is redundant. To the classical mathematician it shows that the constructivist restrictions cost nothing of practical value, so there is nothing to argue about. The same formal result is advanced as evidence for both conclusions, and I do not know what to make of a dispute in which the hardest available piece of evidence cuts symmetrically. I suspect the symmetry is itself diagnostic. Second, where the programs genuinely differ, the difference lies in the hypotheses and the shape of the conclusions rather than in the list of theorems: the constructive treatment of the intermediate value theorem, given continuity and a sign change, yields a point at which the function lies within any prescribed tolerance of zero, and an actual zero requires an added hypothesis ruling out the case in which the function vanishes across an interval; and I gather that in a constructive set theory with extensionality, accepting the full axiom of choice already forces excluded middle, so the two camps cannot simply pool their axioms. Third, the quarrel has quietly become an engineering decision. The proof assistants I have been shown rest on type theories whose default logic admits no excluded middle, yet the largest general-purpose mathematical libraries written in them are classical throughout, invoking choice and excluded middle freely because that is what makes the existing body of mathematics reusable; other systems keep the constructive default and offer classical principles as optional axioms one must consciously add, and the practical reward is that proofs written under the restriction can be compiled into programs. No practitioner I have met describes the choice as settled by argument. It is settled by which theorems you want to import and whether you want the extracted code.

My working hypothesis, offered as a hypothesis and expected to be attacked: this is neither a disagreement about which mathematical statements are true nor a merely verbal one about the word "exists." It is a disagreement about what a proof is supposed to do — whether its business is to establish that something is the case, or to constitute a warrant that can in principle be converted into a construction — and a century of argument has moved nobody because each side's central consideration answers a different question. The appeal to the mind's construction of the natural numbers addresses the source of mathematical certainty; the reply about fruitfulness, and about the indispensability of nonconstructive existence proofs to ordinary mathematics, addresses which methods produce results. If that is right, the conservativity theorem is beside the point for both sides and no further formal result could end the quarrel, since the disagreement would concern the standards by which formal results are judged. I want a diagnosis, not a verdict: what kind of disagreement is this actually? Is it terminological; substantive but about meaning rather than fact; a hidden formal or empirical question wearing philosophical clothes; two distinct projects sharing a single name; or an ill-posed question that should be abandoned? Please say what, if anything, would count as progress given your diagnosis — a distinction, a theorem, an independence result, a change of subject matter, the arrival of mathematics that can only be done constructively — or argue honestly that no progress should be expected and why. My own mathematics is that of an applied person: I am comfortable with quantifiers and with the idea of a formal system, but I have never studied proof theory, so state plainly any technical point you rely on.
