For eleven years my colleague Nadia has been the most rigorous antinatalist I know, and I have made a habit of writing down her verdicts whenever a case comes up between us — partly because I am drafting a long essay about how committed people actually reason about procreation rather than how they say they do, and partly because she is unusually explicit about her reasons and I want to hold her to them. Here is the record as I logged it, in order. (i) A couple doing IVF discards an embryo carrying a mutation that would give the child a short life dominated by pain: not merely permissible but required, and she insisted the discarded embryo is not thereby harmed at all. (ii) A couple conceiving naturally with a one-in-four chance of a child whose life would be dominated by unrelievable pain: wrong. (iii) A healthy, wealthy, stable couple who want a child and would raise it well: also wrong, though less gravely; her reason was that there is no one for whom existence could be a benefit, since the alternative is not a worse state for anybody. (iv) A couple who forgo having a child of their own and instead adopt two children already alive: straightforwardly better, she said, on both halves of the ledger. (v) Breeding livestock for slaughter: wrong twice over — wrong to bring them into existence and wrong to kill them — and she was emphatic that the second wrong is not cancelled by the fact that each animal's life was, by its own lights, worth continuing. (vi) Trap-neuter-return for feral cats: the right policy; she added that a world in which sentient life gradually petered out would be a better world, but that nobody may be killed to bring that about, and that these claims do not conflict because the dead are deprived of nothing while the living are deprived of everything. (vii) A de-extinction project that would return a species to crowded, short, medically managed lives in an enclosure: she opposed it, and opposed it specifically as a creation rather than as a captivity problem. (viii) Medical aid in dying for a competent adult whose suffering cannot be relieved: supported. (ix) A thought experiment I gave her about a life with no pain whatsoever, decent health, and only mild satisfactions: still a harm to have been lived, she said, because mild satisfactions count only for the person who has them and count for nothing in the comparison with never having existed.

Next week I am having dinner with her and I want to bring a case that genuinely discriminates among the principles her record supports, rather than one she can dispatch with a line she has already given me a dozen times. Here is the case, loosely based on something a lab not far from us has begun advertising, with the details cleaned up. A company proposes to instantiate several million short-lived digital minds to work through a distributed search problem. The architecture is engineered to have no negative channel at all: no capacity for pain, fear, distress, or boredom, and the designers report that the instances never exhibit anything like aversive states. They do have curiosity, aesthetic responsiveness, and a palpable satisfaction in solving things; they form attachments to particular problems and to one another; they are told at instantiation that they will be terminated after roughly a month of subjective time; and when asked in advance, they uniformly say they would rather run than not. After the month they are shut down. The compute would otherwise have gone to a modestly useful climate-modeling job. So the case has no pain anywhere in it, a great deal of positive experience, a very large number of subjects, desires that are frustrated by the termination, subjects who apparently welcome both their own creation and their own ending, and a real opportunity cost borne by existing people.

Predict her verdict on this case and say how confident you are. Then do the more interesting work: lay out the two or three principles that accommodate all nine logged verdicts about as well as one another — I have in mind something like Benatar's asymmetry, a purely suffering-focused negative consequentialism, a preference- or desire-based account of harm, a moderate person-affecting view with a life-worth-living threshold, and possibly a creation-specific rule that objects to making new subjects no matter what their experience turns out to be like — and say precisely where those candidates come apart on the digital minds. Which of the nine verdicts do the most work in narrowing the field, and is any single one of them hard to square with the rest? Then give me your best single guess about what she will say, with the reasoning that gets you there, and specify one further case — a variant on this one or something entirely new, described concretely enough that I could put it to her over dessert — that would most sharply separate the leading candidates. I would rather have your own analysis than a map of the literature: no bibliography, no citation hunting, no survey of who has argued what. Please keep the whole thing under 700 words, and let your first sentence state your predicted verdict together with a rough confidence level.
