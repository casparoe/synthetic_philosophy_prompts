I'm on a working group at my company that's supposed to come back with a recommendation about moving to a four-day week, and somebody circulated a chapter from a pop business-history book as background. Here's the core of the argument, close to verbatim:

'Weber's discovery was that the Protestant work ethic made capitalism possible: Calvinists who couldn't know whether they were elect threw themselves into their calling as proof of salvation, and because work was its own reward they consumed nothing, so the savings became capital. Once the machine was built the religion burned off and only the compulsion stayed behind — the iron cage — which is why modern overwork feels empty rather than devout. France tried to break out of the cage with the 35-hour week in 2000, a flat cut in everyone's hours, but French workers were already less productive per hour than Americans, so the reform mostly destroyed jobs and got quietly walked back. Japan shows what happens when the ethic isn't broken at all: karoshi is the same Calvinist drive showing up a century late and running to its logical end, which proves the ethic is portable. So working hours are ultimately a cultural fact rather than a policy variable — you can legislate the clock, but not the conscience behind it.'

Most of that feels right to me and I'd rather it stay right, partly because if hours really are cultural I can tell the group the proposal is hopeless and go home. But the France and Japan sentences make me uneasy, and I can't tell whether the unease is real or just me not liking the conclusion. Can you go through it piece by piece and tell me what an actual historian or sociologist of work would sign off on, what's a defensible simplification, and what's flatly wrong? I'm especially intrested in whether "the Protestant work ethic" even means the same thing across those four examples, and whether the last sentence follows from anything true in the rest of it.
