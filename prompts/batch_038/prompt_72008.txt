I chair a residents' association in a mid-sized English town, and in the spring I have ninety minutes for a session on the franchise: compulsory voting, votes at sixteen, and votes for residents who are not citizens. I have no academic training in any of it — I have come to it by reading around and by watching our own meetings go wrong — and I want the plan of the session, not the argument itself.

Two hunches of mine, which I would rather you tested than took for granted. First, the three questions tangle because people reach for whichever criterion suits the conclusion they already want: membership when the subject is foreign residents, competence when it is sixteen-year-olds, stakes when it is themselves. Second, compulsion sits on a different axis from the other two, concerning how the electorate behaves rather than who composes it. If either holds, in what order must the pieces go, and what does each depend on?

Which single case should carry the whole thing? I have been considering a local bus consultation in which a third of those affected cannot vote and some of the remainder are sixteen, but I fear it is too live to be useful; tell me what the case must contain, what it must conspicuously leave out, and whether an invented one would serve better.

Then the misconceptions: which ones the room will bring, in the order they arrive — I expect 'compulsory voting is only a fine, so nobody is coerced' and 'if sixteen, why not twelve?' — and at what exact point in the sequence each should be headed off. Add one closing question that would show me the distinction has landed, and a plain statement of what to omit at this level and announce that I am omitting.

Finally, say how confident you are in the ordering you propose, and single out the part of it you are least sure of.
