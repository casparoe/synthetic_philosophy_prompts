I'm running a small interdisciplinary seminar on work, leisure and the good life, and next week we're pairing Keynes's 1930 essay on the economic possibilities for his grandchildren with Ishiguro's The Remains of the Day — which is mostly set in 1956, so a quarter of the way into Keynes's hundred years with the rest still to come. My students have all landed on the obvious reading: Stevens is what happens when work crowds out everything else, so the novel is a warning about the wrong relation to labour and it basically endorses Keynes's hope that once the economic problem is solved we'll finally be able to value ends above means, and spend our hours on each other and on the arts of life. I want to push back on that but I can't quite articulate why, so I'd like you to do the reading properly rather than the thumbnail version.

Things I want you to get your teeth into. First, the novel is full of people who already have leisure and are wrecked by it: Lord Darlington, pottering away at amateur diplomacy at the conference he hosts in the mid-thirties and destroying his reputation and his house for it, and then the American who buys the place in 1956, Mr Farraday, who can't fill the house, keeps a skeleton staff and whose idea of the good life is motoring about the West Country making small talk at people. Keynes's worry wasn't only that the workers would never stop working — it was that the rich would arrive at abundance with no traditions and no class to lean on and no arts of life to fall back on, and go quietly mad, and that for them the problem would be genuinely terrifying. Does the novel make that case better than the essay does, and is Farraday — the man who hands Stevens the use of his car and weeks off, i.e. Keynesian leisure delivered on a plate — the only person in the book who understands anything, or is he the joke?

Second, and this is the bit I really want: does Stevens belong in Keynes's frame at all? He isn't overworked by the standards of anyone in 1930; he's under-leisured only because he refuses leisure as a category. His whole theory of dignity is that a great butler is one who never takes off the professional mask, and the novel's most famous scene — his father dying upstairs while he goes on serving drinks downstairs — looks to me less like the triumph of means over ends than like a man for whom the means *were* the end, because they connected him to something larger than himself. Keynes's essay has no concept of dignity and no slot for meaning that comes from subordinate attachment to a great household, so does the novel expose a hole in the essay rather than illustrate it?

And then the ending on the pier at Weymouth, with the stranger telling him that evening is the best part of the day and Stevens resolving to practise bantering. Is that Keynes's advice — learn the arts of life, pluck the day, be like his lilies of the field — arriving forty years too late, and does Ishiguro think it's enough? Tell me which scenes you'd point the group to, where the pairing genuinely illuminates, and where it strains and the honest critic has to admit the two texts are talking past each other. I'm not after a summary of either, and definately not a list of further reading; I want the argument about what the novel shows that the essay can't.
