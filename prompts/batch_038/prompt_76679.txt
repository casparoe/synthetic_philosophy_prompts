I am one of three organizers of a two-day workshop that opens next Thursday, and I have been handed the opening remarks: six minutes, about forty people in the room with poor acoustics, and a livestream I have been told to ignore. The workshop exists because of an argument that started at a conference dinner and never got settled. A group working on mechanistic anomaly detection reported that a probe — a small classifier trained to read a model's internal activity — which had been validated on one model as a flag for something like planning-behind-the-evaluator's-back also fired reliably in a second model trained quite differently. The interpretability side of the room took this as evidence that the underlying mechanism is the same in both models, and therefore that this class of monitor transfers. Several of us noticed that the intervention results did not line up: removing or patching the flagged activity in the first model changed the target behavior, while doing the same in the second model changed very little, or changed something else. Nobody has said outright what the disagreement is actually about, and my job on Thursday is to say it in six minutes without insulting either half of the room. The audience is roughly twenty alignment and interpretability engineers from two labs, ten philosophers and biologists who work on systematics, development, and how traits get individuated across species, two program officers from our funder, and one science journalist who has repeatedly asked me whether these monitors work and deserves a better answer than the one I have. I should mention that I am the only organizer without a lab affiliation and the only one who was trained in philosophy of biology, so each half of the room already suspects me of being an agent of the other.

Please write the remarks, and build them around a distinction that I think is the real content of the dispute: that "the same mechanism" can be cashed out in at least three non-equivalent ways — shared descent, shared causal role under intervention, and shared job in producing the behavior — and that these come apart in trained models in recognizably biological patterns. I want the descent point made concretely, because it is the part the engineers tend to miss: two checkpoints fine-tuned from a common base model stand in something like a genealogical relation, so a claim that the mechanism is the same across them is partly a historical claim about inheritance; a model trained from scratch on broadly similar data is a convergence case, where similarity of role tells you nothing about shared ancestry; and distillation sits awkwardly in between, since a student model trained on a teacher's outputs is not a descendant of that teacher in the ordinary sense but is hardly independent either. On the biology side, use only what earns its place — the fact that homology has been defined by ancestry, by structure, and by function in ways that yield different verdicts on the same pair of organs, and the cases where very different-looking structures turn out to be built by overlapping parts of an ancient shared genetic toolkit. Every term from either field gets one clause of gloss, and the biology must be deployed only where it would change what an engineer does on Monday. The speech should not resolve the question and should not be a tutorial on homology for non-specialists; it should leave the room with something contestable. In particular, it should make the monitoring stakes explicit: which of the three readings of "same mechanism" one adopts decides whether a probe firing in a new model is evidence about that model or only evidence about the probe.

Formally: roughly 900 words, first person, written for the ear rather than the page, in American English. Include one joke that both halves of the room can enjoy without having read anything beforehand, and let it be affectionate rather than deflating. Include a short passage in which I name the failure mode everyone is privately worried about — that we spend two days producing vocabulary and no experiments — and say plainly what would count as the workshop having succeeded by Friday afternoon. End with three questions put directly to the room, each phrased so that an engineer and a philosopher would object to it for different reasons. No citations, no named researchers, papers, companies, or funders, no acknowledgments (I will handle those separately), and do not put any words in quotation marks as though quoting a real person or text. After the speech, add a brief note of at most two hundred words telling me which of the three questions you expect to draw the most resistance and from whom, and pointing to the exact spots where the biological material is load-bearing; if there is a spot where it is merely decorative, say so, and I will cut it.
