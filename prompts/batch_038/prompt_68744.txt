The examination was three hours long, closed book. Question 3 was worth 25 points and read as follows:

> State the Dutch book argument for the claim that a rational agent's degrees of belief must satisfy the probability axioms, making explicit the connection between credence and betting behaviour on which the argument depends. Then assess whether an argument of the same kind supports the stronger thesis that a rational agent must act so as to maximize expected utility. In the course of your assessment, identify any assumptions such an argument requires beyond the axiom or condition it is meant to defend, and say what conclusion survives if those assumptions are denied.

The rubric allocates 8 points to the statement of the Dutch book argument and the betting interpretation it relies on; 8 points to the identification of auxiliary assumptions, including for the utility-side argument; and 9 points to the assessment of the expected utility extension and to the final question about what survives.

Here is the student's answer, reproduced in full:

> A Dutch book against an agent is a set of wagers, each of which she is disposed to accept at her own stated prices, whose combination guarantees her a net loss in every possible state of the world. The argument requires a bridge between credence and behaviour. On the betting interpretation, an agent whose degree of belief in A is p is disposed to accept any bet that pays $S if A and nothing otherwise at a price of $pS, and to accept nothing more expensive. Credences on this view are not inner episodes that we posit in order to explain behaviour; they are dispositions that we read off it.
>
> Given the bridge, incoherence is a liability. Suppose an agent assigns 0.7 to A and 0.7 to not-A. At those prices she will pay $70 for a bet returning $100 if A and $70 for a bet returning $100 if not-A. She has paid $140 and will collect $100 whichever way things turn out, a guaranteed loss of $40. A bookmaker who knows her prices can construct such a package for any credence function that fails the axioms: any that assigns negative value to some proposition, fails to assign 1 to a tautology, or fails to be finitely additive over mutually exclusive propositions. de Finetti proved the converse as well, namely that an agent whose credences are coherent can be exposed to no such package. The upshot is that incoherent credences are, in Shimony's phrase, pragmatically inconsistent: the agent's dispositions are jointly self-defeating even though each is one she would endorse taken on its own.
>
> Lewis showed that the same strategy works over time. An agent who plans to revise her credences on learning E by some rule other than conditionalization can be offered a bet now and a further bet later which together guarantee a loss. Rational revision is therefore conditionalization.
>
> The harder question is whether the same style of argument reaches expected utility maximization. I think it does, and that it reaches it more strongly than the probability case, because the representation theorems supply the utilities that the Dutch book theorem leaves out. Ramsey, and later Savage, showed that an agent whose preferences over acts satisfy a small set of structural conditions — completeness, transitivity, and a constraint on how preferences behave with respect to consequences held fixed across states — can be represented by a probability function and a utility function, the latter unique up to positive affine transformation, such that she ranks one act above another exactly when the first has greater expected utility. Savage's version assumes that the agent already has probabilities over states and derives the utilities from her choices, which is why it is the more restricted of the two results: Savage conceded that his framework applies only in small worlds, whereas the earlier von Neumann and Morgenstern theorem, which works directly with lotteries over outcomes, carries no such restriction.
>
> The counterpart of the Dutch book on the utility side is the money pump. An agent who prefers A to B, B to C, and C to A can be traded around the cycle, paying a small fee at each step, and ends where she began with less money. Intransitivity is therefore exploitable in just the way incoherent credences are. The same holds for violations of the independence condition, which is what the Allais and Ellsberg cases are standardly taken to exhibit. Since an agent who is exploitable in this way is not merely unlucky but is committed to a package she would decline if she saw it whole, the coherence arguments answer those cases. Whatever intuitions the Allais and Ellsberg agents have, an agent who acts on them can be made worse off, and being made worse off in this sense is what the irrationality consists in.
>
> What survives when the auxiliary assumptions are questioned is considerably less than the usual presentation suggests. The Dutch book argument assumes the agent's utility for money is linear, since a wager acceptable at one stake need not be acceptable at another; it assumes the bookmaker knows her prices; and it assumes she evaluates wagers one at a time. If any of these fail, the argument shows only that her dispositions are internally inconsistent, which is a weak and largely formal norm, and not that her beliefs are defective as representations of the world.
