So I write a science-and-ideas column — mostly about how researchers actually reason, statistics gone wrong, cognitive science, that beat — and twice a year my paper co-hosts a free Saturday thing at the public library. It's a workshop, not a talk, and it's genuinely open to anyone, which means I get a room of about 25 adults who don't know each other: a few retirees, some grad students, and reliably one guy who will let everyone know he's read all of Taleb. I've got 75 minutes, five tables of five, a whiteboard, index cards, sharpies, and about 300 poker chips. No budget beyond that and nothing participants can be asked to read beforehand. My editor has warned me that the word "rational" makes people defensive, so maybe we don't lead with it.

What I want to run is a session on the idea that if your choices don't hang together — you take A over B, B over C, and then C over A — a patient person can walk you around in a circle and finish with your chips, and that this walk-you-in-circles thing is basically the whole argument for why coherence is what rationality amounts to. I want at least some of them to have *been* the person getting pumped, from the inside, and to be a bit rattled by it, before anyone names what happened. But I don't want the intransitivity to be handed to them as a trick in the rules, because then the debrief just sounds like "aha, we cheated you," and they'll chalk it up to bad design instead of to their own judgment. My instinct — tell me if it's dumb — is to have them playing commissioning editors at a magazine ranking incoming pitches on three or four attributes (how novel, how reported, how likely to get shared, how much work), where the tradeoff they make between attributes quietly shifts depending on which two pitches are sitting in front of them. So the flip comes out of totally ordinary-seeming weighing, not from a rigged table. Please actually design the items: give me the pitches, the attribute values, which comparisons are on the table at each round, and why that particular arrangement produces the cycle in most people's hands. If the editor-ranking thing won't generate real intransitivity at a table of five strangers, propose something else that will.

Two more things I want baked in. First, a friction: every swap or reconsideration costs a chip, so that somewhere around round four or five the pump should start stalling for anyone paying attention, and the room ends up arguing about whether the person who refuses to trade because it costs them a chip is being irrational or is the only sane one there. I want them to discover that the exploitation story leans hard on trading being free, not be told it. Second, tell me whether the pump should be run by me as the house, by a confederate player at each table, or by the tables against each other, and what breaks under each option. Then give me: full rules, materials list, a minute-by-minute timeline for the 75, roles, a debrief that maps specific moments that will have actually happened in the room onto specific links in the argument, what predictably goes wrong the first time I run this and how I get out of it, and a scaled-down version in case only twelve people show up. Last thing, and don't skip it: an honest paragraph on what this activity cannot reproduce and I'll have to supply by mouth — including the fact that showing someone's behavior can be *described as* maximizing something is not the same as showing they ought to maximize it, and I'm pretty sure a game like this risks smuggling the second claim inside the first. Flag exactly where my game does that so I don't teach the wrong lesson confidently.
