Our Thursday reading group (mostly beer, occasionally philosophy, in Dana's kitchen) has been doing the Chinese stuff for the last month — Mencius and Xunzi on whether people are good. Last week one of the guys, who quotes everything from memory and gets it half wrong, shut down a point I was making about whether my daughter's daycare should be allowed to hand out timeouts at all: "Mencius already settled this — human nature is good, so you just cultivate it and it grows." Then someone said Xunzi argued the exact opposite, and a third person said they were obviously just using different definitions of nature and were both right, which felt like a cop-out but I couldn't say why, and I've been chewing on it for a week.

Its not academic for me — my kid is five months old and my wife and I are genuinely split on whether she needs structure imposed or just room and good company, so I want to know what the slogan is even claiming before I let it do that work. Can you lay out the distinct precise claims hiding in "human nature is good," ordered from the most nearly-trivial to the most radical? For each I want the claim stated so it's actually arguable and not just a vibe, a tag for whether it's a platitude, substantive-but-defensible, or false, and a note on whether it would license the timeout-dismissal my friend was using it for. Please also say which of those readings Mencius's own examples actually seem to support — the kid about to tumble into the well, the water that runs downhill rather than up, the sprouts, that hill where the trees keep getting lopped and grazed until everyone assumes the mountain was always barren, and Gaozi's business about bending a willow into cups — and which reading Xunzi's objection really bites on, given that he seems to mean by nature just what you're born with and by goodness something you have to manufacture. If on some reading the two of them aren't disagreeing at all, tell me which one, and whether that reading is the deep insight or just a dodge that makes the whole debate empty.

Format: exactly six numbered readings, nothing before or after them — no intro, no wrap-up, no "great question." Two or three sentences each. If one of the six turns out to be two claims fused together, split it inside the item rather than adding a seventh.
