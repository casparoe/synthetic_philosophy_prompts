Forty years I spent in a colloid lab, and only since retiring have I been reading philosophy properly, which mostly means reading ethics and understanding about half of it. I've just worked through some Ayn Rand — don't laugh — and there's a question my reading group keeps steering away from, so I'll ask you instead.

Back in 1942 a sociologist called Merton listed the norms he thought held science together, and two of them have stuck with me ever since. One he called communism, a word that aged badly, and which people now tend to render as communalism: your results aren't really yours, they go into the common stock, you publish, you hand over your data and your reagents to people you're competing against, and your only claim on what you found is recognition. The other is disinterestedness: you aren't supposed to have a personal stake in how things turn out, and you referee your rivals' papers for nothing. I lived inside that economy from 1971 to 2011 and it never once felt like charity. It felt like the entry fee.

Rand died in 1982, so she never saw what has grown up on top of it: open-access journals where the author pays to give the article away, preprint servers where you hand your result to the world months before anyone has checked it, the effective-altruism industry with it's earning-to-give career advice, unconditional cash transfers to families in countries you will never set foot in, crowdfunding pages to cover somebody's insulin, and open-source libraries kept alive by volunteers who don't see a penny.

So what would she have made of it? Three things I'd like from you.

First: would she have called the scientific gift economy altruism, or would she have said it is the trader principle wearing a self-denying mask — priority, citations, reputation, the next grant — and that scientists merely imagine themselves selfless? Whichever you land on, get there through what she actually means by sacrifice, the handing over of a greater value for a lesser one or for nothing, rather than through the slogans people repeat about her.

Second: I made up a three-way grid on a walk this morning and I'd like you to test it, not flatter it. (a) giving that is really benevolence toward something the giver already values; (b) giving that is sacrifice and therefore immoral on her account; (c) something that looks like giving but is deferred payment for a benefit the giver receives. Sort each of my modern cases into one of those, then tell me where the grid breaks — which cases straddle two cells, and whether there is an obvious fourth cell I've missed.

Third, the one I genuinely can't work out. Someone writes a software library, puts it under a permissive licence, never charges, and is quietly pleased about the whole thing. Is that a producer's pride in his own work finding its reward, which she would have approved of, or is it a man handing the product of his effort to strangers who've earned nothing, which is exactly what she spent her life attacking? If the honest answer is that her framework cannot settle it, say so plainly instead of picking a side to look decisive.

Two ground rules. Write in British English — I'm in Edinburgh and the Americanisms get tiring. And no bibliography, no citations, no reading list; I'm not going to chase any of it up, I just want the argument. But do keep three levels visibly apart: what Rand actually wrote and isn't up for grabs; what people who study her disagree about, since I gather there's a live row over how far her ethics permits or even requires benevolence, and over whether her real target is charity as such or need treated as a claim; and what is simply your extrapolation to a case she never met, which is most of this and ought to be marked as such. If you tell me a contested reading is the consensus view you'll lose me.

One last thing: a sentence or two on where you think my own framing is leading the witness. I've been wrong about bigger things than this and I'd rather hear it now than in six months.
