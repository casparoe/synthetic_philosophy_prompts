Our reading group has been working through the big-history accounts that treat money, states, corporations, and human rights as collectively imagined — orders that exist only for as long as enough people accept them and leave nothing behind once acceptance is withdrawn. One member has produced an argument against that whole approach which I believe is bad but cannot diagnose. He insists he is using nothing but the method these books themselves recommend: explain what people believe by the social work the belief does.

(1) Whether a claim is true or false depends only on whether it corresponds to mind-independent facts.
(2) On the thesis in question, imagined orders have no such facts behind them.
(3) So claims made from inside an imagined order — that this paper is money, that this body is sovereign, that people have rights — are neither true nor false; they are coordination devices.
(4) The thesis itself has come to be widely accepted, and accepted for exactly the kind of reason the thesis says such things are accepted: it does useful work for a large audience, flattering readers with a picture of their own species as makers of worlds.
(5) So, by (3), the thesis is neither true nor false; it is a coordination device about coordination devices.
(6) A claim that is merely useful is not one anyone has reason to believe.
(7) Therefore no one has reason to believe the thesis.
(8) And since (4) through (6) are perfectly general, no one has reason to believe any functional or genealogical account of why people hold the beliefs they hold — including the ones this group has been relying on to criticize grand narratives, and including the alternative histories offered by their critics.

He presents (8) as a reductio of big history. I find (8) unacceptable, because it condemns the critical and historical work we prefer right along with the work we distrust, and I cannot locate the mistake. Which step should be rejected? Explain why that step looks unobjectionable in context, and then price the alternatives: what does a defender of the thesis surrender if she rejects (1), if she rejects (2), or if she accepts (5) and denies (6) instead? For what it is worth, I would rather give up (6) than (1), but I cannot tell whether that is a real option or merely where my sympathies lie. And if you think the argument is sound and (8) is simply the correct conclusion, explain what survives of the project of accounting for belief in terms of its social function.
