I'm 69, retired four years ago after thirty-five years running field and greenhouse experiments in plant ecology, and I've fallen into philosophy late and hard -- mostly causation, since in a way that was my day job. I've got maybe two years of focused reading and writing in me before I run out of steam: no graduate program, decent math, essentially zero metaphysics training, and I'd like to come out the other end with one long essay that's actually worth something rather then a pile of half-digested notes.

So here's what I want your help with. In the running fight between counterfactual accounts of causation and the interventionist or manipulability accounts, which of the open disputes should I actually spend those two years on? My shortlist, as best I can build it from outside the profession -- please add to it or cut it if my map is bad:

- Redundant causation: preemption and overdetermination, where the effect doesn't counterfactually depend on the thing everybody agrees was a cause.
- Whether interventionism is circular or vacuous, given that an intervention itself seems to have to be characterized causally -- it has to be surgical, changing the target variable and nothing else except through it.
- Absences and double prevention. My field says without blinking that a missing frost, or a patch that never got grazed, caused the outcome.
- Non-modularity at coarse levels. You cannot wiggle something like species diversity or soil carbon without wiggling a dozen other variables along with it. There was a genuinely nasty argument years ago over whether the extra productivity in the more diverse experimental plots was a real diversity effect or mostly an artifact of the odds -- add more species to a plot and you're likelier to include one dominant, highly productive one. If a clean intervention on a variable like that isn't even available in principle, what licenses causal talk at that level?
- Whether the two camps are rivals at all, or interventionism is just counterfactual dependence restricted to the variables in somebody's model.

For each of these I want: how much of the rest of the debate hangs on it; whether it's tractable for a person who can follow a bit of formal apparatus but will never publish in the journals; whether settling it would change how working scientists talk and argue, or only how philosophers talk; and whether it's secretly a word fight with no work to do. Then give me an ordering, state the criteria you used and the trade-offs you're making between them, and tell me what would move something up or down the list for someone in my position specifically -- for instance, does the ranking change if the essay is meant for other retired scientists rather than for philosophers?

Don't hand me a reading list or citations; I can't chase them and I'd much rather have your reasoning. Finish with the ranking as numbered lines, and then a last line in exactly this form:

\answer{the single dispute I should spend the two years on}
