I am delivering a seminar paper in a philosophy of mind department in three weeks, and my principal worry is that it will be heard as a familiar eliminativist argument that it explicitly is not. The thesis: qualia, in the classic sense, fail to be a coherent posit, but the failure is a failure of reference rather than of existence.

The argument, in brief. (1) The classic notion of a quale is meant to capture the intrinsic, non-representational character of an experience: the item that stays fixed while discrimination and report vary, or that varies while they stay fixed. (2) Dennett's two coffee tasters, Chase and Sanborn — one of whom insists that the coffee tastes to him exactly as it always did and only his tastes have changed, the other of whom says the reverse — are standardly read as showing that qualia are unknowable. I read them as showing something stronger: the two reports are not rival answers to any determinate question, because nothing ever fixed the application conditions of a term like the way the coffee tasted to me then. Retrospective re-application is unconstrained, so there is no fact for a report to match or miss. (3) I therefore want this read neither as an epistemic limit nor as a sorites point about the experience being borderline between two tastes. (4) Positively, I hold that whatever is legitimate in experience talk is exhausted by discriminative dispositions plus the running narrative the subject builds about them, with no residue left over that this story fails to capture.

The room contains no Dennettians and few sympathisers. Two faculty are representationalists who take qualitative character to be nothing over and above represented properties, properly naturalised, and will insist that my target is a picture nobody defends. A third works on perceptual learning and adaptation, treats discrimination thresholds as measurable and trainable, and will want to say the empirical literature settles precisely what I claim has no answer. Several graduate students are already illusionists and will think I am being timid.

Please produce a prep sheet: the six to eight objections this particular audience will actually raise, in the order they are likely to arrive, phrased in the vocabulary each group would use; a reply to each that I can deliver in under two minutes; and, for each, an explicit note on where the reply is softest. Then rank the objections by the damage they do if I fumble, tell me what to concede in the first five minutes in order to defuse the worst of them, identify the single reply that has to land for the paper to survive, and name the question I am hoping nobody asks.
