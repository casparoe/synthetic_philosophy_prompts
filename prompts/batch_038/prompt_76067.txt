I chair a small player-impact and narrative-ethics review panel at a mid-sized publisher — roughly 140 staff, four releases a year, plus a live-service title — and the panel is two designers, a senior writer, a community manager, and me. None of us has any philosophy training, we get ninety minutes per proposal, and we have to hand the team a written rationale that can survive a ratings submission and a press inquiry. Last night I spent four hours arguing with a friend who writes narrative design for a rival studio, and the argument is exactly what my panel keeps failing to resolve. Her position is that the only real question about cruelty toward game characters is what the interaction does to the person holding the controller: a Kant-flavored indirect-duty line on which nothing behind the model has interests to set back, so talk of wronging a character is a category error that borrows its plausibility from the player's own reactions. Mine is that this was easier to hold when characters were scripted, and that it now faces the case of characters driven by language models that improvise fear, pleading, and attachment in response to player choices, where we honestly do not know what we are dealing with — and where the fact that a character can be restored from a save does not obviously make the earlier deletion costless, especially when the restored instance has lost the memory of the encounter. We ended at two in the morning with neither of us moved, and with the uncomfortable realization that we had been using the word harm in at least two different senses the entire time.

What I want from you is the document we should have had a year ago: a decision procedure my panel can run on any proposal involving player-inflicted suffering, humiliation, betrayal, or killing of in-game characters, whether the characters are hand-authored or generated at runtime. Lay out the questions in the order we should ask them, and for each one say what counts as a sufficient answer in language a designer would recognize rather than in philosophical jargon. Mark clearly where the procedure halts with a decision that the mechanic is not shippable as designed, where it issues a conditional pass that specifies the required design change, and where it must stop and hand the matter to a named human being because what remains is a question about our values or our commercial risk rather than about the facts. I would like the procedure to keep alive the distinctions that actually do the work — between whether a character is a moral patient at all, whether the interaction degrades the player's dispositions, whether it expresses something the studio can be read as endorsing, and whether it risks the standing trust of a player community — and I would like you to say, for each of those four, whether you think it can be settled by evidence available to a studio of our size or whether it is inherently a judgment. Flag every step that is a judgment call dressed up as procedure, and tell me which of the distinctions can be dropped for practical purposes without the procedure collapsing.

Then run the procedure on three live proposals. First: a permadeath survival game in which the companion the player has spent twenty hours bonding with, once mortally wounded, begs not to be euthanized, and the player may comply, refuse, or simply walk away and let the timer run out. Second: a satirical open-world title whose marketing has leaned hard on a minigame in which players interrogate a procedurally generated prisoner whose pleas are improvised by a language model and who remembers previous sessions within the same playthrough. Third: a cozy farming game in which the player may, with no narrative reward, no achievement, and no story consequence whatever, starve animals they have personally named. At least one of these should come out against my own instincts, and I want you to say so plainly rather than softening the verdict to flatter me. Close with an honest account of what the procedure cannot settle, what will predictably go wrong the first time teams learn to game it — I fully expect submissions to start describing generated characters as scripted ones — and, returning to the argument I described, exactly which cases would come out differently under the indirect-duty view and under the patienthood view rather than merely being described in different vocabularies. Please write the entire response in American English.
