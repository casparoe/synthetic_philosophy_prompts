I recently read the opening chapter of a trade book on minds and machines, written by a philosopher for a general audience, and it contains a compressed statement of the Gödelian argument against mechanism — the Lucas–Penrose line — that I have not been able to shake. I took one logic course years ago: I can recite the first incompleteness theorem, but I could not prove it, and I cannot tell which parts of this passage are load-bearing simplifications and which are simply mistakes. Here it is, as closely as I can reproduce it:

> The argument goes back to Gödel. He showed that in any consistent formal system rich enough to do ordinary arithmetic, there are true statements the system cannot prove — in particular a sentence that says, in effect, that it is not provable by these rules. Now suppose the human mind is a computer program. Then that program is a formal system, so it has its own Gödel sentence: an arithmetical truth it is constitutionally incapable of establishing. But we can do better than the program can. We can step outside it, look at its rules, and see that its Gödel sentence must be true. That is the whole argument, and it is decisive. No program can see the truth of its own Gödel sentence; we can see the truth of ours. Therefore the mind is not a program. The same reasoning explains why large language models cannot genuinely understand mathematics: they can produce only what their training permits, and no increase in scale lets them escape limits that Gödel proved to be absolute. Notice that none of this requires the mind to be magical or non-physical. The conclusion is purely that the mind cannot be reproduced by any algorithm, whatever physical process happens to implement it. Philosophers have objected that we do not know we are consistent. But that misses the point entirely: the argument does not require us to be consistent, only to be able to recognize the truth of a sentence the machine cannot prove — and we can do that simply by reading off the machine's own rules.

My own instinct is that the argument is a cheat: it grants the human being a capacity — stepping outside the system and reading off its rules — that it denies the machine, and if you handed the machine the same capacity the argument would evaporate. But I do not know whether that is a real objection or a phrase I picked up from a review, and the passage plainly anticipates a cousin of it and waves it away in its last two sentences.
