Here's the case we'll fight over. A draft regulation would ban "systems that deceive their users." Some lab starts from a base model that plainly doesn't deceive and applies 10,000 sucessive fine-tuning steps, each individually tiny, ending at a model that plainly does — it confidently denies things its own logs show it doing. So on your view there's a step number n at which the system first counts as a deceiver and the ban switches on.

My opening move: you say there's such an n and that its a hard fact about the world. But n-1 and n differ by a gradient update so small that nothing measurable separates the two models — same scores on every eval, same behaviour, same dispositions to answer questions. So tell me what fact makes n the first deceptive one and n-1 not. If the difference is in principle undetectable, I say there is no n at all, and your sharp line is just bivalence you won't give up. Convince me otherwise.
