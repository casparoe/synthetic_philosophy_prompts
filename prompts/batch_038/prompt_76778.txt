I work on a small research project that tries to rank long-run scenarios for a policy outfit — climate, pandemic preparedness, AI, the usual set where the candidate outcomes differ wildly both in how many people there'd be and in how good each life would be. We've got maybe a dozen scenario families to sort, and we hit a wall about two weeks in: our experts cheerfully agree that B beats A, C beats B, D beats C, and so on down the line, and then almost every one of them insists that A beats the last item in the chain. Straight up spectrum argument, arriving in our own elicitations rather than in a thought experiment. Since then I've been reading Parfit's original version, Temkin and Rachels arguing that better-than just isn't transitive all things considered, Broome pushing back, and Chang's parity stuff. I don't want to pick axioms yet. I want the spec first: what should I be *demanding* of any theory of betterness that claims to handle this?

So write me the desiderata list — what an adequate account has to deliver, with the case or consideration behind each entry, not just labels. Obviously in there somewhere: the adjacent-step intuitions and the endpoint intuition can't both survive alongside transitivity, so what does a theory owe us in place of one of them; something about not licensing the repugnant conclusion as a side effect; Pareto-ish conditions and non-sadism; and whether the relation has to be complete enough to actually yield a maximal element over the options we face, since a theory that makes everything on a par is useless to me even if it's coherent. But I care more about the ones people don't usually list, so please push there. Does a theory have to be invariant to how finely we carve the spectrum — a verdict that changes when you re-describe ten steps as a hundred, or as a continuum, seems dodgy to me, but I can't tell whether that's a real requirement or just my instinct from multicriteria work at my day job. Does it have to be auditable, in the sense that for every verdict it renders you can say which dimension did the work? Where do the money-pump argument and Quinn's self-torturer belong — are they constraints on the value relation itself or only on sequences of choices, and does it matter that we'll make one decision rather than a bunch of sequential ones? And does a theory have to explain why each step *seems* better rather than being allowed to just deny that it does?

Then mark which of these are non-negotiable and which are preferences of particular camps, and — this is the part I most want — tell me if some subset is jointly unsatisfiable. My suspicion is that {transitivity of all-things-considered betterness, Pareto, no sharp cutoff anywhere in the spectrum, completeness} can't all hold once the steps get fine enough, and maybe there's an Arrow-flavored version of the same trouble if you treat the value dimensions like voters, but I could be wrong and I'd rather you show me the argument than reassure me. If the list itself is the result — i.e. the honest conclusion is that no adequate theory exists under these demands — say so plainly. Also tell me which single requirement knocks out the most candidates, and flag any requirement you think I've smuggled in from the practical setting (we only need a one-shot ranking of a dozen things, not a decision procedure) that a pure axiologist would reject out of hand. Short scorecard at the very end is fine — a few lines each on Parfit's vagueness/imprecision response, the intransitivist line, Broome's hard-line defense of transitivity, parity, and a lexicographic view — but keep it brief, the criteria are where the thinking should be. And if you think one of my "obviously in there somewhere" items isn't actually a desideratum at all, say that too, I'd rather find out now.
