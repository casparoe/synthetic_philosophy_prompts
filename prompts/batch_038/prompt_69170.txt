I teach a philosophy of science seminar, 16 students (mostly third and fourth years plus two grads), one 110 minute block. We're in the middle of the stretch on auxiliary hypotheses and underdetermination — Duhem, Lakatos, the whole "you can always save the phenomenon" business — and I want to use Malthus as the running case, because its the one example where the prediction horizon is so long that falsification basically never arrives inside a human career.

Here's the rough thing in my head. Turn it into something I can actually run.

The game, working title "The Rescue Ledger": four groups of four. Every group owns the same theory — the Malthusian claim that population outruns subsistence, so any rise in per-capita living standards gets competed back down again — but each group starts play in a different decade and moves forward through history round by round. Each round I reveal a data card. Rough content: the Irish famine, English yields climbing faster than English heads, the Green Revolution, the postwar fertility collapse in East Asia, the 2020s where half the world is below replacement, and one special card for Malthus's own 1803 second edition adding moral restraint as a new check alongside vice and misery. For each card a group has to do exactly one of three things: claim it as confirming, pay a rescue (write an auxiliary hypothesis on an index card and hand over one of a limited number of credibility tokens), or concede a local defeat and take the penalty.

The thing I want them to run into without me naming it: a group that never rescues is refuted by round three and then has nothing to do for an hour, while a group that rescues every time survives to the end — except their stack of index cards no longer describes anything a reader of the 1798 Essay would recognize. At the end the other groups read each survivor's stack aloud and vote on whether it's still the same theory. Then, right before the debrief, I want an "asymptote card": something in the modern world that looks like the prediction came true after all, but by a mechanism the theory never contained. That's when the survivors get to argue they were right but early.

Scoring has to be built so right-but-early pays noticeably less than plain right, and — this is the part I want to be contentious — possibly less than being cleanly and quickly wrong, on the grounds that the honestly refuted group at least got an answer out of the world. I want at least one student to be properly angry about this and say so out loud.

Two home-grown terms, use them or improve on them: I've been calling the accumulated auxiliary stack "rescue debt," and the situation where the only survivable outcomes are died-honest and lived-degenerate I've been calling the honest corpse problem. If you can add a mechanism that makes the debt feel like a real cost rather than an abstraction — something with interest, or something the other groups can call in — do it.

What I need from you:

- Full rules and the whole card deck. Write out about 12 data cards with the actual historical content and the date range each covers, plus 3 asymptote cards.
- The token economy, and how many rounds genuinely fit in 110 minutes with a real debrief left over. Be realistic, my students argue slowly.
- Materials beyond index cards and a timer.
- A debrief that maps specific moments in the game onto the Duhem-Quine point, onto Lakatos's progressive versus degenerating distinction, and onto the separate and harder question of whether "right but early" is a coherent verdict at all or just an unfalsifiable compliment you pay a dead theory.
- What will predictably go wrong the first time I run it. My guesses: one group writes rescues so vague no card could ever count against them; the refuted group checks out and goes on their phones; and the two history students nitpick my data cards instead of playing. Tell me the rule that fixes each one.
- And be straight with me about what the game cannot reproduce. I suspect it's that real Malthusians were never scoring themselves against a token budget, and that the theory's actual fate got settled as much by politics, poor law arguments and rhetoric as by crop yields. Tell me what I have to supply by hand in the last ten minutes because no mechanic will deliver it.

No bibliography and no reading list, please — I know the literature and I disagree with a good chunk of it. Just the runnable thing.
