For thirty-two years I worked on performance assessment for deep geological repositories — modelling radionuclide transport, and, more to the present purpose, convening formal elicitations of expert probability for scenarios that no one will live to see tested. I retired four years ago and have since taken up philosophy with more seriousness than I expected of myself. I have been reading what goes by the names of epistemic utility theory and accuracy-first epistemology, and I find myself in the curious position of recognising my former professional toolkit being put to work deriving conclusions about rational belief that I would not have arrived at from inside the profession. I now sit on the advisory board of our national regulator, and at next month's meeting we must settle a procedural question that I should like your assistance in framing.

The case before us is this. The board is required by statute to publish a single number: the probability that a specified canister-corrosion scenario produces a release exceeding the regulatory dose limit within the compliance period. A panel of seven experts has returned individual estimates, and they are wildly dispersed. Three cluster near 0.02, two near 0.40, one holds that the figure cannot coherently be placed below 0.001, and one — a corrosion chemist of real standing — maintains 0.000005 and has stated in writing that he will not move. The published number feeds a cost–benefit rule: below a certain threshold no additional engineered barrier is required, and that omission is irreversible on the timescale in question.

The board's draft guidance proposes to settle the aggregation question by appeal to accuracy in the technical sense: adopt a scoring rule, then choose the pooling method that minimises the panel's total expected inaccuracy. I have myself argued for something like this. I have come to think it conceals the decision rather than making it, and I want the memo to say so plainly and then to say what we should actually do.

As I understand the mathematics — and please correct me if I have it wrong — the answer depends entirely on which strictly proper scoring rule one selects: a quadratic rule points toward the arithmetic mean of the individual estimates, a logarithmic rule toward the geometric mean. Under the logarithmic option our chemist's near-zero assignment drags the published figure toward zero almost by itself, which is to say that the choice of scoring rule quietly hands one member a veto. Accuracy-first epistemology, so far as I can tell, permits any member of a family of legitimate measures, so the instruction "be most accurate" does not choose among them.

Please draft the memo I intend to table. It should:

1. Reach a determinate recommendation — a specific pooling rule, or a specific alternative procedure — together with a fallback should the board reject it, and stated so that a secretariat with no philosophical training could execute it.
2. Explain why the draft guidance's appeal to accuracy does not by itself resolve the matter, making the measure-relativity explicit rather than merely asserting it.
3. Address the assumption that an agent scores her own opinions by her own lights. Accuracy-first arguments seem to require it; I am unsure it survives transplantation from the individual believer to a committee that must justify its published figure to people who do not share its measure, including to the dissenter. If there are self-undermining worries of the sort I have read about, say whether they bite here or are merely dialectical.
4. Confront the fact that proper scoring rules earn their keep through long-run calibration across many forecasts, whereas we face a single, unrepeatable event with no feedback loop.
5. Give the strongest available case for the option you reject. In particular: the chemist may simply be right and the majority wrong; averaging can swamp a well-informed minority; and if experts learn that outliers are averaged away, what does that do to the candour of future panels? Treat that incentive problem seriously rather than dismissing it.
6. State what would show, at the next quinquennial review, that the board had chosen wrongly — and note whether the statute's demand for a point value forecloses the obvious alternative of publishing the panel's dispersion instead.

Aim for roughly fifteen hundred words, with headings. Do not include a bibliography or named references; I will supply those myself, and a guess at them would be worse than none. Assume two of my colleagues on the board have no mathematics beyond school algebra, so keep any formalism to what can be explained in a sentence.
