I serve on the franchise and electoral-boundary commission of a mid-sized state — nine members, published reasoned opinions, and by long-standing practice we treat our own earlier opinions as binding on us in the manner of a court rather than a legislature. We are fourteen months into a general review, and our record so far runs as follows. (1) We held that fixed-term seasonal workers on a nine-month rotating contract, who pay payroll tax and are subject to our full criminal jurisdiction, have no claim to the vote at any level, because their stay is temporary and their lives are centred elsewhere. (2) We held that permanent residents of five years' standing may vote in municipal elections but not in national ones, and we reaffirmed that position for a petitioner at twenty-two years' continuous residence. (3) We held that citizens who emigrate keep the vote for ten years of absence, after which it lapses unless they return or are posted abroad by the state. (4) We held that a person born abroad to our citizens, who has never lived here and whose parents emigrated before the birth, has no vote. (5) We held that sixteen- and seventeen-year-olds may vote municipally but not nationally. (6) We held that prisoners serving custodial sentences retain the vote, since imprisonment does not end membership in the polity. (7) We held that adults may not be disenfranchised by diagnosis but only by an individual finding that they cannot understand what voting is, and we struck down a blanket exclusion of everyone under plenary guardianship. (8) We held that a chamber of commerce whose members are bound by a regional water authority's rules has no vote in that authority's elections, on the ground that only persons can be members of a demos. (9) We held that dual nationals resident here may vote here even if they also vote in their other state's elections, but that no one may hold two registrations in two of our own constituencies at once. (10) We held — this is the ruling that has caused the most internal trouble — that when a city annexed an adjoining district, the district's residents had a claim to vote on the annexation itself, although none of them was a city resident at the time and none had been consulted.

The matter now before us arises from a treaty ratified four years ago, under which we received some 40,000 people from an island state whose territory has become uninhabitable. The treaty grants them permanent residence in perpetuity, requires them to pay taxes and sit on juries, subjects them to our criminal and administrative law in every respect, and confers birthright citizenship on their children born here; it also bars them, expressly and permanently, from naturalising or voting in our national elections, while preserving their citizenship in the home state, which continues to function as a government recognised by us, with a sovereign wealth fund, a passport, a school curriculum delivered here under licence, and its own elections — on whose roll our 40,000 remain, and in which most of them voted by mail last year. Two questions have been referred to us. First, a petition asking us to declare that the treaty's national-franchise bar cannot be reconciled with the principles implicit in our own rulings. Second, a reference from the ministry asking whether, if we were to recommend national enfranchisement here, those voters could remain on the home state's roll given our single-registration ruling and our dual-national ruling, and whether the ten-year sunset we imposed on our own emigrants has any application to people whose homeland has ceased to be habitable rather than merely left.

What I want is committed rather than diplomatic. Begin by reconstructing the rule or rules our ten rulings can be read as running: I know the phrase "boundary problem" and I have seen the all-affected principle invoked around it, but nothing in our record looks like pure all-affected to me, and I would rather you tell me what does fit than survey who has argued what. Then (a) give a definite recommendation on the petition — grant or refuse, and if grant, at which level — identifying which of the ten rulings is the closest analog and which one you would have to distinguish or overrule to get there; (b) give a definite answer on the ministry's reference, including the sunset question; (c) set out the candidate principles our record genuinely leaves open, and for each state what it commits us to on (a) and (b), so that I can see whether we are deciding a case or deciding a theory; and (d) describe one further case, concretely, the way a petitioner might actually frame it, that would most sharply force us to choose between the principles that fit our record equally well. Rank the candidates by how much of the ten rulings each really explains rather than presenting them as equally respectable. Two colleagues have already staked out positions — one thinks the annexation ruling was an aberration to be confined to its facts, the other thinks it is the only ruling in the set that states a principle rather than a preference — and I am not looking for a split-the-difference compromise between them. If you conclude that some pair of our rulings is flatly inconsistent, say which pair, say which one should give, and say what we lose by giving it up.
