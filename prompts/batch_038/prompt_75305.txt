I convene a 90-minute week-5 seminar for 18 second-year philosophy, politics and economics students. So far they have met collective decision-making only as a black box: a vote goes in, an outcome comes out. I want them to run into the discursive dilemma — or the doctrinal paradox, whichever label you prefer to introduce in the debrief — through their own deliberation, and to leave having felt that the procedure, rather than the evidence, settled who was found responsible. The room has a whiteboard and a document camera, and every participant will get a printed card; there is no budget for real money and no tablets.

The fictional case I would like built around is a university disciplinary panel. The regulations, as I have sketched them, make responsibility turn on three findings that must all hold: that the conduct breached the code, that the student knew or ought to have known it did, and that no excusing condition applies. I would like a second, disjunctive route to the same verdict available as well — an alternative ground on which the panel may find responsibility — so that the order in which the chair puts questions to the vote changes the result. If that second route makes the exercise too slow for 90 minutes, tell me so and propose a leaner variant.

I have in mind three panels of six, each with five voting members and one clerk who tallies and records findings but does not vote, with the three clerks then convening as an appellate tier that must aggregate what the panels found. The recursion is deliberate: I want the higher body to inherit the same difficulty rather than resolve it. Please tell me where this arrangement will not work — panel size, parity, the clerk's neutrality, the time the appellate stage will actually consume — and revise it if you can do better.

Two further constraints. First, no lecture beforehand: participants encounter the rule as practitioners, and the names of the phenomenon arrive only at the end. Second, the incentive scheme must give at least one member on at least one panel a reason to vote against their genuine view of a finding, because the recorded findings have consequences that outlive the verdict — a future appeal, say, or a disclosure obligation — so that a member can lose on the verdict yet gain on the reasons. That wedge is what I want them to notice.

Please write up the activity under these headings, in this order:

1. **Overview and learning target** (about 150 words): what they should be able to do afterwards that they could not before.
2. **Amendments to my sketch** (about 150 words): what you have changed and why.
3. **The case file and the rule** (about 300 words): the scenario as the participants will read it, including the exact wording of the findings and the alternative ground.
4. **Roles, materials and judgment profiles** (about 400 words): private role cards, plus a fully worked set of individual judgments for each panel, laid out in handout-ready tables with the tallies shown explicitly so that I can check by inspection that the reversal genuinely occurs. State for each panel which procedure yields which verdict, and where the order of questions bites.
5. **Run sheet** (about 300 words): minute by minute, including the appellate stage and the point at which the reversal becomes visible.
6. **Scoring and incentives** (about 200 words): a points scheme requiring no money, showing precisely which member is induced to misrepresent which finding and what it costs them.
7. **Debrief script** (about 500 words): mapping named moments in the run onto finding-wise versus verdict-wise aggregation, onto agenda dependence, and onto the strategic vulnerability of each procedure; then naming the ideas; then a closing move in which the groups must choose which of three commitments to abandon — that any pattern of individual judgments is admissible, that the group's findings remain mutually consistent, or that the procedure treats every proposition in the same way. Flag for me whether that triad is a fair informal gloss of the relevant impossibility or whether it misleads, and how you would sharpen it.
8. **First-run failures and fixes** (about 250 words): what predictably goes wrong the first time this is run with 18 students, and what I should do in the room.
9. **What the activity cannot reproduce** (about 150 words): which feature of the problem the exercise leaves out and that I will have to supply myself.
10. **Cuts and variants** (about 150 words): a 45-minute version, and a version for a cohort that has already met the material.
