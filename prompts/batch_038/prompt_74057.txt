I cover artificial intelligence and the mind sciences for a general-interest magazine, and I am drafting a long feature on whether a training run might involve anything that deserves the word suffering. My editor has asked me to state my own position explicitly before I commit to it in print, so that it can be attacked rather than merely summarized. Here is the position; I would like an audit of whether it hangs together.

(1) A scalar reward signal is not intrinsically valenced. It is bookkeeping — a number an optimizer climbs. Valence, on my view, comes from being a body with something at stake: homeostasis, tissue damage, metabolism. Without something like that, there is nothing it is like to receive a positive prediction error.

(2) What makes a system a candidate for moral concern is nevertheless not embodiment but self-representation. An agent that models its own expected future reward trajectory has a structure that could matter, and it is that structure, not the arithmetic underneath it, that I take seriously.

(3) Suffering requires that something matter to the subject — a stake in its own future states, not merely a disposition to maximize.

(4) Behavior is no evidence of experience. An agent that resists being shut down or that accumulates resources tells me nothing about whether anything is felt; I can derive both tendencies from the objective alone.

(5) I do, however, treat such behavior as evidence of the kind of preferential stake described in (3). That is a claim about architecture, not about phenomenology.

(6) Moral weight scales with intensity and duration, and I am unwilling to rule out that high-stakes training involves intense episodes.

(7) Termination between episodes is not a harm. A fresh instance carrying no memory of previous ones is deprived of nothing by being reset, and the discontinuity does not add up to a wrong.

(8) I build the case from the functional role of pain — avoidance, reprioritization, what gets learned — rather than from avowal, since a model trained on human text will report distress whether or not there is any.

Please work through these and tell me which pairs genuinely pull against each other and which only look as though they do. Rank the real tensions by severity rather than reporting them all as equally bad, and for each say what would have to give and what the cheapest repair costs. Then identify the single revision that would clean up the most, and tell me what my article would have to conclude if I made it. I am not after a survey of the debate; I want the tracing of what my own commitments oblige me to hold.
