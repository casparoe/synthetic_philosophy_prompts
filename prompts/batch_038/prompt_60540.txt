So here's something I can't get straight in my head. When my mom was diagnosed with cancer the oncologist gave her a percentage — something like 7 out of 10 women her age at her stage still alive at five years. I looked into how those numbers get made, and its basically: take everyone ever diagnosed with the disease, sort them into boxes (stage, age band, year of diagnosis, sometimes the hospital, sometimes a gene marker), and whichever box you land in gives you your number. Everybody agrees the narrower the box the better the estimate, right up until the sample gets too small.

Then my brother, who does philosophy, pointed out that the narrowest true description of my mom isn't any of those boxes — it's "roughly the nine-hundred-thousandth person ever to be diagnosed with this disease." And if you're allowed to use that box, you get a real prediction: the total number of people who will ever get it is probably not vastly larger than that, or else she'd be sitting absurdly early in the sequence. He said it's the same move as the doomsday argument. Something has obviously gone wrong, but I can't say what, because it looks exactly like the rule her oncologist used. Meanwhile the same doctors who insist on the narrowest possible box also tell patients not to go look up their own survival curve, because the curve is about a population and not about you.

What theory of probability — or of what makes one group the right group to compare a person to — does the practice of quoting stage-and-age survival numbers actually presuppose? Why is narrower supposed to be better, and what's the stopping point that keeps my brother's version out: is it something the practice is genuinely committed to, or could the whole thing be run on a different account of these numbers that would license the rank reasoning too? And what would have to change in how the numbers are computed and handed out if that presupposition were dropped?
