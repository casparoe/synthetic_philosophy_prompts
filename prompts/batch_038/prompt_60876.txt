I cover science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and I am reporting a piece on the aftermath of the March 2024 decision by the Subcommission on Quaternary Stratigraphy to reject the proposed Anthropocene epoch — 12 votes to 4, with two abstentions, against a formal proposal built on the varved sediments of Crawford Lake in Ontario and a base dated to 1952 by bomb-test plutonium. In the course of reporting I interviewed two people who then argued at length, in front of me, in a way I could not referee. I would like your help doing that.

First, a role and a method. Approach this as an expert in the philosophy of science and Earth-system history, equally at home with the conventions of the International Chronostratigraphic Chart (what a GSSP is for, the supermajority rules, the difference between a boundary marker and a causal narrative) and with the historical literature on periodization and responsibility. Think carefully rather than quickly, and do not settle for a comfortable both-sides split. Begin by listing, in three or four lines, the distinct questions you think the two speakers are running together or trading places, and only then turn to the moves themselves.

The argument, as I recorded it:

1. Dr. N., a Quaternary stratigrapher, opened by saying the vote settled a labelling convention, not a fact about the planet. A golden spike exists so that geologists in different basins can correlate the same horizon; the only question it can answer is whether a signal is sharp, globally synchronous, and identifiable in one continuous section. On that question the Anthropocene fails not because human impact is unreal but because an interval opening within living memory is orders of magnitude too young to be an epoch, when the Holocene itself already runs some eleven and a half thousand years.

2. Prof. A., an environmental historian, replied that "the only question is correlation" quietly does political work. Which signals are globally synchronous depends on which human activities moved enough matter to leave a legible trace, and that is a fact about power rather than about nature. Plutonium is legible everywhere because a handful of states detonated weapons; the 1610 Orbis spike — the dip in atmospheric carbon dioxide following post-contact depopulation of the Americas, advanced in Nature in 2015 as a marker meeting the formal criteria — is legible because of colonialism, extraction, and enslaved labor. Fixing on plutonium credits the first story and demotes the second, and a convention that does that is not neutral.

3. Dr. N. said this confuses a marker with a meaning. Boundary markers are chosen for sharpness, not for telling the true causal story: the iridium horizon at the Cretaceous–Paleogene boundary does not narrate the extinction either. Once a horizon is fixed, historians remain free, indeed better placed, to write whatever causal account they like about what follows. She added that the working group that selected the mid-century marker had historians among its members and endorsed it by a large internal supermajority in 2019, so this was not geologists simply overriding the humanities.

4. Prof. A. answered that the marker/meaning separation collapses in practice, because the marker is what travels. Announce an epoch beginning in 1952 and the operative public claim becomes that humanity as such did this — and the whole point of her objection is that responsibility is not distributed across the species. Formalization converts a critical concept into a natural kind; declining to formalize would have preserved its edge.

5. Dr. N. retorted that this is an argument against her colleague's own preferred outcome rather than against the vote: if formalization destroys what the concept is worth, the rejection should be welcome. The subcommission's stated reasons did not deny anthropogenic change; they held that the Anthropocene is better treated as an ongoing geological event within the Holocene than as a new subdivision of the time scale.

6. Prof. A. said that modesty is selective. The same community that disclaims competence over what the term means keeps publishing confident start dates and books announcing them. And an informal concept with no bounds cannot do the work the term is asked to do: someone writing about 1850 needs to know whether that year falls inside or outside the period, and "we will settle this informally, eventually" is not an answer.

7. Dr. N.'s final move: dates in geology are conventions answerable to evidence, not to a discipline's need for tidy boundaries. Wanting a line is not a reason to draw one. A historian may adopt the term with whatever bounds suit her explanatory purposes — that is what periodization has always been — but she should not expect the rock record to ratify it, and she should not describe the 2024 outcome as geologists having decided when the Anthropocene began.

Afterwards each told me the other had missed the point. Please adjudicate moves 1 through 7 individually, rating each from clearly mistaken to clearly right and justifying each rating in a sentence or two; then say which specific claims each of them got right and which they got wrong, who is more right overall and why, and what both of them are failing to address.
