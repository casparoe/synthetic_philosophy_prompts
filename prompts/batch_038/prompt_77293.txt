I am preparing a seminar handout on the Nyāya account of prāmāṇya and I have reconstructed an objection to it that I can no longer control. The reconstruction is my own, though it is meant to press the kind of regress challenge that Buddhist critics of the pramāṇa framework raised against any theory that makes warrant a matter of how an awareness is produced and checked.

The Nyāya commitments I take as fixed for the argument are these: a pramā is a veridical awareness; a pramāṇa is the most efficient instrumental cause of such an awareness, so that the pramāṇas are individuated by their causal role rather than by their subject matter; and validity is extrinsic both in its arising and in its being known. It arises from the excellences, the guṇas, present in the causal complex that produces the awareness, and it is ascertained only through the awareness's capacity to occasion successful activity, pravṛtti-sāmarthya. The Mīmāṃsaka, by contrast, holds that validity is intrinsic both in its arising and in its being known.

Here is the argument.

(1) For any awareness A, A's being valid and A's being ascertained to be valid are distinct; on the Nyāya account the second is had only through A's capacity to occasion successful activity.

(2) Registering that the activity succeeded is itself an awareness; call it B.

(3) B enjoys no exemption from (1). Nyāya recognizes no privileged class of self-certifying awarenesses, so B's own validity is ascertained only through B's capacity to occasion further successful activity, registered in a further awareness C.

(4) Therefore the ascertainment of A's validity rests on an open-ended chain B, C, D, and so on, in which every link requires the next.

(5) Nothing is grounded by a chain in which no member is grounded; grounding of this kind transmits rather than originates, and the chain has no first settled link.

(6) Hence on the Nyāya account the validity of no awareness is ever ascertained.

(7) But ascertaining validity is a commonplace achievement, and the criterion of successful activity was introduced precisely to explain it: the traveler who was told there is water at the well walks there, drinks, and thereby takes the testimony to have been sound, without any further certification being demanded of him. Nyāya wants to explain this, not deny it.

(8) So (1) is false. Either validity is self-certifying in ascertainment, as the Mīmāṃsaka says, or ascertained validity is a philosopher's fiction.

Two complications, which I suspect are doing more work than I have admitted. First, a colleague's case: a physician in a district with no laboratory treats a fever-and-rash patient under an inherited nosology that attributes the condition to an imbalance and prescribes accordingly; the patient recovers. On the criterion, the recovery is successful activity and certifies the diagnosis. But the same nosology predicts recovery without treatment as well, since the illness is self-limiting, and had the patient died it would have explained that too by a further imbalance. No possible outcome would have failed to certify it. Second, a loop rather than a chain: a gauge whose calibration was fixed by the very predictions it is now used to confirm, so that the successful activity certifies the original awareness by way of the original awareness.

What I want from you:

(a) Which numbered step do you reject, or which transition between steps fails to hold? Be precise about the reading on which the step looks acceptable and the reading on which it is needed for the conclusion.

(b) Why did that step look innocent when I wrote it down?

(c) Price the escapes. I can see at least four: deny (5) by distinguishing a chain whose links are available on demand from one that must actually be completed; deny (3) by admitting some self-certifying class of awarenesses; deny (1) by making validity a purely causal matter about guṇas and giving up on ascertainment as a theoretical job at all; or accept (6) and let (7) go, treating certification-talk as loose description of ordinary life. Tell me what each costs, in particular what each does to the Nyāya distinction from Mīmāṃsā and to the account of doubt and of doṣa.

(d) Say whether the argument proves too much in the stronger sense: whether it would equally defeat the Mīmāṃsā alternative, and whether it generalizes into an argument that no account of empirical warrant can ever yield a first case of known warrant. If it does, tell me what that shows about the argument rather than about the accounts.

(e) Take up my two complications separately. Are they independent damage to pravṛtti-sāmarthya as a criterion, or are they only cases in which the causal complex contained a doṣa that a more careful practitioner would have caught? If the reply is that the doṣa is in principle detectable, does that concede that what does the certifying is the absence of an undefeated defeater rather than the success, and if so what is the success still for in the criterion?

If I am forced to choose I would rather keep (1) than (7), since the tie between warrant and what happens when one acts is what makes the account worth having; but I want to know exactly what that preference commits me to.

Finally, a request about how you present this. Please keep clearly apart what is settled about Nyāya's actual commitments from what is genuinely contested among people who work on this material, and do not hand me a contested reconstruction as though it were the consensus reading. That applies especially to whether successful activity is offered as a mark of validity itself or only as a way of coming to know a validity that is already there, to how the guṇa and doṣa apparatus is meant to handle the self-limiting-fever case, and to whether Nyāya has any resources for a non-question-begging reply to the regress rather than a dialectical one. Where you are reconstructing rather than reporting, say so; where you are unsure whether a claim belongs to Nyāya proper or to a particular later author, flag the uncertainty instead of resolving it silently; and do not supply textual references you are not confident in, since I would rather have an explicit gap than a plausible-looking locator.
