I'm doing an evening class in aesthetics — Tuesday nights, in the back room of a library, nine of us, mostly retired people and me — and this week we did the tragedy thing, why we like being wrecked by sad art. My tutor said Hume has an essay where the suggestion is roughly that the beauty and eloquence of the writing get hold of the sorrow and convert it into something pleasant, and that real misfortune, artlessly reported, doesn't please at all. Which sort of works but I don't think it fits how I actually behave, and neither does the answer someone else gave, the make-believe one where we aren't really sad because we know it isn't real. I'd rather go by my own verdicts and see what they add up to, because honestly they don't look consistent to me.

Here they are, roughly in the order I thought of them. After my last breakup I listened to nothing but slow miserable songs about being left for a fortnight, on purpose, and I'd have said I enjoyed it — not as a distraction either, I wasn't trying to think about anything else, I wanted the feeling. I've watched a film where the old man dies at the end maybe six times and I'd tell anyone to watch it and I'd watch it a seventh. But last month I put it on and fast-forwarded through the death scene, which I cant really explain. A documentary showing an actual child dying of something preventable — I wouldn't sit through that, and I know I'd be glad afterwards and probably send money somewhere, so "glad afterwards" can't be the whole story or I'd watch it. Grisly horror, even well-made, I avoid completely; but a film that makes me properly angry at a character I enjoy fine, so it isn't just that negative emotions are safe in art. A series I loved killed off a favourite character in the last episode purely to wring tears out of us, and I felt used and rate it worse for it — whereas in another one I knew from the first ten minutes how it had to end, and that inevitability is what I loved most. So more sadness isn't better; something about the sadness being deserved or necessary matters. For about two years after my friend lost her baby I couldn't watch any film about losing a child, and then I did, and it might be the most valuable thing I've seen in my life: same film, opposite verdict, only the timing changed. And a piece for solo piano that I'd happily call grief-stricken — though I also think nothing in it is literally sad and nobody in it is grieving — gives me exactly the same kind of evening the film does, which seems to break the make-believe story entirely. Also, for a certain sort of comfort rewatch I actively want the happy ending and feel cheated without it, so I'm not someone who just prefers tragedy across the board.

So: is there a principle underneath all this, or a small number of candidates? I'd like it stated properly, clause by clause, with which of my cases each clause is there to handle, and precise enough to make predictions — so tell me what it says about ones I haven't mentioned (I'm thinking of a war memorial that's supposed to make you cry, a comedian doing a whole set about his dead brother, a novel where the awful stuff happens offstage and you only hear about it afterwards, and a tacked-on happy ending the studio forced onto a finished tragedy). And be honest about the cost: if the simplest principle that covers most of my list has to overturn a verdict or two, say which and why I'm wrong, rather than bolting on extra clauses until everything fits. The one I keep going back and forth on is this: a friend of mine built a sound installation out of her late mother's voicemails, it flattens everyone who hears it, there's a queue round the block for it, and she says she made it partly because she likes watching people cry in the room. When I go and stand in there and sob, is that the film, or is it the documentary I said I'd never sit through?
