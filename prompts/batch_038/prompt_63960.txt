Bit of background first: I spent about 35 years in numerical weather prediction, so my whole working life was discretising equations and running them on machines that got embarrassingly faster every few years. Retired six years back and fallen into philosophy late, mostly through a Thursday reading group at the local library. We're a retired teacher, two ex-nurses, a lawyer and me, and we're way over our heads but enjoying it.

Last week we were on minds and machines. I asked what struck me as a fair question: suppose you built something that talked and behaved exactly like a person — would there be anything it was like to be it, or could all that outward behaviour be there with the lights off inside? One of the regulars waved a hand and said the brain is just a computer, so the question doesn't even arise: same computation, same mind, whatever it's running on. Everybody nodded and we moved on, and I was the only one who noticed that nothing had actually been establshed.

So here's what I'd like. Take that sentence and pull it apart into the distinct precise claims it could be making — the whole menu, from the ones that are true but empty right through to the ones that would be a genuine discovery about the world. Two things I've already tripped over that I want handled explicitly:

First, which notion of "computer" is in play. In my old life a computer meant a von Neumann machine chewing through floating point numbers in discrete steps. But as a young man I also spent time around the old tide-predicting machines — brass wheels, discs and dials that added up harmonic components and drew you a continuous curve of the tide, with nothing remotely like a symbol anywhere in the works. Do both count as computers in the sense that sentence needs? Does the answer change depending on which one you had in mind?

Second, what the "is" is up to. Is it claiming the brain literally is such a machine, or that talking about it as one is just a convenient description we could equally do without, or something else again? I spent a career saying things like "the atmosphere is a fluid" and I can't tell whether that sentence is the same kind of thing.

Then, for each reading you give me, say whether it's actually plausible, and — the part I care about most — whether it would get my friend his conclusion, i.e. whether it would show that the exact behavioural duplicate has to have an inner life rather than merely suggesting it might. My hunch is that he needed something far stronger than what he said and never got near it, but I'd rather be told I'm wrong than flattered. And where a reading is really just an IOU against future research rather than a claim a working scientist would recognise as testable, say so plainly. Sorry for the lenght — assume I know the ordinary words but none of the literature, and I genuinely have no idea what people in this field count as settled.
