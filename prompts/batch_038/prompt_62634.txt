I'm trying to pin down when an inverse-reinforcement-learning system is entitled to say that the reward it recovers is the agent's preference rather than an artifact of the inference. Here are my verdicts on a set of cases, and I'd like to know what principle, if any, they add up to.

1. A driver takes a longer route every day to avoid the toll booth. Recovering a penalty on tolls: right.
2. A person buys the cheapest coffee at the station every morning and leaves a generous tip in the jar. Recovering a preference for cheap coffee: half-right at best; the better story is that they are constrained on one axis and not on the other.
3. Someone works until midnight, declines every invitation, and does all of it smoothly and deliberately. The recovered reward says they prefer work to friends; I want to say that is not their preference, even though the behavior is the only evidence I have.
4. A reach toward a cookie that is arrested halfway. I take the arrest to be the evidence, not the initiation.
5. An agent that plainly pursues a goal and fails at it clumsily, over and over. Recover the goal and charge the failures to competence rather than to indifference.
6. The same fluent, repeated pattern as in case 3, except I am told it was installed by conditioning the person never noticed. My verdict flips, though the trajectory data are identical.

Cases 3, 5, and 6 are what bother me: in 5 I let the desire survive the failure, in 3 I let it be defeated by the success, and in 6 I let identical data support opposite conclusions. Is there a single account of when inferred reward equals preference that delivers all of these, or nearly all? State it precisely enough that I can see which of my verdicts it cannot keep. Then tell me what it implies for a case I have not listed: an agent who pre-commits by removing the option — throwing out the cigarettes, deleting the app — where treating every choice alike recovers a preference for both states at once.

Two further things. In every case my verdict seems to turn on an assumption about how good a planner the agent is; say whether the behavior itself can settle that assumption, or whether it has to be handed to the inference from outside, and what follows if it has to be handed in. And if the simplest principle that saves 5 and 6 must give up 3, say so plainly and tell me why I should let 3 go.
