A philosophy podcast I've been listening to did an episode on perceptual justification, and the host glossed the easy-knowledge problem like this:

'Cohen's 2002 argument showed that foundationalism refutes itself. If a perceptual belief can count as basic knowledge — known without any antecedent reason to trust your senses — then closure lets you look at a red table, note that it looks red to you, and straightaway know both that the lighting isn't fooling you and that your vision is reliable. Since no serious epistemologist accepts that reliability can be manufactured that cheaply, basic knowledge has to go, and your only remaining options are Dretske and Nozick's denial of closure or outright skepticism; Cohen himself took the skeptical route. Bootstrapping is just Vogel's name for this same argument.'

I found this persuasive, and I'm inclined to think something in the vicinity has to give — but I don't know enough to say whether the host has stated the problem correctly or only a caricature of it. Please audit the passage sentence by sentence: what a specialist working on this debate would accept, what is a defensible simplification, and what is simply wrong. In particular I'd like to know (i) whether the target really is foundationalism, or something wider, and who exactly is on the hook; (ii) whether the one-step red-table inference and the bootstrapping pattern are the same argument, and if not, what turns on keeping them apart — including whether Vogel was objecting to the same kind of view Cohen was; (iii) whether "no serious epistemologist accepts" is a fair report of the dialectic, or whether there are live responses that keep basic knowledge without skepticism and without denying closure; and (iv) whether the concluding trilemma survives the corrections. Finally, restate the argument in the form you think its defenders would recognize, and tell me what remains genuinely problematic about it once the misstatements are removed.
