I'm a novelist, mid-draft on my third book, and I've fallen down a research hole I can't climb back out of. The novel is set in a river town that has held the same ceremony every October for a hundred and forty-odd years, commemorating a company of local men who were wiped out in the closing weeks of a war their side was already losing. The book isn't really about that war — it's about the ceremony, and the people who run it, and my narrator who grew up reciting at it and has started to notice what she's reciting. But the more I read around the thing, and I've been reading a lot of the same material everyone reads, the Confederate memorializing, Masada, Kosovo, Dunkirk, the Jacobite stuff, the more I keep hitting one specific pattern that I can't find a name for. I want to know whether it exists as a concept somewhere or whether I've assembled it out of three hundred pages of notes and a deadline.

Here it is as concretely as I can put it. Take a cause that lost, lost badly, and was widely understood at the time to be the wrong side. Then wait a generation or two, until the last people who could plausibly be damaged by it are dead. What you get is not denial. Nobody at my town's ceremony disputes what those men were fighting for — ask them straight out and they'll tell you the aims, and a lot of them will even agree the aims were bad. What happens instead is that the aims quietly stop being load-bearing. The losing does something to them: it converts a live political position into scenery, and all the emotional weight migrates onto the bare fact of the loss — they went out, they stayed, they didn't come home. And then the symbol goes public. The losing side's flag motif is on tote bags in the gift shop and gets bought mostly by tourists with no ancestral connection to it at all, including people who'd be appalled by the actual politics. The admiration outlives the complete evaporation of the thing that was supposed to be admired. Two details convince me this is a real mechanism and not just vague nostalgia. First, the contrast case: a movement that won can have martyrs, can have people who died horribly for it, and nobody romanticizes them at all, because it's still around, still acting, still available to be blamed — winning keeps the aims load-bearing, so the sacrifice can't be enjoyed separately from them. Second, the lag: straight after the defeat everybody is still furious and the losers are hated; the warmth only shows up later.

So — is there an established concept for this? I know it sits next to some things. The Lost Cause is the obvious instance rather than the concept, and I've half-registered phrases like heroic failure and chosen trauma from book reviews and podcasts I wasnt really listening to, but none of them seem to be about the specific move I mean, where the content of a cause is dropped rather than defended-then-denied, and the feeling gets inherited by people with no stake in it who would reject the content on sight. Tell me which of those neighbors is closest and exactly where each one stops fitting; and if there's genuinely no term, coin one for me and give me a definition with edges — what counts, what doesn't, and one case that looks exactly like this but isn't. I don't want a bare label, I want the carving, because there's a chapter that turns on whether my narrator is participating in this or merely describing it. And be straight with me about where you're most likely to be wrong: what would change your mind about the boundary you draw, and what consideration would make you say this isn't one phenomenon at all but three familiar ones stapled together by a novelist with a fixation?
