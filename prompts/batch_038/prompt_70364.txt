I chair the ethics consultation service at a 900-bed public referral hospital in a Southern African city. Between four and eight consults a week reach us in which the treating team and a patient's kin disagree about who is entitled to be in the room when a serious decision is made: disclosure of a terminal diagnosis, consent to high-risk surgery, withdrawal of support. Our written policy is an imported autonomy framework, and staff increasingly invoke ubuntu as the alternative to it — in a way that settles nothing. The maxim that a person is a person through other persons is cited by both sides, once to justify including the family and once to justify excluding it. I want the framework replaced by a procedure my committee can actually run.

Constraints. Each consult is completed in a single forty-five-minute sitting. The committee is a surgeon, two nurses, a social worker, a chaplain and a lay member; none has training in philosophy, and none will read a background paper beforehand. The output must be a one-paragraph recommendation entered in the chart, naming who holds the decision. The treating team may decline the recommendation, and the legal office reviews it on request, so it must be defensible to a reader who has never heard the word ubuntu.

Three confusions in our current practice that the procedure must be built to prevent:

1. Our staff move without argument from the claim that relationships constitute a patient to the claim that a patient's relatives speak for the patient. The first is an ontological claim, the second a decisional one. I want a procedure in which the first informs the second but never silently becomes it.
2. There is a strand of this literature on which personhood is not a status one is born with but something one attains and can forfeit. If the procedure scores patients by the thickness of their relational networks, it will downgrade precisely those who most need protection: migrants without local kin, the estranged, patients whose kin are the source of the harm, patients with advanced dementia. Tell me whether a graded notion of personhood can enter an operational rule at all. If you use it, show the guardrail that keeps it from becoming a sociability test administered to the isolated.
3. We slide from treating harmony as what a good decision aims at to treating the absence of expressed dissent as evidence that the decision was right. The procedure must be able to record a decision as sound when it leaves a family divided.

Deliverables, in this order: an ordered set of questions, each specifying who is asked, what counts as a sufficient answer, and what that answer licenses next; the stop rules, meaning the conditions under which the procedure returns no recommendation and escalates rather than deciding, and to whom it escalates; a marked list of the steps that are judgment calls dressed as procedure, together with what would make one member's judgment better than another's at those steps; an account of which distinctions from the relational literature you have preserved, which you have deliberately dropped, and one sentence on the cost of each dropped distinction; the three worked cases below; a failure-mode section stating which patients the procedure will get wrong, in which direction, and what a chart audit would look for to detect it; and a ten-line version usable by a night charge nurse without reference to the rest.

The cases:

- A 61-year-old man, newly diagnosed with a terminal cancer, with fluctuating capacity from hepatic encephalopathy. His daughters ask that he not be told. Two years earlier, when his brother was dying, he told our social worker that in his family the elders hear such news first and decide together what the sick person is told; that note is in the record. He is lucid this morning and asks directly what the scan showed.
- A 34-year-old woman, fully capacitated, scheduled for high-risk surgery, who has had no contact with any relative for eleven years and has asked in writing that none be informed or admitted. Her brother has travelled from another province and is in the waiting area. Our chaplain holds that the estrangement is itself a relational injury which the surgery is an occasion to repair.
- A 28-year-old woman in her first pregnancy, capacitated, who states plainly and repeatedly that her mother-in-law holds authority over decisions concerning her body, and asks that she be permitted to decide. The mother-in-law refuses a recommended caesarean section. The obstetric team wants the committee to declare the delegation invalid so that the patient's own interest becomes controlling. State whether the interests of the fetus fall inside or outside the scope of your procedure, and why.

In the third case I expect the procedure to reach a conclusion the obstetric team and I will resist. If it does, do not soften it. If the procedure genuinely cannot settle it, say so and identify the fact that would settle it.

I do not want a bibliography, a reading list, or pointers to further literature; I want the reasoning built into the questions the committee asks. If you judge that one of my three stated confusions is not a real hazard, or that a fourth matters more than any of them, say so and build the procedure accordingly rather than conforming it to my specification.
