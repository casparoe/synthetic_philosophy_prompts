ok so i just spent three hours arguing with my roommate about whether regions should be able to just leave countries, and he kept reaching for the same analogy: secession is divorce. no-fault divorce specifically. his whole pitch was — we used to make a spouse prove cruelty or adultery to get out of a marriage, everyone now agrees that was grotesque, the reform was to let someone leave without proving cause and without the other party agreeing, and by exactly the same logic a region shouldn't have to prove oppression to leave a state. a simple majority vote is enough, and demanding "just cause" or a supermajority or permission from the rest of the country is the same illiberal gatekeeping we already dumped in family law. he says if your ok with no-fault divorce you owe him no-fault secession, and that the Scotland/Catalonia type cases are basically people being told they need their spouse's signature to leave a marriage.

i pushed back all night and got somewhere but couldn't articulate it cleanly. can you do the audit for me? i want the correspondence laid out explicitly, table is fine: what's the marriage, who are the two spouses (this is where i think it already gets weird, because there aren't two of them), what are the vows, what exactly is the no-fault reform, what plays the role of the custody arrangement, what's the house and the debt, who are the kids. then where the mapping breaks — and be picky about which breaks actually matter for HIS use of it versus which are decorative. i don't care that a state isn't literally a person. i do care about the fact that after a divorce neither ex ends up with legal authority over the other, whereas in a secession the new state gets jurisdiction over everybody inside the borders, including the big minority who voted to stay and now has no exit of their own unless they get to secede from the secession. is that the load-bearing disanalogy or is there a bigger one i missed?

also, he was extremely confident that no-fault divorce is simply unilateral — one person files, marriage over. i think that's not right, that some no-fault regimes made you sit through a separation period or needed both signatures, and the genuinely unilateral version is a subset of the category. i couldn't nail it down and he wouldn't concede. does that detail matter to his argument or is it a nitpick i'm hiding behind? if you're not sure about it say so rather than inventing a jurisdiction.

and then the deeper thing i want you to weigh. the analogy only works if the right comparison class is "two people who made each other a promise." but most members of a state never consented, new ones get born into it, and the borders decide who counts as inside — so maybe divorce isn't the closest thing at all. what IS the better analogy for unilateral secession, if there is one? he had a second one in his back pocket too: he said letting regions leave makes the remaining union better, the same way having the option to divorce made people invest more in marriage, not less. does that one hold, or does it invert, given that in the divorce case the "investment" is being made by two people who can each walk and in the state case the ones making the investment aren't the ones who get to vote on the exit?

end with a verdict: does his conclusion — a unilateral right to secede on a bare majority — survive the audit, and if it survives partly, say exactly which part the divorce analogy was actually carrying and which part he needs a totally different argument for.
