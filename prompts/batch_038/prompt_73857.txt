I edit a small quarterly that publishes short fiction built around formal puzzles — recent issues have carried a story about the surprise examination, one about a predictor that is reliable only 90 percent of the time, and one about a man who cannot stop acquiring self-locating information. For the next issue I would like a story built on the updating puzzle that van Fraassen called the Judy Benjamin problem, and I would rather state the puzzle precisely here than leave you to reconstruct it, because I want the story to get the structure right and not merely the atmosphere. As I have it: an agent begins with credence one-half in some proposition A, and her credence in a further proposition B is also one-half, the two being probabilistically independent, so that the conditional probability of B given A is one-half as well. A trusted informant then tells her that the conditional probability of B given A is three-quarters. She learns nothing categorical, and nothing at all bearing on whether A. And yet the treatments I have seen — the odds-form generalization of Jeffrey conditionalization, and a maximum-entropy treatment of the same constraint — both leave her credence in A at one-half, leave the conditional probability of B given not-A at one-half, and so move her unconditional credence in B from one-half to five-eighths. Other philosophers object: some argue that a report which does not bear on B cannot rationally move her credence in B, and some argue that the report should in fact move her credence in A, since the informant chose to speak about A at all. I want the story to make that disagreement felt from the inside rather than to announce it.

Here is the seed I would like you to work from, though you may adjust the furniture. Set it in a small tidal port. A woman is waiting to learn whether her brother is on an inbound ship; whether the ship comes in by the inner channel or the outer roads depends on the tide, and she is evenly balanced between the two, as she is evenly balanced on whether he is aboard at all. In the town there is a licensed oracle, and this oracle never asserts anything: it sells conditionals. Build the story around the detail that the oracle prices its pronouncements according to how uncertain the customer is about the antecedent, because a conditional whose antecedent is a coin flip does the most work on a customer's unconditional beliefs — it can send her to the quay, or keep her at home, without ever telling her anything that could be false. Let the woman discover, over two or three consultations, that she has been steered entirely by reports of this kind, and that she cannot tell whether she has been informed or merely moved. If you want a second strand, add an underwriter or a harbor master who has to settle a wager between two clients that turns on which updating rule the contract is read to oblige them to use, and let that hearing be the climax.

For the execution: somewhere between 1,500 and 2,500 words, in close third person or first person, restrained and slightly bureaucratic in tone rather than dreamlike. The arithmetic must be present but never delivered as a lecture — put it in betting slips, tide tables, ledgers, the oracle's fee schedule, an argument overheard at a counter; at most one computation should be stated outright, and if you state the move from one-half to five-eighths, state it correctly and only once. Give A and B real weight in her life so that the reader feels the strangeness of her being moved by information that, strictly speaking, told her nothing about the thing she cares about. Do not name any philosopher, paper, updating rule by its technical label, film, or textbook example, and do not include citations, a bibliography, or a closing note explaining the puzzle; the story has to carry the idea by itself. End with the rival ways of taking the informant's report still live and unresolved, and with her doing something — going to the quay, or not going — that does not quite match the number she has arrived at.
