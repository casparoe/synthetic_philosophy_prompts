I am drafting the specification section of a project on what I will call retrospective confirmation: the increase in support a hypothesis receives from a body of evidence that was already in hand, already published, and often already believed, before the hypothesis existed or before anyone saw the connection. The stock illustration is an orbital anomaly tabulated for decades and then accounted for exactly by a new theory of gravitation. Two proposals will land on my desk within the month — one from a colleague working squarely inside the Bayesian calculus, one from a collaborator who wants to operationalize the notion by measuring confidence changes in a trained model that encountered both the theory and the data during training and now reports higher confidence in the theory when the connection is pointed out to it. I want the requirements fixed before I read either, so that the argument in the meeting is about whether a requirement is satisfied rather than about whether it belonged on the list.

Produce the requirements as a numbered list. For each entry give the requirement itself, stated so that it is checkable rather than aspirational; a concrete case or consideration that motivates it — a scenario, not a slogan; and a verdict on whether it is non-negotiable for anything deserving to be called an account of confirmation by old evidence, or whether it is a demand belonging to one camp that could be dropped without the enterprise collapsing. Where a demand that is routinely made in this area strikes you as a prejudice wearing the clothes of a requirement, say so and say whose prejudice it is.

The list should certainly engage with the following, though I do not want it confined to them:

- The arithmetic obstruction: if the evidence is already assigned probability one, conditionalizing on it cannot move the probability of anything, and the phenomenon to be explained is precisely a movement. Whatever else an account does, it must say what becomes of this.
- Oldness is not a switch. Evidence can be known to certainty, known within a margin, recorded in a dataset the agent holds but has never queried, dimly recalled, misremembered, or forgotten outright. Say what the account owes us across that range, and in particular whether the confirmation verdict is permitted to turn on how the agent happened to represent the evidence rather than on the evidence itself.
- Old evidence can turn out to have been wrong. The tabulated anomaly gets revised, the measurement is superseded by a better instrument, and the credit already assigned must be withdrawn. An account that lets a boost survive the refutation of the thing that earned it fails.
- No repeat milking. Pointing out the same connection twice, or pointing it out in two notations, must not yield two increments of support.
- Whose confirmation is it? Decide, as a requirement, whether the increase is a feature of an agent's epistemic situation at a time or a timeless feature of the relation between hypothesis and evidence, and note that the choice is not cosmetic: it determines whether my collaborator's measurement is measuring the same thing my colleague's theory is theorizing about.
- The neighbouring problem of genuinely new hypotheses, which an agent who never entertained them could not have assigned positive prior probability at all, and which therefore threatens to make the old-evidence case the easy one.
- The trained-model case: what could "already knew" mean for a system whose exposure to the evidence consists in gradient updates over a corpus rather than in an assignment of credence to a proposition? State whether an account is required to cover such a system, and if so, what it must say about it.

Then the part I most want. Identify any pair, or larger subset, of the requirements that you believe cannot be jointly satisfied, and demonstrate the conflict rather than assert it. If the honest conclusion is that the conjunction of "purely probabilistic machinery," "a real increase in the agent's credence," and "no sensitivity to arbitrary features of how the evidence is represented" is unsatisfiable, show why and say what must be added or abandoned. Also tell me which single requirement does the most exclusionary work — which one, taken seriously, eliminates the largest number of otherwise attractive candidates.

Close with a short scorecard, a paragraph each, on the four moves that dominate the field: giving up logical omniscience so that learning the connection between hypothesis and evidence is itself new information to conditionalize on; excising the evidence and evaluating confirmation relative to the resulting counterfactual credence function; refusing probability one to known evidence in the first place and updating by kinematics instead; and the deflationary insistence that confirmation is a timeless relation and that the agent's change of mind is beside the point. Score each against the list, not against the others, and keep it brief — the weight belongs on the requirements.

Two notes on presentation. Give a confidence level for the load-bearing claims, especially the impossibility claims and every non-negotiability verdict, since those are what will decide the meeting; I would rather have a hedged claim I can act on than an unhedged one I have to distrust. And single out the one point in the whole list you are least sure of, and say what evidence or argument would settle it.
