ok, I want someone to actually push back on me here instead of nodding along. I've got no academic training in any of this, I just read and think about it on my own, so please tell me when I'm saying something naive.

The view I've landed on: the big outcomes in history are basically inevitable. Not the details — I'm not claiming I could have predicted who won which battle or which treaty got signed — but the broad shape. Industrialisation happening where and roughly when it did, the colonial empires, the two world wars, the networked computers we have now. The slow stuff does the work: what the technology allows, how many people there are and what they need, who has the capital, who has the guns. The individual people are just whoever happened to be standing in the right slot when the slot opened. There's that old line about Cleopatra's nose, from Pascal I think, about how if it had been shorter the whole face of the earth would have changed — my view is the exact opposite of that. It would have changed some faces and not the shape of anything.

Two things that made me more confident. One is that study in economic history where they tried to work out what the US economy would have looked like with no railroads, and the answer came out as "not much different," because canals, rivers and better waggon roads could have carried most of it. That felt like the general principle in miniature: pull one thing out and the structure routes around it. The other is the argument in biology about whether evolution keeps rediscovering the same solutions — same body plans, same tricks, turning up again and again in unrelated lineages — which some people read as evidence that life was heading somewhere. That's the closest thing I know of to an actual repeat experiment on inevitability, and history doesn't get one.

So: what would count against my view? I want the real defeaters, ranked, from "this one kills it" down to "uncomfortable but I survive it." Specifically I'd like you to cover:

- Are there particular counterfactuals that people who study this take seriously as genuinely outcome-flipping rather than just reshuffling the details? Name the strongest candidates and say why they're strong.
- My view can always retreat to timing: fine, it would have happened, just fifty years later, or somewhere else, or in a different form. How much of a retreat is that, and at what point does "inevitable" stop being a claim about the world?
- When you imagine a change, how much else are you allowed to keep the same? If I say no First World War, do I get to also keep the same alliances, the same arms spending, the same economy, or does that break the counterfactual? Someone once mentioned to me a rule that a what-if should count as the smallest possible rewrite of what actually happened — I want to know whether that rule saves my position or just makes it impossible to test.
- My own made-up test: if someone can name one small plausible change — a decision, a death, a storm — that flips the broad outcome rather than delaying it, then inevitability is false for that outcome. Is that even a test? I worry it's measuring how well read and how imaginative the person proposing the change is, not anything about the world. If it's broken, can it be fixed, and how?
- Whether history is just stuck being a single case with no counterfactual evidence available in principle, and if so whether that makes my view untestable in a way that should bother me or in a way that's fine.

And tell me which of these a committed structuralist would just shrug at and which they'd actually have to answer. I don't want a summary of both sides being reasonable either — please separate what's genuinely settled among people who work on this from what's still actively argued about, and don't let a contested position come across as the consensus, in either direction. I'd rather find out my view is a minority one held by a handful of people than be told it's the standard take when it isn't.
