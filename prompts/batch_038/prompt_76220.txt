I'm giving a 25 minute talk at our company's internal engineering conference in about three weeks and I want to get the Q&A prep done early because I know its going to be rough.

My thesis: we should adopt a standing rule that any change which removes behaviour — deleting a branch that looks dead, retiring a feature flag nobody owns, dropping a retry, removing a weird special case for one legacy customer — has to come with a stated account of why the thing is there, and that if nobody can produce one, the default answer is no. Not "go ask around first," actually no. Two supports. (a) The Burke-ish one: the fact that a thing survived three reorgs and two rewrites is itself weak evidence in its favor, enough to move the burden onto whoever wants it gone — sort of Chesterton's fence turned into a code review policy. (b) An epistemic one: a codebase is basically an archive where most of the creators are unavailable for questioning, so the artifact is the only testimony we're ever going to get, and deleting it should be treated like destroying a source you can't reconstruct. I restrict (b) to the genuinely irreversible stuff — data migrations, decommissioned services, dropped logs, queue and storage schemas — because "just revert it in git" kills the argument for everything else, and I say so out loud in the talk rather than let someone spring it on me.

Audience: about 60 people, mostly staff and senior engineers from platform and SRE, one appsec engineer, two directors whose main worry is delivery speed, and a guy with a distributed systems PhD who will go after the epistemology instead of the policy. As a room they think dead code is a liability that compounds, that the cure for lost reasons is docs and tests rather than preservation, and that a rule nobody can enforce with tooling isn't a rule.

Give me the prep sheet: the objections this room will actually raise, roughly in the order they'll come, phrased the way they'd phrase them (please, no philosophy vocabulary in their mouths), how much damage each does, a reply I can deliver in about a minute, and a note on where that reply is soft. Tell me what to concede in the first five minutes to blunt the worst of it, which single reply has to land for the proposal to survive the hour, and the question I'm quietly hoping nobody asks. And separately: where does the fence analogy actually break? My suspicion is that most of the weird old code in there was never built by anyone for a reason at all — it accreted through merges and abandoned experiments — so there's no purpose to recover, only a cause. I'd rather hear that from you now than from the appsec guy on a microphone.
