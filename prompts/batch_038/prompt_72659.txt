I hold a package of views about rational choice under risk that I would like stress-tested rather than defended. It has four connected components.

**First, the normative core.** Rank-weighted, or risk-sensitive, expected utility is the correct account of choice under risk. An agent's attitude to risk is a rationally permissible parameter in its own right, alongside her credences and her utilities, so that maximizing expected utility is not demanded of a rational agent. The familiar pattern in which the same people take the sure thing in one pairing of gambles and the long shot in another, with the probabilities and payoffs arranged so that the two choices are inconsistent with expected utility, is not a mistake to be explained away.

**Second, an interpretive claim.** This risk attitude is irreducible. It cannot be eliminated by enriching the outcome space — by attaching utility to distributions of payoffs, or to final wealth states, or to whatever else — in such a way that the agent turns out to be an ordinary expected utility maximizer after all. If such a redescription is always available, my view is a notational variant rather than a rival, and I want to be told so.

**Third, and this is the part I have worked out least: a reading of what the weighting function measures.** It is not a free-standing taste. On my view a concave weighting tracks *non-fungibility*: a loss outweighs an equally sized gain not because of diminishing marginal utility of money, but because some losses destroy option-sets and cannot be offset by the rest of the agent's portfolio and prospects, while others are perfectly recoverable. Rational risk aversion should therefore scale with the degree to which the downside of the particular gamble is non-recoverable, and should attenuate toward risk neutrality where payoffs are genuinely fungible and restorable.

**Fourth, a locality clause.** The weighting applies to the decision problem as the agent faces it. A package of two simultaneous independent gambles is one problem with one rank-ordering of outcomes, not two problems solved in parallel; and which problems an agent faces is a matter of fact, not of how she describes her situation. Locality is meant to follow from non-fungibility — losing on both gambles at once can be non-recoverable when losing on one is not — but I am not confident the entailment holds.

To fix the discussion concretely: take a coin flip that gains 110 or loses 100, with utility linear in money across the relevant range. Under rank weighting, accepting requires the weight on the upper outcome to exceed 10/21, so every concave weighting function rejects this positive-expectation gamble. My view is then committed to two things: that this small-stakes refusal does not explode into large-stakes paralysis — the same agent who declines that flip should still accept, say, an even chance of losing 600 against gaining 10,000 — and to a specific pattern across package sizes, refusing a few copies of the small gamble offered jointly and accepting sufficiently many.

What I want is a map of the exposure of this package. Please address:

1. The considerations that would count against it — formal results, constructive counterexamples, empirical patterns, interpretive collapses — ranked from fatal to merely awkward, with an explicit statement of which is which and why.
2. For each, what a sophisticated defender of the view would herself concede as counting against her, as distinct from what she would dismiss, and what the dismissal costs her.
3. At minimum: whether rank-dependent weighting can license an act that is dominated state by state, and whether giving up dominance is available at that price; whether the view survives sequential choice, that is, whether an agent who knows her own future choices can be pumped, and whether resolute or sophisticated choice rescues it or merely relocates the difficulty; whether the irreducibility claim can be defended at all against the redescription strategy, and what remains if it cannot; whether non-fungibility belongs in the weighting function rather than in the utility function over final states, so that my third component quietly reabsorbs the second; whether the two commitments just stated are in fact derivable, for which weighting functions, and what their failure would show; whether any finite body of preferences underdetermines the functional form of the weighting; and whether the view extends to collective decisions at all, given that individual risk attitudes need not aggregate coherently.
4. What the position predicts that its rivals do not — orthodox expected utility theory, ambiguity-aversion accounts, and accounts on which the same preference pattern reflects a distortion in how probabilities are weighted rather than an attitude to risk — so that I can see precisely where it is exposed.
5. An honest verdict on whether the package, particularly with the locality clause added, has been qualified into unfalsifiability; and if so, whether that is a defect in a normative theory of this kind or only in what I have claimed about its empirical content.

Work from analysis and construction; I am not asking for a survey of who has argued what. The single thing that would move me most is a construction rather than a complaint: a fully specified finite decision problem in which the four components cannot all be honored at once. If you can build one, build it and show the arithmetic. If you cannot, say so plainly and tell me what that absence does and does not establish.
