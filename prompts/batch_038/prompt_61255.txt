I'm trying to settle a running argument with a friend and I want an actual essay on it, not a both-sides shrug.

The background is the usual one: are religious reasons admissible in democratic politics? Rawls and his proviso, Audi's principle of secular rationale, Habermas wanting translation to happen before anything crosses into parliaments and courts, Wolterstorff and Eberle saying all this asks religious citizens to lobotomize themselves, the convergence people saying the whole consensus picture is the wrong model anyway. You know the terrain better than I do, I'm just setting the stage.

My friend has proposed a test he thinks cuts through the mess, and I can't decide whether its clever or confused. Roughly: take any citizen who backs some law on partly religious grounds and ask a counterfactual — would she still back the law, with the same conviction, if she stopped believing the religious premise? If yes, then the law is publicly justified by whatever secular reasons survive the withdrawal and she hasn't violated any duty of civility. If no, the religious premise was load-bearing and the law fails public justification. Call it the withdrawal test. He likes it because its supposed to be a bright line you can apply case by case without ever having to settle what counts as a "secular" reason in the first place, and because it puts the burden on the believer's own commitments rather than on the vocabulary she happened to use in an op-ed.

Here's what I already suspect is wrong with it, and I want you to either develop these or tell me I'm wrong:

- It seems to make justification turn on a psychological fact about one particular person rather than on the reasons that are actually available to anybody, which feels like a category error.
- The counterfactual might not have a determinate answer. Beliefs come in webs; you can't just subtract "God exists" and hold everything else fixed, because half her other beliefs get rearranged in the process.
- The case I keep throwing at him and he can't handle: the restrained believer. A pacifist, or a Catholic death penalty opponent, whose faith actually moderates him. Withdraw the religious premise and the secular residue that's left supports something *harsher* than the law he actually backs. On his test that harsher thing is the publicly justified one, which looks straight-up absurd.
- Does the test even apply to legislators and judges, or only to voters? Habermas's whole asymmetry hangs on that and my friend keeps changing his answer.

So: write me an essay in American English that works through the withdrawal test and tells me flatly whether it should be adopted, patched, or thrown out. Run it on at least one real controversy of your choosing — vaccine exemptions, a same-sex marriage ban, the religious rhetoric of the civil rights movement, a blasphemy or religious-display case, whatever genuinely stresses the test — and report what comes out the other end, including if what comes out is unhelpful.

Use these headings, in this order, with roughly these lengths:

- The test, stated as charitably as you can — about 200 words
- What it gets right — about 300 words
- Where it breaks — about 450 words, and this is the section I care most about
- How it compares to the existing options — about 350 words, positioning it against Rawls, Audi, Habermas, the conscientious engagement crowd, and the convergence view
- Verdict — about 200 words. Adopt, patch, or toss. If patch, say what the patch concretely is, not just "it needs refinement."

No bibliography, no reference list, no suggested further reading — I can't check any of it and I don't want it. And don't end on a hedge. I want a position I can argue with.
