So my department's little podcast is doing a one-view-one-defender episode, and next week's guest is a political philosopher in the republican camp, but with a twist I think makes them way more interesting than the standard Pettit-style line. Thier twist: they claim domination is nothing over and above a probabilistic structure of possible interferences. Roughly — you're dominated to the degree that, across scenarios weighted by how likely they are, some other agent could interfere with your choices cheaply and at little cost to themselves; on their account arbitrariness isn't a separate normative notion at all, it's a placeholder for that likelihood-and-cost structure. So domination collapses into a sophisticated, modalized version of non-interference, and they're happy to say so out loud.

I want nine or ten interview questions that actually engage the reduction instead of letting them give the usual tour of the republican tradition and its critics. Please make sure there's a question on each of these:

(a) the kind-master / contented-tenant case — someone whose powerful other has never interfered and has every standing incentive not to, so on their expected-interference math they come out looking nearly free, while the republican intuition says they're dominated. Does the reduction bite the bullet, and if so what does it lose?
(b) whether the phenomenology the tradition leans on — being able to deal with others without fear or deference, not having to ingratiate yourself — gets explained by their account or just re-described in new vocabulary.
(c) how they can still hold that law in a well-ordered democracy doesn't reduce freedom, given that law is interference on anyone's description. Do they need a normative primitive sneaking back in exactly where they claimed not to need one?
(d) whether the aggregation over scenarios is expected-value or worst-case, and what actually turns on that choice — including whether a worst-case reading is still a reduction of anything.
(e) the redundancy worry: if the reduction succeeds, was the third concept of liberty ever a distinct concept, or has this whole debate been verbal all along?

Their go-to deflection on (a) and (e) is: arbitrariness just IS the likelihood-and-cost structure, so we aren't disagreeing with the negative libertarians, we're describing the same thing at different levels of abstraction. Build the sequence so that move gets progressively harder to pull off, and close with one question about what would count as evidence or argument against the reduction — a concrete case or finding that would actually make them revise, not a polite shrug.

For each question, add a short follow-up keyed to the answer they're most likely to give, written as if they say X, push with Y. And flag which two of the questions you think are load-bearing, with a sentence on why.

One more thing: I have a specific case I want used wherever it fits best. A tenant on a month-to-month lease where the landlord can raise the rent or end the tenancy on thirty days' notice, who has in fact never once been hassled; versus a tenant with a five-year fixed lease at the same monthly rent. The first clearly has more power held over them, but the two have nearly identical expected interference. Ask them whether those two are differently free, and if so whether the difference is big enough to matter for policy.

No biographical questions please — nothing about what drew them to political philosophy or who they studied with.
