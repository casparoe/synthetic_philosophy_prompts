ok so I've been going in circles on the Born rule problem in many-worlds and I want to know whether I'm missing something or whether the literature is just overwrought.

My sense is that it's been solved like three times over: Everett's own relative-frequency argument, the Deutsch–Wallace decision-theoretic one, and Zurek's envariance. And honestly I don't think you need any of that machinery, because there's a shorter route. Here's my sketch: let the "weight" of a branch be some function of its amplitude that is (i) continuous, (ii) additive across orthogonal outcome branches, and (iii) invariant under relabelings of the basis, so that two decompositions which are physically indistinguishable get the same weights. Those three, plus Gleason-style reasoning, pin down amplitude-squared, and none of them are claims about probability or belief or rationality — theyre just structural facts about the state, which decoherence has already handed you. So the derivation isn't assuming what it's trying to prove.

Separately, I take the "but what would probability even mean when every outcome happens" line to be philosophers fussing over a word. Once you've got a well-defined measure over branches, telling an agent to bet in proportion to it is just ordinary decision theory applied to a measure, and nobody thinks decision theory needs a metaphysics of chance first.

So, concretely: is my sketch a derivation, or does it just move the problem somewhere else — and if somewhere else, where exactly, and what is the premise doing the work that I think is innocent? And is there any form of the incoherence complaint that survives it (branch counting, the fission analogy, whatever you think is strongest), or would I be right to drop the whole thing and spend my thesis chapter on something else? Not after a survey of the debate — I want the load-bearing parts.
