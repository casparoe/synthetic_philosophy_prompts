I am in my final year of high school and am writing a research paper for my modern European history course on the origins of the First World War. Nearly every source I have consulted — including the textbook the course is built on — describes Europe in the summer of 1914 as a powder keg, with the assassination at Sarajevo as the spark that set it off. My teacher uses the same image, but he makes it do more work than the textbook does: he argues that anyone asking what caused the war has asked the wrong question, since the keg rather than the spark did the work, and that it follows that responsibility cannot sensibly be assigned to the assassins, to the Austrian ultimatum, or to any individual decision-maker. I want to push back on that in my paper, but I would rather do it precisely than just announce that the metaphor is unhelpful, so I need to know exactly how much weight it can bear.

First, could you set out the correspondence explicitly? I want to know what plays the part of the explosive material, what plays the part of the container, what plays the part of the spark, and what plays the part of the single mechanism by which ignition spreads through the whole assembly. One thing I have not been able to resolve on my own: the alliance system and the mobilization timetables are sometimes presented as the containment that was supposed to keep the peace and sometimes as part of the fuel, and I cannot tell whether the metaphor allows the same item to occupy both roles or whether that is a sign the mapping has come apart.

Second, please separate the features a keg really has that 1914 Europe shares from the ones it lacks. A keg's contents do not know they are a keg, do not rearrange themselves once they suspect ignition is possible, do not become drier after one spark is successfully smothered, and do not sometimes fail to go off when ignited. My suspicion is that my teacher's conclusion about responsibility depends on the keg's indifference rather than on the accumulated-fuel part of the image, and I would like to know whether that suspicion is right and how to argue for it.

Third, tell me what the metaphor licenses that its users may not intend. Does describing Europe as a powder keg already commit one to war's being unavoidable, to any spark's having done as well as the one that happened, and to nobody's being properly blameworthy? My textbook devotes its next chapter to several points in July 1914 at which the crisis might have been defused, and I cannot see how that chapter and the keg image can both be true.

Fourth, weigh it against the other image people reach for with the same events — that Europe sleepwalked into war, or that the great powers were poker players who kept raising without any one of them deciding to start a fight. The keg makes the outcome look physically necessitated and the second makes it look like an accident nobody chose; say which, if either, suits a case in which many conditions were jointly required and none was sufficient by itself.

Finally, if neither survives, propose a better image for a historical event with that structure. It would need to preserve my teacher's legitimate point — that the assassination is not the explanation anyone is looking for — without quietly turning "there were many causes" into "the outcome had to happen." I would also like to know what such an image has to give up in order to manage that.
