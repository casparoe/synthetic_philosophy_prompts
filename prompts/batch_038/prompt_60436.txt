so — i write novels for a living but this ones real. My oldest friend, a cosmologist, died in March and I'm the one giving the eulogy on Saturday. His whole career was basically a single argument: if the universe is the kind of thing that can randomly fluctuate a lone disembodied brain with a whole lifetime of false memories into being, then there are unimaginably more of those brains than there are people like us, so any given observer ought to bet on being one — and a cosmology that makes you expect that is thereby suspect. He found it funny rather than frightening, and he would absolutely hate a sentimental send-off.

The room: his wife and two grown kids, a chunk of his department who know the argument cold, and a lot of neighbours who've never heard of any of it and mostly want to be told he's at peace somewhere. Call it five minutes. Write the eulogy.

The hard part is that it has to actually run on the argument rather than just name-drop it, and it can't lean on either of the two things eulogies always lean on. He can't be "watching over us" — he'd have called that a category error and said so out loud. And it can't rest on "we'll always remember him," because a fondly remembered him is exactly what a fluctuated brain would be hallucinating; if the memories are the proof, the proof is worthless for him and worthless for the brain alike. So I want it to arrive at something in the love that would survive even if the memories turned out not to be veridical — the part you'd still be right to feel in the middle of a fluctuation — without ever saying the term to the neighbours or sliding into a lecture. Grief is fine, false comfort isn't. Make the physicists feel like he'd have approved, and make his kids cry for the right reasons.
