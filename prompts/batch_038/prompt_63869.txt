I am revising the framing section of a dissertation chapter that compares the Bengal famine of 1943 with the Great Leap Forward famine of 1959–61, with a shorter excursus on Ireland in the 1840s. At a work-in-progress seminar last week, my central comparative question — how far the 1943 mortality is attributable to the 1942 cyclone and rice crop disease and to the loss of Burmese rice imports, as against the boat and rice denial policies, the priority scheme that rationed grain to war workers and officials, and the refusal to divert shipping to Indian ports — was cut short by a colleague. He recited Sen's line from Development as Freedom, "No famine has ever taken place in the history of the world in a functioning democracy," and drew two conclusions from it: first, that a famine is never a natural disaster but always a political crime; second, that my weather-versus-policy question about Bengal is therefore beside the point, since a colonial administration with no electorate covering the starving population was not a functioning democracy and the outcome was settled in advance.

I suspect both moves are too quick, but I cannot yet say precisely why, and my introduction depends on saying it. Please lay out, as a numbered menu, the distinct propositions this one sentence can be read as asserting. For each, give: (a) its precise formulation, making the quantifier scope explicit, along with the threshold for what counts as a famine, the criterion for what counts as a democracy, and what work the qualifier "functioning" is doing; (b) a verdict — platitude, well-supported empirical generalization, doubtful generalization, false, or unfalsifiable by construction; (c) what would count as evidence for or against it, and whether the hard cases genuinely threaten it or merely invite a redescription of the case (Ireland inside the United Kingdom after the Union; Bengal, where the affected population was largely outside the franchise and the colonial state was operating under wartime emergency powers; and whether any post-1947 democracy has starved on a famine scale); and (d) whether that reading, and that reading alone, would license either of my colleague's two conclusions.

I want the causal and the normative readings kept separate. The mechanism claim about information flows and electoral incentives is not the same proposition as the claim that famines are attributable to political arrangements rather than to harvests, and neither is the same as the claim that they are crimes, which requires something about culpability that a correlation across regime types cannot supply. Please also tell me where the slogan runs out — what it says nothing about at all, chronic undernutrition and stunting in democracies being an obvious candidate — since I would rather not hang a chapter on more weight than it will bear. Analysis only: I am not looking for a bibliography.
