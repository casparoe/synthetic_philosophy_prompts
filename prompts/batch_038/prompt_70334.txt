Second year PhD, working on Hume's aesthetics, and I've noticed "beauty is in the eye of the beholder" doing conversation-stopping work in two very different places in my life right now.

One is my seminar: last week two people deadlocked over whether a technically flawless but (imo) completely inert contemporary painting belonged on a prize shortlist, and the convenor ended it with the slogan plus a bit of de gustibus non est disputandum hand-waving, then we moved to a vote. The other is my own chapter, where I have to square Hume's remark early in the essay on the standard of taste — to the effect that beauty is no quality in things themselves and each mind perceives a different beauty, so everyone should just acquiesce in their own sentiment — with everything that comes after it: the ideal critic, the list of qualifications (strong sense, delicate sentiment, practice, comparison, freedom from prejudice), and that wine-tasting story he takes from Cervantes where the two tasters who claim to detect iron and leather are vindicated by the old key with a leathern thong found at the bottom of the emptied hogshead.

Can you pull the slogan apart into the seperate precise claims someone could be making with it? I'm picturing something like: a metaphysical thesis about what kind of property beauty is (response-dependent? not a property at all?); an error theory on which aesthetic judgments aren't truth-apt; relativism indexed to the individual versus indexed to a community, tradition, or historical moment; bare epistemic humility about who's right; a norm of non-interference or politeness that isn't a claim about truth at all; maybe something about there being no reasons or only rhetoric in criticism. For each, tell me whether it's close to trivially true, substantive and arguable, or just false — and, crucially, which of them would actually license what the convenor did, i.e. dissolving a substantive disagreement about a particular work rather than merely declining to referee it. Flag any readings that turn out to be the same claim in different clothes.

Then the wrinkle I can't get straight: on the response-dependent readings, if I found a novelist dazzling at 19 and find her kitsch now, was the 19-year-old judgment false, or was it true then and mine is true now? Which readings of the slogan even allow that question to have an answer, and does Hume's ideal-critic machinery have to take a side on it? I'd much rather have a menu I can react to and argue with than a tidy essay defending one reading, tho do say at the end which reading you think people who repeat the slogan in arguments usually mean, and whether Hume himself could consistently mean it.
