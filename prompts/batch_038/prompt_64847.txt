ok so I'm running film night for our department's philosophy-and-medicine group and we've landed on Memento for the session on decision-making for people who can no longer make decisions. everyone's seen it, so i dont need a plot recap — what I need is an actual critical reading I can hand out, not a summary of the twist.

the thing I've been chewing on: the whole advance-directive debate (Dworkin's precedent autonomy on one side, Dresser's "the demented patient is someone else now, and her experience is all that's left" on the other) assumes the earlier self and the later self are separated by years of slow decline, and that the directive is a sincere attempt to project the earlier self's values forward. Leonard shreds every one of those assumptions. He's separated from his future selves by minutes; his directives — the tattoos, the notes, the polaroid scribbles — get re-read constantly by a self who has all the evidence and none of the memory of writing them; and at the end he knowingly authors a false one. he isnt extending his values into the future at all, he's rigging his future self from the inside, and the film makes you watch him do it and then shows you the effect landing. Add Sammy Jankis, who's basically a case study in a directive that is true but unusable, and the fact that Leonard tells the story as if it's about somebody else when the film keeps hinting it isn't.

here's a distinction I've been playing with that I want you to use, and to push on hard if you think it's bad. Call the first question whether a directive has authority — does the earlier self get to bind the later one? — and the second whether it has executability: can the later self actually carry it out, given what she remembers, understands, and cares about at the moment of execution? Almost all the literature argues about authority and almost none about executability, and Leonard looks to me like a man with catastrophic executability problems and no authority at all, because there's no continuous self there to be authoritative over anything. Read the film through that lens and tell me what it contributes to the debate that the papers can't. In particular I want you to take seriously whether the reverse chronology is doing philosophical work rather than being a gimmick: when you watch a directive get *made* it looks like autonomy, and when you only ever watch it get *obeyed* — which is what the colour segments force on you — the same act looks like coercion by a stranger, and I suspect that asymmetry is an argument. Then tell me where the film resists the reading, and where it's cheating: Leonard's condition is a dial turned to an absurd setting, and I don't want anything that quietly assumes real dementia patients are like him.
