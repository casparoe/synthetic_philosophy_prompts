I am a fourth-year PhD student in philosophy, and each spring our graduate student association prints a small anthology to hand out at the department's open house; the standing brief is that every piece must be funny to philosophers and still legible to the parents and prospective applicants who wander in. My contribution this year is on material constitution, which is also the subject of my dissertation prospectus, and I have a conceit I would like help executing because I keep losing the thread of it in my own drafts. The piece is an internal claims memorandum, at most 700 words, from the fine-art unit of a specialty insurer — call it Vellum & Chace Fine Art Underwriting — written by a senior adjuster to the underwriting committee. The facts: a mid-career sculptor holds a policy with two schedules, Schedule A covering "materials and stock in hand" and Schedule B covering "the finished work." The work, a life-sized figure in unfired clay titled *Study for a Standing Woman*, was flattened when a studio shelf gave way. Before the adjuster arrived, a conservator acting on the sculptor's instructions gathered the debris, re-wedged it, and threw a small vase from the same clay; the vase sits undamaged on the studio table at the time of inspection. There is also a third claimant: a studio assistant who separately insured the figure's left arm as a work in his own right, on the grounds that it was a distinct object all along. The memo must recommend a ruling.

I want the humor to come entirely from getting the metaphysics right rather than from wackiness, so the analysis should turn on real pressure points: the sculptor's argument that the lump and the work cannot be one thing, since they differ in whether they could have survived being flattened and re-wedged, and so the policy owes on both schedules; the rival thought that when two sortals compete for a single object one of them dominates and the loser was never there to be insured at all; the assistant's claim, which drags a familiar puzzle about undetached parts into a dispute over sublimits; and the perdurantist option, on which nothing was lost because the object is spread out in time and only a temporal part was affected — an option the adjuster should take seriously and then either adopt or refuse for reasons a committee of non-philosophers can follow. Keep the house voice deadpan throughout: numbered headings, a findings section, a recommendation, and a short appendix of proposed wording for future schedules, including a clause on items occupying the same space and a clause on reconstitution by third parties. Please do not name any actual philosopher, do not cite anything, and do not let the adjuster break character to explain the joke to the lay reader; if the appendix wording quietly does that work, so much the better.

Two requests about how you handle it. First, commit. The memo has to reach a definite recommendation — pay, decline, or pay once — with stated reasons, not a survey of the competing pictures; a hedged adjuster is both weak satire and weak adjusting. Second, after the memo, add a clearly separated note of at most 150 words saying which position in the constitution debate the piece ends up endorsing, what it had to distort or leave out to get its comic effects, and where a specialist would judge it unfair to a view it caricatures. Hard limit: memo and note together no more than 850 words.
