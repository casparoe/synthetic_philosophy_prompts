I'm a backend engineer, no philosophy background beyond a couple of pop-science books, so forgive me if I describe this badly. I got into an argument with our QA lead last week and I can't shake the feeling that there's a real philosophy problem hiding inside it that I just don't have the vocabulary for. Maybe you can help me name it.

Here's the practice. Our bug tracker has a fixed list of reasons you're allowed to close an issue with, and one of them is literally "Cannot Reproduce." The norms around it are pretty hardline: if a user or another engineer files a report saying "X happened," and nobody can make X happen on demand — no repro steps, no failing test, no logs that pin it down — the issue gets closed after a couple weeks of trying, and its treated as a non-event. Not "we believe you but it's low priority," just closed, as if the report never amounted to a legitimate claim in the first place. If you want your bug taken seriously you have to produce a script or a sequence of clicks that summons it at will, repeatably, in front of witnesses. The unspoken slogan is basically "if you can't demonstrate it on demand, it isn't a real bug." My roommate is a grad student and when I described this he said something about Popper and falsifiability, but I'm fuzzy on whether that's actually the connection or he was just reaching.

The part that bugs me is that we're nowhere near this consistent. Our CI has "flaky" tests that fail maybe once in fifty runs, and the standard move is to quarantine them — mark them as known-unreliable and stop counting them — because nobody can trigger the failure deliberately. So an intermittent failure we cause gets demoted to noise. But when a single intermittent failure showed up in production last year and turned out to involve possibly dropping a write, one occurrence, never reproduced, we did an all-hands retro, treated it as definately real, and spent a month hardening the code path. And separately, when someone proposes a refactor or a new service boundary, nobody asks them for a reproducible demonstration that it'll be better; a confident plausible-sounding story in a design doc is enough, and asking for evidence would be seen as pedantic. So: on-demand demonstration is mandatory for claims coming from users, optional for claims coming from us, and actively not required for claims about the future.

Acting as a philosopher of science who's also spent time working in industry, think carefully about this and tell me: what theory of evidence — or of what makes a claim legitimate at all — does the "Cannot Reproduce" norm actually presuppose? Is it covertly falsificationist, and if so which version, or is it something else (testimony being worthless? only repeatable phenomena being real? causation requiring intervention?)? Be careful to separate what the practice strictly commits us to from what it merely happens to fit well with, because I suspect a lot of it is explainable by boring cost-benefit stuff rather than any view of knowledge. Then tell me whether the flaky-test quarantine and the one-production-incident panic are consistent with the same underlying view or whether we're holding two incompatible epistemologies at once, and what would concretely change in how a bug tracker works if we swapped the whole thing for a straightforwardly probabilistic view where a single credible report just moves a number. Lead with your bottom line — one sentence saying what the norm presupposes — and then give me the reasoning. No preamble, no "great question," and don't restate my setup back at me.
