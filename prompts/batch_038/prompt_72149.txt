For thirty-eight years I directed a small research group in natural products chemistry. Since retiring I have been auditing a Tuesday-evening philosophy circle composed mostly of other retirees, and I find that I now hear certain phrases doing work I cannot quite follow. I should like a careful disambiguation of one of them.

I first met the phrase in 1991. My group had spent two years developing a catalytic method for a transformation that was then something of an obstacle in the field, and we were perhaps five weeks from submitting when a laboratory abroad published a closely related procedure. My department chair, meaning kindly, told me that the time had simply been ripe. Last spring, at my own retirement dinner, an old colleague offered me the same sentence as a kind of benediction.

The second register is the reading group. We have lately been discussing pairs of independent discoverers — Newton and Leibniz over the calculus, Darwin and Wallace over natural selection — and several members reach for the identical idiom, "it was in the air," "the time was ripe," in support of a considerably heavier thesis: that such discoveries were inevitable, that they belonged to their moment rather than to their makers, and that the identity of the discoverer is therefore of no real philosophical interest.

My difficulty is that I do not believe these uses assert the same thing, and I suspect one of them is close to empty while another is close to false. I also have a complication of my own to offer. In 1991 the rival procedure worked only on a narrow class of substrates, and the explanation its authors gave of why it worked invoked a mechanism we now know to be mistaken; ours was general and, so far as anyone can tell, correctly explained. Whether the two laboratories made the same discovery is not obvious to me, and yet the phrase was offered as though that question had already been settled in the affirmative.

So I should like the following. Please lay out, as a menu of candidate readings rather than a single favored gloss, the distinct precise claims that "the time was ripe" could be making. For each reading, state it explicitly: what is said to have been ready, ready for whom, ready at what time, and with what modal force — mere possibility, high probability given how many qualified people were working nearby, or genuine inevitability. Then tell me for each whether it is a truism nobody would contest, a substantive empirical claim about a field that could be supported or undermined by evidence (and if so, by what sort of evidence), or something dubious or plainly false.

Finally, tell me which readings, if any, would actually support the two uses I heard the phrase put to: consoling a scientist who has just been scooped, and the stronger thesis that it makes no difference who makes a discovery. Please point out explicitly wherever an argument slides from one reading to another — for instance from "several people had what they needed" to "almost anyone would have managed it," or from "the prerequisites were in place" to "the outcome was inevitable" — and say whether any of the readings quietly depend on a prior account of when two results count as the same discovery, since that is where I suspect the phrase does its least visible work.

Please write in American English. I am not looking for a bibliography, a list of further reading, or the names and dates of the sources behind these ideas; I want the analysis itself, and I am content for it to stand on its own reasoning rather than on authority.
