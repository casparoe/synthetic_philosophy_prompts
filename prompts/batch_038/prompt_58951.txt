I am in the second term of an MA in science and technology studies, and this week's seminar is on technological determinism. Because the primary literature is rather dense, our tutor suggested we start with a chapter from a recent trade-press book on infrastructure and power, which devotes several pages to Langdon Winner's 1980 essay in Daedalus, "Do Artifacts Have Politics?". The chapter is well written and evidently confident, and I found myself nodding along until I tried to restate the argument in my own words, at which point it seemed to slip sideways. The passage runs as follows:

"Winner's essay is the classic statement of the view that artefacts have politics. His two central illustrations are historical. The overpasses Robert Moses threw across the parkways to Long Island were built to a clearance that buses could not pass under, with the effect — and, on Winner's telling, the purpose — of keeping poor and Black New Yorkers, who depended on public transport, away from Jones Beach; and McCormick installed expensive pneumatic moulding machines in his Chicago reaper works, which more than paid for themselves in output and let him break the skilled moulders' union at a stroke. The general point is that technical arrangements settle questions of power within a community, and go on settling them long after the arguments that produced them have been forgotten; once a system is in place, it dictates how the people around it must organise themselves. Winner then identifies a second and stronger category, that of inherently political technologies, which require a particular kind of social order simply in order to be run at all. Nuclear energy is his paradigm case: a reactor can only be operated by a centralised, hierarchical, closely policed organisation of experts, and the same logic extends to the electricity grid and the motorway network. Because this politics is built in at the moment of design, Winner concludes that it is generally futile to bolt democratic controls onto a large-scale technical system after the event, and that a society which values free political life must choose its technologies accordingly, favouring the small and the decentralised."

Three things puzzle me. First, both illustrations appear to be cases of human beings selecting a design in order to settle a question of power, which does not look like technology determining anything; yet the passage then moves, without marking the move, to the claim that a system in place dictates how people must organise. I would like to know whether that shift is licensed by Winner or is the chapter's own, and whether the word "require" in the account of inherently political technologies is doing more work than Winner asks of it — I have a vague sense that he distinguishes technologies that demand a certain social arrangement from those that are merely strongly compatible with one, but I cannot reconstruct why the distinction matters. Second, the McCormick detail strikes me as back to front: I would have expected the point to be that the machinery was adopted for political reasons rather than economic ones, though I have no way of checking this. Third, a friend of mine who works in transport history tells me the bus-clearance story has been seriously challenged on the archival record, and I cannot tell whether that would wound a thesis that is meant to be conceptual rather than historical, or whether it is beside the point. Could you work through the passage claim by claim and tell me what a specialist in the philosophy of technology would accept as a fair statement of Winner's position, what they would regard as a defensible simplification, and what Winner himself explicitly rejected — in particular, whether he is properly described as a technological determinist, and whether his treatment of nuclear energy really amounts to the claim that it can only be authoritarian? Please also say which of the passage's conclusions survive once it is corrected, especially the alleged futility of adding democratic controls to an existing system and the recommendation to favour small-scale technology, and how much of the argument's weight would be lost if the Moses example turned out not to stand up. I am broadly sympathetic to Winner and would like to defend him in the seminar, but I should prefer to find out now if what I would be defending is something he never said.
