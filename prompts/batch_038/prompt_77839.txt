Novelist here with a consistency problem I can't solve on my own, and I'd rather not hand-wave it in the draft. One of my two narrators chairs the designation commitee of an invented international body — the one that decides, decades afterwards, whether an atrocity gets the word "genocide" or merely something blander. Her earlier rulings are already on the page, so I can't retract them:

Vessan: written orders naming a religious minority, 40,000 of their 60,000 dead. She designated it. The Kesh: nobody killed, but every child under twelve taken to state boarding schools and the language banned in the home. She designated it. Ilmara: libraries, shrines and cemeteries burned, the language outlawed in public, no deaths and no removals. She refused, calling it cultural destruction. The Purge Years: 300,000 dead, all defined by party membership and their families, no ethnic line at all. She refused — crime against humanity, monstrous scale notwithstanding. Tanek: the state requisitions the grain, a quarter of an ethnically distinct region dies, and the files show the ministers foresaw it and carried on. She designated it, which shocked her colleagues. And a militia with a written plan to wipe out a group of 900,000 kills 800 before outside forces stop it: she refused, on the grounds that a plan is not a result.

Chapter 19 hands her a new file. Over fifteen years a state disperses a mountain people into individual flats across the country — decent housing, no violence, no sterilisation, the children still speak the language at the kitchen table — but the villages, the marriage networks and the institutions are gone, and the demographers in the file project the group won't be identifiable within two generations.

What does she rule? I want the candidate rules my six verdicts actually admit of, what each of them says about chapter 19, your best guess at hers and why you prefer it, and which further case I could write that would force the difference into the open. And if she designates it, what principle is she committing herself to that she has kept quiet about so far — would a colleague who thinks the Convention was right to drop Lemkin's cultural-genocide provisions have a clean shot at her, or is there a reading on which she's been consistent all along and merely finally said it out loud?

British English, please. And no headings, bullets, bold or tables — continuous prose, the way you'd write an email.
