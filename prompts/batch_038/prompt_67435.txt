The question was:

"Explain, in your own words, why Hume thought an increase in the quantity of money in a country could be of advantage to that country, and why he thought the advantage could not be sustained. Then explain the mechanism by which he thought the quantity of money across trading nations tends to settle at a level appropriate to each. Here's what the student wrote, copied out as it stands:

Hume's starting point is that money in itself is not wealth. What makes a state powerful is the number of people it can employ and the stock of commodities those people produce, and money is only the instrument by which one man's labour is exchanged for another's. So the quantity of gold and silver in a country does not matter as such. What matters is the proportion it bears to the industry of the country.

But an increase in the quantity of money, if it happens gradually, is on his account favourable to industry, and the reason is the uneven way the new money spreads. It does not arrive all at once in every hand. It comes first into particular hands, the merchant who has just been paid, the manufacturer with a new order, and these people spend it. They hire more labourers and buy more materials, and because the prices of labour and provisions rise only by degrees, the extra money brings previously idle hands and previously uncultivated land into work. The whole benefit lies in this interval between the arrival of the new money and the general rise of prices. Once prices have risen all the way through, the country is exactly where it started, only with more coin chasing the same quantity of goods. A sudden large addition therefore does nothing but raise prices; it is the slow and successive increase that helps.

That is also why I think Hume's argument can be read as a case for managing the money supply on purpose. Since the good effect lives in the interval, a state which increases its money slowly and steadily, at a rate people come to expect, keeps itself permanently inside the interval, and so keeps the benefit to industry permanently. What it must avoid is a large one-off addition. On this reading Hume already has the outline of the modern argument for a low but positive rate of inflation, and his quarrel with the mercantilists is not that they wanted more money but that they went about getting it the wrong way.

The self-correcting part works through foreign trade. Money cannot stay where it is out of proportion. If one country has too much, its goods become dear; foreigners stop buying them, its own people find foreign goods cheaper and buy those instead, and coin flows out until the proportion is restored. A country which has too little finds its goods cheap abroad, sells more, and draws money in. Hume makes the same point within a single country, between the capital and the provinces. The level at which things settle is not fixed by territory or population but by the art and industry of each nation: money follows industry, and a nation cannot hold money above its natural proportion any more than water can be held above its level.

On paper he is a straightforward quantity theorist. Bank notes do not create any new wealth or any new money in the relevant sense; they only substitute a cheaper instrument for a dearer one. Every note put into circulation drives out an equal amount of gold and silver, which is then sent abroad, so total purchasing power inside the country is unchanged. Paper is a way of economising on the cost of the currency, and beyond that Hume is indifferent between a paper and a metallic circulation; his real worry about banks is their instability, not their size. It follows that his mechanism is a mechanism about specie and has nothing to say about credit or exchange.

So, to the last part: no. A persistent surplus cannot permanently better a country's position, because the surplus is precisely the mechanism by which money accumulates and prices rise, and the rising prices destroy the cheapness that produced the surplus in the first place. The gain from a surplus is real but transitional, in the same way as the gain from any increase of money. The only durable advantage a country can win from trade is a larger stock of industry, and once it has that, the money will come to it of its own accord.
