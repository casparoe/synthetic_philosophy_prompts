I'm the training lead at a small charity that runs restorative justice conferences — the ones where somebody who's caused harm sits down with the person they harmed, with a facilitator in the room. Once a quarter I get the new intake of caseworkers for a seventy-minute evening session, and I've made the possibly terrible decision that this quarter's topic is Galen Strawson's Basic Argument against moral responsibility. Twenty of them, mostly social work and criminology backgrounds, one ex-probation officer who did a philosophy degree about twenty years ago and will absolutely steer us onto compatibilism within five minutes, and every single one of them will walk in thinking the session is either a trap or a licence: if nobody is ever morally responsible for anything, what exactly are we asking people to do in a conference room? I'm not after an explanation of the argument, I know it fine — I want a plan for how to teach it. British English please, and no reading list, I'm not sending twenty caseworkers home with a bibliography.

First, the order the pieces have to go in and why that order — what depends on what. My instinct is that the determinism question has to come first and be dismissed, so nobody spends the hour thinking this is the free will problem in disguise, then the step about what you do flowing from how you are, then self-causation, then the conclusion. Tell me if that's back to front. Tell me too whether the conclusion should be stated up front as a claim that then has to be earned, or held back as a reveal. Second, the carrying example. I've been toying with one of my own and I want to know whether it survives contact with the argument or quietly wrecks it: at eighteen you're handed a catalogue of adult temperaments and you get to choose, once, which person you'll be — the catch being that you have to do the choosing with the eighteen-year-old you already are, and if you're allowed to come back and choose again later, who exactly is doing the choosing then? Say what this example has to have and what it must not have to carry the weight. I can already see two ways it goes wrong — it could make self-creation look merely *difficult* rather than impossible, and it could turn the whole thing into a story about being manipulated by the catalogue people, which is a different argument entirely — but I'm sure there are others. If you think there's a better single case to build the hour on, tell me what it is and what makes it better.

Third, and this is the bit I actually care about: the misconception map. Give me the ones they'll arrive with and the ones they'll form during the session, in the order they'll surface, and the exact point in the sequence where each one has to be headed off — a lot of them can only be defused once some earlier piece is in place and I don't want to be swatting them too early and having them come back worse. I'd predict at least: "so it's just determinism again", "so we stop blaming people and let them all go", "but surely you're responsible for the bits you've worked on yourself, like that bloke in my caseload who spent four years learning not to hit people", and "the argument only works if you have to be answerable for literally everything about yourself". Add the ones I can't see. Then fold in our house line, because this is where I'm most exposed: our training handbook says what we're after in a conference is responsiveness rather than responsibility, and I want to know whether the Basic Argument is actually compatible with that or whether I'm teaching people something that guts their own practice — if it does, I'd rather say so in the room than have them work it out on the bus. Last, one question I can put to them at the end that shows whether the argument has landed rather than just made them feel let off the hook; ideally one where the predictable wrong answer is distinguishable from the right one by the *reason* they give rather than the verdict they reach. And tell me plainly what to cut and what to say when they notice the gap — I'm assuming the whole compatibilist literature (Frankfurt cases, reasons-responsiveness, the lot) and anything involving physics, but confirm that, and give me an honest one-sentence holding answer for the ex-philosophy student when he raises them at minute twelve.
