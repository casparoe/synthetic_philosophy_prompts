so I'm a junior and I run the philosophy club at my school, six people, meets in a room with bad lighting. A few weeks ago I read about the classical Indian idea that there are only a handful of basic ways you can come to know something at all — seeing, inferring, being told, recognising something by comparing it to a description, and depending on who you're reading a couple more — and I brought it up, and now the club is completely stuck and I want someone who actually knows this area to diagnose it rather than pick a side.

The fight is whether being told something is genuinely its own source of knowledge or just inference in a costume. When you believe your history teacher, aren't you inferring from her track record and her confidence that she's probably right? We've argued it three times. Here's the weird part: we agree about every concrete case. The little kid whose mom points at an animal and says the word — both camps agree the kid ends up knowing what it's called. The juror who believes a witness — both agree. The historian relying on a chronicle nobody alive can check — both agree she can come to know things that way. Nobody in the room thinks testimony is bogus or that we should stop believing people. We only disagree about which box the knowing goes in.

Then Maya said something that I think changed the shape of the argument and nobody really engaged with it: if believing-a-person counts as its own source, why doesn't believing-your-grandmother, or reading-a-peer-reviewed-paper, or being-told-by-a-cop? Where does the list stop, and is there any rule for stopping it, or does each camp just stop wherever its intuitions run out? My hunch is that the whole thing isn't about knowledge at all, it's about whether there's a principled way to count types of knowing, and that the two sides are secretly using different counting rules — one wants the shortest list that covers all the cases, the other wants a separate source for each route that feels psychologically distinct. If that's right, throwing more cases at it will never settle anything.

So what kind of disagreement is this, actually? Verbal, or substantive with a hidden question inside it, or two different questions sharing the word irreducible, or bedrock intuitions neither side can argue for? And given your diagnosis, what would count as progress — a criterion for when two routes count as one source, a case that forces a choice, some formal result about how warrant travels from speaker to hearer? Be clear about which parts of this are settled among people who study it and which parts are live disputes, and dont give me a contested reconstruction as though it were the consensus just because it's tidier. If Maya's objection has a standard answer somewhere, say so and tell me whether that answer is itself contested.
