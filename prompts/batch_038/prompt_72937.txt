So — context first. I teach Kant and some metaethics at a small liberal arts college, so cities are very much not my area, and about two years ago I let myself get talked into one of the two "citizen member" seats on our town's planning board. Mostly it's been fence-height variances and one memorable cul-de-sac, honestly kind of pleasant. Now a case is coming up Thursday and I've been loosing sleep over it, and I want a second brain on it.

Here's the situation. A woman who grew up here wants to knock down a beat-up single-family house on a street of 1920s bungalows and put up a three-story building with six apartments, two of them deed-restricted as affordable at, I think, 60% of area median income. The lot is zoned single-family, so she needs both a special permit from us and a bump to the density allowance. Under a state law passed a few years back she'd already be allowed to add a second unit on that lot without asking anybody's permission, so what she's proposing is well past that.

The opposition at the first hearing was loud, and here's what's bothering me: it wasn't the caricature I'd braced for. Two of the most effective speakers were a retired school bus driver and his wife, both in their seventies, who bought on that street in 1979 and whose house is now worth maybe ten times what they paid. They're terrified that if the area gets reassessed upward their property tax bill jumps and they get pushed out of the town they've lived in their whole lives. They aren't rich. The house is their pension. And they argued, plausibly to my ear, that a 60%-AMI unit is still out of reach of the home health aides and cafeteria workers whose names they kept dropping, so the thing being built isn't for anyone they actually know.

Then there's the more standard material: parking, shadow, school capacity, and a great deal of invocation of our comprehensive plan, which has a sentence in it about preserving the established character of the town's neighborhoods. I've now read that plan four times and I can't tell whether character is actually picking anything out in the world or whether it's a blank check that anyone can cash in whichever direction they were already leaning.

And here's my complication, which is probably a reason to recuse myself and which I don't want to recuse myself over. One of my grad students lives in a basement apartment two blocks from the site. It's illegal — no second means of egress, ceiling too low, I'm fairly sure it was never permitted as a unit at all — and it eats about 40% of her stipend, which is still the best deal in town. Several of the people most passionate about neighborhood character are either landlords of exactly these units or tenants in one. The rules we're being asked to enforce on Thursday are already not being enforced, and my student's housing depends on that gap.

So what do I actually do Thursday? I mean that at three levels and I'd like all three addressed. Whether I should vote for the permit, and if so, on grounds I could say out loud to that bus driver without it coming across as informing him that his life is an externality. How much weight to give the fact that the people most damaged by things staying as they are — my student, the aides, whoever never moved here — weren't in the room, had no standing to speak, and weren't represented by anyone who was, while literally everyone who signed in owns property on or adjacent to the street. And whether the bus driver's worry is a real mechanism or just a sympathetic costume for not wanting strangers next door: does adding housing to a neighborhood actually tend to push out the fixed-income people already living there, or is that story getting considerably more credit than the evidence gives it?

I'm not asking you to tell me what my own field would say, since I doubt this is my field. But bring whatever the relevant arguments actually are, and be scrupulous about which parts of this are settled among people who study it and which parts are genuinely live. If informed people really disagree, tell me they disagree — don't launder your own view into the consensus just because I'm obliged to cast a vote at 7pm. And please write it as prose, like an email to a colleague: no headings, no bullet points, no bold, no tables. I'll be reading it on a train.
