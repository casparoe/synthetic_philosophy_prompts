I'm co-running the joint reading group between our law society and our philosophy society — about fourteen people, a mix of second- and third-year law students, a few philosophy ones, and two computer scientists who turn up mainly for the free pizza. Seventy-five minutes, one whiteboard, no slides. Everyone's read Hart's chapter on secondary rules for a previous session and nobody's read anything on this topic. My slot is the self-amendment problem: how a legal system can validly change the very rule that authorises changes, when the new amending rule seems to owe its validity to a rule it has just repealed. I'd like to get as far as the Hart/Wade argument about whether sovereignty is continuing or self-embracing, and to touch on Ross's version of the puzzle and on Peter Suber's Nomic — the game that first saw print in Hofstadter's Metamagical Themas column — where certain rules are immutable and have to be transmuted into mutable ones before anyone can touch them. Please don't explain any of this back to me, I know the material. Plan how I should explain it.

What I need most is the dependency structure: what has to be in place before the paradox can look like a paradox rather than a bit of wordplay. My instinct is that they need to have firmly grasped that a rule's validity traces back to a higher rule rather than to its own merits, and that if I skip that the whole session collapses into people saying "well, just vote on it again" — but tell me if that's the wrong first brick or if something more basic comes before it. Then: which single running example should carry the hour. I have three candidates and I suspect at least two are traps. (a) A debating society's constitution containing a clause that reads "this clause may not be amended, repealed or suspended", and then the members vote unanimously to repeal it anyway — concrete, and they'll all have opinions, but I worry it's easy to wave off as a private club nobody has to care about. (b) Parliament entrenching a statute so that repeal requires a referendum first — real, and it drags in the whole no-Parliament-can-bind-its-successors business, but it's fact-heavy and half the room may lack the constitutional background. (c) A game of Nomic in which the players vote to alter the rule about how voting works — self-contained, and they can actually fiddle with it, but does it drain the stakes out? Pick one, say what it must have and what it must not have, and say where the other two could still pull weight as a seperate two-minute contrast rather than as the spine.

Then the misconception map, which is the part I'm worst at predicting. I can guess a few: "it's just the can-God-make-a-stone-too-heavy-for-him paradox with lawyers in it"; "a revolution or a fresh start settles the matter, so there's no problem"; "the old amending rule authorised the new one, so of course it's valid" — which is precisely the question; and "this only bothers positivists, so natural lawyers can ignore it". Tell me which of these will actually surface in that room, add the ones I've missed, and flag anything likely to come out of the computer scientists' corner — I expect at least one of them to want to model the whole thing as a program rewriting its own source code, and I need to know whether to welcome that move or block it. For each misconception, say where in the sequence it should be headed off, and whether it's better pre-empted before it forms or allowed to be said out loud and then used. Finish with one question I can put to the room in the last five minutes that would genuinely discriminate between people who got the point and people who just enjoyed the puzzle, plus a short list of what I should refuse to go into at this level and declare off-limits at the start rather than get dragged into — I'm assuming that's Kelsen and the Grundnorm, the constitutional-statute case law, and regress arguments generally, but you tell me. British English throughout, please, and no readings or bibliographies: the room hasn't been set anything else and I'd rather not have twelve people off hunting down a book.
