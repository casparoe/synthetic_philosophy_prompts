I cover science, technology and ideas for a weekly magazine, and I am finishing a long feature about whether agents who disagree about what is good can nonetheless strike deals that leave each of them better off. The feature turns on a proposal that circulates in AI-governance and rationalist writing under the name of the "safe Pareto improvement." Suppose two agents with incommensurable aims, call them A and B, expect that absent any deal their interaction will settle into some messy default outcome D in which both do poorly. Each can then commit in advance, verifiably, to a conditional program of the form "if you do your part, I will implement your aims in this domain rather than mine," so that a joint outcome is reached which each prefers to D. The supposed appeal is that nobody need concede anything about what is actually valuable: the deal is certified only as an improvement over the default, by each party's own lights, and is therefore safe. The objections I want the sidebar to embody rather than recite are these — that D is not given by nature but chosen, and the choice of bargaining point does most of the work; that each party must model the other's values accurately, with no neutral referee when the models diverge; that being known as an agent who accepts such deals changes how hard others will bargain against you, so safety is measured against a baseline the deal itself has already moved; and that there are indefinitely many agreements of this form, all Pareto improvements over D, with nothing in the proposal to select among them.

My editor has approved a companion piece in the house tradition of mock documents, and I would like you to write it as a parody of an international framework agreement: a Framework Agreement on Precommitted Cooperation Between Agents of Incommensurable Aim, with a formal preamble, twelve to fifteen numbered Articles, two short Annexes, and three or four footnotes in the voice of a weary legal officer. Target roughly 900 to 1,100 words. Invent the signatories entirely — nothing corresponding to a real state, company, laboratory or named individual — and give each a two-sentence characterization of its aims specific enough that the machinery bites; a compact that wants the galaxy's stars left intact for their beauty, negotiating with an ascendancy that wants them disassembled for compute, would serve, though you may find better parties. It should look as though it were typeset by an actual treaty secretariat: article headings, sub-clauses in the (a), (b), (c) style, and a closing article on depositary and authentic texts. Our lawyers will also want a single italic line at the end noting that the document is fictional, and nothing else in that register.

The humour has to come from getting the mechanism right, so that a decision theorist laughs rather than winces, while a general reader still enjoys it as bureaucratic absurdity. Concretely: the definitions article should expose that "improvement" means improvement according to the party doing the assessing, and that the signatories have agreed to disagree about the disagreement point by jointly adopting a default each of them computes differently; there should be a mutual-modeling article requiring each party to certify that its simulation of the other's values is accurate to within a stated tolerance, together with a dispute procedure for the case where the two simulations disagree about whether the deal was an improvement; an article on entry into force that cannot quite avoid referring to each party's expectations about the other's signing; and a signature block in which one signatory files a reservation that quietly reopens everything it has just closed. That reservation is the payoff, so make it precise and devastating rather than merely wry. Keep the register deadpan and unbroken throughout: the treaty must never admit its difficulties, never step outside itself, never explain the joke, and the objections I listed must be enacted in the operative text and the footnotes rather than stated. No citations, no bibliography, no reference to real scholars, real instruments or real doctrines by name. Two colleagues have read my brief and both said a piece like this usually turns into a list of straw men by Article Six, so please make every clause something a genuine advocate of this style of dealmaking would be willing to sign, at least until they reach the reservation.
