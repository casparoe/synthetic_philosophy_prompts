Judges in criminal trials instruct jurors that they may not investigate the case on their own: no searching online, no visiting the scene, no running experiments, no consulting reference works or their own expert acquaintances, and nothing may be brought into the jury room that did not come in through the courtroom record, where both sides had a chance to object and rebut. As I understand it, verdicts have occasionally been set aside for exactly that sort of self-help. Yet the same jurors are told to apply their common sense and everyday experience; a juror who happens to be a nurse is generally not stopped from explaining a medical point during deliberations; and courts will sometimes escort the whole panel to view a scene under judicial supervision. A supervised visit is permitted, a solo dictionary lookup is grounds for a new trial.

Meanwhile, decision theory has Good's theorem, due to I. J. Good: for an agent who updates by conditionalization and chooses by expected utility, the expected value of additional information cannot be negative, since the agent can always ignore what it learns. On its face that says a tribunal aiming at accurate verdicts should want its factfinders to acquire everything relevant, not less.

So what view of evidence, and of what a trial is for, does the no-research rule presuppose? Reconstruct the implicit theory as precisely as you can: does it treat the trial's aim as something other than maximizing accuracy, does it assume jurors are the kind of updaters for whom Good's theorem's conditions fail, or does it locate the cost of the information on third parties rather than on the jury? Distinguish what the practice strictly requires from what it merely fits comfortably with, and say where it is underdetermined — where several incompatible theories would license the same rule. Then tell me whether its parts presuppose consistent things, since encouraging life experience while forbidding reference works looks like a tension, as does the escorted-view contrast. Finally, say what would have to change if a jurisdiction adopted an information-permissive rule — jurors may research freely but must disclose everything found, and the parties may respond to it — and whether that rule preserves or destroys the presupposed theory.

Please separate what is settled among people who work on these questions from what is genuinely contested, and don't present a contested position as though it were the consensus.
