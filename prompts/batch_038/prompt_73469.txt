I am running a ninety-minute session on program equilibrium for final-year computer science undergraduates. They have completed one game theory course: they can define a Nash equilibrium, they know that mutual defection is the unique equilibrium of the one-shot prisoner's dilemma, and they have met the folk theorem for infinitely repeated games. None of them has taken computability theory or mathematical logic, and only about half have ever written a program that treats source code as data. I have a whiteboard and no slides. The session exists because the two-week project that follows it requires each student to write a bot that receives the opponent's source code as input and returns a move, after which the bots are run round-robin against one another; a bot that fails to terminate scores nothing.

What I need is a plan for the explanation, not the explanation.

1. The dependency structure. Set out the order in which the components must be introduced and what each depends on. In particular: should the definition of a program equilibrium — no player can do better by substituting a different program, holding the opponent's program fixed — come before or after the first concrete pair of bots? And given that these students already carry the folk theorem, is their prior coursework scaffolding I can build on or a trap I must disarm first? If it is a trap, say precisely what goes wrong when they import it: what in the repeated-games picture has no analogue here, and what looks analogous but is not.

2. The carrying example. I want one pair of programs, in no more than about twelve lines of whiteboard pseudocode, that carries the entire idea: the bot that cooperates if and only if the code it is handed is character-for-character identical to its own source, its unconditional-cooperation counterpart, and at least one asymmetric encounter in which the match fails, so that the default branch is doing visible work. Tell me what this example must have, what it must deliberately lack — no randomness, no third player, no dependence on knowing a particular programming language — and whether the prisoner's dilemma is the right host game at all, or whether a coordination game or a game of chicken would carry the concept better and why.

3. Where self-reference enters, and what I do about it. A bot that decides by comparing the input string against its own source terminates immediately; a bot that decides by simulating the opponent's program on its own input must simulate itself simulating itself, and does not. My students will hit this in the project and it is the thing I most need them to leave understanding. Tell me at what point in the ninety minutes to raise it, how much of the underlying obstacle to expose to an audience with no computability theory — enough that they do not conclude it is a bug they can patch with a timeout, not so much that the session becomes a logic lecture — and where the honest stopping point is. I also want your view on whether the syntactic-match construction should be presented as a solution to the regress or as a way of dodging the question, since the answer changes how much of item 2 I can afford to lean on.

4. The misconception map. I expect at least these four: that this is tit-for-tat under another name and the folk theorem already covers it; that transparency merely means perfect information about a fixed opponent, so a player who can read the other's code can compute the other's move and the dominant strategy still wins; that the matching bot is a syntactic cheat, because no two real programs are character-identical; and that the whole apparatus is ordinary precommitment of the binding-device kind. Give me the ones I have missed — including anything specific to students who are competent programmers but have never thought of a strategy as a program — and for each misconception name the exact point in the sequence where it has to be headed off rather than argued with afterwards, together with the shortest thing I can say or write at that point that actually kills it.

5. The check. One question or short exercise I can put to them in the final five minutes that separates students who understand the idea from students who can reproduce the phrase, and a description of the wrong-but-fluent answer I should be listening for.

6. What I should refuse to cover, and say so out loud. My candidate omissions are the formal treatment of programs as machines, the undecidability of determining what an arbitrary program does on a given input, the existence and multiplicity questions for program equilibria, games with more than two players, and the variant in which cooperation is triggered not by syntactic identity but by the existence of a short proof, in some fixed weak proof system, that the opponent will cooperate. Tell me which of these I can drop without damaging the idea and which I cannot, and give me one sentence per omission that I can say to the class so that it reads as a deliberate boundary rather than as a gap in my own knowledge.

I am not asking for readings and will not be assigning any; the deliverable is the ordering, the example, the misconception map, and the check.
