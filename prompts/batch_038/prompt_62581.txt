I'm reviewing a trade book about machine-generated language for a science-and-ideas column, and the author — a linguist — sets out the philosophy of assertion in a boxed passage before building his central argument on it. It runs:

'Philosophers of language have converged on what is called the knowledge norm of assertion: you may assert something only if you know it. An utterance that falls short of knowledge isn't really an assertion at all, but noise dressed up as one. Because knowledge amounts to certainty, and no one can be certain about what hasn't happened yet, the norm has a striking consequence for our present technology: a language model, which knows nothing, cannot produce a single proper assertion, and everything it outputs is either a lie or an imitation of speech. Critics have pressed lottery-style cases as counterexamples, but these have been decisively answered.'

Before I go to print I would like to know how much of that survives scrutiny. Please work through it claim by claim: which sentences a philosopher working on assertion would sign, which are defensible simplifications of a real position, and which misstate the view they purport to explain. I'm especially unsure about four points — whether the norm is best read as a rule speakers are obliged to follow rather than as something constitutive of the act type, whether breaching it means no assertion occurred, whether the literature glosses knowledge as certainty, and whether there is in fact convergence. Say what the leading rivals to the knowledge norm require instead, and note that I have a vague sense his remark about lotteries has the dialectic backwards, though I can't say why.

Then assess the payoff claim. Does the conclusion about language models follow from anything true in the passage, or does it collapse once the misstatements are corrected? I'd like to know specifically whether an improper assertion is a lie, what the book is tacitly assuming about who counts as the speaker of a model's output, and what would actually have to be established to show that such output cannot be assertion. Close by flagging the one or two places where I should correct him on the record rather than let them stand as popular license.
