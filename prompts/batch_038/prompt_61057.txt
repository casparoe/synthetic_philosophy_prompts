I have been collecting my own reactions to scenarios in which humanity does not continue, partly to test the claim Scheffler presses in Death and the Afterlife — that we depend on a collective afterlife, on other people living on long after our own deaths, more than we usually admit. But my reactions do not look like a single commitment, and I cannot yet say what they add up to. Here they are, in the order I wrote them down.

1. I am told that thirty years after my death the Earth is sterilised by something unavoidable, and that everyone then alive dies within minutes and without knowing what is happening. No one I love is affected, and no one then alive is anyone I could ever have met. My reaction is not resignation but something close to collapse: the book I am writing, the students I train, the arguments I am assembling, all lose their grip on me within a day. And I want to insist the badness here is larger than the badness of those strangers' deaths, not merely a sum of them.

2. Same scenario, but the sterilisation happens five hundred years after my death instead of thirty. This is genuinely less bad for me, and I do not think the difference is a failure of imagination. I can hold both timelines in mind equally clearly, with equal vividness about the people involved; the difference in my reaction survives that effort and stays put under reflection.

3. Now a case with no extinction in it at all: a single child will be tortured in thirty years, or the same child, with the same life, will be tortured in five hundred years. I cannot find any discount here whatsoever. The second is not mildly worse, or vividly worse; it is the same.

4. I am told I will die at seventy, with humanity continuing in something like its present shape. This I largely accept. There is dread, but my projects keep their grip right up to the end, and I would not trade thirty years of ordinary continuation for anything on offer.

5. Same death at seventy, but within a year of it every trace of my work is destroyed, every reader of it dies, and everyone who knew me dies. This is worse than case 4 by a margin I cannot locate in my own experience, since by hypothesis I experience none of it.

6. Human extinction as in case 1, except that a successor civilisation takes over the planet — call them visitors, or descendants of some clever bird — and carries on for a billion years with science, grief, music, argument, and the attempt to understand what we were. This removes most of the badness for me. But not all of it: there is a residue I still feel, and I want to say the residue is not mere loyalty to my species, though I cannot say what else it is.

7. No extinction at all: humanity survives indefinitely, healthy, safe, affectionate, long-lived, and permanently capped below any achievement. No science, no art, no philosophy, no reflective love, nothing that anyone now would recognise as an accomplishment — just contentment, forever. I judge this worse than case 2, and roughly equal to case 1. That verdict startles me, since on any accounting of future welfare this world is far better populated than either extinction world.

8. A pill is offered that removes the feeling in case 1 while leaving all my beliefs about the facts untouched. I would refuse it: the devastation seems to me a correct response rather than a malfunction. The identical pill is offered for the feeling in case 2, and I would take it, because there the devastation seems excessive. I am therefore treating one and the same reaction as tracking the facts in one case and distorting them in another.

9. On direction of regard: I feel real gratitude toward anonymous copyists of a thousand years ago whose work was not aimed at me, and I feel a real obligation toward a stranger in 2300 whom I could help at trivial cost to myself. But toward the particular individuals who would have existed had case 1 not occurred, I feel nothing individually identifiable. The loss I register seems to be of a shape the future would have had, not of the people who would have filled it.

My tentative principle is this: extinction is bad beyond the deaths it involves because it destroys the ongoing carrying-on — the projects, inquiries, and traditions of understanding whose point depends on their being taken up and extended by people who are themselves in the business of extending them. What is lost is not future welfare but the continuation of activity that makes welfare worth having.

I want you to tell me whether anything like this can be made to work, and to do the following.

- State one principle, or at most two competing ones, precisely enough that its clauses can be checked. For each clause, name the case above that forces it in.
- Say explicitly which of my nine verdicts the principle delivers, and which it does not. Where it does not, say whether you take the defect to lie in the principle or in me.
- Apply the principle to at least two further cases of your own devising that you think are diagnostic — cases designed to separate the candidate principles from one another, not to illustrate the one you favour — and predict what my verdict should be, so that I can check it against my gut.
- Address the pair that seems to me hardest to reconcile, which is 2 with 3: why distance should matter to the value I lose in extinction while mattering not at all to a harm I can prevent. If your principle needs a separate clause for impersonal value and another for harm to individuals, say so plainly and defend the asymmetry rather than hiding it.
- Tell me what to do with case 7, since it is the one my principle handles worst: a capped but endless humanity stops the carrying-on just as extinction does, yet I rank it differently depending on how distant the extinction is.

Be committed rather than even-handed. I do not want a survey of possible accounts with the choice left to me; I want you to pick the principle you would defend, give your reasons, name the one verdict of mine you would advise me to abandon, and say what would have to be true for you to switch to the rival. You do not need to cite or recommend anything — I want the argument, worked from these cases, not the references.
