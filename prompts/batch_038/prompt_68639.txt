I cover science and ideas for a weekly current-affairs magazine, and for the past year I have been reporting on the panel that rules on contested entries in our national register of memorial sites — the body that decides, when a community's memory and the documentary record disagree, what the register is entitled to assert. Its published guidance pulls in two directions at once: one clause holds that oral tradition carries a presumption of good faith and is not to be dismissed merely for want of documentary support, while another gives the written record primacy wherever it speaks directly to the question at issue. The panel's decisions, so far as I have been able to track them, are these.

A town maintained, on the strength of a song still sung there, that a railway viaduct had been built by men brought in from one particular county; the company's payroll archive showed a largely itinerant workforce drawn from all over the country. The panel entered the documented workforce in the register and recorded the song beneath it as a surviving local tradition, saying nothing about whether it was true.

A village held that a cholera burial ground lay beneath what is now a car park; no map or register marked it, though the vestry minutes referred to ground set aside and not to be dug, and a geophysical survey showed disturbed earth. The panel upheld the claim provisionally and protected the site pending excavation.

A church roll of honour omitted a man whose family insisted he had died of his wounds and been buried at home; the regimental return listed him as missing, presumed deserted. The family produced a photograph with a name and date pencilled on the back and a death certificate from a neighbouring district, and the panel corrected the entry over the regimental association's objection.

A community identified the field in which a strike had been called in the 1870s; a newspaper of the period placed the meeting two miles away in another parish, and the panel followed the newspaper, noting in its reasons that the local account appeared to date from a pamphlet published in the 1960s by a man who had come to the area as an adult.

The matter before the panel next month is the one my piece will turn on, and I would like to predict the ruling before it is announced. A former mining community holds that after a pit disaster in 1907 seven men were buried at night in an unmarked plot on the colliery's own land, because the company would not meet the cost of sending the bodies home; the tradition names the seven and points to a particular parcel. The coroner's inquest file records that all the bodies were identified and released to their families, and the company's accident register lists the seven as sent home. Against that: the inquest was conducted by the county medical officer, who sat on the colliery's board; the pit-head witnesses were examined as a group rather than singly; a newspaper report filed three days after the disaster, before the inquest opened, said that families were still waiting for news of the seven; and a deed shows the company acquiring the parcel six weeks later, with no stated purpose, never used and sold on in the 1950s. A privately funded ground-penetrating radar survey of the parcel shows anomalies consistent with either disturbed ground or a disused culvert. The fullest written account of the tradition is a 1934 pamphlet by a local schoolmaster; of three people interviewed for an oral history recording in 1978, two could plausibly have read it, but the third — who named the seven in a different order and added an eighth man the pamphlet does not mention — had left school at nine and is recorded as saying she had never read anything about the disaster.

What I need is the following. First, set out the candidate rules that would account for all four of the panel's earlier decisions, and say for each which precedent it is being stretched to fit; I suspect that at least two of these rules can be told apart only by their reasons rather than by their results, and I want to know where. Second, say what each rule implies for the mining case, including whether any of them converge on the same verdict by different routes. Third, give me your best prediction of what the panel will actually do, with your reasons for preferring that rule to the others as a description of its practice. Fourth, since I intend to argue in the piece that it should rule one way rather than the other, tell me which of the four precedents is the closest analogue and what the panel would be committing itself to by following it — in particular, whether deciding this case on the provenance of the inquest file would leave the viaduct decision intact. And finally, if this case genuinely fails to discriminate between the leading rules, name the sort of further case the panel could take next that would.
