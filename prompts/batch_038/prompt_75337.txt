I'm a jurisprudence doctoral student clerking for the constitutional court of a state now in its sixth year after an authoritarian regime, and I'm trying to work out what our own line of cases commits us to. Four holdings, in order. A statute dispossessing members of a disfavored sect of all inheritable property — enacted by the ordinary procedure, published in the gazette, general in form, prospective, and administered exactly as written — the court declined to give effect to, saying only that it had been validly enacted but "is not law in any sense that obliges a court." An unpublished internal directive ordering that grain shipments be given priority during a famine, which a supplier invoked to claim payment from the state: held to create no rights or duties, on the ground that a command subjects cannot know is not law. A retroactive amnesty vacating every conviction for political speech: given full effect, the court reasoning that retroactivity which undoes an earlier injustice does not carry the usual vice. A published statute denying a tax credit to one religious community: applied without hesitation, as unwise but within the legislature's competence.

Now before us: a regime-era decree requiring members of a certain ethnic group to obtain permits for internal travel. It was published, its criteria were clear on their face, and it was prospective. But the permit-office archive shows grants and refusals routinely made on grounds the decree nowhere mentions, with no reasons recorded, and several of the convictions under review were obtained for travelling without such a permit. Should the decree be treated as law, and do those convictions fall?

Tell me which of our four holdings is the closest analog; which two or three different principles the record so far is equally consistent with; what each of them implies about this case; which one explains all four holdings best and therefore what I should recommend; and, if the record genuinely underdetermines the matter, what principle we would be committing ourselves to by deciding either way. Finish by naming the further case that would tell those principles apart.
