So there's a thing I keep running into in arguments about civil disobedience and I don't have a word for it. The usual definition (Rawls's, I think) says that for lawbreaking to count as civil disobedience rather than just crime, you have to be willing to take the punishment — you do it openly, you don't run, you stand there and accept the sentence, and that's what shows you're sincere and that you still respect the legal system in general. But a lot of actual cases don't look like that. Someone blocks a road or refuses to hand over records, gets arrested, hides nothing, and then spends the trial arguing that the law they broke is invalid or that what they did was justified, and asks to be let go. They're treating the courtroom as the place to make the case, and they'd count a conviction as a wrong done to them. They're not evading punishment, but they sure aren't accepting it either. Same shape in the draft-refuser type of case: the person isn't trying to change a law so much as declining to do something they think is wrong, and they'll tell the judge to his face that convicting them would be a miscarriage.

Is there an established term for this — breaking a law openly and non-evasively, but contesting the penalty instead of submitting to it? And how does it actually sit relative to the neighbouring categories: is it civil disobedience minus one criterion, or a genuinely different kind of act? What I really want is the carving, not the label. Where's the line between this and someone who evades arrest entirely, and between it and someone who takes the sentence deliberately as a piece of testimony? And if two people do the identical illegal act and one pleads for acquittal while the other asks for leniency after a guilty plea, is that a difference in the kind of act, or only in how they present it afterwards? If nobody's named this exact thing, just say so and give me your best definition with the boundary cases spelled out.
