Every autumn I run the same session in my philosophy of science seminar — Galileo's two bodies strapped together, Einstein's train and the lightning strikes, the elevator in free fall — and every autumn the discussion ends up parked in the same spot: the standoff between the platonist camp (Brown and company) and the argument camp (Norton and company). The first says a genuinely good thought experiment gives you an a priori glimpse of how nature must behave, that you see the conclusion rather than deduce it, and that no amount of experience underwrites it. The second says that's mystery-mongering dressed up as epistemology: every thought experiment is a picturesque argument, you can always unpack it into explicit premises and steps, and its epistemic force is entirely inherited from that unpacking — so there's nothing special left to explain. Thirty years on nobody has moved an inch. The argument camp keeps producing reconstructions; the platonists reply that the reconstruction isn't what the reasoner actually went through, and that the premises it leans on had themselves to be intuited first; the argument camp answers that "went through" is psychology and the question was never about mental episodes. Round and round.

Last term I tried to break the loop with a device of my own, and it may have just relocated the problem. I call it the narrative residue test: write out a fully explicit reconstruction of some thought experiment, then go back and ask whether anything in the original telling was doing justificatory work that the reconstruction can't capture — leftover content, not decoration. My thought was that the two camps should be able to agree on the test even if they disagree about the outcome. Instead the class split on the first case. We did the train, and one student's reconstruction got the right answer only after he added a premise about light propagating at the same speed in both directions relative to the embankment — something the narrative never asserts, it just lets you assume it. Half the class said that's the residue showing up, that the reconstruction had to be paid for with an extra unearned premise; the other half said it's not residue at all, it's just a suppressed premise that was always there tacitly, and calling it "intuition" instead of "premise" is a word game. Which made me wonder whether my test is even coherent, since what counts as a premise the reasoner already has versus one they had to see first seems to be exactly what's in dispute.

So: what kind of disagreement is this, actually? My working hypothesis — and I want you to push on it, not just flesh it out — is that it's at least three disputes wearing one coat: a verbal one about the words 'argument' and 'intuition', a difference in explanandum where one side is describing the phenomenology of being convinced and the other the structure of a warrant, and possibly a hidden empirical question about which cognitive process actually happens in people's heads, which nobody wants to own up to being empirical. Is that right, or am I being too irenic — is there a real, substantive, maybe even formal question buried in here about whether reconstructibility has an upper bound? Diagnose it by reference to what each side actually says when they argue (no bibliography, please, no name-dropping of papers — I know the literature, I want the analysis), and then tell me what would count as progress given your diagnosis: a distinction, a criterion for reconstructibility that could be stated precisely enough to fail, a cognitive experiment, a reframing of the question, or an honest argument that no progress should be expected and why. And if you think my residue test is worthless rather than merely contested, say so and tell me what diagnostic would be better.
