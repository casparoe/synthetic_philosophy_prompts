ok so i'm in year 13 and me and my mate run a lunchtime philosophy thing at our school, and we've been going round in circles on the wrong kind of reasons problem for about three weeks now. Our teacher reckons we should get it written out properly, but neither of us can do both sides without making one of them sound thick. Could you write it for us?

I want a dialogue between two people who disagree about whether the problem is even a problem.

Side A: someone who thinks value comes first — to be admirable just is to be such that there are reasons to admire you, to be fearsome just is to be such that there are reasons to fear you, etc. They think the wrong kind of reasons wreck this, because there are cases where you've got excellent reasons to hold an attitude towards something that plainly doesn't deserve it.

Side B: someone who thinks there's no problem whatsoever. On their view those reasons aren't reasons *for* the attitude at all. Reasons for admiring X always bear on the question "is X admirable?", whereas the demon-style reasons bear on a completely different question, "would it be good for me to be in an admiring state?" — so at best they're reasons to want to admire, or reasons to get yourself to admire, never reasons to actually admire. The two kinds never collide, so there's nothing to solve.

Then I want a case for them to fight over, one that's meant to break B's tidy split. Here's my attempt, sharpen it if you can:

There's a girl at our school, Nadia. Honestly she isn't admirable — she's snide, she put her name on someone else's coursework, she's horrible to people when she thinks no teacher is watching. But she's also completely on her own, and the small handful who do genuinely admire her (not performing it, actually feeling it) are the only reason she turns up at all. And the bit that messes me up: the admiration seems to be working. She's noticeably less awful than she was a year ago.

So does anyone have a reason to admire her? And if acting on that reason changes her into someone who eventually deserves it, what does that say about whether the attitude was fitting at the start? I want B to actually have to deal with that rather than slide past it — does the wrong kind of reason turn into a right kind one halfway through, and is it even coherent for an attitude to be unfitting while you're having it and fitting later on, retroactively?

And A doesn't get to win by default. B should press hard on whether the object-given vs state-given line is doing any real work or is just the thing A wanted to prove wearing a different coat. And A should have to say why the Nadia case isn't the boring old demon case with the serial numbers filed off — if it is, they should admit it and explain whats left of their argument anyway.

Some things I'd like:

- neither of them should be a strawman, they should both land proper hits and both have to revise something
- they should respond to what the other one literally just said, not keep restarting their own speech from the top
- no name dropping philosophers and no references to papers or books, we haven't read any and it'd just look like they're hiding behind dead people
- about 900 to 1200 words
- set it somewhere dull, like the queue for lunch or a bus stop in the rain, not a conference or a seminar room
- end with no winner, but with each of them saying in a sentence or two what they now think the actual crux is, and whether they think it can be settled at all
