I spent thirty-eight years running a water-quality lab and only started reading philosophy in earnest about two years ago, mostly because I retired and my wife got tired of me reorganizing the garage. I now help out with an evening discussion group at our public library — 16 to 20 people, nearly all retirees, two of them ex-engineers, nobody's done math past high school algebra but they're sharp and they will argue with you. One 90-minute session a month, and next month's is on decision under uncertainty. What got me down this road is the view that says expected utility isn't the only rational option: the one where your attitude toward risk is a separate ingredient from the shape of your utility curve, so instead of bending the curve you reweight the probabilities according to where each outcome sits in the ranking of how good it is. I believe it's the risk-weighted expected utility thing from Buchak. The claim that hooked me is that on this view someone can be thoroughly risk-averse across the board while having a completely linear utility function, and that expected-utility theory simply has no way to describe them — the risk attitude and the curve get conflated.

I want the group to feel that rather than listen to me explain it at a whiteboard, so can you design a runnable activity for us? Constraints from my end. Payoffs can't be real money; I was thinking tokens that buy something the group actually cares about, like choosing which of next quarter's three books we read, with the bottom scorer making the coffee for a month, but tell me if there's a better currency and make sure the outcome structure is graded with at least three levels rather than just win-or-nothing. I'd also like the units to be something physical and concrete rather than dollars — a remote field station rationing generator hours, say — but I specifically don't want units where people instinctively assume each extra one is worth less, because that would bake a bent utility curve into the setup and muddy exactly the distinction I care about. Most important, build in a set of choices that lets me tell afterwards whether a participant's pattern is genuinely rank-dependent or whether it could be explained just as well by a concave utility curve, or by plain old overweighting of long shots. My hunch is that you can't separate those with two-outcome gambles, but if I'm wrong about that, say so and tell me what the minimal structure is that does the job, and how I'd read the results off a printed tally sheet.

Then give me the practical stuff: rules, materials (paper, dice, spinners, whatever you need), a minute-by-minute timing so we don't run over, some device for keeping people from seeing each other's choices since this room is terrible at that, and a debrief that maps particular moments onto the distinction — where they notice their own picks look inconsistent, and where we ask whether that's irrationality or just a different but defensible risk attitude. They shouldn't hear the name of the theory until after that moment. Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it; in past games this group either starts trading tokens with each other or the two ex-engineers compute expected values out loud and everybody else just follows. And be straight with me about which feature of the view the activity cannot reproduce and that I'll have to supply by talking — I suspect it has something to do with sequential choices and whether an agent like this can be pushed around over time, but I'm not sure and I'd rather you told me than have me discover it in front of eighteen retirees.
