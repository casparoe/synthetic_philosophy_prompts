QUESTION 3 (40 marks; both parts are compulsory).

(a) State and explain the distinction between lying and misleading. Your answer should make clear what it is about a lie that makes it a lie, and should explain how a speaker can bring it about that her hearer holds a false belief without having lied.

(b) Many philosophers hold that lying is *pro tanto* morally worse than merely misleading. Set out the best reasons that have been offered for this view. Then set out the strongest objection to it, namely that the distinction cannot carry the moral weight placed upon it. Conclude by saying where you think this leaves the asymmetry thesis.

STUDENT SCRIPT.

A lie is an assertion made contrary to the speaker's own belief. What matters is not what is in fact true but what the speaker puts forward as true while taking it to be false, so the lie is complete at the moment of assertion, whether or not anyone is taken in by it. This is why a failed lie, one the hearer sees through, or one that turns out to be true because the speaker was mistaken about the facts, is still a lie in the sense that matters morally: the wrong has already been committed. Misleading is different in kind rather than degree. In misleading, the speaker says only what she believes to be true, but says it in such a way that she expects the hearer to arrive at a false belief. The familiar case is the politician who, asked whether he met a certain lobbyist, replies that he had no meeting with that lobbyist at the White House, when the meeting in fact took place elsewhere; the sentence is true, and yet it is offered precisely in order to produce the belief that no meeting occurred at all. Likewise the seller of a second-hand car who says that the engine has never been rebuilt invites the buyer to conclude that the car is sound without ever asserting that it is.

Both cases involve an intention to deceive, and that is where their moral similarity lies. But there is a genuine difference, and it is usually put as follows. To assert is to do something for which one stands behind the truth of what one says; assertion carries a warrant that ordinary speech does not. When one lies one abuses that warrant directly, and deprives the hearer of the means by which she might have evaluated what was being said to her. When one misleads one works within the truth, and leaves the hearer's own reasoning engaged. A second reason is that a lie cannot be checked, since the hearer has been handed a false content as though it were true, whereas the hearer who is misled has, in principle, all the true materials in front of her and could by attending to them more carefully have avoided the mistake.

There is, though, a deeper point in favour of the asymmetry, which is that in misleading the speaker does not cause the hearer's false belief at all. She says something true, and the false belief is produced by the hearer's own inference; responsibility for an inference lies with whoever draws it. The misleading speaker is therefore not the author of the error, and has at most failed to prevent one. This is also why misleading statements feel deniable in a way that lies do not. Misleading operates by means of conversational implicature, and an implicature can be cancelled, so the speaker can always protest that she said no such thing; lying offers no such escape, because the false content is inside the assertion itself.

As for Kant, he plainly condemns both equally. On his view the wrong of lying consists in the intention to deceive another person, and a misleading statement has that intention just as fully as an outright lie, so the distinction cannot be of any interest to him. Any position on which deliberately misleading could be permissible is not a Kantian position.

The strongest objection to the asymmetry is that it does not survive contact with cases. A misleading statement can do far more damage than a lie, and it is often the more cowardly of the two, since the speaker arranges matters so that she can disclaim responsibility afterwards. If misleading can be worse in every respect that matters, then the claim that lying is worse must be false. I conclude that the distinction between lying and misleading is real but morally insignificant: it describes two routes to the same wrong, and neither route is worse than the other.
