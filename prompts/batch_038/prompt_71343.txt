I serve on the housing committee of a neighborhood association in a mid-sized American city. We review discretionary-use permits and variance requests before they reach the planning commission; our recommendations aren't binding, but the commissioners read them, and applicants negotiate around them. Last month we took up a proposal to replace one single-family house with four townhomes on a lot zoned for a single unit, and afterward two members of the committee — I'll call them Marisol and Devon — argued in the parking lot in a way I couldn't referee. I'd like you to try.

Round one. Marisol said the people entitled to an opinion about this project are the ones who live near it, since they're the ones who absorb the traffic, the shadow, and the change in the street, while everyone else gets the upside of a decision without any of its cost. Devon said the people with the strongest claim on the outcome are the four households who would live in the new units, and that our process erases them by construction: they aren't parties yet, they can't testify to a harm they don't currently have, and no one at the hearing spoke for them.

Round two. Marisol said you cannot make a hypothetical resident a party to a proceeding — there is nothing to cross-examine — and that in practice the future resident is always ventriloquized by the applicant, who is present, solvent, and represented by counsel, so Devon's principle amounts to giving the developer a second voice. Devon said that's an observation about who happens to show up, not about who has standing, and that the remedy is to seat an actual advocate for future residents rather than to discount their interests on the grounds that nobody is presently asserting them.

Round three. Devon said the specific harms Marisol wants honored — parking, lost light, neighborhood character — are claims on things no one owns: the street is public, the sky is public, and the composition of a block is not a deed restriction. Marisol said the reliance is real even if the entitlement isn't; people paid what they paid here because the city's own zoning promised a certain density, and a government that induces expectations and then breaks them inflicts a harm that no property right need underwrite.

Round four. Devon said Marisol's reliance is reliance on the state's power to keep other people out, so honoring it means paying residents to exclude, and that if a promise was broken the answer is compensation for those who relied on it, not specific performance against everyone who might have moved in. Marisol said she would accept compensation in principle, but the city never offers any, which is precisely why the hearing is the only forum where her interest gets counted at all.

Round five, the one that went nowhere. Marisol said the slogan Devon kept reaching for — the right to the city — treats a city as an open-access commons and thereby licenses any newcomer's claim to override the arrangements that made the place worth living in. Devon said that slogan, which goes back to Lefebvre, names the point exactly: a city is a collective product, built and maintained by people including the ones now priced out of it, and its use belongs to those who need it rather than to those holding a claim on what it can be sold for.

I want three things from you. First, work through the five rounds and tell me who is right about what: assign each round a rating on a simple scale running from clearly mistaken to clearly right, stated in the running text, with a sentence or two justifying each rating, and then say who comes out ahead overall and whether the margin is wide or narrow. Second, I have a distinction of my own that neither of them used, and I want to know whether it would have helped or whether it collapses on contact with this case. Call an objection within-boundary when it allocates a good among people already acknowledged as members of the community whose decision it is, and boundary-shifting when granting it changes who counts as a member. Marisol's reliance objection looks within-boundary and Devon's standing objection looks boundary-shifting. Does that diagnosis hold, or does it beg the question, since the entire argument is about who counts? Third, tell me what neither of them noticed — the consideration that, had someone raised it, would most likely have changed the shape of the dispute.

Please write in continuous prose only, with no headings, no bullet points or numbered lists, no bold or italic emphasis, and no tables — the way one would write a considered letter to a colleague. Use American English.
