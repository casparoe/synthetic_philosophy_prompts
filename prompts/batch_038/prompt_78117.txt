**Damping: higher-order evidence as a feedback loop**

The debate about epistemic akrasia is almost always framed as a question about a state. Is the combination *believe p* + *believe that my evidence doesn't support p* rationally permissible? One camp says never: your belief about your evidence is itself evidence, so the mismatch can't survive contact with it. The other camp says sometimes, in exactly those cases where the higher-order evidence is misleading, and that we should learn to live with the mismatch. Both sides are arguing about whether a certain snapshot is stable, and they seem to assume that stability is the same thing as permissibility.

I want to suggest that this assumption is what's generating the stalemate, and that the right object to look at is not a state but a trajectory.

Treat your doxastic attitude toward p as the output of a system with two inputs: your first-order evidence, and your view about what that evidence supports. Getting higher-order evidence — you learn you're sleep-deprived, or drugged, or that you're in the minority of your peers — perturbs the second input. If you then allow your view about your evidence to bear on p, you perturb the first output, which changes what your evidence now looks like to you, which perturbs the second input again. That's a loop, not a one-shot update. And like any loop it has a gain: how much of a change in the second input gets transmitted back into the first.

Call that transmission weight g.

At g = 0 you have pure steadfastness at the first-order level: nothing you come to believe about your evidence ever moves your credence in p. The loop is cut. The mismatch is perfectly stable — and you've paid for that stability by making your own meta-beliefs inert, which looks less like a solution and more like a refusal to answer the question.

At g = 1 you have full enkratic responsiveness: p is always dragged into line with your current view about your evidence. Now nothing damps the loop. Each correction to your view of your evidence licenses a correction to p, which licenses a correction to your view of your evidence, and so on. The regress here isn't a merely dialectical worry about justification; it's a worry about whether there is any resting point at all.

Between them there's a range where the iteration converges — a weighted-average update rule gives you the usual stability condition, and the fixed point is neither your original attitude nor full-blown level-splitting but something in between. Call it damped convergence. The important thing about this range is that akrasia in it isn't a state. It's a transient. The agent in the middle of the loop looks akratic from outside and is on the way out of it from inside, and the enkratic requirement isn't violated because there's no settled mismatch to violate it.

Three things seem to follow, and the third is the one I actually care about.

First, the standard intuition pump for permitting akrasia — the case where you learn you've ingested something that impairs your reasoning — may be doing different work than everyone thinks. When we judge that case, we're picturing the agent mid-loop, and the permissibility we're detecting is the permissibility of a transient. If so, a good chunk of the debate is an artifact of evaluating a moving object at a frozen instant and then arguing about the photograph.

Second, and this is a prediction the static framing doesn't make: the asymmetry between the two directions of higher-order evidence should come out differently. Evidence that your faculties are *worse* than you thought damps. Evidence that they're *better* than you thought — you've just been told you're the expert in the room, or that the dissenters were compromised — has gain greater than one in the natural parameterization, and should run away. Self-trust inflating cases ought to feel unstable rather than merely uncomfortable, and I think they do: what's weird about them isn't that the conclusion is surprising, it's that the agent seems unable to stop.

Third, once you see the structure, akrasia stops looking like a special problem about belief. It's the general problem of using a measurement to calibrate the instrument you're using to take the measurement. That suggests the literature has been treating a symptom.

Worries I already have, in roughly the order they scare me:

1. g might not be a thing. If there's no independent handle on it, I've introduced a free parameter and tuned it until I got the answer I wanted. This is the one I can't get past.
2. Rational requirements are synchronic and my story is diachronic. I might be smuggling a process in where the debate is about a state, and then congratulating myself for dissolving a puzzle that was never about processes.
3. I've quietly moved to graded attitudes. Half the people in this debate are talking about outright belief, where none of the fixed-point talk applies.
4. I haven't checked that the fixed point is unique, or that the map is even monotone. I suspect it isn't, in which case "converges" is doing a lot of unearned work and the middle range might contain cycles instead.
5. Damped convergence might not evade the enkratic requirement so much as satisfy it slowly, which would make the whole thing much less interesting than I'm claiming.
6. The runaway prediction in point two might just be a story I like.
