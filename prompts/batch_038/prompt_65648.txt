For three election cycles I have volunteered as a precinct captain for a nonpartisan organization whose only purpose is raising turnout: we register people, we knock on doors, we send mail, and we take no position on any candidate or ballot measure. I have no academic training in philosophy — I left school early and have done my reading on my own — but a training session I sat through last spring left me with a question I have not been able to put down, and my brother, who does data work for a living, agrees that the whole enterprise rests on something we cannot quite name.

Here is the practice as I know it from the inside. Every script we are handed says some version of the same thing: your vote counts, elections are decided by a handful of ballots, and if you do not vote you have no standing to complain. When a voter says what I would once have said — that one ballot among two hundred thousand cannot possibly change anything — the answer we are trained to give is that if everyone thought that way nobody would vote, and then the result really would be decided by no one. In private we do not pretend otherwise: our own materials state plainly that a single vote almost never decides a national election, and we go out anyway, at a cost we compute per additional ballot produced. Two things about this strike me as strange. First, the mailing our trainers hold up as the most effective technique ever tested says nothing about outcomes and nothing about duty; it shows a household which of its registered members voted in the last election, shows what the neighbours on the same street did, and promises to send an updated version after the next one. In the study we were shown, that letter moved turnout by roughly eight points, while the letter that simply told people voting is a civic duty moved it by less than two. Second, we work from a list built by a model that predicts whether you will vote from what people like you — your age, your address, your neighbours, your past behaviour — have done. So the organization treats your decision as reliably tracking the decisions of your reference group, while the rhetoric treats it as a free act whose value lies in the example it sets. My own inarticulate hunch is that the assumption underneath is something like this: if I am the sort of person who votes, then so are a great many others who think as I do, and my ballot is not a drop in the ocean but one visible sample of a pattern — though I cannot tell whether that is what we are committed to or merely what I would like to be true.

What I want is a reconstruction of what this practice takes for granted. Which view of the worth of an individual act makes it coherent to spend money producing ballots whose direction you do not care about? Does the practice commit us to the idea that one person's decision gives others like them a reason to decide the same way, or can it be explained without any such idea — by duty, by self-expression, by habit, by the bare fact that mobilization is cheap and the slogans cost nothing? I would like to know which rival accounts of why people vote it rules out and which it merely fails to settle; where its parts disagree with one another (the efficacy talk against the duty talk against the neighbour letter against the predictive model), and whether those disagreements are real or only apparent; and what would have to change in how a canvass is run, and in what volunteers are told, if we adopted the view that only the total number of ballots cast has value and no individual act carries weight beyond its causal contribution — would we still knock on doors, and would we still send that letter? Please give me the reconstruction in prose first, and then a summary table with exactly these five columns: `presupposition` (the view the practice only makes sense under, in plain language); `where it shows up` (the element of the practice that requires it); `what it rules out` (the rival view that element is inconsistent with); `strength` (one of strictly required, strongly suggested, or merely compatible); and `reservation` (where the practice is underdetermined, or where you have read it one way rather than another). Order the rows by strength, strictest first. And throughout, please separate what is settled from what is genuinely contested among people who work on these questions — I would much rather be told that a piece of your reconstruction is a live dispute than have it presented to me as consensus.
