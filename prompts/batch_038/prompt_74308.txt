Retired physical chemist here — forty-one years working on reaction kinetics and phase behaviour, mostly industrial catalysis, and since I stopped going in I've been reading philosophy for fun, which my wife finds both endearing and faintly alarming. I've been slogging through Parfit's Reasons and Persons with a library reading group of six (average age somewhere north of seventy, one former Anglican priest who keeps trying to make it about the soul). Halfway through the personal identity chapters I started noticing a pattern in how people's judgements behave, and I dont have the vocabulary for it, and nobody in the group could supply one either. Maybe you can tell me if it's a thing.

Here's the shape of it. Take the case where you replace a person's brain cells one at a time over thirty years, so that at the end none of the original matter is left but the memories, character and habits all carried over smoothly — nearly everyone in our group says that person survived, it's obviously still them. Now take the *same* total change and do it in one operation: scan everything, destroy the original, build the finished product a metre away in a tenth of a second. Final composition is the same, the causal history is arguably just as good, and now nearly everyone says the original died and there's a copy walking around. The only difference is the timescale. Same for the firm: a company that replaces every one of its employees, its premises and its equipment one departure at a time over a decade is unhesitatingly called the same firm, still bound by its old contracts; dissolve it and refound it the next morning with an identical staff list, identical charter and identical address, and everyone says that's a new firm. And there's a third wrinkle that came up over coffee: do the slow version but stop halfway, leave the person in the half-replaced state for a year, then finish — several of us thought the pause changed something, and nobody could say why, and the person at the end is identical either way.

So: does this have a name? I mean the specific feature — that the verdict tracks the *rate* at which an otherwise identical change is delivered, and that people simultaneously accept every individual small step as harmless and reject the whole change delivered at once, which is exactly the situation where, in my old field, you'd say the measurement is picking up a path-dependent quantity rather than a property of the end state. I know it's in the neighbourhood of the teleporter material and I know there's some standard move available about not being able to point at which little step was the fatal one, but please don't just say that and stop — I want to know whether the speed of the process is doing any real theoretical work in the best accounts of this stuff, or whether it's a red herring that only feels important. Which established concepts come closest, what's wrong with each as a label for this, and if there genuinely isn't a term, coin me one with a definition and some boundary cases — tell me what would *not* count as an instance, because I suspect some of the rate-sensitivity is really about something else entirely (information? reversibility? the fact that the fast version involves a moment when nobody is there at all?). Also worth answering: is this a quirk about persons only, or does the firm case show it's something more general about how we think of things lasting through change? And be blunt with me about my kinetics analogy — whether it's a genuine structural parallel or just a retired chemist reaching for a familiar word.
