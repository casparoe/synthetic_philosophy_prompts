I keep hitting the claim that consciousness is a user illusion — the GUI metaphor, apparently coined by Alan Kay for interfaces, picked up in Tor Nørretranders' book and then by Dennett, and sitting next to Metzinger's transparent self-model and the predictive-processing talk of perception as a controlled hallucination. My suspicion is that "user illusion" is doing two quite different jobs under one label, and I want them prised apart.

Here's a distinction I've been kicking around. Two ways the interface story could go:

- **Redaction.** The interface omits and compresses. What it shows you is accurate at its own level, just much thinner than the machinery underneath. Conscious experience isn't false, it's low-bandwidth: you don't see the parallel drafts, the latency, the subpersonal bookkeeping, the way binding actually gets done. Nothing in the content is wrong; there's just a lot you're not shown. This is pretty much what a desktop icon is — it doesn't lie about the file, it hides the filesystem.

- **Caricature.** The interface doesn't just hide, it positively misrepresents. It introduces properties that nothing in the machinery instantiates: a punctate now, a seamless unified field, a single locus of authorship behind action, a self that owns the experience. Here the content of experience has false parts, and the theorist is closer to an illusionist in the eliminativist sense.

Compare these two readings as rival accounts of consciousness, and then tell me which one the people who use the user-illusion talk are actually on the hook for. What I want:

1. Walk through three or four cases where the readings genuinely diverge in what they predict or imply, not just in how they sound. I have in mind the felt unity of the visual field, the specious present / the "now," the experience of willing an action (including the confabulation and choice-blindness style results where people give confident reasons for choices they didn't make), and maybe transparency itself — is transparency a fact about what's hidden, or a feature that has to be built in?
2. For each reading, what does it buy and what does it cost? I think redaction is cheap but arguably not an illusion at all in any interesting sense — if all it does is omit, why call it deceptive? And caricature has real philosophical teeth but owes an answer about how the theorist's own reports escape the distortion. Say whether you agree, and where I've got it wrong.
3. My main charge: does the view get its intuitive plausibility from the redaction reading (harmless, engineering-flavored, everybody nods) while its philosophical force comes from the caricature reading (which is much more controversial)? If so, is that a slide, a rhetorical strategy, or something defensible — e.g. maybe you can only earn the right to the stronger reading by starting with the weaker one?
4. Does the distinction even survive contact with the predictive-processing framing? "Controlled hallucination" seems to bundle both words: hallucination is caricature, but constrained-by-evidence-to-be-useful sounds like redaction. Tell me whether that framing collapses the distinction or is just equivocating.

Give me a verdict at the end — which reading you think is the right one to build a theory on, and whether you think the label user illusion should be kept, retired, or split.
