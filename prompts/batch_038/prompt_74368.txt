I run a grad reading group on consciousness and we keep circling back to the same stalemate, so I want a diagnosis rather than a survey. The setup: Dennett's multiple drafts model versus the people who say he has simply denied the explanandum — the theatre, the unified field, whatever you prefer to call it. Everybody knows the colour phi case, and everybody knows the two stories you can tell about it: the Orwellian one, where the subject really did have the intermediate experience and it got quietly rewritten in memory, and the Stalinesque one, where the brain finishes the show before there is any experience to report. And everybody agrees the two are behaviourally and introspectively indistinguisable from the inside. Thirty years on, nobody has budged. His critics say that is exactly the problem: a model that can absorb any possible report isn't a model of consciousness, it's a refusal to have one. The defenders say the critics are smuggling the theatre back in by treating the absence of a fact of the matter as a failure of explanation rather than a finding.

So: what kind of disagreement is this, really? A substantive one about whether there is any fact about the temporal order and content of experience? A verbal one, turning on what awareness or the content of an experience is supposed to mean? Or two parties answering different questions — one about the causal architecture of the brain, one about what it is like — filed under a single heading? My own hunch, and I may well be wrong, is that the fault line is over standards of success: whether underdetermination by all possible evidence counts as a defect in a theory or as its central discovery. Tell me whether that hunch survives contact with what each side actually says, and then be honest about what would count as progress — a new distinction, a result about when in processing the colour change gets represented, a change of question — or whether we should expect none at all, and why.

British English, please.
