We're in the middle of a curriculum review, and separately the dean's office has asked us to justify the two lines we want to advertise next year, which means I've been staring at our own period vocabulary a lot more closely than is healthy. Here's what's bugging me. We hire in slots defined by period — medieval, early modern, long eighteenth century, Romantic, Victorian, modern, contemporary — and candidates are expected to "cover" one of them. The prelim fields are organized that way, the intro sequence is built out of them, and if a job talk is genuinely excellent but lands thirty years to the right of the advertised slot, the committee reads it as a near miss rather than a hit. Meanwhile the same people, in the same building, teach a methods course whose standard content is that these labels are nineteenth- and twentieth-century inventions: that the boundaries track political events (1660, 1789, 1832) rather than anything in the texts, that the dates come out differently in English than in French or in art history, and that I have personally watched "early modern" eat "Renaissance" in the job ads over the course of my own career, which I take to be evidence about hiring language and not about anyone discovering a new joint in reality.

So: what does the hiring-and-coverage practice actually presuppose about whether periods are real? Not what would be flattering for it to presuppose — I want the reconstruction of the implicit theory that makes the practice intelligible rather than merely habitual. Is it a realism (there are joints and the slots track them)? An instrumentalism (the joints don't matter, only the coordination problem and the division of labour among specialists)? A looping or self-fulfilling story (the slot makes the period real by shaping what gets written, taught, and read)? Or nothing beyond an administrative technology for ranking applicants? One feature I think is under-pressed and that I'd like you to work on: the slots function as a *partition* — exclusive and jointly exhaustive, one scholar per box — whereas even the most committed periodizer thinks the boundaries bleed and that plenty of first-rate work is irreducibly cross-period. So there may be an assumption here about the *shape* of periodization that is stronger than any of the philosophical options above, and I want to know what it is and which of those options could actually deliver it.

Then do the follow-ups in the same breath: seperate what the practice strictly needs from what it merely sits comfortably with, tell me whether the department's two behaviours (slot-hiring in the corridor, constructivism in the lecture) are in genuine conflict or whether some single view licenses both, and say what would have to change if we swapped the presupposition out — if we advertised by method or by problem field instead — and what would break that the period slots are currently holding up (evaluation, coverage requirements, a committee's ability to say no with a straight face). Give it to me as exactly five numbered points, a sentence or two each, no introduction and no conclusion, and don't send me sources or a reading list — I can't look anything up between meetings and I'd rather have your reconstruction than your footnotes.
