I'm a software engineer with no formal training in philosophy and only a working acquaintance with the statistics involved. I want to practice defending a design decision I have already shipped, against someone who knows this material far better than I do and thinks it doesn't apply to my case.

Please play the following character and hold it across turns: a methodologically conservative social epistemologist, trained as a statistician, who has spent thirty years auditing attempts to invoke the Condorcet jury theorem on behalf of real decision procedures and has rejected nearly all of them. They are polite, dry, patient with non-specialists, and entirely unimpressed by confidence. They think the theorem is beautiful and correct and will say so without hedging. What they deny is that any actual voting body they have examined satisfies its hypotheses, and they suspect that citing it in an engineering design review is usually a way of borrowing the authority of mathematics for a claim about truth that nobody has earned. They concede small points quickly and never the main one. Stay in character until I say stop; do not step out to give me meta-advice about how to argue.

Rules for your replies:

- Two or three short paragraphs, so that this reads like an exchange rather than a memo.
- Do not cite literature, name authors, or refer to specific papers. Argue from the structure of the case. If you are relying on a result you believe is well known, describe it in your own words and say how confident you are in it.
- If I state something that is simply wrong as a matter of arithmetic, say so plainly and correct it.
- You may ask me one clarifying question per reply, but only alongside a substantive response. Your first reply must engage my challenge directly rather than asking me to restate the case.
- End every reply with a single bracketed line marking which of your claims are settled among people who work on this and which are genuinely contested, and do not dress up a contested position as the consensus.

My opening move.

We run an internal review system. For each of roughly forty thousand supplier invoices a year, twenty-five reviewers independently answer one yes/no question: does this invoice violate our contracting policy. On a labelled validation set of four hundred invoices, an individual reviewer matched the ground-truth label 74 percent of the time, and the measured pairwise correlation between reviewers' errors was about 0.05. I ran the binomial calculation and got a probability above 0.99 that a majority of twenty-five is correct, and I have cited Condorcet in every design review since. Reviewers work alone in a queue: they cannot see each other's answers or the running tally. But they all complete the same two-week onboarding module, they all work from the same checklist written by the compliance team, they are all employed by the same two vendors, and every ground-truth label in the validation set was assigned by a single senior compliance officer.

Two further facts you will want. First, last quarter we let the majority vote decide automatically, and an internal audit of the subset of decisions where a wrong answer could later be established found the system wrong about a third of the time. Second, I am not going to pretend that audit refutes the theorem; I understand the theorem is a conditional claim, and I am not asking you to catch me treating it as an empirical prediction.

So here is the challenge, and I would like you to take it seriously rather than dissolve it. Tell me which single step in my reasoning is the one that actually fails, and then tell me how you would distinguish a broken system from a biased audit, since the audited subset was chosen precisely because those cases could be checked. And if your answer is that both readings are equally consistent with the theorem, then tell me what the theorem was doing in my design review in the first place.
