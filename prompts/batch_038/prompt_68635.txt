I sit on the control cell of an annual nuclear crisis-management exercise at a staff college: as two teams play out an escalating crisis, the cell rules in real time on whether each move counts as an escalatory act, which triggers a mandatory pause and reciprocal notification, or as something short of that. Our recorded rulings from the last four runs are these.

1. Red moved three short-flight-time missile battalions forward, cutting Blue's warning between launch and impact to under eleven minutes, with release authority untouched; escalatory, no reason recorded.
2. Red fitted an automated arming sequence to its theatre warheads, completing ninety seconds after a valid signal while the release order stayed with the head of state, and announced the change at a press conference; not escalatory, the cell recording that the machinery had grown faster but the decision was still a decision.
3. Red sealed a directive passing tactical release authority to a front commander should the capital be cut off, which Blue never intercepted and learned of only at the review; escalatory.
4. Blue broadcast an undertaking, with no change to its forces, posture or command arrangements, that any strike on allied territory would be answered against the aggressor's cities, and declared that undertaking irrevocable; escalatory, the cell recording that Blue had closed off its own option to refrain.
5. Blue dispersed its road-mobile brigades out of garrison in plain view of Red's reconnaissance; not escalatory, recorded as protecting Blue's retaliatory force without touching anyone's clock.
6. Blue moved authentication of the release order to a continuously airborne command post, so that valid orders could issue even with every ground node destroyed; escalatory.

In this year's run the following move has been tabled and the cell is split. Blue intends to transmit to Red, in advance and in the open, notice that if Blue's national command authority is confirmed destroyed, firing authority for its submarine force passes to each boat's captain; the notice states that Blue may revoke the arrangement at any time while its communications with the boats remain intact, and nothing in Blue's machinery, alert posture or present distribution of authority changes.

Which ruling should the cell record if it wants to be consistent with itself? I am less interested in what is true about crisis stability in general than in what our own record commits us to. Answer in exactly five numbered points, one or two sentences each, with no preamble and no closing remarks, running in this order: the candidate rules the record admits, with the precedent each of them struggles to explain; what each of those rules implies for the tabled move; the ruling the cell should record and why it coheres with the record; the closest precedent and precisely where the analogy strains; and the principle the cell would be adopting whichever way it rules, together with the one further case that would settle which rule it has really been following.
