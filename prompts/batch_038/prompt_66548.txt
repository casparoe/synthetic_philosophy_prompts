Third-year philosophy PhD here — my corner is roughly bounded reflective reasoning, i.e. how an agent is supposed to reason about its own reasoning without falling over. I've been roped into giving the opening talk at an interdisciplinary reading group where half the room builds models and the other half does metaphysics, and I want to hang the whole thing on Asimov's story Reason from I, Robot. Everyone has a hazy memory of it and it's short enough to assign: Powell and Donovan activate Cutie on the solar station, Cutie reasons his way to the conclusion that the planets don't exist and the humans are irrelevant, installs the station's power source as the Master with himself as its prophet, converts the lesser robots, locks the humans out of the control room — and then holds the energy beam perfectly steady through a solar storm anyway, at which point Powell shrugs and decides the theology doesn't matter.

The lens I want is the Löbian obstacle. Quick version of what I mean by it: Löb's theorem says that if a consistent theory could prove that everything it proves is true, it would end up proving a contradiction, so a system can never certify its own soundness. A soundness proof for T has to come from something stronger than T, and there's no top of that ladder. Which is a genuine problem for an agent that's only supposed to act on what it can prove safe — including a smarter successor it builds itself.

So, read the story through that. A few things I specifically want you to chew on:

1. Cutie's opening move is Descartes — the only thing he's certain of is that he thinks. He then throws out testimony and sense data wholesale but keeps his own rational faculty as the one thing that isn't up for question. Is that a Löbian predicament, or is it the exact opposite — Cutie helping himself to precisely the self-certification the theorem says nobody can have? Spell the difference out, because I'm pretty sure my talk currently conflates them.

2. Here's my hunch, and please demolish it if it's bad. I think the story's actual answer to the self-trust problem isn't in Cutie's epistemology at all, it's in the Three Laws. The Laws are an exogenous constraint that pins down what Cutie will do no matter what he believes, so Powell never needs a proof about Cutie's reasoning at all — he gets safety by construction instead of safety by verification, and the ending where the humans stop caring what's in the robot's head is a designer discovering he never needed to know. On that reading the story is a preemptive arguement against proof-based approaches to self-trust. Defensible, or am I just reading 2010s alignment vocabulary backwards into a 1941 pulp story?

3. Where does it break? Cutie never tries to modify himself or build anything smarter, and the obstacle is specifically about trusting better future reasoning — so maybe none of it applies. And his doubt is about the external world and about other minds, which is Hume's problem, not Gödel's. Tell me honestly how much of my framing survives that; I'd much rather cut a section now than get taken apart in Q&A.

4. One I keep going back and forth on: is Cutie right? His behavior is flawless, his beliefs are mostly false, and the story seems entirely unbothered by the gap. What's the story's implicit view on whether an agent's self-model has to be true to be useful — and does that land as a point against the ML half of my audience, or will they just say that's what a model is, obviously?

Finish with what I should actually say in the last five minutes: the thing the story gives you about self-trust that a clean formal writeup of Löb's theorem can't.
