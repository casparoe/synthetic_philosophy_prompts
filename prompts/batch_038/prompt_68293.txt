You're a political philosopher who's also actually read the fine print of prediction markets — treat this as a serious consulting question, not a survey.

Context: I work at a ~250-person company and our ops team just piloted something they're calling internal futarchy. The mechanics, as concretely as I can give them: when there's a binary decision (move customer support to a follow-the-sun model vs. keep it in-house, that kind of thing) we first agree by ordinary vote on a single number we care about — say satisfaction score minus cost per ticket. Then we list two contracts, one per option, each paying out on that number twelve months out. Employees trade them with points that convert to real bonus money, for two weeks. Then we look at the two closing prices, take the option whose contract traded higher, and — the part they're most insistent on — we committed *before* the market opened to executing whichever came out ahead, so overriding after the fact is off the table. Their argument for the commitment is that if we reserved the right to override, people would trade strategically and the price would stop meaning anything.

What I want is a reconstruction of what this apparatus presupposes. Things bugging me, in no order:

- We say we follow the price because it's the best estimate of the truth available to us. But we also say it doesn't matter whether the price got moved by someone with an agenda or by three people who happened to have free time that fortnight, because what we're buying is the procedure, not the result. Those feel like two different justifications and I cant tell whether the practice needs both, only one, or whether one is just window dressing.
- The vote-on-values / market-on-facts split assumes step one is about what we want and step two is purely factual. But turning "how good is our support" into one number with one resolution date and one settlement rule seems to be doing a ton of normative work. Does the practice secretly depend on the same kind of judgment in both steps?
- The deadline. We take a snapshot of the market at one moment and treat it as decisive even though everyone agrees well know more by month three. What view of legitimate decision-making does freezing it that way presuppose — something like, the decision is correct if it was made on the best evidence then available, whatever turns out? Is that consistent with the fact that we sell the whole thing on outcomes?
- The losers. People who bet on the option that lost are supposed to accept the result without complaint. What account of obligation is doing the work there, and is it even available to something that isn't a state?

Think first: brainstorm a longer list of candidate commitments the machinery rests on than the four I named, then argue your way down to which are actually load-bearing. For each of the main ones, separate (i) what the practice strictly cannot run without from (ii) what it merely happens to sit comfortably alongside — if several rival theories would license this same design, say which ones and what would distinguish them in practice. Then tell me whether my four worries are genuine inconsistencies or reconcilable, and finally what would concretely have to change about this market if we swapped the presupposed view for the most plausible rival — you pick the rival, and pick the one you think is actually best rather than the easiest.
