You are an expert in animal ethics and animal welfare science who has advised retail food-welfare standards bodies before. Think carefully, and before drafting, spend a few sentences laying out the criteria you take the decision to turn on; then write the document.

I sit on the animal welfare advisory panel of a national grocer. Our "Higher Welfare" shelf label is defined only as covering products that "materially improve the lived experience of the animal," and we have never had to apply that phrase to a case where the animal's capacities, rather than its conditions, are what changed. A supplier now seeks certification for a line of broiler chickens in which the affective-motivational component of nociception and the stress response to crowding have been suppressed: the birds show no aversive reaction to noxious stimuli, no learned avoidance, and no elevated stress markers at stocking densities we would otherwise reject, while reflex withdrawal, social behavior, and basic cognition are reported intact. The supplier's argument is that the bad has been removed and nothing else touched, so these birds are strictly better off than any conventional flock. One panel member replies that this is not improvement but deletion of the party who could have been improved; another says that if we refuse, we are conceding that our standard tracks the visible expression of suffering rather than suffering itself.

Draft the written opinion I will circulate before the vote. It must give a clear recommendation — certify, refuse, or certify subject to conditions you can state precisely enough to be audited — together with reasoning the panel can act on, and an honest statement of the strongest consideration on the losing side, including what would have to be true for that side to prevail. Address explicitly what "improving a lived experience" commits us to once capacity for bad experience is what has been edited, and whether the human analogue (engineering prisoners, or warehouse workers, to mind their conditions less) is a reason to accept the supplier's proposal or to reject it. Aim for roughly 700–900 words.
