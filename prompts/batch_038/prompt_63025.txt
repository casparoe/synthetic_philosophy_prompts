I'm not an academic — I look after my mum three days a week since the dementia got bad, and I've been reading about care ethics on my own, mostly in the gaps between shifts. I've tried to write down what I actually believe rather than what sounds good, and I want to know whether it all hangs together, because something feels off and I can't locate it.

(1) The basic moral thing is responding to the needs of the actual particular people in front of you. Obligation doesn't get handed down from principles, it grows out of relationships.

(2) You can't care for everyone, and it isn't a failing that you don't. Care is finite and local and that's just what it is.

(3) A society that hands its old and sick people over to underpaid strangers is getting something morally wrong, and its not only that the pay is bad.

(4) The daughter who gives up her health and her job to care for a parent is admirable, but she shouldn't have to, and the fact that she's expected to is the real problem.

(5) Guilt when I let someone down isn't noise to be argued out of me. It's information about what I owed.

(6) Concretely: my brother pays for a carer on the days he isn't here. Mum is better looked after than she'd be if he did it himself — he's much calmer with money than with her. I still think he's doing something worse than I am, and I don't think that's just sibling resentment talking.

Go through these and tell me which ones genuinely pull against each other, worst first, and what I'd have to give up to stop each of them pulling. Some probably only look like they conflict, so say which those are and why the conflict isn't real. And be straight with me about (6): can anything I've said above actually deliver that verdict, or am I claiming something my own view has no route to? No headings, bullet points, bold or tables please — just continuous prose, the way you'd write an email, so I can read it straight through.
