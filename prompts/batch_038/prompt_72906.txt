Been going down a rabbit hole on money pumps and diachronic rationality and I don't want another essay about it, I want a story. Here's the setup I want you to build — it's mine, I made it up, so please don't sand it down into something more familiar.

A harbor town has a class of people who reset every time they sleep. Legally they're one continuous person — same name, same body, same debts, same lease. Experientially, whoever wakes up is a stranger to whoever went to bed. They inherit the memories perfectly, they just don't inherit the *wants*; those get rebuilt from scratch each morning out of whatever the current situation is. So you get an incoming self who can read last night's self's handover letter and think, sincerely and correctly, that the person who wrote it was an idiot.

The one institutional protection the town has: nobody in that class is allowed to own anything that outlasts a night. No savings, no boat, no share in the cannery. Their whole estate is a bowl of small change and a set of tools that get re-issued each dawn. The reason for the rule is never stated in the story, but the reader should be able to work it out from what happens.

My plot: a clerk who keeps the morning ledger — she's the one who witnesses and records trades in the first hour after waking, before the incoming self has read their letter. She talks a woman in that class into an exception, gets her a durable holding, genuinely believes she's lifting her out of it. And then discovers that once someone holds something across a night, you can trade with each successive self in turn and take a little off the top each time, and every single one of those trades is a real bargain from the point of view of the self making it. I want three trades, on three consecutive mornings, and I want each one to be locally sensible — show me what she gets that morning and why it beats what she had, in her own eyes, right then. If I can't reconstruct the cycle from the three trades alone, the story has failed, so make the goods concrete and the terms explicit.

Constraints, and these matter more than the prose:

- Nobody, at any point, uses the vocabulary. No "rational," "preferences," "utility," "consistent," "transitive," "irrational," "paradox," "Dutch book," "money pump," "time-inconsistent." The mechanism has to be visible purely in what gets handed over and what gets recorded.
- No character explains the philosophy out loud. If someone needs to give a speech about what it all means, cut the speech.
- The clerk is not a villain and doesn't become one. She should think she's doing right for most of the story, and the turn should be her noticing something about the numbers rather than about her own soul.
- The tenants are not wise simple folk and not victims-as-decoration. They have a culture, they have jokes about it, they have opinions about the handover letters that differ from each other.

The ending beat I want: the clerk tries to lay out the accumulated loss to the third morning's self, and that self isn't upset — not because she's brave or in denial, but because from where she's standing nobody she is was ever cheated, and she's right. Then land the last movement on what the town's rule was actually buying: that the only thing keeping these people safe is that they're not allowed to have anything worth stealing, and that this is the same fact as their not being allowed to have a future. Don't say that. Just make it the thing the reader is sitting with when they put it down.

First person, the clerk's voice, bureaucratic and getting steadily less steady. Around 1,500 words, give it a title. One more thing: don't resolve it. No reform, no redemption, no burning of the ledger, no final image of the sea.
