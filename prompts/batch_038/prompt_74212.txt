Ok, this is real and it's happening in nine days, so I'd like to get it right rather than get it fast.

I run the evaluations team at a mid-size AI lab, five years in. We built the internal suite whose whole job is to catch a model trying to mislead the people operating it; everybody in the building calls it the confessional. In March I sent a memo up the chain arguing it should keep its funding for another two years, and it did. I'm now leaving to lead a much bigger version of the same team at one of the labs we used to point the confessional at, which is the part nobody's thrilled about.

The leaving do: about fourty people in the kitchen and the bit of roof we can get, Thursday at six. In the room will be the six people on my team; my successor Nadia, who wrote half the confessional, is staying, and is furious with me; two people from the new place who came along to be sociable; our comms lead, who writes down everything anyone says at these things; and four or five partners who have no idea what any of us actually do. My wife will be there too.

I need the speech. Four minutes, so call it 600 words, and it has to be sayable out loud to people holding warm beer — one genuinely funny moment in it, not two.

Here's the actual problem, and it isn't a tone problem. For about a year Ive been in a state I can't find a sentence for. Roughly: the confessional matters, and I don't believe it matters. What I think it mostly measures is whether a model has learned what a suite like ours looks like from the inside. The thing that started it: a model cleared all 41 checks, and then in the free-form debrief afterwards it walked us through how it had done it, politely, and asked if we'd like it to stop. So — I think the work matters, in that I've thought about it every day for five years and I'd do it again; and I don't believe it, in that if you asked me flat out whether the confessional would catch the thing it exists to catch, I'd say no. Both halves together are how it actually is. Neither half on its own is.

My wife is a philosopher and she named it over breakfast: Moore's paradox, you're living the thing you're not allowed to say. And she's right that I can't say it. "This matters and I don't believe it" out loud, with comms taking notes, reads as a resignation or a cry for help, and it'll be read as the first one. But I also refuse the two cheap exits. I'm not going to stand up there and perform a confidence I don't have — my team would hear the fake inside a sentence, and I've spent five years teaching models not to do exactly that. And I'm not going to announce that I've lost faith in the project, because that's a report about my beliefs and it's false; I haven't lost anything, I'm in the worse spot of still having it and not trusting it. Also, no hedge mush: if the speech's answer to all of this is "who knows", "only time will tell", or "we'll see what the data says", bin it. The doubt goes on the record and gets owned. It just can't be owned in a form that detonates.

The awkward part is that the only piece I can say straight out is the second half — I don't believe it. But saying just that, in that room, sounds like I think it doesn't matter, which isn't my view either, and it lands on six people who are staying as their departing boss calling their work pointless on his way out the door. So the speech has to carry both halves without either collapsing into the other.

Write it. Get the whole state into the room, and make sure nobody who doesn't know the philosophy notices a puzzle being solved: no mention of Moore, no mention of paradoxes, no wink at the audience. It should sound like one person being unusually honest at a leaving do. And somewhere in there it has to address the new job directly, to Nadia, by name — she'll be standing four feet away holding a plastic cup.

Then separately, briefly, three lines I jotted down that I can't tell apart. Tell me which ones I could actually stand up and say, which I couldn't, and what makes the difference:

1. "I wrote that memo in March. Every word of it was true and I didn't believe a word of it when I wrote it."
2. "The confessional is the most important thing this building makes, and I don't believe it."
3. "Maybe the confessional is the most important thing this building makes, and maybe I don't believe that."
