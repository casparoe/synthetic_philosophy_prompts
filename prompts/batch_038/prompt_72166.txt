A philosopher colleague walked me through a position I want translated into my own terms before I decide whether it has any content for me. It is the view that rational agents need not maximize expected utility, but should instead maximize what is called risk-weighted expected utility. As I understand the proposal: order the possible outcomes of a gamble from worst to best by utility; the utility increment gained by moving up from one outcome to the next better one is not weighted by the raw probability of that increment, but by a risk function *r* applied to the probability of ending up at least that well off, where *r* maps [0,1] onto [0,1], is continuous and strictly increasing, fixes 0 and 1, and expresses risk aversion when *r(p) ≤ p* throughout. So a gamble paying 100 with probability *p* and 0 otherwise is worth *r(p)* times the utility of 100 rather than *p* times it. Three further claims were attached: (i) risk attitude is a third independent component of a rational agent — alongside beliefs and desires — and must not be folded into the curvature of the utility function, since curvature of utility already does something else; (ii) this lets the theory vindicate Allais-type preferences, which expected utility rules out; (iii) the risk attitude is a global or holistic feature of how an agent relates to a whole gamble, not a local sensitivity at the margin. The author also concedes that these preferences can look unstable when the same agent faces a sequence of choices over time.
