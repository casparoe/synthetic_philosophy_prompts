Sunday reading group, been going for about fifteen years, mostly ex-blog-comment-section people who have argued about the same six topics since forever. This month we landed on whether hedonism about wellbeing even has a subject matter. Our resident hedonist — call him D. — keeps saying the standard objection is a cheap shot: sure, cold water on a hot day and the click when a proof finally closes don't feel alike in any interesting way, but hedonism is a theory about which states are good for you, not a theory about a phenomenological natural kind, so who cares if "pleasure" is a grab bag. The rest of us think that's dodging, but nobody could say crisply what we're accusing him of. My hunch after trying and failing to draw this in a diagramming app is that the disagreement is actually several disagreements stacked on top of each other, and that what we need is a sorting device: a tree people walk by answering questions about cases, so they get filed by what they actually believe instead of by which label they shout.

So here's the ask: build a taxonomy / decision tree of the answers to "what do all pleasures have in common, if anything?" for an audience of smart non-specialists who've read a bit but have never taken a class. Hard rules. Every branch node is one question, phrased entirely in terms of a case or a yes/no about a case — never in terms of a theory, and never in a way that lets the answerer guess which leaf they're steering toward. Build the space before you fill it: tell me what the real axes are and what combinations they permit, then locate the actual positions in those cells, and for every cell that comes up empty say whether it's empty because the view is incoherent, because it's just unattractive, or because nobody has bothered yet. I want the tree to separate at least these: the felt-quality view (there is one common phenomenal feature after all), the motivational/dispositional view (what they share is that you want more of them while you're having them), the relational or higher-order attitude view (a pleasure is an experience that's the object of a favorable attitude, or of a desire to be having it), some version of the adverbial or "ways of experiencing" move, the disjunctivist who says pleasure is a genuine disjunction and that this is no problem at all for value theory, the cluster/family-resemblance person, the eliminativist who wants the concept scrapped and replaced, and D.'s value-first position where whatever turns out to be good-making gets called pleasure by stipulation. Then flag honestly which pairs of leaves my tree can't actually tell apart — I expect the disjunctivist and the cluster person are going to be trouble — and if you think one of my required leaves is bogus or two of them are secretly the same view, say so rather than padding.

Use my cases as the branch material, since those are the ones the group fights about. (1) Someone drinks a bitter herbal tea every morning, says it tastes bad, would miss it if it disappeared, and keeps pouring. (2) Direct brain stimulation, no reportable content whatsoever — the subject can't say anything about how it feels but presses the lever harder each time. (3) A song that devastates you and you play it four times in a row. (4) The undirected good mood on a spring morning, pleasant about nothing in particular. (5) The case where you'd say in the moment that you want the experience to continue but afterwards deny that you enjoyed it — and the mirror image, where you insist in retrospect that you loved it though you kept trying to make it stop. (6) The taste-blind sommelier who can't describe any wine but reliably reaches for the same bottle. (7) The forced comparison: rank the pleasure of a warm bath, a solved problem, and being understood by a friend, and notice that you can rank them without feeling any shared ingredient. One more thing that matters to me more than it probably should: keep the two questions apart that D. keeps collapsing — what pleasures have in common, and what makes them good for you. I want at least one branch node that asks whether the answer to the first question has any bearing on the second, because that's the node where I think he and I actually split. About ten to fourteen questions, eight to ten leaves, indented text tree is fine, no citations and please don't name-drop philosophers unless you're confident you've got it right — I'll put the names in myself afterward if the thing survives contact with the group.
