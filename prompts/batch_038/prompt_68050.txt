I'm three quarters of the way through a novel and one of my characters has an argument I can't beat, which is a problem because he's supposed to lose it in chapter 31.

Setup: three ferry companies working the same strait. They undercut each other on fares and on maintenance until all three are barely solvent, the boats are worse then the old state monopoly's were, and every owner admits the joint schedule they ran in the 90s left them all richer. My antagonist, Varga, says none of this is a real trap. Here's his argument as I've drafted it:

1. Call something a genuine trap only if there's an arrangement every participant can see, would prefer, and could move to.
2. If everyone can see an arrangement that's better for all of them and they could move to it, and they're competent and out for themselves, they move to it.
3. So a genuine trap can't persist among competent self-interested people who understand their own situation.
4. So wherever something looks like a persistent trap, one of three things holds: they don't understand it, or they aren't really out for themselves, or there was no such arrangement and it isn't a trap.
5. Of those three, understanding is the only one a book could repair — so any trap that could be dissolved by being described is dissolved by being described. My novel is a no-op and my ferry owners are just thick.

I can't accept 5, but 1 through 4 read to me like they follow, and my editor thinks Varga basically wins the scene as written. So: which step do I have my other characters attack, why did that step look innocent on the page, and what does each candidate rejection cost — because if 2 goes, my heroine's whole reading of the strait goes with it, and I need to know that before I rewrite her. And tell me whether Varga can patch it with a weaker 1 and still get something close to 5, because if he can, I'd rather write him winning.
