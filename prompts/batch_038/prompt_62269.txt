Question 3 (25 marks; suggested length 800–1,000 words). Some philosophers argue that advanced autonomous systems create a "responsibility gap": harms caused by such systems for which it would be unfair to hold any human being morally responsible.
(a) Explain why systems that continue to learn from experience after deployment are thought to generate such a gap. Make clear which conditions on moral responsibility are said to fail, and for whom. [8 marks]
(b) Distinguish the responsibility gap from the "problem of many hands" in large organisations. Is the gap simply the many-hands problem in an extreme form? [7 marks]
(c) It is sometimes proposed that a regime of strict liability, backed by compulsory insurance, closes the gap. Evaluate this proposal. [10 marks]

And here is the candidate's answer, complete.

(a) The gap arises where the two conditions usually taken to underwrite moral responsibility both come apart from the agent's conduct. Call them the epistemic condition — that the agent know, or be in a position to know, what they are doing and what it is likely to bring about — and the control condition — that the outcome depend on the agent in such a way that they could have done otherwise. A conventional defective artefact satisfies both, at least in principle: if a brake assembly fails or a drug produces an unlisted reaction, we can identify a decision and examine it, asking whether the testing was adequate, the warnings sufficient, the risk knowable. A system that goes on learning after deployment removes the object of that examination. Its conduct in the fourteenth month is a function of the environments it has met, the data it has accumulated, and the modifications it has undergone, none of which its designer selected, and about none of which there was, at the time of design, any determinate fact. What the designer chose was a process, not a disposition. The epistemic condition therefore fails, and fails in an interesting way: not because the designer neglected to look, but because there was nothing there to look at. The control condition fails for the user rather than the designer. A supervisor who can intervene retains a formal power of interruption, but a switch that can only be thrown once the harm is already under way is not control of the harm. This is why the gap is said to be new rather than merely old: it is not that the harm stands unattributed, but that there is no agent to whom it could be attributed, even in a world of complete information.

(b) The problem of many hands concerns large collaborative undertakings — a hospital, a ministry, a consortium — where a harm issues from the aggregated contributions of many people, each of whom did something small, individually unobjectionable, and causally marginal. In such a case responsibility is not absent; it is distributed and hard to see. Each contributor can say with some justice that the outcome would have been the same without them, while the aggregate crosses a threshold that no single contribution crosses. The difficulty is epistemic and apportioning: we know the kind of thing we are looking for and cannot isolate it. The gap is the opposite. There the investigation could in principle be completed — every line of code, every training run, every input recorded — and a completed investigation would return nothing. Many hands is a failure of our sight; the gap is a failure of the situation to contain the thing we are looking for. It follows that the gap is not the many-hands problem intensified, though the two are easily confused, since in both a victim is left with no one to hold to account. What differs is where the failure sits: in us, or in the world.

(c) The proposal I am asked to evaluate is that strict liability, backed by compulsory insurance, closes the gap. In my view it does, and it does so in the only way that is practically worth doing. The regime works by detaching payment from fault: the party who deploys a system pays for the harms it causes whether or not they were careless, and the insurance requirement makes the payment collectible rather than nominal. Its great advantage is that it does not ask the question the gap makes unanswerable. We need no longer establish what anyone knew or could have known; we need only establish that the system caused the loss. Two features deserve notice. The first is that the regime changes the currency of responsibility from blame to cost-allocation. Because liability attaches without fault, the party who pays is not thereby being accused of anything: they are being assigned a share of the losses generated by an activity they elected to undertake and from which they may have profited. Strict liability is entirely compatible with impeccable conduct, which negligence liability is not. The second is that it declines to place responsibility on the machine. Some writers suggest that because the system made the decision, the system is the responsible party, and that the gap is an artefact of a legal vocabulary that has not caught up with a novel kind of agent. This must be resisted. A thing that cannot be resented, that has nothing to lose from sanction, and that cannot be called on to explain itself is not a candidate for responsibility, however central it is to the causal history. Deciding is not the same as being answerable. Strict liability gets this right: it keeps the response inside the human world, where alone it can mean anything. What the regime cannot supply is guidance. Compensation is retrospective, whereas the gap also concerns who is to direct and constrain these systems prospectively, and no insurance schedule settles that. But that is a limit of scope, not a failure of the proposal itself.
