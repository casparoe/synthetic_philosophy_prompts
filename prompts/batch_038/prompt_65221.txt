I have been trying to settle what I actually think about precautionary reasoning, and I have got to the point where I can list my verdicts on particular cases but cannot state the principle that generates them. I would like help stating it, or being told that it cannot be stated.

The principle I keep reaching for is this. Call it P: where an activity threatens a harm that is (a) serious, (b) not credibly quantifiable in advance, and (c) not undoable by later action, the burden of proof shifts on to whoever proposes the activity, and unless they discharge it the activity should be restricted — whatever the restriction costs. I suspect P is too crude, because of what follows.

My cases and verdicts:

1. A water utility proposes replacing its disinfection regime with a cheaper one yielding a different set of by-products. The toxicology on the new profile is thin and would take a decade to firm up. Restrict until the evidence improves: yes.
2. A hospital wants to deploy a triage tool that is measurably better than current clinical judgement on average, but whose errors are known to be unevenly distributed across patient groups in ways nobody has characterised. Deploy at scale: no. Run it in shadow mode for a year, making no decisions, purely to learn its error profile: yes — and I think refusing even that is imprudent.
3. A novel systemic insecticide with plausible harm to pollinators that cannot be quantified for a decade, where the yield loss from refusing it is real and countable. Restrict: yes.
4. New nuclear build. Long-lived waste is irreversible and the tail risks are, on any honest account, not credibly quantifiable — and yet I do not feel the burden shifting here in the way it does in 3. Permit: yes.
5. Stratospheric aerosol injection. Refrain: yes. But I also do not think that staying on the current emissions path counts as taking the precaution, and I cannot find a way of saying both.
6. A vaccine during an outbreak, with a plausible signal of a rare adverse event. Keep using it: yes. The deaths averted are countable and large, so here I insist that the harms of restraint be counted.
7. A herbal preparation for which there is no evidence of harm whatever, and equally no quantified risk. I would not take it, and I would object to its being sold as though benign. Note that this is the same agent as in 6, the same exposure, and entirely self-regarding.
8. An existing industrial solvent with weak evidence of harm stays on the market, while a chemically similar newcomer with equally weak evidence is refused. I endorse that asymmetry, and I know how much it resembles mere protection of incumbents.
9. A ban on a novel drought-tolerant variety that pushes growers back into a more pesticide-intensive practice. The ban is imprudent and not itself precautionary, because the displaced risk exceeds the avoided one: yes, that is my view. Yet the same displacement argument is available in 3, and there it moves me not at all.
10. A confined, self-limiting trial of a sterile-insect programme on one island: yes. An open-ended release across a continent using the same mechanism and by then a better evidence base: no.

What I want back:

- A principle, or a small set of competing candidates, stated precisely, with each clause tied to the case that forces it in. Please do not send me a reading list; I want the formulation and the argument for it.
- An account of where P breaks. I expect at least 4, 5, 7, 8 and 9, but tell me which of those it can in fact absorb and at what cost.
- The dilemma I cannot escape: a principle strong enough to deliver 1, 3 and 5 seems to forbid every novel activity and thereby to consecrate the status quo, while one weak enough to permit 4, 6 and 9 seems to collapse into "weigh all the evidence", which was never in dispute. Is there a formulation between those, or is one of my verdicts simply wrong?
- Which concession is cheaper: abandoning 5, and so conceding that aerosol injection is the precautionary option; or abandoning 4, and so requiring of nuclear build the same evidential showing I demand of a novel insecticide. Recommend one and say why.
- Whether the burden-shifting idea must be indexed to who is deciding — regulator, institution, individual. Cases 6 and 7 are what make me ask, since the only difference between them seems to be who bears the risk and who is counting. Is that one principle with a parameter, or two principles that I have been running together?
- Verdicts on three cases I have not listed, so that I can see whether the principle is doing work or merely restating my prejudices: retail approval of a cultivated-meat product; a temporary moratorium on germline editing that keeps being renewed; and a school district adopting a contested new reading curriculum, where the harm is not physical but the years of a small cohort are not recoverable. That last one should tell me whether clause (c) is about the irreversibility of the harm or the irreversibility of the time.
- Finally, a clause on information-gathering, if the principle needs one. Case 2 suggests that the option set is not simply act or restrain but includes learning-then-deciding, and that refusing to learn can itself be the imprudent choice. How do I give learning its weight without licensing indefinite delay on the grounds that more data would always be worth having?
