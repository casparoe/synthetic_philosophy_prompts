you're going to hold two roles at once for this one: a philosopher of mind who actually works on higher-order theories of consciousness, and a poet who writes in strict fixed forms. don't start with the poem. first, in about six to eight lines of plain prose, brainstorm out loud: pick your six end-words and say in one clause each why that word is the right one and which move in the arguement it's going to carry. then write the sestina.

the subject is the regress objection to HOT-style accounts, and i want the form to do the arguing instead of the poem announcing a thesis. content that has to be in there, stated accurately: the transitivity idea (a state is conscious just in case the subject is aware of being in it, non-inferentially and not by observing herself); the objector's move (that awareness looks like it needs a state of its own, and that one needs another, all the way up); and the theorist's escape (the higher-order state is not itself conscious, so the ladder stops rather than climbs). no name-dropping inside the lines — if the reader has to know who Rosenthal is to follow it, the poem failed.

formal requirements: six sestets plus the three-line envoi, 39 lines, standard end-word rotation. here's the part i care about most. that rotation is a permutation of order six, which means a hypothetical seventh stanza would land back on the first stanza's exact order. the poem should make the absence of that seventh stanza feel like a decision somebody made and can be held responsible for, not just the point where the form happens to run out. the envoy, where the end-words double up two to a line, should carry the theorist's claim that one state can hold a content and an awareness-of-that-content without a third thing coming in — and it should sound less triumphant than the sestets, because that's the honest bit.

also work in, lightly, the empty case: a higher-order state with nothing under it. at least one end-word should turn up in a stanza reporting on something that was never there.

house rules: no archaism, no 'alas' or 'behold' or 'doth', no em-dash pileups, unrhymed is fine, loose pentameter is fine but don't wobble. a grad student working on consciousness should read it and think that's exactly the argument; somebody who's never heard of any of this should read it and just think it's a good poem about paying attention to yourself. both at once. after the poem, stop — no gloss, no footnotes, no explaining what you did.
