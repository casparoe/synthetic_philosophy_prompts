I've been asked to give the opening remarks at a two-day workshop we're running next month — working title "Trading Values: Bargaining Across Disagreements That Aren't About Facts." Eight minutes, nine at a stretch. I'm one of the organisers so I built the agenda and now I have to open it, which is my own fault.

The room is three groups. Maybe twenty economists and game theorists who'll relax the second somebody says Pareto. A dozen political philosophers who think putting the word trade next to the word value *is* the corruption we should be studying. And ten people from a community coalition two hours upriver, who are in the room because the workshop's case study is their town, and who are pretty sure they're about to be turned into a spreadsheet.

The case: a quarry wants to expand. The coalition says no — the ridge above the bend has a burial ground on it and a stand of old growth, and their position, sincerely held, is that there isn't a number. Not a high number, no number. The town side says no too — two hundred jobs, and the school district's funding rides on the tax base, and they insist that isn't negotiable either. Two years of nothing. Then somebody noticed the company actually cares about the depth of the cut and which seam it follows, not about the ridge as such, and the deep seam runs south, away from the burial ground and the stand. But a south cut floods the haul road they've already built, which costs them real money. So there's a shape of deal where the coalition keeps the ridge and the company keeps the depth, and what's actually being traded is who eats the haul road cost and how the mitigation gets sequenced. Both sides end up with more of the thing they said was non-negotiable than the stalemate ever gave them.

That's what I want the eight minutes to make, and I want it made as a general claim, not as a lucky local story: different value systems generate trades that identical ones can't, because the same features of a situation get weighted differently by different people, and its the weight differences that make exchange possible. Where everyone agrees and weighs everything alike, there's nothing to trade and the only question left is who wins.

The constraints are the part I actually care about:

I can't open by locating common ground. Not "we all want what's best for the town," not "we all care about the river." The coalition has been handed that sentence monthly for two years by people who then went off and did something else, and the philosophers will hear it as a dodge. The remarks have to start from the disagreement being real and irreducible and treat that as the asset rather than the thing to soften.

I also can't wave away the objection. Somebody in that room is going to say, roughly: you found a price, that's all you did — the existence of a trade doesn't make it legitimate, and if the ridge genuinely has no number then a deal that leaves it standing while selling off everything around it is still a sale, just a slower one. That needs to be met in the speech, seriously, for a minute, not deflected and definitely not joked about.

No names. No philosopher, no theorem, no citation anywhere in it. It's an opening address; half the room would switch off and the other half would start grading me.

I do want one passage, a minute at most, that the economists will recognise as a real argument and the coalition will hear as plain speech: the baseline problem. Whether a deal counts as everybody gaining depends entirely on what you measure the gain against, and the stalemate we're all treating as the neutral starting point was itself produced by who had legal standing to block what — so "better than no deal" is doing a lot more work in that sentence than it looks like. Say it in ordinary English, no jargon at all, and don't resolve it, because that's day two.

And the ending tells the room what the two days are for without promising the workshop will settle the quarry question. It won't, and everyone there knows it won't.

Write the remarks.
