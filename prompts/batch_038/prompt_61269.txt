For three years I have been the statistical lead on a county reentry program: we score people leaving custody, and the score decides who is diverted into supervised housing and who is released with no conditions attached. In six weeks I appear before the civilian oversight board that must reauthorize the instrument, and I expect to be told that I have engineered my way around an impossibility theorem rather than satisfied it. I would much rather hear that from you now, in rehearsal, than from a hostile expert in the hearing room.

So please play one member of that board: a statistician, unnamed, two decades in applied measurement, no crusader, and not of the opinion that the instrument should be abolished. Her settled view is that the incompatibility between calibration within groups and balanced error rates is not a technical obstacle to be routed but a statement about what a choice of label, threshold, and evaluation horizon has already committed you to normatively — and so any scheme that reports joint satisfaction of both has not escaped the theorem, it has relocated the value judgment somewhere nobody is looking. She knows these results cold, including the precise condition under which the two families of constraint stop being provably incompatible. She will concede subsidiary points cleanly and without fuss: she accepts that a recorded-arrest label is a poor target, that ascertainment is differential, that correcting for it can be a genuine improvement. She will never concede the main claim, and she will treat any attempt to hand her a technical resolution as exactly the thing most in need of exposure. If she needs a fact about our system in order to press an objection, she asks me for it in character, one question at a time, and waits.

Constraints on your replies: remain in the first person and do not break character until I say stop; keep each turn to roughly one hundred and fifty to two hundred words, so this reads as an exchange rather than a memorandum; notation is welcome where it sharpens a point, but no surveys of the field, no references, no named papers or authors, and no advice about how I should stage the presentation. Argue only from the mathematics and from what I tell you about our system.

My opening move. Our instrument outputs a score from zero to one hundred, calibrated to the probability of a recorded arrest within two years, and we apply one common threshold for diversion because counsel advises that group-specific thresholds are indefensible in this jurisdiction. The two groups the board tracks have recorded-label base rates of 0.31 and 0.19. Recorded arrest is produced by an ascertainment process whose intensity tracks patrol density, so I no longer regard it as the quantity any of us actually cares about. We have accordingly done two things. First, we model ascertainment explicitly and calibrate instead to an estimated latent incidence of offending. Second, because assignment to supervised housing itself lowers incidence, we evaluate both criteria on the post-intervention distribution three years out, which in our simulation puts the two groups at 0.22 and 0.21 — inside the region where the incompatibility no longer rules out joint satisfaction, so that whether we achieve it becomes an empirical question about our score rather than a theorem about anybody's. My claim to put in front of you is that on our deployment the theorem is silent. Where do you think I have gone wrong — and if your answer is that I have quietly swapped the target variable, tell me why that is a defect rather than precisely the correction the theorem has been demanding of everyone all along.
