I'm trying to pin down the legal philosophy of the two great postwar tribunals, and what puzzles me is that their features do not add up under any single label I have heard. The Nuremberg Charter was an agreement among the four victorious powers, each of which supplied both a judge and a chief prosecutor; it directed the court at "the major war criminals of the European Axis" and confined its power to punish to persons who were "acting in the interests of the European Axis countries"; and it created a crime — planning and waging aggressive war — carrying individual criminal liability up to death that no statute had previously carried. The Tokyo tribunal was more unilateral still: constituted by proclamation of the occupying supreme commander rather than by treaty, with judges of his appointment, and it drew a full dissent from its Indian member on the ground that the aggression charge had no legal existence when the acts were committed. Yet the same Nuremberg court, shown that the United States Navy had waged unrestricted submarine warfare in the Pacific from America's first day in the war, declined to assess Dönitz's sentence on that conduct — an appeal to the other side's identical behavior that the Charter's own jurisdictional clause had already ruled out of bounds. Meanwhile the Soviet prosecution charged the Germans with Katyn, and Allied bombing of cities was never within reach at all.

What conception of law, and of who may punish whom, does this bundle presuppose? Under what account of legality does such a court's pronouncement count as law rather than as an act of force by the winners, and which rival accounts — strict *nullum crimen* positivism, a higher-law view binding all parties alike, a consent-based international law — does the practice rule out as opposed to merely strain? Is there one theory that licenses both the retroactivity and the one-sidedness, or does the bundle cohere only because each half is licensed by a different theory? What does the Dönitz episode show about a symmetry principle operating at sentencing that the jurisdictional text had rejected? Where is the practice philosophically underdetermined, such that incompatible theories would equally license it — and what would have to change to replace the presupposition, including whether a permanent, prospective court resting on state consent and Security Council referral escapes it or merely relocates it?
