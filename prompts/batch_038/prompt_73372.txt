So for about six years I've been driving for a volunteer thing that takes older people to their dialysis appointments, and lately my wife and a couple of the other drivers and me have gotten stuck on when something counts as above and beyond versus when it's just what you're supposed to do. I want to lay out my gut calls and see whether they add up to anything, because I have a feeling they don't.

Here's what I actually think, case by case. The guy two doors down who shovels my driveway after a storm without me asking: great guy, but if he'd walked right past and left it, he wouldn't have done anything wrong. The lifeguard at the city pool who watches a kid go under ten feet from him: wrong, no question, he's paid to be in that water. But I also think the random person on the beach who's a strong swimmer and watches the same kid go under has done something wrong too, and that's the one everyone tells me I'm not allowed to have. Now freeze the scenario — same kid, but the water's near freezing and there's slush, and going in is a real chance you don't come back — staying on the sand is fine and going in is amazing. Then kidneys. Donating one to a total stranger: obviously beyond the call of duty, nobody could ask it of you. Donating one to my own brother: I think if I said no I'd have done something wrong, even though the surgery and the risk to me are exactly the same. My sister's a nurse and I'm not, and if someone drops at a family barbecue I think she's more on the hook to jump in than I am, even though were both equally just relatives standing there holding potato salad. And the one I keep flipping on: a guy who gives away most of what he makes, so his kids are always in hand-me-downs and never get taken anywhere. Half of me says that's saintly, half says he's shortchanging his own kids, and I cant get the two halves to shut up. Then there's a different kind of case that doesn't fit any of it. My uncle sends my grandma a birthday card with cash every single year and has never once phoned her. I can't name a rule he's broken, not one. But I'd rather have a grandson who forgot her birthday once and took her to lunch the other fifty-one weeks than have him. He's doing nothing wrong and he's still worse somehow.

My rough theory going in was: if it costs you basically nothing and someone else gets seriously hurt otherwise, you have to do it; if it costs you a lot, it's optional and praiseworthy. That gets the pool and the slush and maybe the shoveling, but it flat-out can't handle my brother's kidney versus the stranger's, or my sister versus me, and it doesn't have anything at all to say about my uncle. So — is there a principle underneath these verdicts, or a small number of them, that could be stated crisply? Say which of my cases each piece of it exists to handle, then run it on cases I haven't brought up: whether someone who gives a kidney anonymously into a chain and then won't stop talking about it gets less credit for it; whether the same act can be required of one person and above-and-beyond for another, which is what I seem to be committed to with the nurse; and whether driving for the volunteer outfit has quietly become something I'm obligated to keep doing, given that I've done it for six years and people now count on me, or whether it's still purely optional like it was the first week. And be straight with me about which of my verdicts the principle can't save and which one you'd tell me to give up. I'm not after book recommendations or names of people who've written about this, I just want to know what I'm actually committed to.
