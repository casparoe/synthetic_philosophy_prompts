I run a two-day workshop for people who negotiate shared budgets for a living — mostly local authority and charity types — and I want to hand them a decision tree that sorts out which fairness principle they're actually using when they say 'that split feels about right'. It should start from something everybody accepts (any deal has to beat both sides walking away) and end at a named answer: Nash, Kalai–Smorodinsky, equal-gains egalitarian, maximise-the-sum, leximin, and whatever sits in the equal-concessions family if you think that's genuinely a separate thing rather than KS in disguise.

Build the whole tree on one running example with small numbers they can hold in their heads — say two bodies sharing a £900k pot with walk-away values of £200k and £100k. Every node has to be a question about that case, not about an axiom; nobody's allowed to spot which concept a branch leads to until they've answered. Three branches I specifically want, because that's where my group always splits: what happens to the right answer when the pot changes in a way that only raises one side's best conceivable outcome; whether they're allowed to randomise (lotteries over splits) or the feasible set is just the deterministic options, and what breaks if it stops being convex; and whether the walk-away numbers are treated as a fixed fact about the world or as something the parties themselves manufactured by threatening. That last one should be the branch that leaves the standard tree, with the leaves beyond it being the views that say the disagreement point isn't neutral.

Then two bits of housekeeping. Mark every combination of answers your tree makes available that no well-known principle actually occupies, and say for each whether it's empty because it's incoherent, because it's unattractive, or just because nobody's bothered. And flag the pairs of leaves your numbers cant seperate — with a £900k pot along a straight frontier I think Nash and Kalai–Smorodinsky land in exactly the same place whatever the walk-away values turn out to be, so tell me if I'm wrong about that, and if I'm right, tell me the smallest change to the example that pulls them apart.

Finish with how confident you are in the tree as a whole, and name the single node you're least sure is doing any diagnostic work at all.
