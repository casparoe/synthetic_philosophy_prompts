I cover science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and I'm currently reporting a piece about precommitment — the apps that lock away your phone, the contract clauses that bind a company's future self, and, at the frontier, the proposals for getting advanced AI systems to make credible commitments about their own future behaviour. Tracing the intellectual ancestry of that theme led me, as it always seems to, to Derek Parfit's hitchhiker from the first part of Reasons and Persons: you break down in the desert at night, the only passing driver will take you to the nearest town on condition that you promise to pay him once you arrive, and by the time you are safely in town the money can buy you nothing that you couldn't already have had. I put the case to two people I interviewed separately for the article — Helen, a moral philosopher who works on theories of practical rationality, and Raj, a machine-learning researcher who works on how to design agents that can bind themselves — and when I brought them together over the phone for a follow-up they ended up talking past each other for forty minutes. I've tried to reconstruct their exchange faithfully below, and I'd rather not put either of them in print until I understand which moves actually work.

Here is the argument as best I can render it. (1) Helen opened by saying the case shows that the mistake is to assess rationality one act at a time: if the driver can read your dispositions, the only travellers who get rides are those for whom repayment is not up for renegotiation once they are safe, so it is rational — rational on the traveller's own prudential terms — to become someone for whom paying is not a live option at the moment of choice, and Parfit's real point is that a theory telling you to maximise at each choice point can be indirectly self-defeating, with the remedy being to change the unit of assessment from acts to dispositions or policies. (2) Raj said this restates the problem rather than solving it: grant everything about character formation, but in the town, with the money in your pocket, paying gets you nothing you want and is therefore still irrational by any act-based standard; calling the acquisition of the disposition rational does not transfer rationality to the act, it merely shows that rationality as Helen defines it fails to be consistent across time. (3) Helen replied that consistency across time is precisely what is at issue, that there is nothing incoherent in a policy-based account whose outputs look suboptimal act by act — that is how every precommitment device works — and that Raj's act-based standard, applied at every moment, would also forbid the very precommitment that made the ride possible, so it cannot claim to be more rational, only differently indexed in time. (4) Raj's response was that the disposition view proves too much: if the operative question is whether having a motive would be good for me, then a disposition to surrender a large fraction of my income whenever anyone asks, a disposition to take pride in finishing worthless projects, and — in the epistemic parallel — a disposition to hold comforting beliefs regardless of the evidence all become rational to acquire, which collapses the view into a consequentialism about one's own psychology that cannot tell a rational disposition from a useful self-deception; and if it cannot draw that line, Helen can keep the hitchhiker only by arbitrarily refusing the belief case. (5) Helen said the doxastic parallel is disanalogous twice over: belief aims at truth and is not directly under voluntary control, so it cannot simply be acquired for its benefits, and self-deception is inherently unstable because it depends on concealing evidence from oneself, whereas promise-keeping is stable and public — so Raj's reductio shows at most that some dispositions cannot be bootstrapped, not that the disposition view is false. (6) Raj's last move was that stability and transparency cut against Helen rather than for her: if the driver can read dispositions, then someone whose promise-keeping is held only because it pays will be visible as a fair-weather promise-keeper, exactly the sort who will not pay when it costs, so the view requires you to acquire a non-instrumental motive held for its own sake and retained even when it stops paying — but a motive you must hold for its own sake, on pain of being transparently found out, cannot be justified by the benefits it brings, since the instrumental argument for acquiring it erodes the thing to be acquired; he called this a bootstrapping problem and concluded that the idea of rational irrationality is a temporary illusion that dissolves the moment you inspect it.

What I want from you is a referee's report rather than a summary. Take the six moves in order and give each a verdict — sound, partly sound, or unsound — with a sentence or two of justification, and wherever one of them is sliding between senses of a term without noticing (rationality of acts versus motives versus policies, say, or prudence versus morality, or transparency as an empirical assumption about drivers versus transparency as a philosophical device), say so explicitly and say which sense the move needs in order to work. Then tell me who is more right overall, and whether the disagreement between them is substantive or largely a choice of framework that both would accept if it were named. I would also like you to identify something that neither of them deployed — a distinction, a counterexample, or a third position — that would most change the exchange if it were introduced at move (2), and to tell me, for a non-specialist audience, what the hitchhiker case does and does not establish about rationality. Finally, flag any point at which either of them is misdescribing Parfit's own claim, since I need to characterise his argument accurately in print and I would rather correct it now than after publication.
