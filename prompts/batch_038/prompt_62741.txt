I am the volunteer coordinator for a citywide winter shelter network: roughly 420 members, recruited mostly through congregations and neighborhood associations, and each year we house about 180 people between November and March. Nearly all of our labor comes from members, and nearly all of it comes from a small core — last winter 61 people covered 94 percent of the overnight shifts. Each September the steering committee (three of us, none with any training in ethics beyond an undergraduate course) must set the coming year's ask: how many overnight shifts and how many supporting hours we request of each member, and how the ask may vary between members of different capacities. The committee meets once, in the evening, for about three hours; in November the full membership votes on the ask. The stakes run in both directions. If we ask too little, we turn people away in January. If we ask too much, our reliable people quit and the network collapses within two seasons, which is worse than turning people away.

Our own reasoning is straightforwardly consequentialist: we count beds filled. And the objection our members press against us is precisely the one pressed against consequentialism — that it demands too much of a person. The literature I have read addresses the individual deciding what to give: the fair-share idea that what I owe is what I would owe if everyone did their part; the claim that a person is permitted to give substantial weight to her own projects and relationships; the objection that a theory requiring someone to abandon the commitments that give her life its shape has misdescribed what makes a life go well; the category of the supererogatory. All of it is written for the agent. I have found nothing written for the body that decides what to ask of agents, and that is the translation I need.

Please write a decision procedure our committee can run in a single evening, in plain language, without reference to any sources or to the underlying theory. It should be a numbered sequence of steps with a stated input and a stated output for each, and it should satisfy the following.

First, specify the inputs we can actually gather in September: the number of beds we have committed to (we plan for about 38 beds a night across roughly 105 nights, and each overnight shift needs four volunteers plus a driver, so on the order of 525 shift-slots), the membership count, what we know about members' capacities and constraints, last year's actual compliance, and how many members left after last year's ask. Then specify the outputs: a standing ask per member, a differentiated ask wherever differentiation is justified, and a ceiling above which the committee should shrink the program rather than raise the ask.

Second, tell us what to do about non-compliance. Set against full participation by all 420 members, the arithmetic gives about one overnight shift per member per year; set against the 61 who actually show up, it gives eight or nine. The procedure must state whether the ask is computed against full compliance or against observed compliance, and it must give us the reasoning we can say aloud in November either way — including what we say to the 61.

Third, handle capacity. One of our members is a nurse who can cover shifts with a medical need that no one else can staff, and each additional shift she takes relieves another volunteer. Say how the procedure treats a person whose usefulness makes her hours worth more than others', and be candid about whether that amounts to penalizing competence and to rewarding the competent with more work.

Fourth, handle the member who cannot. We have a member who provides full-time care for an adult son with a severe disability and can offer perhaps two hours a month. Say whether the procedure records her as meeting her share, falling short of it, or standing outside the scheme, and say what that record is for.

Fifth, separate demanding from expecting. Where the committee concludes that a member morally ought to do more than we are prepared to request, say what we are to do with that conclusion — publish it, hold it privately, or discard it.

Sixth, give us a reply to the member who answers the ask by saying the request is supererogatory, that we are asking for something morality does not require, and that this ends the discussion. Either the procedure absorbs that objection or it concedes; say which, and say why, and say whether the answer differs depending on how many other members have already said it.

Seventh, mark every step that is a value judgment dressed as a calculation, and mark separately the steps at which the procedure runs out and the committee simply has to choose.

Then run the procedure end to end on three cases.

Case A: an ordinary September with the numbers above, 61 reliable volunteers, and the nurse willing to take more.

Case B: an emergency in mid-January. A cold snap doubles the number of people seeking beds for nine consecutive days. We can reach only about 40 members by telephone, and the reliable 61 have already given exactly what the September ask specified and have said they are spent.

Case C: a proposal from a member that we raise the ceiling and accept a contract for 60 additional beds. The contract would fund a part-time staff position and take pressure off volunteers, but we would be obliged to honor it even in a bad year, and honoring it in a bad year would fall on the same 61 people.

For at least one of these the procedure should reach a conclusion the committee will not like, and you should state it plainly rather than soften it. Say also where the procedure produces no verdict at all.

Finally, state what the procedure cannot do: which questions it appears to settle but does not; the ways a committee that already knows what it wants can steer it to that answer through the inputs; which of its steps will be contested by members who accept everything it says about the arithmetic; and the specific feature of the demandingness debate — the part that depends on the ask being made by morality rather than by an organization with a budget, a vote, and a need to retain its volunteers — that is lost in the translation.
