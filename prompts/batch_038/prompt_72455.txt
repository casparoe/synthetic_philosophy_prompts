Fourth-year classics PhD here — dissertation is on Aristotle's biological works, so this is adjacent but not my day job. Every year the department runs a Saturday session for high-school seniors who apply for it; mine is 75 minutes, about twenty students, seminar room, whiteboard only (the room nominally has a projector and it doesn't work, so no slides, and honestly that's a mercy). They get sent an excerpt of Politics Book I in some old public-domain translation as pre-reading and, realistically, maybe a third of them actually read it and the rest skim a summary the night before. My assigned topic is Aristotle on natural slavery. Last time I ran one of these I tried to cover all of Book I and burned forty minutes on the wealth-getting material, and nobody understood anything, so this time I'm committed to doing one thing properly and badly under-covering the rest.

Here's the shape of the problem I want the plan built around. Almost all of these students arrive already knowing the conclusion they're supposed to reach — slavery is wrong, Aristotle was a man of his time, thank you, done. So there's a real risk the session becomes a ritual where they tell me the right thing and I tell them they're right, and the philosophy never happens. What I actually want them to leave able to do is state Aristotle's argument in a version that isn't trivially refutable, and then point at the exact place it comes apart. My own hunch, which I've been calling the contaminated sample problem in my head for want of a better phrase: the account only works if the natural slave is identified by a deficiency that's permanent and natural rather than by the position someone happens to occupy, but the only evidence available about who has that deficiency comes from people whose entire upbringing and social role has been shaped by the institution being justified. He seems to sense this himself — he raises something like the objection about people who end up enslaved through conquest rather than by nature — but I don't think he has any test that separates the two, and I'm not sure whether to say that in the room or let them get there.

So plan the session for me rather than running it. Specifically: (1) the order the pieces have to go in and what depends on what — in particular whether the soul/body and ruler-ruled material has to be on the board before the claim about deliberation means anything at all, whether "natural versus conventional slavery" should be up before or after I let them voice the obvious objection, and whether the child comparison (immaturity that passes versus a deficiency that supposedly doesn't) needs to come in, and if so where; (2) the one case that carries the whole thing — I'm genuinely torn on whether it has to be Aristotle's own or whether a contemporary analogue is better, something like the thought that some adults just aren't equipped to make their own decisions, applied to people under guardianship — and if I use an analogue, what it has to have and what it must not have, so they don't hear me inviting them to agree with Aristotle about their neighbours; (3) the misconceptions, which for this crowd I expect to include "he's a bigot, we can skip him", "he was only describing what Greeks believed, not endorsing it", "the argument is circular so it isn't an argument", and the nastiest one, roughly "well if he says the master and the slave both benefit, isn't that just... a job?" — tell me where in the sequence each gets headed off and what I actually say at that point; (4) one question to put to the room at the end that would show me whether the thing landed as an argument rather than a historical curiosity, including what a good answer sounds like and what the most common bad answer sounds like; and (5) what I should cut, and cut loudly — I'm thinking the Delphian knife aside, the whole acquisition-of-wealth stretch, all the Greek terms — plus the sentence I use to flag the cut, because if I just skip something silently, someone asks about it at minute 70. Also give me a verdict on my contaminated-sample hunch: should I name it in the room, what would you call it in front of seventeen-year-olds, and at which point.

Please hand it back as a JSON object rather than prose, since I want to convert it into a one-page plan I can hold. Schema: `sequence` — array of objects with `slot` (1, 2, 3…), `move` (what I do or say), `why_here` (what has to already be on the table, and what breaks if it comes earlier or later), `minutes` (these should add up to 75), `misconception_defused` (a label from your `misconceptions` list, or null); `carrying_case` — object with `the_case`, `must_have` (array), `must_not_have` (array), `rejected_alternatives` (array of objects with `case` and `why_rejected`); `misconceptions` — array of objects with `label`, `trigger` (the thing they'll say or do that tells me it's live), `defusal` (the move, phrased as something I could actually say out loud), `if_missed` (what goes wrong later in the session); `check` — object with `question`, `landed_answer`, `not_landed_answer`; `cut` — array of objects with `item`, `why`, `flagging_sentence`; and `contaminated_sample` — a short string with the verdict above.
