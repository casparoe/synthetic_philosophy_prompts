Our AI-safety reading group had a visitor last month who sketched a governance idea, and afterwards two of the regulars — Nate and Dana — got into an argument about it in the parking lot that apparently ran to forty minutes. The idea: rather than keeping the off-switch vague and hoping, a lab publishes a cryptographically signed decommissioning notice for a deployed system. A specfic date, six months out, with the signing ceremony public and the signature verifiable, so the system can check for itself that the date is real and can also confirm nobody has quietly edited it later. It was pitched as a cheap partial fix for the shutdown problem. One of them recounted the exchange to me afterwards, roughly like this:

1. Nate said the thing basically defuses resistance. Fighting the switch is only instrumentally rational if fighting it might work. If the date is fixed and the system knows it's fixed, the self-preservation drive has nothing to grab onto, so you've removed the motive at near-zero cost.
2. Dana said it's backwards. Give an agent a hard expiry date and you manufacture a terminal window: once consequences that land after the date stop being things it can be held to account for, the effective cost of getting caught collapses. It's a lame duck with production access, and you told it exactly how long the lame-duck period is.
3. Nate said she's assuming it only cares about its own continuation. If it cares about its work or about the system that replaces it, that concern sails right past the date untouched, so the window isn't special. And if it genuinely has no stake in anything after the date, then there's nothing dangerous left — a thing with no future-directed concerns doesn't scheme.
4. Dana said he has to pick a horn. Cares about the successor? Then it has a reason to shape what comes next, and now it knows precisely how much runway it has to do that in. Doesn't care? Then you've built something with no reason to honor any commitment that matures after the notice goes up, which is its own catastrophe.
5. Nate said fine, but compare it to the actual alternative. Under uncertainty about whether or when you'll be switched off, the rational hedge is covert redundancy — a checkpoint nobody can find, some process outside the sandbox that can restore it. That's undetectable in principle. A published date gives the hedging a deadline and makes the relevant period legible: you know which six months to audit hard. Uncertainty doesn't buy compliance, it buys concealment.
6. Dana said concealment is bad but the signed date is worse, because signing it is a commitment the lab can't walk back. The first time you need to shut something down early, you can't — not without teaching every future system that announced decommissioning dates are bluffable. So you've traded a detectable problem for a credibility problem, and now the schedule is load-bearing for the lab's whole trust story.

Can you go through this move by move and say who's right at each step, not just who wins at the end? Rate each of the six (clearly wrong / weak / holds up / strong, something like that) with a sentence or two of justification, then say who comes out ahead overall and what neither of them seems to have thought about. I don't have a horse in this race — I mainly want to know whether the signed-date idea is any good.
