I am in the fourth year of a philosophy of mind PhD, and the second chapter of my dissertation is supposed to answer the question of what makes pain bad. My supervisor has now told me twice that the chapter "has no spine," and I have come to think he is right in a diagnosable way: I keep moving back and forth between candidate accounts — the hedonic-tone or felt-quality view, evaluativism, imperativism, Hall's inclination-based account, Grahek's two-factor proposal — without ever having fixed what any of them is being asked to deliver. So before I revise, I want the specification rather than the scorecard. Please write out the desiderata: the list of requirements an account of pain's unpleasantness and badness must satisfy to count as adequate. The purpose I have in mind is deliberately double, and you should treat it as fixing the standard of adequacy. The account must be good enough to settle a philosophy-of-mind question — what, if anything, distinguishes unpleasant pain from merely intense bodily sensation, and what explains why we have reason to stop it — and good enough to underwrite claims I want to make in a neuroethics section about who can be harmed by noxious stimulation, including patients with insular damage, neonates, and non-human animals. If those two purposes pull the requirement list in incompatible directions, I need that stated rather than papered over. Two things I want policed throughout. First, the distinctions my chapter keeps collapsing: (a) being in pain; (b) the sensory-discriminative character of pain; (c) its unpleasantness; (d) felt aversiveness, or minding it; (e) an inclination or desire that it stop; (f) actual avoidance behaviour; (g) badness for the subject's well-being; (h) the subject's having a reason to relieve it. For each desideratum, say which of these it constrains and which it licenses an account to separate. Second, the modal profile: whether the tie between pain and unpleasantness is claimed to be conceptual, metaphysical, or merely nomologically robust. Hall's 1989 argument that pains are not necessarily unpleasant turns on exactly this, and an account that never declares its modal commitments cannot be assessed against him.

Here is the material I want the list anchored to, because the stock puzzles are already in my draft and I need the harder cases. Pain asymbolia: the syndrome Berthier, Starkstein and Leiguarda characterized in 1988 as a sensory-limbic disconnection following unilateral insular and opercular damage, in which patients recognize a stimulus as painful and localize it, yet show no withdrawal, no distress and no subsequent avoidance, sometimes smiling or laughing under pinprick, while still managing to protect the affected limb by deliberation rather than by reflex. Its mirror image, the dissociation Grahek discusses under the heading of painfulness without pain. Anterior cingulotomy patients who report that the pain is still present and qualitatively unchanged but that they no longer mind it. Hypnotic suggestions that selectively reduce unpleasantness ratings while leaving intensity ratings intact, with anterior cingulate activity tracking the affective rating rather than the sensory one (Rainville and colleagues, 1997). Opioid analgesia, where first-person reports tend to have the same present-but-not-bothering shape. Masochism and the chili-burn case: pains that are actively sought and enjoyed without the subject's denying that they are pains. Congenital insensitivity to pain, where the open question is which component is missing. And the part my draft is weakest on: there is now a live challenge to the standard reading of asymbolia, alleging that it is not a unified syndrome at all, that some reported patients may not be having pain experiences in the first place, and that the deficit may lie in bodily self-representation or in the concepts and reporting capacities needed to describe an experience rather than in the affective component of the experience itself. A requirement list that quietly assumes the standard reading is doing philosophy on borrowed evidence, so I want a desideratum forcing an account to declare which reading of that literature it depends on, and what would discriminate between the readings — lesion-mapping specificity, the reliability of retrospective report, dissociation between avoidance learning and verbal rating, and so forth.

Each requirement should say what it excludes, not merely what it wants: a specific claim or move that would fail it. I also want you to flag any item that is really a prejudice of one camp rather than a neutral demand — for instance, whether the insistence that unpleasantness be intrinsic to the experience is doing work for subjectivists that objectivists about pain's badness are entitled to refuse. Then, in prose after the list: identify any subset of the desiderata you believe is jointly unsatisfiable and show the conflict rather than asserting it. My own suspicion is that requiring unpleasantness to be intrinsic to the experience, so that it can do reason-giving and welfare work from the inside, is incompatible with requiring it to be independently modifiable, so that it can come apart in asymbolia, cingulotomy, hypnosis and masochism; if the conflict is real, tell me whether the list is then a negative result about the whole project, and if it can be escaped, tell me which requirement must be weakened and what explanatory work we lose by weakening it. Finally, name the single desideratum that eliminates the most candidates.

Please give the main list as a markdown table with exactly these columns: ID (D1, D2, and so on); desideratum (one sentence, phrased as a requirement on an account); motivator (the case or consideration behind it, named concretely); what_it_excludes (a specific claim or move that fails it); status (one of non-negotiable, negotiable, camp-specific); if_camp_specific (which camp demands it and why it is a prejudice rather than a requirement, otherwise leave blank); constrains (the letters from (a) through (h) that the requirement bears on). Follow the table with the prose sections described above, and then a compact scorecard, no more than one line per contender, as a second table with columns view | fails_IDs | one_line_verdict, covering the felt-quality view, evaluativism, imperativism, Hall's inclination account, Grahek's two-factor proposal, and the view that pain's badness consists in its disruption of the subject's agency. Skip the reading list entirely and do not invent citations; the standard labels for the views are all I need.
