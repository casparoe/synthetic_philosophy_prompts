I sit on the editorial board of a journal that publishes a great many adaptationist claims about human psychology, and I want to replace our one-paragraph reviewer guidance with a real specification. Write the desiderata that an adaptationist explanation — a claim that trait T was shaped by natural selection for the function it now performs — must meet before a reviewer should let it through, given the force of the Gould–Lewontin spandrels critique.

Two things I care about especially. First, that critique gestures at several distinct non-adaptive sources of organic form — architectural byproduct, co-option of a structure that arose for some other purpose, developmental and allometric constraint, phylogenetic legacy, drift — and I suspect our reviewers treat these as a single bucket called "alternatives to selection." The list should force a submitted claim to say which of these it has ruled out and by what means. Second, make the list sensitive to the gap between a trait's origin and its maintenance: a trait can be superbly engineered for what it does now and still not be an adaptation, and a genuine adaptation can be functionless in the environment of its subjects today.

For each desideratum, state the failure mode it exists to catch and give one concrete illustration drawn from popular explanations of human behaviour. Then mark which requirements are non-negotiable and which are the preferences of particular camps, and identify any pair you think cannot be satisfied jointly — my hunch is that the demand for genuinely testable alternatives pulls against the demand for evidence about the actual historical origin, but convince me or correct me. Finish with a short assessment of how current practice in evolutionary psychology measures up.

End with a last line of the form "Verdict: " naming the single desideratum that does the most to exclude otherwise plausible claims.
