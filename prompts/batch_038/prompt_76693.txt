So I work at a place that does political risk forecasting, and we inherited our report format more or less wholesale from the government intelligence style: every key judgment gets two separate marks, not one. The first is a word off a fixed ladder saying how likely the event is -- something in the "probable / likely / roughly even odds / unlikely" family, which internally everyone agrees corresponds to a rough band of percentages. The second is a completely seperate word, low / moderate / high, saying how confident we are in the judgment, and that one is officially about something else entirely: how good and how corroborated the sources are, how big the gaps in our collection are, and whether the topic is even the kind of thing that can be analyzed well rather than just guessed at.

The thing I keep tripping over is that these two axes get used as if they were independent, but I can't tell what that means. If our confidence is low because the sources are thin and the gaps are huge, then what exactly is the likelihood word a report of? Is it a sharp probability that exists out there and we just haven't pinned it down -- in which case the second axis is really about our estimate of our own estimate -- or is it that the likelihood word was always a compressed way of naming a range, and the confidence word is a coded report of how wide that range is? Those seem like very different pictures of what probability even is, and the format doesn't say which one it's assuming.

And there's a leak in practice. Analysts at my shop quietly downgrade the likelihood word when the evidence is thin -- a judgment that would be "likely" on good sources gets written as "possible" on bad ones, even though nothing about the world changed. So the axes aren't actually independent; the evidential stuff is seeping into the probability word. But then the confidence axis is doing the same work again, and I don't see what it's adding. Meanwhile the two get weighed very differently downstream: "unlikely, high confidence" gets treated as actionable and changes what clients do, while "likely, low confidence" gets shelved until collection improves. Which looks like the second axis is doing the real decision work that the first axis is supposed to be doing.

So -- what picture of probability and evidence does this whole format presuppose, once you make it explicit? I'm after a few things specifically:

Does printing a likelihood word for a one-off, non-repeatable geopolitical event presuppose that there's a fact of the matter about the probability of that single event at all, and if so which kind -- somebody's coherent degree of belief, a real single-case tendency, something else? Which views of probability does the format quietly rule out just by being usable?

Does it presuppose that the strength or weight of your evidence is a genuinely seperate dimension from the magnitude of your probability, so that two bodies of evidence can license the same number and differ in some further respect? Is that a commitment you can cash out formally, or is it just a reporting convenience? Try to give me a reconstruction in which both axes carry real, non-redundant content -- sets of priors instead of single numbers, or something along those lines -- and then tell me honestly whether the reconstruction makes the confidence axis necessary or makes it disappear.

Is the format vulnerable to the weird thing where a range gets wider after you learn more, so new information makes you less determinate even though you didn't learn anything false? Our practice of dropping confidence when a judgment becomes more contested sounds to me like exactly that, and I want to know if that's a bug in the format or a bug in my understanding of what the confidence word is supposed to track.

Finally: suppose we scrapped the two-axis thing and just printed an explicit interval for every judgment, like "40-70%", with no seperate confidence word. What would survive that swap and what would be lost that the interval can't encode? And what would readers have to start doing differently -- is the interval enough to tell them when to act, or does the shelving behavior come back wearing a different hat?

Give your overall assessment of your own answer in the shop's vocabulary -- a likelihood-style word for how right you think you are, plus low/moderate/high confidence, with a sentence on what that confidence marking is based on -- and then single out the one claim in your response you'd mark lowest confidence on and say why. I'd rather you flag the soft spots yourself than have me find them.
