Two people on our town's board of zoning appeals got into it at a public meeting last month, it never got resolved, and I've been turning it over ever since. I was just sitting in the back of the room, so I'm reporting it rather than taking a side.

Background: the board has five appointed members and votes yes/no on variance requests. A couple years back somebody did an informal study comparing the board's decisions to what the state appeals board eventually did with the same cases. Each of the five members' individual votes lined up with the eventual outcome about 60 percent of the time, and the five-person majority lined up about 80 percent of the time. Now there's a proposal to expand the board to fifteen by adding ten residents drawn at random from the voter rolls, sortition style, with a two week period in which all fifteen meet and deliberate together before voting.

Dana is for it, and her argument went like this: individual competence is above one-half, we measured it at 0.6, and Condorcet says that as a jury grows the majority's reliability approaches certainty, so adding ten more members can only help. She said the town is leaving a free improvement on the table.

Omar said that's exactly the wrong inference. His first point was that 0.6 was measured on people who were appointed for their experience, and Dana is transferring it onto ten people nobody has tested. If their competence is below one-half the theorem runs backwards and a bigger board is worse than a smaller one, so the expansion isn't a free improvement, it's a bet.

Dana said fine, but you can't assume they're incompetent either, the burden runs both ways. And she said even if the ten were literally coin flippers they'd be harmless, because a bloc that carries no information can't make the majority less likely to be right than the five already are. Worst case you're adding noise, and noise averages out.

Omar said noise doesn't average out when it outnumbers the signal. Ten coin flippers plus five members at 0.6 isn't a slightly worse version of the old board, it's a board where the outcome is settled by the flippers and the five competent votes are a permanent minority. And he said that was the optimistic case, because thier votes aren't going to be independent anyway: all fifteen hear the same staff report at the same hearing and then spend two weeks in a room together, and the amplification effect depends on independence. Correlated errors don't get averaged away, they get voted together.

Dana said then change the design: draw the ten from different precincts, let them read the materials separately, keep the secret ballot. And she said Omar was proving too much, because if independence is that fragile then the current board doesn't have it either, those five having sat in the same room together for years, so his objection condemns the status quo he's defending.

Omar's last move was against the 0.6 number itself. He pointed out that the board approves roughly three out of five requests and the state board also ends up siding with applicants about that often, so a member who voted approve every single time without reading anything would score 0.6 against that benchmark. Which means the study may not have measured competence at all, just agreement with the base rate.

Dana said that cuts against him too: if 0.6 doesn't establish competence for the incumbents, then the theorem never got off the ground for the existing board either, and Omar has no grounds for claiming five is better than fifteen. She added that she has reasons for the expansion that have nothing to do with Condorcet, like the fact that people trust a board that looks like the town. Omar said that was a concession. She said it wasn't, it was an additional argument, not a replacement for the first one.
