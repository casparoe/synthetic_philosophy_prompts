I'm TAing a second-year game theory section and we just did Nash's bargaining solution — maximize the product of the two sides' gains over the disagreement point, all four axioms on the board. Afterwards one of my students asked why we don't instead split things "in proportion to what each side could realistically have hoped for," and I told her that's basically the Kalai–Smorodinsky solution from 1975: you throw out independence of irrelevant alternatives, put in a monotonicity condition, and take the point where the straight line from the disagreement point to the ideal point (each player's best feasible payoff, given the other at least clears their walkaway) crosses the Pareto frontier — same thing as the deal where both parties get an equal *fraction* of their maximum possible gain. The axioms did nothing for her. "Equal fraction of what you could have hoped for" made her sit up. So I want to hand her a story instead of a proof, and I want it to be good enough that I can also give it to the guys who think bargaining theory is just arithmetic with a moral attached.

Write a short story, somewhere around 1500–2000 words, that makes the gap between the Nash answer and the Kalai–Smorodinsky answer to *one and the same* bargaining problem feel like a gap between two kinds of fairness rather than two formulas. Hard constraint: nobody in the story names either solution, writes anything down that looks like math, or says words like "axiom," "Pareto," or "utopia point" — the theory has to be in the shape of the argument, not the vocabulary. Setting seed, change what you like: drought year, one river, an almond grower holding a senior water right and a small town whose wells are going brackish, and a semi-retired county watermaster mediating who keeps sketching the feasable set in the dust on the hood of his truck with a stick. Make the disagreement point concrete and bad for everybody — years of litigation with a curtailment order in force while it runs, so both sides have a floor to fall back to and the floor is miserable. Then, two-thirds of the way in, drop an event that expands only one party's best case: a data-center operator offers to buy the grower's whole allocation at a number nobody saw coming, or the state announces a grant that would pay for the town's treatment plant. Nothing about the frontier near where the deal will actually land has changed; only one side's "most I could ever hope for" jumps. Have two characters fight over whether that changes what's owed — one insisting a possibility nobody was ever going to take is irrelevant to how you split what's actually on the table, the other insisting the whole menu matters, that you can't ask someone whose hopes just got bigger to settle for the same slice they'd have accepted yesterday. Don't resolve it with a speech or a wise-old-mediator coin flip. Let the deal they finally strike be recognizably one of the two answers, and let the person who argued for the other one notice, out loud or not.

After the story, give me a short table I can project in section, with exactly these columns: **Beat** (3–6 word label for the moment), **What happens** (one sentence), **Theory content** (which feature of the bargaining problem it dramatizes — the disagreement point, the shape of the frontier, the ideal point, the equal-ratio-of-gains reading, the product-of-gains reading, the irrelevant-alternatives objection, the monotonicity objection), **Nash or KS** (which solution that beat's reasoning lines up with, or "both" / "neither"), and **What I'd say in section** (one plain sentence of how I'd use the beat to teach the point). Four to six rows, and keep the last column free of jargon.
