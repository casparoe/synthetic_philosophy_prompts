I am preparing a session for a graduate seminar on the philosophy of uncertainty, and I have become fixated on a pair of documents that a single inflation-targeting central bank publishes each quarter. Let me stipulate the details so that nothing depends on your knowing the institutional background. Document A is the inflation forecast, presented as a fan chart: a central projection for inflation several years out, surrounded by shaded bands demarcating, in the bank's own description, the deciles of a probability distribution — the outermost band covers outcomes to which the bank assigns ninety percent probability, and so inward. As I understand the construction, the widths of these bands are not derived from any model of the economy's behaviour; they are derived from the pooled distribution of the errors that the bank's past forecasts have actually made, scaled up by a discretionary factor that the committee sets by hand, on the stated ground that the past record of errors may itself be a poor guide to the future spread of errors. The bank tells readers that the numbers attached to the bands represent the committee's best judgment of the likelihood of each range of outcomes, and it treats the resulting distribution as something against which the bank's forecasts can later be scored. Document B is the bank's financial-stability stress test. Here the same institution names a handful of severely adverse scenarios — a deep recession combined with a disorderly unwind in asset markets, a collapse in commercial property values, a prolonged operational disruption — and asks how the banking system would fare in each. It assigns no likelihoods whatsoever to these scenarios, insists that they are not predictions, and says in effect that attaching probabilities to them would convey a precision that the institution does not possess.

My seminar students found this juxtaposition baffling, and so, honestly, did I. In one department, profound uncertainty about the economy is rendered as a single additive probability distribution over outcomes, complete with a hand-turned dial for the uncertainty about the uncertainty. In the other, comparably profound uncertainty is rendered as a set of named possibilities with no numbers at all. Both documents issue from the same committee structure and are defended as epistemically responsible. My working hypothesis, which I would like you to test rather than accept, is that Document A's discretionary widening factor is not in fact an acknowledgment of ambiguity in the sense familiar from the two-urn experiment we discussed — where subjects bet on drawing a red ball and prefer the urn whose composition is known to be half red even though they have no reason whatever to think the unknown urn less favourable — but is instead a device for reabsorbing ambiguity into risk, so that the institution can continue to present one distribution and be scored against it. On my hypothesis, Document B is the philosophically honest of the two, because it declines the reabsorption.

What I want is a reconstruction of the theory of uncertainty that Document A presupposes, and a judgment on whether that theory is one the bank is committed to or merely one that fits the artefact comfortably. Specifically: what must be true of uncertainty, and of the relationship between past error and future error, for a pooled historical-error fan chart with a discretionary widening to be a legitimate representation of a committee's beliefs rather than a decorative one; what does the widening factor represent, and can a single number scaled by hand coherently stand in for uncertainty about the shape of a distribution; which rival accounts of uncertainty — a multiple-priors or maximin account, an account on which some uncertainty is unquantifiable in principle and should be handled by robustness rather than by numbers, and a strict subjective-Bayesian account on which the committee already has a single credence function and the historical errors are irrelevant to it — are quietly ruled out by the practice, and which would in fact license exactly the same picture? Then diagnose the apparent inconsistency between the two documents: is it a genuine contradiction in the institution's epistemology, two different questions answered under one heading, two different standards of evidence, or no inconsistency at all once the differing purposes of communication and resilience-testing are taken into account? Say whether either arm would survive being rebuilt on the other's presuppositions, and tell me concretely what Document A would have to become if the bank adopted a multiple-priors representation, or abandoned numbers altogether in the manner of Document B — including what it would lose in the way of accountability, and whether what it loses is worth keeping. Where the practice is genuinely underdetermined, say so plainly rather than manufacturing a single implicit theory. Please write this as connected prose of the kind I could hand to my students, and then append two structured summaries: first, a table with the columns feature_of_practice, candidate_presupposition, status (one of strictly_required, compatible_among_several, incidental), rival_view_excluded, and change_required_if_replaced; and second, a JSON object with the fields implicit_theory_of_uncertainty, strictly_presupposed (array of strings), merely_compatible (array of strings), views_quietly_excluded (array of strings), treatment_of_the_widening_factor, diagnosis_of_the_inconsistency (one of real_contradiction, different_questions, different_evidential_standards, no_inconsistency), verdict_on_my_hypothesis, what_would_be_lost_without_numbers, and single_most_contestable_step (the one premise in my hypothesis you think most likely to be wrong).
