Consider the four counts of the Nuremberg indictment — the common plan or conspiracy count, crimes against peace, war crimes, and crimes against humanity — and rank them by how exposed each is to the *nullum crimen, nulla poena* objection: that is, by how much of the count's legal work had to be supplied by the tribunal's own construction rather than by law already binding on Germany when the charged conduct occurred. Assign each count a score from 0 (essentially immune to the objection) to 1 (wholly dependent on retroactive construction), with a sentence or two justifying each placement, and do not resolve your uncertainty by giving every count the same number.

In scoring the fourth count, take account of the Charter's requirement that crimes against humanity be committed in execution of or in connection with other crimes within the tribunal's jurisdiction, and of what the tribunal consequently made of persecution predating the outbreak of war; also say whether the 1915 joint Allied declaration on the Armenian massacres gives that count more pedigree than its absence from any treaty would suggest.

Then answer two further questions. First, does your ordering shift for Tokyo — in particular for its broader crimes-against-peace conspiracy charge, its murder charges resting on command responsibility, and the dissent that would have acquitted the defendants outright? Second, on which count, if any, is the retroactivity charge largely a red herring, obscuring a more serious complaint about the proceedings, such as the exclusion of the victors' own conduct or the jurisdictional narrowing of the fourth count?
