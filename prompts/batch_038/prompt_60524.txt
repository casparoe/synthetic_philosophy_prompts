I sit as the lay member on the Health Sciences Research Ethics Committee of a public university in the Eastern Cape, and I have been asked to circulate a written opinion before Thursday's meeting on a protocol that has left us deadlocked at four–four, with the chair unwilling to break the tie until he has seen the argument in writing. Our standing operating procedures were revised three years ago to include a clause that is unusual for a South African REC: it directs the Committee, when reviewing protocols sited in communities with recognised traditional leadership, to evaluate them under a relational account of personhood in the ubuntu tradition — an account on which personhood is not a binary status one holds from birth by virtue of species membership, but something one grows into through incorporation into a network of reciprocal obligations, and something that can therefore be more or less fully realised, or allowed to atrophy. The clause has never been interpreted in a decided case, and it says nothing at all about consent. This protocol is the first to force the question, and it does so from an unexpected direction.

The study is a five-year longitudinal investigation of cognitive ageing across three villages, involving repeated testing and annual blood draws. The principal investigator, Dr Baloyi, argues in her cover letter that under a relational account the signed consent form is not the moral event the standard REC template treats it as, and she draws a distinction the Committee keeps circling without resolving: consent as *authorization*, which is the permission-slip a bounded self hands over for something to be done to it, versus consent as *incorporation*, whereby a person is admitted or readmitted into a web of mutual obligation and thereby becomes someone to whom the community is answerable. On that footing she proposes three things. First, she will seek the approval of each village's traditional council at the outset and treat it as genuine gatekeeping consent rather than ceremony, and she has told us that in one village the council expects to see the data before any publication. Second, for roughly forty residents the team has identified locally as people "without others" — a widower who has lived alone since a land dispute in 2011, three adults who returned after decades in the city and are still treated as strangers, and a scattering of others — she proposes enrolment on the strength of the council's authorization plus a simplified assent, on the explicit ground that under the very account she is invoking these residents are persons in a diminished degree and cannot be the sole authors of a decision about themselves. Third, and pulling the other way, for participants with moderate dementia who remain densely embedded in kin networks she insists that their own assent be sought and renewed at every visit and that a proxy's authorization alone is insufficient, because their personhood is sustained precisely by those relations and overriding it would begin to sever them. At a community meeting in the second village, meanwhile, a group of younger women objected that a council decision should not bind them over the testing and the blood draws, and Dr Baloyi's protocol is silent on whether their dissent is recorded, honoured, or overridden.

Please draft the opinion I will circulate — around 800 words, no bibliography and no citations, and nothing that reads as a survey of the literature on ubuntu. It must close on a definite recommendation, approve, reject, or approve with conditions, and if it is conditions they must be specific enough that Dr Baloyi could tell by Monday morning whether she has met them: what the team must actually do about the forty "without others," what it must do about renewed assent in the dementia cases, what status the council's approval has relative to individual dissent, and what happens to the data-sharing expectation. Two further things it has to settle. It should say plainly whether our clause can bear the weight Dr Baloyi puts on it or whether the clause itself should be referred back to the Committee for revision, and if the latter, what a workable version would have to say. And it should give the strongest form to the objection from my colleague on the other side — a clinician who is not hostile to ubuntu, but who holds that any account on which moral standing can be earned or forfeited is disqualified from research ethics, because the people it demotes are invariably the ones least able to protest, and who would rather we retreat to the moderate communitarian position associated with Gyekye, on which individuals have a standing they do not derive from the community even though their character and their obligations are shaped within it. State that objection at its best, then say why the recommendation survives it, or concede that it does not.
