So — 25 minute slot at an interdisciplinary workshop in about three weeks, co-hosted by a law school's AI center together with the CS dept, and im honestly dreading the Q&A a lot more than the talk itself. Here's the thesis I'm defending: the familiar impossibility results in algorithmic fairness — the ones showing that when base rates differ across groups you can't have calibration-within-groups, equal error rates, and demographic-parity-style independence all at once — get taught and cited as if they'd established a *trade-off*, like fairness is a dial and every practitioner has to pick a setting. I think that's a category mistake. The criteria in question are properties of different objects: some are properties of a score, some are properties of a decision rule, and some are really properties of the whole pipeline that manufactures the label you're scoring against. Once you separate the objects, the theorem stops telling you to choose a metric and starts telling you that the metric has to attach to the *decision*, while the score can stay calibrated and untouched. So my positive proposal is: keep your calibrated risk score, and make the allocation rule on top of it openly group-sensitive, with the group-sensitivity itself having to survive a public justification about who bears which errors. The impossibility largely "dissolves" for a big class of cases — but only because I've now explicitly endorsed differential treatment by group, which is (a) close to legally radioactive under disparate treatment doctrine, and (b) going to be heard by most of the ML half of the room as a confession that I smuggled something like affirmative action in through the back door of the statistics.

The room will be maybe 25 people. Roughly half are ML researchers, two of whom have published in exactly this literature and are thouroughly bored of hearing their own theorems restated by someone who isn't a theorist; there's one employment-discrimination law professor; one economist who works on statistical discrimination and will care about incentives, about gaming, and about what happens to the data once your rule is public and knowable; and two people from legal-services / advocacy orgs who work on tenant screening and credit scoring, and who mostly care about which population ends up eating the false negatives. Nobody in the room is a philosopher, though a couple of them have picked up some of the vocabulary secondhand and will use it slightly wrong.
