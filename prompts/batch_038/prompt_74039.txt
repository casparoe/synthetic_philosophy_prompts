I'm on a small working group drafting a policy on official apologies, and before we start squabbling about individual cases I'd like to know what we'd even be squabbling about. So: write the spec. What does a theory of state apology have to deliver before it's any use to us?

Give me the requirements list, each one with the case or consideration behind it — what the theory has to explain (why some official apologies seem to change something and others are just noise), which distinctions it can't collapse, which plain facts about states it can't contradict. Then mark which requirements are non-negotiable and which are really one camp's commitments dressed up as criteria, and if some subset of them can't be jointly satisfied, say so and say which one gets dropped — I've a feeling that's where the actual answer is hiding.

Two things make our situation unusual, and I want the list built around them rather than around the easy textbook cases:

1. The state doing the apologising isn't the state that did the wrong, and not merely because of the usual gap of generations. Think of a successor state that came into being partly *because* of the wrong — founded by people the old regime persecuted, now governing the territory, treaties, reserves, buildings, debts and legal obligations it inherited from that regime, while officially treating the regime as illegitimate and itself as one of the regime's victims. So it keeps the assets and repudiates the deeds. A theory that demands identity with the wrongdoer makes apology impossible for such a state; a theory that says repudiation is enough lets it keep everything and pay nothing. I need the list to say what it owes us here instead of dodging between those two.

2. Some of the wrongs are ongoing — the policy being apologised for hasn't stopped, or has stopped only in the sense that its effects are still being administered by the same office that's now saying sorry. So don't let the requirements assume an apology is a one-off speech act about a finished past. Tell me what has to be true of a state for an apology about a still-running practice to be anything other than a performance.

Also, please don't let the list quietly assume the apologising state is a democracy speaking on behalf of consenting citizens. Half our comparators aren't. I'm not convinced the criteria change, but if you think they do, flag which requirement is doing the changing and why.

Once you've got the list: name the single requirement that knocks out the most otherwise plausible theories, and say what giving it up would cost us. And briefly — very briefly, a few lines, I don't want a scorecard — how the two accounts people actually reach for in practice do against it: the one that treats the state as a continuous corporate person who can repent, and the one that treats apology as a gesture aimed at the living rather than at the dead.

British English please. No reading list, no citations — we'll do our own homework, I just want the criteria.
