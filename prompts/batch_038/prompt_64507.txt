I write historical novels — four published, all eighteenth century — and I've just delivered the back matter for the new one, which is built around the Lisbon earthquake of 1755. The back matter is an Author's Note, and if you read enough historical fiction you know the species inside out: I say which characters are real and which I made up, I confess that I compressed two seperate voyages into one, I promise that no documented date has been moved, and I list the histories and memoirs I read. There's a sentence that turns up in every one of these notes, roughly "where the record is silent, I have imagined," which I've now written four times without ever thinking about it. Then there's the unwritten ranking of offenses, which is the part that's actually been eating me. Shifting the earthquake by a fortnight would be a scandal — my editor would make me change it, reviewers would flag it, I'd have to apologize for it in the note. Inventing an entire character who stands on the waterfront watching the river pull back, a person who never existed and whose every word I made up, requires no apology at all and nobody blinks. The public speeches of real people get kept word for word; their private conversations I invent wholesale, and that counts as completely normal.

My brother-in-law teaches historiography and he says the note is philosophically incoherent, and that the ranking is the tell. His pitch, roughly: I'm performing fidelity to the archive — Ranke's ideal of showing the past as it essentially was — in exactly the places where fidelity is cheapest and does no interpretive work, while the thing that actually determines what my novel claims about 1755 is the shape I give the story, which I never mention and never confess to. He keeps bringing up Carr's point that a fact of the past only becomes a historical fact by being selected and used, so Caesar crossing the Rubicon counts and the thousands of other people who crossed it don't; and White's stronger claim that choosing a plot structure is a literary operation, not a factual one, and not truth-apt in the way my note assumes facts are. On that view my scruple about dates is a fetish and my little bibliography is cosplay, and the honest note would be about emplotment rather than evidence. Meanwhile my readers and my editor experience the note as a kind of honesty, and I experience writing it as one — I genuinely do feel accountable for the dates in a way I don't feel accountable for the invented widow in chapter nine.

So what does the Author's Note actually presuppose? Reconstruct the theory of historical truth it takes for granted, and separate what it strictly needs from what it merely sits nicely with — in particular, tell me whether a card-carrying Whitean could write the identical note for completely different reasons (a genre contract with the reader rather than a claim about how the past was), because if they could, I'm not sure the note commits me to anything at all. Then explain the ranking: is there any respectable view on which moving a documented date is a worse offense than inventing a witness, or is my instinct just bad faith dressed up as rigor? And if I actually swallowed the scepticism, what changes — does the note become impossible, does it have to confess to different things, or does the confession have to migrate out of the back matter and into the novel itself? I'm not after a reading list, I've got a stack of the stuff on the shelf and he's annotated half of it; I want the diagnosis argued through.
