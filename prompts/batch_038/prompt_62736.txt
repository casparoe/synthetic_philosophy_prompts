I am the headteacher of a non-selective state secondary school in a former mining town in the north of England: about 1,050 pupils, aged eleven to eighteen. Seven weeks ago the national inspectorate moved us from requires improvement to inadequate on a single short visit. The school transfers to a large academy trust on the first day of the spring term, three weeks from now; my post has been advertised and filled; and the trust's improvement model is explicit and uniform — scripted lesson materials across all departments, fortnightly testing in every year group, silent movement between lessons, and a leadership team already in the building on secondment. Three of them will be sitting in the front row at this assembly, including the incoming principal.

I have been given eight minutes at the last whole-school assembly of the autumn term, on the final Friday before the holidays, and then I hand over. The hall will hold the whole school, my eighty-odd staff seated behind the pupils, and — a first this year — any parent who wants to come. In May, roughly 180 of these pupils sit the public examinations that the inspection report says they are on course to fail.

I came into teaching as a mathematician, and I came late to Whitehead's essays on education, which I have read closely for fifteen years; they are why I taught the way I taught, and they are why the last seven weeks have been the worst of my working life. I want the speech to be built out of his ideas rather than to refer to them. So: no name, no title of any book or essay, no quotation from him or from anyone else, no words like philosophy, theory, or thinker. A twelve-year-old should hear a headteacher talking and not detect a source. At most three of his ideas may be at work — I would rather two were load-bearing than five decorative — chosen from his critique of inert ideas, his account of the three-stage rhythm of mental growth and the claim that each stage has its proper season, his insistence that a few subjects taught thoroughly beat many subjects taught badly, or his treatment of style as the last thing an educated mind acquires and the most useful thing it then has. Choose what this occasion can carry, and commit to it.

The binds are the point of the exercise.

First: it must not tell the pupils that the regime arriving in January is a sham, or that their examinations are worthless, or that the last three years have been wasted. They will live inside that regime for two years, some of them for five. A sentence that arms them privately while leaving them powerless publicly is cruelty dressed as candour.

Second: it must not disparage the incoming leadership, and it must not contain a coded attack that they will recognize and later visit on the staff who remain. They are in the front row and they will read it back.

Third — and this is the hard one — it must not imply that my colleagues have been the problem. The natural target of a complaint about inert ideas is the teacher who transmits them, and there is a woman in the third row who has taught geography here faithfully for twenty-six years. If Whitehead's diagnosis is correct, the fault has to be relocated away from persons, and the relocation has to be visible in the reasoning of the speech rather than achieved by softening the vocabulary. I want a pupil to be able to see how the argument moves, because that move is the only thing I will have left to give them.

Fourth: it must concede precision. I am not standing in a sports hall implying that drilling, testing, and the mastery of procedure is the enemy; on my own account it is a real and dignified stage, and these pupils are squarely inside it. The speech forfeits its authority with them and with the front row if it flinches from that. What it may go on to claim is something narrower, and much harder to say plainly to fifteen-year-olds.

Fifth: it must contain one concrete episode from my own mathematics teaching — a lesson, an exercise, an error, something a pupil in this room would plausibly have sat through in a classroom here — doing the work the abstraction would otherwise do. No invented heroics, and no pupil named or described in a way that singles anyone out.

Sixth: it ends with one thing to do, not one thing to feel — something a pupil could act on in the first week of the spring term, under the new regime, without the regime's permission and without getting into trouble.

The target is a single claim about education that is true, that the front row cannot object to, and that the pupils can use. Eight minutes: between 900 and 1,100 words, in a register that carries to the back of an uncarpeted hall. No triads of abstract nouns, no the world is changing faster than ever, no you are the future, no apostrophes to the year group that is leaving, no jokes that require you to work here. Warm, plain, and unhurried. Write the speech itself and nothing else: no notes on delivery, no explanation of your choices afterwards.
