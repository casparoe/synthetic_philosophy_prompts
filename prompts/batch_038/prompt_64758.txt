I'm the science program director at a mid-sized private foundation. I run a coastal ecosystems portfolio — about $6M a year — and twice a year a panel and I have to decide which incumbent programs keep their money, which get conditions attached, and which get wound down over a few years. Our reviewers are working marine ecologists and biogeochemists, no philosophers, and we allow ourselves roughly forty minutes per renewal, most of which goes to arguing about the PI's last three papers.

Here's the problem I'm trying to solve. Our memos and decline letters have quietly drifted into reasons that don't actually justify anything: "this framework isn't testable," "they keep patching it," "nobody in the field believes it anymore," "it hasn't been refuted but it hasn't produced anything either." Each of those sounds like a verdict and is really a mood. I've been reading Lakatos on research programmes and I think his apparatus is much closer to what we're groping toward than our reviewers realize, but I obviously can't hand a panel a sixty-page essay.

So: build me the decision procedure. Steps in order, what at each step counts as a sufficient answer versus a dodge, what the possible outputs are, and the points where the procedure stops and hands the call to me or to the board because what's left is a question about our risk appetite rather than about the science. Assume the people running it are smart and busy and have never heard the term protective belt — so I need you to be explicit about which of Lakatos's distinctions absolutely have to survive the simplification, because dropping them turns the thing back into ordinary peer review with extra vocabulary, and which ones I can safely throw away. Two constraints you should design around: our bylaws require every proposal to get a continue-or-stop recommendation with no abstentions, and our outside counsel insists the word unfalsifiable never appears in a letter. Tell me where those rules collide head-on with the theory.

Then run it on three things I inherited, all aimed at the same question — why some seagrass meadows knocked out by nutrient runoff never come back even decades after the runoff stops:

1. "Bistable meadows." Core: a meadow sits in one of two self-reinforcing states, and collapse is a switch between them rather than a smooth response to load. Twenty-two years of our money. Every anomaly since — sites that recovered with no obvious feedback change, sites that collapsed at very low loads — has been answered by adding another stabilizing mechanism to the belt: sulfide intrusion, waterfowl grazing, sediment resuspension, seed-bank depletion. Each is independently plausible, none was predicted in advance by the core, all were proposed after the anomaly. Two of the four now imply opposite management actions. The program has never called a result in a system it hadn't already studied. Huge lab, famous PI, half the field trained by her, our board adores her.

2. "Supply-side recovery." Core: whether a cleared site recovers is set by propagule supply from upstream source meadows, not by local conditions. It made two risky predictions — reseeding fails at sites far from a source no matter how clean the local water is, and recovery time scales with distance-to-source in a particular way — and both were confirmed by unaffiliated groups in two other regions. One anomaly: a site recovered with no plausible source within sixty kilometers. The answer was "cryptic local refugia," small surviving patches below survey detection, which is now doing a lot of heavy lifting and is being tested by a new deep-water survey method, results due next spring. The program says nothing at all about the collapse side.

3. "Microbial legacy." Core: what makes a site unrecoverable is a persistent change in sediment chemistry and microbial community — the sediment remembers. Four years old, one PI, two postdocs, four papers, no big field campaigns. The core implies a testable and rather surprising claim, that sites with near-identical water-quality histories will differ in recovery according to a measurable pore-water signature, and a pilot assay is running now. Nothing confirmed, nothing refuted, and the PI is candid that if the assay fails the core is in trouble. Cheapest of the three by a wide margin.

One of those three is going to come out in a way I'll hate. Say which and why, and don't soften it.

After the runs, the limits section, and this is where I most want you to be blunt: where will this procedure systematically misfire — what kind of science does it reward and what does it punish, given that it seems to love cheap risky predictions and instrument-building programs that take fifteen years will look inert? What should it do in a field where we are the only funder and there is exactly one program, no rival at all, which describes maybe half my portfolio? How does it handle the case where two programs are both progressive and nothing in the rules picks between them — I don't want a fake tiebreaker, I want it to say so out loud. And I have a vague memory that Lakatos himself had an example, something in the history of atomic weights, where a written-off program came back decades later; tell me if I'm misremembering, and if I'm not, how the procedure is supposed to carry that worry without paralyzing every decision we make. Finally, point out where my reviewers' instincts are actively anti-Lakatosian, and where the theory is going to hand respectable cover to something we all privately think is bad science.

Please write in American English. No reading list, no bibliography, no footnotes — I want the procedure itself, short enough that a panelist could hold it in one hand, with the three case runs and the limits after it.
