I sit on the Forecast Governance Committee of a mid-sized climate-risk analytics firm that sells probabilistic forecasts to reinsurers. Next month the committee has to allocate capital against a single claim: whether named-storm landfalls on the Gulf and South Atlantic coast over the coming decade will exceed the trailing thirty-year average. Two senior analysts were asked, separately, to produce a probability for it. After six weeks of independent work they report 0.71 and 0.34, and neither will move. Their models overlap in places, but each insists the other is missing something structural, and neither will hand over a working model — partly because of a proprietary-methods clause, partly out of professional pride. We are deadlocked, and the allocation deadline does not care.

One colleague has proposed a protocol he calls posterior-only exchange. For five consecutive days, each analyst publishes exactly one number — their probability for the claim, rounded to two decimals — and nothing else. No evidence, no model documentation, no argument, no conversation, no explanation of changes from the previous day. His rationale invokes Aumann's result that agents with a common prior who know one another's posteriors cannot rationally continue to differ. On his telling, the protocol is therefore a win either way: the numbers converge and the dispute resolves itself without anyone disclosing proprietary work, or they fail to converge and we have learned something important — that at least one analyst is not, in the relevant sense, reasoning honestly or competently, and the committee can act on that.

The committee is split. Some of us find this elegant and costless. Others think it is a parlor trick about an idealized model being smuggled in as a decision procedure, or an integrity test wearing the costume of epistemology. A third faction, quieter, worries about incentives: that a protocol with no arguments attached simply rewards whoever is more stubborn or better at anchoring, and that once analysts know their numbers will be published to a colleague, reporting anything other than their sincere credence becomes strategically attractive.

Please draft the memo I will circulate before Thursday's meeting. It should reach an actionable bottom line — adopt, reject, or adopt with specified conditions — and give the committee a concrete procedure with dates keyed to the allocation cycle, including a stated trigger for what we do if the numbers stall. It should be written for a mixed audience: two of the seven members have no formal training in probability or philosophy, so gloss technical terms as you go, keep notation to a minimum, and make the memo fully self-contained rather than leaning on references we cannot check.

At minimum, the memo should take a position on each of the following, and where you think a point is decisive, say why:

1. Whether the antecedent of the theorem plausibly holds here. Do two analysts with different training, different careers, and different heuristics share a common prior? Does the answer matter for what we may infer from convergence or non-convergence?
2. Whether a no-evidence rule is even coherent. My colleague's number is itself information bearing on the claim; if the protocol forbids discussion but permits me to update on his published credence, is that a bug or the entire engine of the procedure?
3. What the analysts are supposed to report. Their raw credence, or their credence conditional on the protocol itself being public and commonly known — including conditional on the fact that the other analyst knows they are being observed? If the latter, does the protocol have a well-defined solution at all, or does it generate a self-referential loop that two sincere Bayesians could rationally fail to resolve?
4. Whether iterated rounds are guaranteed to settle. Can such an exchange stall, cycle, or converge only in the limit, and what does rounding to two decimals do to that?
5. Whether non-convergence licenses any conclusion about a person. Our colleague wants to read a failure to converge as evidence of dishonesty or incompetence. Under what conditions, if any, does that inference go through, and what are the alternatives he has not ruled out?
6. Misreporting and anchoring. If the protocol creates any incentive to inflate or harden a published number, propose a fix or concede that the objection stands.

If you recommend adoption with conditions, the conditions must be specific enough that the analysts and I could tell whether they were being met. If you recommend rejection, propose the fallback you would run instead in the three weeks we have left, and say what it costs us relative to the protocol.

Whatever you conclude, treat the strongest consideration on the losing side as seriously as you treat the case for your recommendation — name it in its most forceful form rather than in a version that is easy to dismiss, and explain what would have to be true for it to overturn your bottom line.

One further request about how you handle the intellectual terrain. There is a difference between what is actually settled in the formal literature — the precise hypotheses of the agreement result, what follows from them, and what does not — and what is genuinely contested among people who know this material well: how much work the common-prior assumption is doing, whether the result has any normative bite for real disagreement between bounded human beings, and whether a failure to converge in a procedure like this one tells you anything at all about a person's rationality or good faith. Please keep those two registers visibly separate. Flag the contested parts as contested, state your own view where you have one, and do not present your preferred side of a live debate as though it were the consensus. If your recommendation ultimately rests on a contested premise rather than a settled one, identify that premise explicitly, so the committee knows precisely where it is placing its weight.
