For two years I worked as a research assistant to a retired psychiatrist — call him Dr. Ellery — helping to catalogue the growth of the diagnostic manuals since mid-century, and he describes himself, only half in jest, as the last Szaszian in the state. He keeps a disintegrating copy of *The Myth of Mental Illness* (1961) by the door; he practiced and defends voluntary psychotherapy and is at pains to insist that his quarrel is with coercion rather than with suffering. Pressed for his criterion, he says that a condition is an illness only if there is a describable deviation in the structure or functioning of some part of the body, the sort of thing a pathologist could point to, and that in its absence psychiatry is diagnosing "problems in living." Because the project required me to log his verdicts case by case, I have an unusually clean track record, and I want to know whether I actually understand it: he is eighty-four, and this looks to be the last substantive dispute he will weigh in on. His verdicts, as recorded. A farmer with a herniated disc who cannot work: illness, no argument. A ten-year-old with type 1 diabetes — thirst, weight loss, nothing visible to the eye: illness, because the deviation is specifiable in the body even when it cannot be seen. Parkinson's disease: illness, and he stresses that its recognition begins with watching how a person moves, which he takes to show that starting from behavior is not in itself the problem. A facial vascular malformation with no functional impairment whatever, which the patient wants lasered because strangers stare: illness. General paresis: illness — but never a *mental* illness; once the infection was identified it ceased to be psychiatry's business and became internal medicine, and he treats that migration as confirmation of his thesis rather than a concession to it. A man with a frontal meningioma who begins embezzling, brought in by a family alarmed at headaches he had been ignoring: the tumor is the illness, the embezzling is his conduct, which the tumor may excuse but which is not a second disease. Schizophrenia: not an illness, but a judgment of disapproval in medical dress. Two decades of heroin use: not an illness, a habit, however ruinous — and the disease label does double damage, taking away the user's agency and supplying a warrant for compulsion. A two-year grief after a spouse's death that meets criteria for major depression: not an illness. Chronic widespread pain with no demonstrable lesion: not an illness, though he is emphatic that this does not make the pain unreal or the person unworthy of help, and he saw such patients for years. Two suicide attempts: no illness evidenced, and he opposed the involuntary hold that followed. He also once refused to certify a defendant's cannabis dependence as an illness for a mitigation hearing, on the ground that he was being asked to convert a moral plea into a medical one.

The case he has now been sent is this. A group at our institution proposes a new category, provisionally named acquisitive impulsivity disorder. They recruited from court and insolvency records — adults with three or more documented ruinous decisions: repeated bankruptcies, large gambling losses, several driving offences — and compared them with matched controls. They report a stable physiological difference between the groups: blunted cortisol awakening response together with reduced cortical thickness in ventromedial prefrontal cortex, present in roughly two thirds of the selected group and in almost none of the controls, detectable by ordinary assays and structural imaging, and predictive of further financial self-destruction better than any questionnaire they tried. Two features complicate the proposal. First, most of those who test positive do not experience themselves as ill; a good many are successful and content, and there is no complaint anywhere in the record, from them or from anyone close to them. Second, the difference was found only by starting from people whose conduct we already disapprove of, and the authors cannot at present say what the relevant bodily norm is except by reference to that conduct. The construct is to remain a research construct for the moment, but the authors have said publicly that they intend to seek inclusion in the manual and to argue the finding as a mitigating ground at sentencing.

Predict his verdict, though I care more about the rule than about the label. At least three candidate rules accommodate every entry in my log and come apart here: illness just is a describable deviation from a bodily norm, whether or not anyone suffers from it; or illness requires such a deviation plus a complaint, from the patient or from those around him, that the deviation accounts for; or illness requires that the deviation be specifiable without appeal to our disapproval of the person's behavior — which is why Parkinson's passes and schizophrenia fails on his account, and which the new proposal may fail for reasons that have nothing to do with lesions. A fourth possibility is that his operative criterion is institutional rather than ontological: what makes a diagnosis illegitimate is what it licenses, in which case the answer turns on the authors' stated ambitions and not on the cortisol at all. Tell me which rule, or which combination, best explains the whole record; whether any single precedent is in genuine tension with the one you favor, and if so which and why; what each candidate predicts about the new case, and which you expect from him, with your confidence and your reasons. Then name the one further case that would most sharply separate the candidates you think are still live — I have been trying for a week and cannot construct one — and say what each rule would say about it. Finally, since I would rather argue with him than merely forecast him: which of these rules would he find hardest to abandon, and what would he have to concede about the earlier cases if he gave it up? Please reason from the cases themselves rather than pointing me toward the literature.
