I run a twice-monthly recorded discussion series out of my department — political theory and philosophy of religion, about forty-five minutes an episode, an audience of graduate students plus a handful of faculty, with the recording afterwards posted for the undergraduates who missed it. Last season's episode on this general territory degenerated into a quarrel about whether Rawls was a crypto-Kantian and drew almost no listeners, so this time I want to do better. Next month's guest is a professor of political philosophy who has just published a book arguing that the mature Rawls is considerably more hospitable to religious argument than either of the two standard caricatures allows. Against the secularist reading she insists that Rawls never asked ordinary citizens to leave their comprehensive doctrines at the door, and that the constraint falls on how the exercise of political power is justified rather than on how believers speak. Against the religious critics she insists that the ideal of public reason is not a gag rule and that its bite is confined to officials, candidates, judges, and the like. Her signature move, used in print and in conversation alike, is to say that the political conception is freestanding and functions as a module a religious citizen can embed in her own doctrine without residue, and that anyone who thinks otherwise has conflated the citizen with the officeholder. Her second signature move is to wave away biography: she has written that Rawls's intensely religious undergraduate thesis on sin and faith, and his loss of Christian belief while serving as an infantryman in the war, have nothing to do with interpreting the later work. I want to be a fair but demanding host — no ambushes, but I do want her to have to think on air. A Kant scholar and a theologian will both be in the room, and since my own training is in Kant I will not be able to adjudicate textual disputes live, so the questions need to be intelligible to a listener who has never read Rawls; a gloss of a clause or two is fine, a lecture-length setup is not.

Please write me six to eight questions, arranged in the order I should ask them, with the conversation escalating from a question of interpretation into a question of substance. Think carefully about where the serious literature actually presses on a defender of this accommodating reading, rather than where the view's popular reputation is vulnerable, and cover at least the following ground: the timing of the proviso, since Rawls allows comprehensive doctrines into political argument on condition that public reasons be supplied at some later point, and I want her to say who fixes that deadline, what becomes of a citizen who never supplies the public reason, and whether the proviso is enforceable in principle or merely hortatory; the burdens of judgment, and whether they amount to a neutral description of how hard it is to reason well or to a contestable doctrine about the reach of human reason that a tradition holding itself to possess revealed truth can reject without thereby becoming unreasonable — and if rejecting it does make a tradition unreasonable, whether the exclusion has been removed or only relocated from religious reasons to religious epistemologies; the case of the abolitionists, of Lincoln, and of King, which Rawls raises himself when he revisits public reason and which he treats as appeals to comprehensive doctrine that ended up strengthening the ideal, so that the same rhetoric counts as a violation or a vindication depending on how history turned out — ask her what her reading says about a present-day religious movement arguing from its doctrine on a matter of basic justice that is never vindicated; the asymmetry between home and abroad, since Rawls refuses religious grounds for coercive law inside a liberal democracy while tolerating, as members in good standing of the society of peoples, decent but non-liberal societies whose institutions favor one faith while still honoring basic rights; the module claim under strain, where I want her to take a concrete case in which justice as fairness does not embed cleanly into a doctrine that has something to say about the ordering of the whole of life, and tell me what the believer is then supposed to do, and whether her answer amounts to the believer having to reinterpret her own faith until it fits; the cost of her reading, in the form of a demand that she name the Rawlsian claim that comes out false on her account and the concession she is least happy making; and one biographical question, the only one I will allow, on whether the young Rawls's theological preoccupations — sin, the need for grace, the rejection of any picture on which human beings author their own righteousness — leave a trace in the later insistence that a political conception cannot be justified by the whole truth, and what her interpretation looks like if that thesis is taken seriously rather than waved away. Close with a question that is not about interpretation at all: ask her to identify a live political dispute on which her reading and the secularist reading yield different practical outcomes, and to say which of the two she would rather be wrong about.

Approach this as an expert on twentieth-century political philosophy and on the reception of Rawls who has moderated demanding but scrupulously fair academic interviews before. Begin with a short brainstorm of five to eight bullets listing the pressure points on her position and the reply she has available at each, and show me that list before the questions, with one line after it saying which pressure points you deliberately left out and why. For exactly two of the questions — the two where you think she has the most room to maneuver — supply a pivot rather than a plain follow-up: state the answer she is most likely to give and the short question that keeps the pressure on if she gives it, plus a different follow-up for the case in which she simply concedes the point. Keep every question to two or three sentences of spoken prose, since I will be reading them from a phone; no question may be answerable by summarizing the idea of public reason, and none may be deflected by distinguishing the reasonable from the rational without the follow-up bringing her straight back to the issue. Do not put any words in Rawls's mouth inside quotation marks — paraphrase him, and if you are not certain of a detail, frame the question so that it does not depend on that detail. Do not ask her to recommend further reading, and do not invent any biographical fact beyond what I have given you above. Finish with one short paragraph telling me which single question she is most likely to handle badly, what the best answer available to her is, and how I should follow up if she gives that answer.
