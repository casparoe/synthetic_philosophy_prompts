For my government class we have been studying judicial review, and the standard justification we were given is that when a court strikes down a statute it is not really overruling the people at all, because the Constitution is itself an act of the people, adopted by supermajorities and amendable only by roughly three-quarters of the states, while an ordinary law reflects a bare majority of one legislature on one afternoon. That is supposed to answer the objection that judicial review is undemocratic. But the same courts apply almost the opposite rule in other cases: if a legislature regulates business or sets economic policy, judges will uphold the statute so long as any conceivable reason supports it, and they say explicitly that they are not there to second-guess lawmakers.

What account of political legitimacy does the first practice presuppose, and does the second practice presuppose a different one? Please reconstruct the view of democracy and of lawmaking authority that a court must hold in order to treat one statute as something it may invalidate and another as something it must defer to, and tell me whether the two practices can be made consistent — for example by saying that some rights are special in kind and not merely in strength — or whether the courts are implicitly working with two incompatible theories. Finally, state what judicial review would have to look like if the presupposed view were replaced by one on which legitimacy comes only from what legislatures decide at the time they decide it.
