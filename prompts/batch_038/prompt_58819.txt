I am a retired plant physiologist. For some thirty-five years I studied water relations in field crops at an agricultural research station, a post that made me as much an engineer of pumps, valves and controllers as a biologist. In retirement I have taken up philosophy, and I have been reading on causation. I am writing to ask whether a pattern that I spent decades recording in incident reports and harvest memoranda has a name, because I have not been able to find one that fits.

The pattern is this. In the summer of 1998 a block of trial plants at the station died during three weeks without rain. Our report read, in effect, that they died because the irrigation controller did not deliver the scheduled water; we traced the failure to a moisture probe, replaced it, and rewrote the procedure. In the same weeks, on an unmanaged slope a few kilometres away, a natural stand of the same species died, also for want of rain. No one wrote a report about the slope, but if I were pressed I should say the plants there died for the same reason: the water did not come.

The two statements, however, are not on the same footing, and the difference troubles me. In the first, the item credited with causing the deaths exists only because there was a standing arrangement for water to be delivered: a schedule, a machine built to a purpose, a technician with a duty, a budget line. Remove the arrangement and there is nothing left for the world to have failed to do. On the slope there was no schedule, no purpose, no one whose business it was, and yet I still want to say that the water's not coming killed those plants too.

I see that this presses in both directions, and I have not found either branch of the difficulty squarely addressed. Suppose whether an absence counts as a cause depends on some arrangement, expectation or function. Then the causal history of a hillside depends partly on what people nearby were doing or were supposed to do, and an account of what physically produced what has been built partly out of bookkeeping and social facts. That is a conclusion I should very much like to avoid, and I do not see how. Suppose instead that it does not so depend. Then the qualifying absences multiply without end: my trial plants died because the probe failed, but equally because nobody checked the probe that morning, because my predecessor did not specify a redundant sensor, because no rain fell that week, because none fell in the week before, and so on backwards indefinitely and sideways to every person on earth who did not water them. I understand that the second branch is a familiar complaint in the literature. It is the first that interests me, together with a second observation which may or may not be the same phenomenon.

In a piping diagram, a shut valve may be logged in either of two ways. One entry records something that happened: the valve moved to its closed position and thereby blocked the flow. The other records something that did not happen: the valve failed to open, and so the flow did not resume. Which entry the investigator makes determines what he looks for next — in the first case, whatever moved the valve, a signal, a spring, an operator; in the second, the command that was never sent or the maintenance that was never done. Nothing in the metal distinguishes the two, and what settles the choice appears to be what the component was for. A non-return valve that slams shut is doing precisely its job, so that the very same closing which in one circuit is a failure to open is in another the fulfilment of a design intent.

So my question is whether there is an established concept for the following: that absences enter causal explanation only against a background of what was supposed to occur, and that the same physical occurrence can accordingly be booked either as an event or as the absence of one. I know the general heading under which failures and absences are discussed in this literature, and I have come across talk of contrasts and of default conditions in causal models, but none of what I read seemed to be about this dependence on arrangements and purposes, nor about the event/absence reversal. I should be grateful if you would tell me what the nearest established concepts are, in what respect each differs from what I have described, and which of them comes closest to it. If none of them fits, I would rather you said so plainly and proposed a term of your own, defined in such a way that the boundary cases are decided. In particular I should like to know whether the unmanaged slope belongs to the phenomenon or falls outside it, whether the valve case is the same thing under another guise or a merely similar one, and whether the whole matter is a discovery about the world or only about which of the many true causal sentences we elect to utter. A bare label will not serve my purpose; what I am after is the carving — what lies inside the phenomenon, what lies outside it, and where the edges run.
