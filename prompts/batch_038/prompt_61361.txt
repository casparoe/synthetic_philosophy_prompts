Three years of research went into my novel about a block fight in a port city, and the body that has invited me to open its proceedings is not a literary one: the room will be urban geographers of the Lefebvrian line, tenant organizers, and a handful of property scholars. My thesis is unfashionable, and I would rather have it stated sharply than survive by vagueness. Lefebvre's right to the city is a right vested in the habitant — a claim to participation and to appropriation, made on behalf of those who already inhabit a place, against exchange value and the planning that serves it — and it therefore encodes incumbency: the exclusionary leverage it hands existing residents belongs to its grammar rather than to its implementation. It follows that when density advocates dismiss the phrase as nostalgia for a settled population, they are not misreading it; they alone are taking it at its word.

Prepare me for the question period. Give me the eight objections this audience will actually raise, in the vocabulary they would use — use value against exchange value, co-optation and the empty signifier, the displacement literature, the charge that I have read a slogan as though it were a doctrine — ordered by how much damage each does. For each, tell me what I should concede up front, what I must hold, and how I might answer it in under two minutes. Then close with the weakest joint in my own argument, and the question I am praying no one asks.
