I'm assembling a small anthology of formal verse about philosophical puzzles, and I'd like to commission a pantoum on Wittgenstein's account of religious belief — in particular his suggestion that the believer and the sceptic only appear to be giving rival answers to the same factual question. When a believer avows a Last Judgment and a sceptic replies that such a thing is possible but hardly certain, the reply does not contradict the avowal, because the avowal was never a forecast held at some degree of confidence: it is closer to a rule around which a whole life is organized.

I'd like the form to carry that argument rather than merely illustrate it. Please write five quatrains following the pantoum's usual relay — each stanza's second and fourth lines becoming the next stanza's first and third — with the final stanza taking the opening stanza's third line as its second line and the opening stanza's first line as its fourth, so the poem closes on the line it began with. Roughly four-beat lines, no fixed rhyme. The recurrence should be the point, not the ornament: let one relayed line be the shared avowal and the other the sceptic's non-committal reply, and arrange matters so that the same words, arriving in a new position, are visibly doing different work — the way one sentence does different work in the mouth of a believer, in the mouth of a polite doubter, and in the mouth of someone for whom it has gone idle, retaining its wording but none of its job. By the last stanza the two positions should have traded places without ever having met.

Do not name Wittgenstein, do not quote him, and do not resolve into either apologetics or debunking. Someone who has never read the lectures should find it a good poem; someone who has should know immediately which move each turn is making.

Please give the poem first, followed by one titled section, "Notes on the machinery" (roughly 200 words), explaining which lines migrate where and what changes in their function when they do.
