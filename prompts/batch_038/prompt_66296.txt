**Peerhood Is Not a Starting Point: An Argument Against Conciliationism**

*Section 1.* Conciliationism holds that discovering an epistemic peer's disagreement with you about a question is itself a reason to move your confidence toward hers, in the simplest version by splitting the difference. Steadfastness denies this, or holds that the reason is easily outweighed by your own first-order evidence. The two positions share a framework: first you establish that someone is your peer, then you ask what the disagreement does to your belief. My draft argues that the second step cannot be honestly reached, because the first step already presupposes the authority it is meant to limit.

*Section 2.* To count someone as my peer on a question Q, I must judge that she is roughly as well-informed, as able, as conscientious, and exposed to the same evidence as I am. Each of these is a substantive claim about her cognitive conduct, and I have no access to that conduct except through its outputs, which are her conclusions. So the evidence available to me for the peerhood judgment consists largely in her agreement with me on Q and on questions adjacent to Q. If that is right, then the premise "she is my peer" is partly constituted by the fact that she reached the answer I reached. A principle telling me to concede when a peer disagrees therefore cannot be triggered by a peer who was certified as a peer on the strength of her agreeing with me. I will call this the certification problem. Conciliationism presents itself as a check on self-trust, but on inspection it constrains self-trust only in cases where self-trust has already been vindicated, and it goes silent in exactly the cases that were supposed to motivate it.

*Section 3.* The familiar objection to conciliationism is that it is self-undermining: if my peer disagrees with me about conciliationism itself, the view instructs me to lose confidence in the view. My argument is structurally different, and I think more damaging. The self-undermining objection attacks conciliationism at the meta-level, saying that the view generates paralysis when applied to disputes about how to apply it. The certification problem attacks it at the object level, saying that the view never receives a licensed input at all. The difference matters because the meta-level objection is escapable: conciliationists have replies in the neighborhood of restricting the scope of the view, or holding that when the disputed question is itself about the correct treatment of disagreement, one may rely on one's own view of the matter in working out who counts as a peer. (I believe Elga resists the objection in roughly this way, but I am not confident that I have characterized his reply correctly; please say so if I have not.) My argument is not escapable in that manner, because it does not depend on the disputed question being a question about disagreement.

*Section 4.* Objection: I have described only one route to peerhood, the route through agreement, and there is a better route. Credentials, training, and demonstrated performance on questions with independently checkable answers can establish that someone is my peer or my superior regardless of whether she agrees with me. Reply: that route functions only where an independent check is available, and the disputes that motivate the conciliationism debate are precisely the ones where it is not. Where credentials do predict accuracy, it is the independent check that is doing the work and the credential is only a summary of it; where no check exists, a credential is a proxy whose correlation with the truth I cannot estimate without already trusting my own judgment about which credentials are relevant. So the structural route is either redundant or unavailable. I want a stronger conclusion here and cannot get it: I cannot rule out that credentials do correlate with accuracy in philosophy even though I cannot verify the correlation.

*Section 5.* A concession that may sink the argument. What I have said appears to make disagreement evidentially inert, which would license fanaticism: a hundred careful people against me and I do not budge. That is clearly unacceptable. My proposal is that numbers matter, but not by moving my confidence in Q directly. They matter by undermining the conscientiousness component of the peerhood judgment. If a hundred apparently competent people reach the opposite conclusion, the most likely explanation is not that a hundred people reason badly but that I am missing something: evidence they have, a distinction I have blurred, a premise I have treated as obvious. Widespread disagreement should therefore return me to my first-order evidence under the suspicion that I have mishandled it, rather than instruct me to average my conclusion with theirs. My attitude is revised by whatever that re-examination turns up, not by the disagreement as such.

*Section 6.* If this is right, the debate between conciliationists and steadfast views is misdescribed. It is not a debate about how much evidential weight disagreement carries. It is a debate about who is entitled to declare that two people have responded to the same evidence equally well, and my answer is that only each of them, reasoning from inside her own case, is positioned to say.
