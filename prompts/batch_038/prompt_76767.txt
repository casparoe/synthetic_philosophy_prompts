For six years I have chaired a small university panel that distributes "access supplements" from a fixed fund to graduate students whose standard stipend leaves them unable to take full part in the program. We publish a one-line reason with each decision, and I am now trying to establish, for a paper and for next Tuesday's meeting, what rule our own record commits us to.

The record so far. We paid the medication and travel costs of a student with a chronic condition, on the ground that she had started behind through no doing of her own. We paid a single parent's childcare. We paid the difference between a shared room and a single for a student whose condition is aggravated by noise. We refused an applicant who had left a well-paid job to write poetry and now had almost no income, on the ground that we do not underwrite choices. We refused an applicant who wanted high-end audio equipment, on the ground that preferences are not needs; our file on that last case does not record whether he welcomed the taste or resented it. Every vote was unanimous. Our charter, which I did not draft, says only that the fund exists to equalize students' realistic prospects of completing the degree.

Next week: an applicant who, since a serious illness, cannot enjoy music except on equipment of a quality far beyond the stipend, and who says plainly that she regrets the taste and would surrender it tomorrow if she could. Nothing in what she is able to do has changed — she researches, teaches, travels, and works at the same level as her peers — and the entire cost falls on her budget and on how her life feels to her.

Predict what the panel will do, and then say what we should do if we are to be consistent with our own record rather than with the charter. Lay out the candidate distributive metrics that fit all five precedents, what each implies in this case, which precedent is the closest analog and how much weight it can bear, and what principle we would be adopting whichever way we vote. Then name one further application that would discriminate among those candidates. Close by telling me how confident you are in your prediction and which single point in your reasoning you are least sure of.
