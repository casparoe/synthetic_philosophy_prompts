ok so I've been reading this stack since the Overcoming Bias days and I've finally forced myself to write down what I actually believe about the far future and the s-risk stuff (astronomical-scale suffering locked in by future technology, you know the thing). I want an audit, not a lecture — assume I've read all the standard arguments and know all the standard moves. Just tell me where the set breaks.

Here's the list:

1. On moral uncertainty I'm roughly 70% classical total view — hedonistic-ish, symmetric, every future life counts the same — and 30% some suffering-focused view on which intense suffering has strong priority over comparable amounts of happiness. I don't want to commit on whether that priority is lexical or just very heavy.

2. I aggregate across those two by maximizing expected choice-worthiness with the usual cross-theory normalization. No lexical vetoes, no only-count-it-when-both-theories-agree shortcuts.

3. Extinction is the biggest tragedy in expected-value terms. Trillions of flourishing lives foreclosed. That's what I lead with when I'm explaining my views to anyone, and I mean it.

4. But if you ask me point blank whether I'd push the button that ends humanity rather than let a competitive-AI-driven future of large-scale involuntary suffering lock in, I say yes, and I'm not saying it as a rhetorical flourish. I've said it in writing and I'd say it again.

5. I think the astronomical-waste argument is basically correct — foreclosing the future throws away a staggering amount of value, and it's the strongest argument against extinction risk that I know of.

6. Practically: buy time. Slow things down, put money and career capital into AI governance, on the theory that a faster race means worse coordination and worse values getting baked in.

7. I'm deeply agnostic about the actual shape of any post-transformative-AI future. Singleton or not, uploaded or not, how many minds, what their values are — wide error bars on basically everything descriptive.

8. I also don't think permanence is really available. No intelligent system stays coherent forever; whatever gets built will drift, decay, or be replaced. So lock-in is doing a lot more work in this discourse than it can bear.

9. And yet the suffering-focused 30% does almost nothing in my actual giving. Global health and AI governance, basically nothing to wild-animal welfare or anything aimed at reducing the number of future sentient beings.

What I want:

- Which pairs are in genuine tension, ranked worst-first, with what specifically has to be given up in each case. Don't just list all of them as equally bad — I want the ranking to be doing work.
- Which ones only look like a tension and dissolve once you're careful about what's actually being compared. I suspect at least one of these and I want to know if I'm right about which.
- On 3 and 4: under my own rule in 2, roughly what would have to be true for the push-the-button answer to come out right? Give me either the credence in the suffering-focused view that would be needed, or the extra claim about the net value of a suffering-heavy future that I'd have to adopt, and show the order-of-magnitude reasoning rather than waving at it. I want to know whether 8 makes that harder or easier.
- Whether 9 is evidence that my stated 70/30 in 1 is not my real credence, and if so what I should update it to.
- Finally: the single cheapest revision that fixes the most damage, and what it costs me. Assume I'm not abandoning expected-value reasoning wholesale and I'm not going to pretend suffering-focused ethics isn't a respectable view.
