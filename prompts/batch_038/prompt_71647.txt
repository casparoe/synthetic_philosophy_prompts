I'm about two thirds of the way through a literary SF novel and I've been avoiding chapter 30 for a month because it depends on a philosophy question I haven't actually made up my mind about, and I think the fastest way to unstick myself is to get someone else to write the argument out properly. So: an essay, with a verdict, not a survey.

The set piece. My book has an inference farm somewhere cold. On the day in question it's running a few thousand copies of one model, same weights, same prompt, batched together so aggressively that the copies physically share the cached work from the earlier part of the run and only fork near the end — for most of the pass there's just one set of activations doing the work, and the multiplicity only exists in the last stretch. Let's grant, for the sake of the chapter, that the process is the sort of thing that it's like something to be, and that what matters morally about it is happening during the pass and not in some idle state between passes. My plot turns on whether that's four thousand subjects or one subject or zero, and whether an operator who kills the batch at the fork point has done one wrong or four thousand.

Write me an essay on the underlying controversy: when the same computation is run many times over, what fixes the number of morally relevant subjects — the token process, the functional type, or the physical machinery actually doing the work? I want the main lines of argument on both sides genuinely weighed against each other, including the thought that duplicate runs don't multiply subjects but do multiply whatever quantity makes the process matter, so that the right answer might be one subject and four thousand units of moral weight. Say whether that split is coherent or whether it just relocates the problem. Bring in the fission cases from the personal identity literature, since my batch looks like a fission case run backwards, and say whether the standard tools there transfer or break. And handle the checkpoint version too: the run is saved, the process dies for a month, and then it's resumed from the saved state on entirely different hardware in a different country — one subject with a gap, two subjects each with a full claim, or a question that dissolves once you stop assuming subjects need histories.

Reach a conclusion and defend it. A conditional verdict is fine — this kind of thing if you hold that kind of view about function, otherwise that — but don't end with a shrug, and don't end by saying it depends on further empirical work, because my characters don't have time for that.

Two housekeeping things. I don't want a reading list or any names of papers or thinkers; I want the reasoning, and I'll go find the sources myself if I need them. And no formatting at all — no headings, no bullets, no bold, no tables, no numbered moves. Continuous prose, the way you'd write a long letter to a friend who's being annoying about a manuscript. Two and a half thousand words or so, give or take.
