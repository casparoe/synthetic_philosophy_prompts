I cover science and ideas for a magazine, and last month I sat in on the second evening of a festival panel about accountability in research institutions. The advertised topic was bland, but the argument that actually broke out was about hypocrisy and the right to blame, and I have been turning it over ever since. Two panelists did the work: Nora, a moral philosopher who writes about blame and resentment, and Ezra, a lawyer who teaches jurisprudence. I was in the audience taking notes. I don't have a horse in this race, and I would rather you not be able to guess from my write-up which of them I found the better speaker.

The case under discussion was one I had reported on earlier in the year. The director of a research institute publicly reprimanded a postdoc for failing to declare a paid consultancy, in language that made the omission sound like a defect of character rather than an administrative lapse. It later emerged that the director's own declarations had been late and incomplete for years, in the same category.

Here is the exchange as I recorded it.

1. Ezra opened by refusing the frame. The reprimand was accurate: the postdoc did fail to declare, and nothing in the director's own file makes that false, or makes it less of a failure. Blame, on the ordinary understanding, is a judgment that an act was wrong plus a negative attitude toward the agent's having done it, and neither component is sensitive to who is doing the judging. What Nora wants to call standing, he said, is a rule of court practice smuggled into ethics: the clean-hands idea is a discretionary bar on equitable relief, confined to misconduct bearing on the very subject of the claim, available only as a defense raised by the opposing party, and administered by an institution that has to decide whether to act. Morality has no docket, no discretion, and no opposing party to raise the defense.

2. Nora said this depends on an impoverished picture of blame. To blame someone is not only to be right about them; it is to address a demand to them as a fellow member of a community that holds itself to common rules, and the demand carries an implicit term, that the addresser is inside those rules too. The director exempts himself from a rule he is pressing on someone junior to him. The defect is not in the content of his judgment but in his making it, and making it is exactly what blaming consists in.

3. Ezra pressed the consequence. If the condition on blame is that the blamer be clear of the relevant fault, then almost nobody clears it: most of us have criticised conduct we are not clean of, and much of that is never known. A doctrine that renders nearly all actual moral criticism unauthorized is not a refinement of blame, he said, but a reductio of the doctrine. He added that Nora's condition is also badly over-inclusive in the other direction, since it strips standing from someone who broke the rule once, decades ago, confessed it, and changed.

4. Nora said the condition is graded rather than a switch, and that she is untroubled by the reductio. If it turns out that a large share of ordinary moral criticism is compromised to some degree, that is a finding about how readily criticism licenses itself, not a flaw in the analysis. She also said Ezra had changed the subject: her claim was never that the director's reprimand was false, only that it was not his to deliver in that register.

5. Ezra then set out what he took to be the decisive case. Imagine two directors identical in conduct, identical in wording, identical in their relationship to the postdoc; one carries an undeclared, undiscovered, unrepented fault of exactly the same kind, the other does not. On Nora's view one lacks standing and the other has it. Yet nothing an observer, the postdoc, or a colleague could register differs between them: the address is word for word the same, the demand is the same, the relationship is the same. So either the difference is inert, in which case standing does no explanatory work and the dispute is over a shadow; or the real variable is the director's dispositions and commitments, in which case the honest formulation is that blame is undermined by the blamer's lack of commitment to his own standards, and there is no need to posit a moral status called standing.

6. Nora said the case is not inert and that Ezra was assuming standing must be detectable in order to be real. A promise made by someone who already intends not to keep it binds him all the same, and the invisibility of his intention does not make the difference between that promise and an honest one irrelevant. What standing governs is not the truth of the reprimand but what the blamer may insist on afterwards: whether the postdoc owes him an apology, whether his resentment is apt, whether third parties should join in. A man who does not hold himself to the rule has no basis to insist on it.

7. Ezra replied that she had now conceded his point, because what she described is a fact about the blamer's character and commitments, insincerity in the ordinary sense, and not a fact about a status he occupies. He asked her to say what distinguishes "you should not have delivered that reprimand" from "you could not have."

8. Nora said the difference is that the second claim is agent-relative: it explains why the postdoc is entitled to decline to answer, why bystanders are under no obligation to reinforce the criticism, and why an apology extracted by the director is not something he is owed. Ezra's last word was that agent-relativity without a mechanism is a label, not an explanation.

They never resolved it, and I am not going to try to resolve it in print. But I would like your adjudication, and I would like it granular.

(a) Rate each of the eight moves on a fixed scale, clearly right / defensible / weak / clearly mistaken, and say briefly why. Rate the move as an argumentative step, not as a summary of that speaker's overall position; a panelist can be clearly right in move 3 and clearly mistaken in move 5.

(b) As you go, flag wherever the two are using "standing" in different senses, for instance as an authority to address someone, as the permissibility of the act of blaming, as an entitlement to press for a response, or as an epistemic qualification to judge. I suspect several moves only appear to clash because of a slide between these, and I want to know which ones.

(c) Say specifically which of the following Ezra wins and which Nora wins: whether blame is sensitive to the blamer's own conduct; whether the equity analogy is load-bearing or merely decorative; whether the nobody-clears-the-bar consequence is a reductio or a finding; whether the two-directors case shows standing to be inert; and whether the agent-relativity point in move 8 answers Ezra's demand for a mechanism.

(d) Tell me what both of them are missing, something neither raised that would change the shape of the argument. I have a suspicion there is at least one such thing, but I want it from you rather than from me.

Please be explicit about your confidence. Tell me which of your ratings you would defend under pressure, which you would revise if a better-informed reader pushed back, and the single point in the whole answer you are least sure of.
