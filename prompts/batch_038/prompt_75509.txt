I've got a fight on my hands at the small family foundation where I do grantmaking (about $12M a year) and I need something in writing before Tuesday.

We're at the final ranking stage, where proposals denominated in completely different currencies get mushed into one spreadsheet column. Two are live:

- A: a maternal mental-health program in western Kenya. An outside consultancy evaluated it in WELLBYs — wellbeing-adjusted life years, built off self-reported life satisfaction scales — and projects roughly 4,200 WELLBYs for $900k.
- B: a palliative-care expansion in our home state, evaluated in QALYs off a published trial, roughly 310 QALYs for $850k.

Our template has a hardcoded exchange rate, 1 WELLBY = 0.75 QALY, that somebody added maybe six years ago. Nobody can find where it came from. On that rate A wins comfortably and takes the money.

The committee is split three ways. One faction says the rate is fake precision and we're laundering a made-up number through a spreadsheet — kill the cross-program comparison, fund both, or just vote. A second faction says the comparison is confused at a deeper level: you can't weigh one person's happiness against another person's, let alone run it through a conversion factor, so we should only ever rank programs measured with the same instrument. A third faction says some rate like this is unavoidable whether we write it down or not — it's already sitting inside our gut rankings — so the honest move is to pick one openly and show how the ordering shifts as we vary it.

There's also a wrinkle I cant figure out how to weigh. The life-satisfaction instrument behind the WELLBYs was calibrated mostly on Western, educated samples, and there's a decent literature showing that people in severe deprivation adapt, reporting higher satisfaction than an outsider would predict. I genuinely can't tell whether that means we're undercounting how bad poverty is, or whether it means the instrument stops measuring what we think it measures once you move it across contexts. Or both, differently for different people.

Draft the memo I'll circulate Monday night. It needs to land somewhere concrete — fund A, fund B, split the money, or change how we score, plus what we actually do this cycle — rather than surveying the three factions and shrugging. And give the strongest honest case for whichever faction you end up rejecting, in their own terms; if it reads like a strawman they'll know immediately. Assume the board is smart, hasn't taken a philosophy course since college, and will smell hand-waving.
