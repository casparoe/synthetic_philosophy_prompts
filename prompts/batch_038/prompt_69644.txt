In three weeks I am giving the civic address at a federal naturalization ceremony in the county courthouse where I live. I teach philosophy at the nearby state college, and the chief judge invites a local speaker each quarter to say something about citizenship in the interval between the opening remarks and the oath; this time it is me. Roughly a hundred and eighty candidates will be in the room, from something like sixty countries, along with their families, a scattering of local officials, and the county commissioner who runs the property-rights advocacy group here and who has said publicly, more than once, that all taxation is theft. He is not a crank and he is not my enemy. We have argued amiably for years.

Two things fix the subject. First, at the orientation session candidates attend beforehand, one of them — a woman who kept a small business in a place where the collectors came with rifles — asked the clerk what the difference was supposed to be between that and what happens here, other than manners. The clerk did not have an answer, and the woman did not press it, which tells me she already knew what the answer would be. Second, the handbook given to candidates lists paying taxes honestly and on time among the responsibilities attaching to the status they are about to receive, and the oath they will take commits them to support and defend the Constitution and laws of this country. The question is therefore on the program whether or not I raise it. I have decided to raise it.

Please write the speech. It should run about six minutes when read aloud, so seven hundred to nine hundred words. Three constraints are load-bearing rather than decorative, and I would ask you to treat them as such.

1. It must state the theft charge in its strongest form, in language a twelve-year-old in the back row can follow, and it must concede plainly what the charge gets right: that the taking does not depend on the individual's consent, and that what stands behind it is force. A speech that reaches its conclusion without granting this is worthless to me.

2. It must then do actual argumentative work instead of delivering a slogan. In particular, I want it to keep separate the questions that are ordinarily collapsed into one another: whether an institution's takings are permissible at all; whether a person subject to them owes compliance; and what it is about a scheme of rule-governed, answerable, reciprocally justified coercion that could distinguish it from a robbery that happens to be well organized. The speech may end wherever it likes on the underlying question, but it may not smuggle the verdict in.

3. The following moves are banned, because they are what a speechwriter reaches for and because I think they are either bad arguments or bad manners: tacit consent, and anything of the form "you chose to come here, so you agreed"; the ledger of public goods — we all pay for the roads, the schools, the fire department; "taxation is the price of civilization"; "if you do not like it, move"; any comparison between this country and the countries the candidates left, flattering or otherwise; and any reference to immigration, borders, or being a nation of immigrants. That last family of notes is the easy register for this occasion and I want it shut off completely. The speech must also not name a philosopher, a book, a doctrine, or a term of art. The room's sound system is poor, half the audience is hearing English as a second language, and the sentences need to be short and the images concrete.

The hardest thing I am asking for is this: the speech has to be worth hearing by the woman who asked the clerk her question, and at the same time it must not insult the commissioner — ideally it should contain at least one sentence he would be willing to say out loud himself. It should close on the oath the candidates are about to take, and it should include one passage addressed to them directly, in the second person, that still holds even for someone who thinks the charge is true.

Give me the speech, followed by a separate note of no more than two hundred words on its architecture: which distinction is carrying the weight, what you deliberately left out and why, and the objection the commissioner is most likely to raise afterward and how the speech absorbs it.
