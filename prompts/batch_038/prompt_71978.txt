I am a junior at a public high school in central Ohio, and I am editing my way into a problem I would like your help solving. Our literary magazine has an annual themed issue, and this year the theme is Already; my contribution is due in three weeks, and my faculty adviser has told me, politely but firmly, that my first two drafts read like a physics essay with the equations taken out. She is right. For the past year I have been reading on my own about the argument that runs from the relativity of simultaneity to the conclusion that the whole of spacetime exists all at once — the one pressed independently in the nineteen-sixties by a Dutch mathematician and by an American philosopher, and later popularized through a version involving two pedestrians and a distant galaxy. My understanding of the mechanism is this: each observer has a slice of events that counts as present for them, and that slice is tilted relative to the road depending on how fast and in which direction they are moving, so two people passing on the sidewalk disagree about which events, very far away, are already settled. At the distance of the Andromeda galaxy the disagreement can amount to days; across a truck-stop parking lot the same two people, walking in opposite directions, shift one another's distant present by a few quadrillionths of a second. Nothing they can see or signal changes, and the shift grows only with distance, which is why an event a person has already watched happen up close cannot be walked back into their future by turning around. I also know that a number of physicists and philosophers of physics object that the present in relativity is a local notion that cannot honestly be stretched into a global hyperplane, so that the whole move from tilted slices to a fixed four-dimensional world is a piece of metaphysics smuggled in through a convention. What frustrates me about almost everything I have read on this is that it ends in one of two places: either gloom about fate, or a shrug to the effect that the argument does no work and can be ignored. I want a story that treats the objection not as a debating point but as something that happens to a person.

Here is the story I have in mind, which you should feel free to alter in detail but not in shape. The narrator is Delia, forty-seven, a long-haul driver who has run the same westbound overnight route between two inland cities for nineteen years. Eight years ago she took a community-college astronomy course and learned the argument, and ever since she has kept a spiral notebook in the cab, next to her duty log, in which she records at every rest stop what she takes to be already decided out at Andromeda, along with the date and the direction she was heading when she made the entry; she has seen the galaxy herself exactly once, as a smudge from a pullout under dark skies, and the notebook is her way of keeping company with something she can never receive news from. Her mother died in April. Delia understands perfectly well that the notebook cannot help with that, for both of the reasons I gave above, and she keeps it anyway — that gap between what she knows and what she does is the engine of the story, and it must never be explained away as confusion or denial. On this particular night she has a passenger: her nephew, a second-year physics student riding along for the run because his father wanted him to see the family before he drops out. He knows the argument better than she does and is delighted by it, and somewhere between the second and third rest stop she persuades him to get out of the cab so that they can stage the pedestrian case properly — she walking west, he walking east, notebooks in hand, comparing entries afterward in the sodium light. Please make that scene physically scrupulous and humanly ridiculous at once: the comparison yields nothing, because there is no way for either of them to check, and both of them know before they start that there will be no way to check. He then tells her, at the kitchen-table register rather than the lecture-hall register, what he thinks is wrong with her version of the idea — that she is treating a way of slicing as a way of being — and, crucially, the story must also give Delia one moment in which she is right about something he dismisses, so the objection does not simply win.

Craft constraints, and please honor all of them. Between 1,400 and 1,900 words, past tense, close third person on Delia, one continuous overnight run with at most one flashback of a paragraph or less. No aliens, no fleets, no time travel, no faster-than-light messages, no dream or vision sequence, and no physicist-explains-to-layperson dialogue; no character may say the words block universe, four-dimensionalism, or plane of simultaneity, and no philosopher's or physicist's name may appear anywhere in the story. The physics has to be carried by objects and habits — the notebook, the duty log, the on-ramp chosen for its direction, the coffee going cold — and if any arithmetic appears at all it should be a single line in Delia's own handwriting. The ending may not be despair, may not be a warm reconciliation, and may not have her throw the notebook away or find it meaningful again; it should leave the ritual intact and recontextualized, so that a reader can feel both that the objection lands and that she will write in the notebook at the next stop anyway. After the story, please add an author's note of about two hundred words, with no citations or recommended readings of any kind (I cannot chase sources down before the deadline, and my adviser has forbidden footnotes in the issue), in which you state which reading of the physics the story assumes, exactly what the nephew's objection costs Delia's practice, what you would have to change if the story were written throughout on the assumption that the present is only ever local, and, in one final sentence, whether you think the story ends up endorsing or merely recording what she does.
