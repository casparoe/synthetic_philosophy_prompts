I directed a sensory physiology laboratory for thirty-four years, most of that time on nociception, and since retiring I have taken up philosophy with more leisure than discretion. I am now trying to establish what I actually believe about when a pain is bad for the person who has it, proceeding case by case rather than by slogan, and I have accumulated a set of verdicts that I cannot arrange under any principle I am able to state. They are as follows.

(a) A patient who has had an anterior cingulotomy for intractable leg pain tells me the pain is exactly where it was and exactly as intense as before, describes it in the same words he used before the operation, and yet says it no longer bothers him; he is quite cheerful on the subject. That pain is not bad for him — at most it is bad in some attenuated sense that does not deserve the name of suffering.

(b) The same patient, some months later, is offered a tablet that would abolish the pain, and accepts at once: it breaks his concentration when he reads, and he finds himself rubbing the knee. Now I want to say the pain is bad for him, mildly but genuinely, although nothing in his description of the sensation has changed in the interval.

(c) A woman who climbs mountains and who enjoys the burning in her thighs at the end of a long ascent, and would pay to have the sensation back, describes it in terms I should call a report of pain: intense, burning, located. It is not bad for her; if anything it is good.

(d) A patient under a high dose of opioid, or under hypnotic suggestion, reports that the pain is still there but distant, as though behind glass, and happening to somebody else. As in (a), not bad, or very nearly not bad.

(e) A patient given a drug producing anterograde amnesia for a procedure screams, flushes, and shows every autonomic sign of distress, and afterwards reports nothing at all and has no wish about the matter in either direction. It was bad for him while it lasted, and just as bad as the identical pain that somebody does remember. This is the verdict I hold least confidently, but I have not found a way to give it up.

(f) A newborn's heel is lanced. She has no conception of a future, no enduring desires, and no memory that will outlast the hour. That pain is bad for her — the paradigm of a bad pain, in fact — and if a theory denies it, I shall discard the theory rather than the verdict.

(g) An adult whose lesion leaves him feeling pains as intensely unpleasant as anyone feels them, but incapable of caring about anything that happens to his body: indifferent to the pain, indifferent to the loss of the limb it is in, indifferent to his own death. That pain is not bad for him, and I feel no temptation to say otherwise.

(h) A child with congenital insensitivity to pain accumulates unnoticed fractures, burns, and corneal abrasions, and dies young. Her never feeling pain is a serious harm to her, although I do not think any particular pain she failed to feel would have been good for her to feel. I recognize this may not be a claim about particular pains at all, and if the escape is to say so plainly, I want it said plainly.

(i) Labor pain, or the pain of a physiotherapy session that restores movement: bad in itself, and all things considered good to have. I want the two levels held apart rather than cancelled against each other.

The pair I cannot reconcile is (e) and (f) against (g). In each of the three, someone is undergoing a sensation that would be described as intensely unpleasant, and in each they lack the desires, the caring, and the retention that the accounts I have read appear to require; yet I call the first two bad and the third not bad at all. The only difference I can name is that the infant and the sedated man are still people to whom their own bodies matter, whereas the lesioned man's no longer does — which strikes me as a restatement of my prejudice rather than as a principle. I am also uneasy about (a) and (b), since the sensation is by hypothesis unchanged while my verdict is not.

What I want from you is an argument, not a survey of who has said what. Please answer in American English, as exactly five numbered items of one or two sentences each, with no introduction and no conclusion. The first four items should each state one candidate principle about what makes a pain bad for the person who feels it — stated precisely enough that I could apply it myself to a case I have not met — together with which of my lettered verdicts it fails to deliver; the four must be genuinely distinct principles and not four wordings of one. The fifth item should say which single verdict of mine you would have me retract, and what your preferred principle then says about a case I have not listed: a patient in a minimally conscious state after trauma, in whom grimacing and nociceptive reflexes are intact but of whom nothing can be established about caring, desire, or memory.
