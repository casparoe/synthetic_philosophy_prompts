Here's the view. I've become conviced that the standard story about why process-based supervision beats outcome-based supervision on stuff like math reasoning is mostly wrong. The standard story goes: process signals give denser, better-localised credit assignment, so learning is more sample-efficient and the model is less able to hack the final answer. My view is that the real mechanism is different — call it the legibility prior hypothesis. Process labels don't reward correct reasoning, they reward reasoning in the *form humans can follow*: named methods, tidy ordered steps, no big inferential leaps, no weird notation. So a process reward model is largely scoring stylistic conformity to a human template of what a solution looks like, and the reason it wins is that in the narrow domains where anyone has bothered to collect step-level labels, that template happens to be strongly correlated with correct-and-checkable solutions. The predictions I'm attached to: the advantage should shrink as base models get stronger and their own solution strategies drift away from textbook human ones; it should be near-absent in domains where correctness is cheaply verifiable but there's no canonical human derivation style to conform to; and most of what people credit to dense rewards should be reproducible by an outcome-only signal plus a data-curation filter that throws out stylistically wierd-but-correct solutions.
