My mum retired in June after 31 years on the family court bench — custody and contact, mostly, the cases where nobody agrees there's a right answer and everyone has to live with the decision anyway. She was also, without ever using the word, a moral particularist of the Dancy sort: she thought a written-down principle was at best a crutch and at worst a way of stopping looking, and her least favorite line to hear in anyone's judgment was 'it is trite that'. I'm giving the toast at her retirement dinner.

Room: maybe half former colleagues — judges, clerks, two appellate types who'll be listening for a maxim they can cite back at her — and half family, who switch off the second it sounds like jurisprudence.

Write the toast, four to five minutes spoken. The constraint that matters: it has to make her view audible without ever stating a principle she'd recognise as one. So no 'every case is different' (she said thats a rule wearing a disguise), no 'it all comes down to judgement' as a punchline, and no sequence of her virtues that quietly adds up to a lesson about how to be a good judge. It also can't be affectionate mush — the appellate end of the table should hear the actual view in it, including the holism part: the feature that counted in favour in one family could count against in the next and that's not a defect in the practice.

And there's one objection I want taken on inside the speech rather than dodged: the line she got for thirty years, that if there are no principles there's no appeal, no precedent, nothing to argue with — just one womans eye, and you're stuck with it. Give that an answer in the register of a dinner, not a seminar.

Then, separate from the toast, a short note: where you've taken liberties. I know the careful version of particularism says the rationality of moral judgement doesn't depend on a supply of principles rather than that no principles exist, and that Dancy keeps room for default reasons, so tell me if the speech has flattened that into a slogan it can't defend. Also say whether your answer to the appeal-and-precedent objection is one a particularist can actually give, or one you've borrowed from the generalists without noticing — and what would change your mind about whether an occasion like this can carry the view at all.
