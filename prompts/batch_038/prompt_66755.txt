**Draft: Comparing Curves Rather Than Verdicts: A Proposal for the Dispute About Cross-Cultural Intuitions**

**1. The dispute I am entering.** Around the turn of the millennium, researchers associated with what is now called experimental philosophy reported that philosophical intuitions may not be uniform across human populations. In a much-discussed 2001 study, Jonathan Weinberg, Shaun Nichols and Stephen Stich presented American and East Asian undergraduates with short cases, including cases of the kind Gettier made famous, in which a person's belief is true and well supported but is true only by coincidence. The participants were asked whether the person in the case really knows the fact or merely believes it, and East Asian participants were more likely than the Americans to say that he really knows. A few years later Edouard Machery and colleagues ran a parallel study using cases about the reference of proper names of the kind Kripke discussed, and again reported a divergence between the groups. Stich drew what seemed to him the obvious conclusion: if intuitions of this sort vary with the upbringing of the person having them, they cannot serve as evidence about knowledge or reference, and philosophers who appeal to them are quietly generalizing from their own culture. Subsequent studies have not reproduced the East/West difference for other Gettier cases, and some have reported striking agreement across very different populations, so the empirical picture is now much less clean than it was.

**2. The option both sides seem to miss.** When these results are argued over, the candidate explanations are usually sorted into two boxes. Either the groups have different intuitions, in which case at least one group is mistaken about the very subject matter philosophy is trying to get right; or the groups have the same intuitions and differ only in how they answer a survey question, in which case the data are philosophically inert. I think a third possibility is hidden by the way these boxes are drawn, because both assume that a participant approaches a case with a determinate answer already fixed, and that the experiment measures either that answer or the reporting of it. A participant might instead not treat the question as having a determinate answer at all. Whether a belief that is true and well supported but luckily true counts as knowledge may strike someone as a question whose sharpness is an artifact of how it was asked. I will call this attitude the determinacy presupposition: the assumption, usually silent, that a philosophical question has a single correct verdict that a competent respondent can be expected to give. If the determinacy presupposition varies across groups, then mean verdicts can differ without any difference in first-order intuition and without any difference in reporting conventions, because there is no fixed answer being reported more or less faithfully.

**3. The proposal.** To separate these readings I want to stop comparing verdicts and start comparing curves. The design begins with a defeater ladder: ten cases that start with a clean instance of justified true belief, end with a paradigmatic Gettier case, and in between vary how blatant the element of luck is. For each participant I record the proportion of rungs at which he says the subject knows, plot each group's curve across the ladder, and compare two parameters, the midpoint, which is the rung at which the group reaches fifty percent, and the slope, which is how fast the curve falls. This is borrowed from psychophysics, where a threshold and a slope summarize a subject's behaviour across an ordered series of stimulus strengths. Two readings then become distinguishable. A difference in midpoint with matching slopes would mean the groups are equally sensitive to what varies along the ladder and merely place the boundary differently; I will call this the threshold reading. A difference in slope would mean the groups differ in how sharply they track the ladder at all, which I will call the sensitivity reading, and which would be a substantive difference in intuition, though a much narrower one than a difference in content.

**4. What I think this would settle.** If the curves differ only in midpoint, then the groups see the same dimension in the same way and disagree about where to draw a line on it. That outcome would show, I think, that the dispute is not about intuitions at all but about thresholds, and it would leave Stich's conclusion unsupported, since nothing about knowledge itself would have been shown to vary. If the curves differ in slope, the result is worse for the appeal to intuition, though still local. I also want to measure the determinacy presupposition directly instead of inferring it from the curves, using two things that no verdict-based study records: how often a participant declines to answer or says the case is too unclear to judge, and how long he takes on each rung. A group that doubts the question has a determinate answer should abstain more often and should be slowest near the middle of the ladder, where the decision is hardest.

**5. What culture is doing in these studies.** A further worry is that culture is treated in this literature as a single between-groups variable when it is really a bundle: language, schooling, familiarity with test-taking, exposure to philosophy, and the norms of a particular university department all travel together. Any single study can therefore locate a difference only somewhere inside the bundle. I think this matters for my proposal as well as for the original studies, because the ladder asks me to assume that the rungs are ordered the same way for every participant, and that ordering is itself something a study of this kind cannot take for granted.

**6. What I could actually run.** My resources are limited. I can recruit about sixty students at my own school and roughly thirty-five at our partner school in Singapore, both of which are academically selective, and everyone would take the study in English. A ten-rung ladder with fewer than a hundred participants will not estimate slopes very precisely.
