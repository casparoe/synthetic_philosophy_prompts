Please translate the debate over interpersonal utility comparisons into the vocabulary I actually think in — Lamport ordering, vector clocks and the partial orders they yield, clock skew, bounded-error synchronisation, epochs, and what a system must sacrifice to promise external consistency — and then give me a residue report.

Set the translation up by treating each agent's utility function as a local monotone counter, meaningful only up to an unknown positive affine transformation at each node, and render in those terms Robbins's scepticism about interpersonal comparisons, Harsanyi's aggregation theorem, and Sen's distinction between unit and level comparability. Say precisely what a utilitarian aggregator demands of the system that a merely Pareto-respecting one does not. Do not smuggle my framework's presuppositions in and call them translation: distributed systems assumes there is a real quantity out there that is merely hard to measure, and I want to know whether that assumption is doing covert work on the economics side.

Then attack a hunch of mine. I suspect the interesting position is a TrueTime-style middle rather than either pole: each utility carries a declared uncertainty interval, comparisons are licensed only where two intervals fail to overlap, and aggregation is admissible only in the commit-wait regime, where non-overlap is guaranteed rather than hoped for. Is that a substantive third view, or the sceptic's view with better marketing — and does the welfare-economics debate already contain it under another name?

Close with the residue: which claims translate cleanly, which survive only with distortion, and which have no counterpart at all in my vocabulary. For each failure, say whether the defect lies in the clock-and-consensus framing or in an original claim that was less clear than it looked.
