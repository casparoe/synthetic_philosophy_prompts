A departmental workshop on the foundations of analysis has agreed to run an adversarial collaboration between two parties who have spent a decade talking past each other, and I have been asked to draft the joint statement they would sign. Please produce that document.

The parties are as follows. Party A is a Bishop-style constructivist: she holds that to assert an existential claim is to stand in possession of the relevant construction, that a set of reals is given only together with an approximation or decision procedure (hence locatedness and apartness are substantive notions, not bookkeeping), that the reals do not form a completed totality, and that the law of excluded middle is not generally licensed for statements whose verification would require such a totality. She accepts countable choice and regards Markov's principle as an optional, contested addition rather than a cornerstone. Party B is a working classical mathematician: he accepts excluded middle without restriction, holds that a statement's content is fixed by its truth conditions rather than by our means of establishing it, and freely concedes that many of his proofs yield no algorithm, that some classical existence claims require choice-type principles, that independence phenomena are real, and that adding a classical axiom to a proof assistant blocks program extraction from the resulting development. Neither party is to be caricatured, and neither should be allowed to win by definition.

The deliverable should have the following parts.

1. A numbered section of agreed claims. For each, state whether both parties read it univocally or whether they sign the same words while attaching different contents to them; where the latter, specify each reading. Do not pad this section with platitudes both would sign only because it says nothing.

2. A disagreements table with columns: the issue; the formulation Party A would accept as a statement of her own view; the formulation Party B would accept as a statement of his; classification as substantive, verbal, or mixed; and what would have to be settled for the classification to change.

3. A cruxes section. I suspect one crux is whether a proof's supplying an algorithm belongs to the content of the statement asserted or is a further feature of the particular proof, and another is whether the two parties are quantifying over the same objects at all when they say "the real numbers." Add or replace as you judge. For each crux, name the concrete case, theorem, or metatheorem both parties accept as bearing on it, and record each side's prediction in advance, phrased so that it could be shown to have failed.

4. An inventory of the merely verbal component of the dispute, with reasons. If you conclude that some of it is not merely verbal after all, say so and say why the temptation to call it verbal is itself misleading.

5. A short list of formulations each party agrees to stop using, and what it will say instead.

Please run the following dossier through parts 1–4, assigning each item and justifying the assignment: the negative-translation results embedding classical propositional and first-order reasoning into intuitionistic systems; the one-directional soundness observation that whatever Party A proves, Party B also holds true; the approximate form of the intermediate value theorem available constructively and the extra hypotheses needed for an exact root; the constructive treatment of compactness and the failure of the Heine–Borel formulation for the unit interval; a Specker sequence — computable, strictly increasing, bounded above, with a classical limit that is not computable; the undecidability of equality for computable reals and the weak-counterexample style arguments built on undecided number-theoretic conjectures; existence results that genuinely resist constructive treatment, such as bases for arbitrary vector spaces and the full Hahn–Banach theorem; the known unwinding and conservation results bearing on whether classical proofs of concrete computational statements can be matched by constructive ones, with an honest account of what those results do and do not establish; and the question whether the two parties share a subject matter.

Finally, audit three candidate sentences that both parties are tempted to endorse: "the constructivist proves fewer theorems than the classical mathematician"; "a classical existence proof shows that a construction exists without supplying one"; and "the two parties accept the same mathematical practice and differ only in their account of it." For each, say whether it can be signed as written, signed only after a stated repair, or must be struck — and identify whose interests the unrepaired version serves.

Where you believe a residual disagreement should be recorded as one that no argument currently available to either side would settle, record it as such rather than dissolving it.
