I argued with a friend for most of last night about a handful of cases we could not resolve, and I want an outside assessment. These are my settled judgments, reached case by case rather than from any theory.

1. A stranger told me in advance, credibly, that he could predict my answers. He flipped a coin yesterday: if it came up heads he would give me a large sum this morning, but only if he had predicted that I would hand over a small sum to him if it came up tails. It came up tails, and he is now asking. I should pay.
2. My car dies in the desert. A passing driver will take me to town only if I promise to send him money for fuel afterward, and he is the sort who can tell whether I mean it. In town I will never see him again. I should mean it and I should send the money — but this does not feel like ordinary promise-keeping, since being rescued at all depended on my being someone who would pay.
3. A prediction of my behavior was made from a scan taken when I was six, and I have changed enormously since. Here, unlike in case 1, refusing feels permissible: that scan was not a prediction about me.
4. A man drives home past two identical unmarked exits; the second gets him home, the first strands him, and he cannot tell them apart. Before leaving he plans to drive past both. Reaching an exit and unsure which it is, I think he should hold to the plan.
5. My friend resolved in January to stop eating sugar and reconsiders every evening with full knowledge of the facts. She is not irrational to reconsider. Cases 4 and 5 look alike — a choice made now against a choice made earlier — and I cannot say why they come out differently.

Is there one principle about when an earlier decision binds and when new information should change my mind that delivers all five? State it precisely, and tell me what it says about the point we never settled: whether it matters that the prediction in case 1 was made before the coin landed rather than by someone who simply watched me decide and reported it.
