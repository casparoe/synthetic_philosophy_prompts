I am revising an essay for a literature-and-philosophy workshop on testimony, and I would like help working through a reading of Ian McEwan's *Atonement* against the contemporary debate over the norm of assertion — the disagreement about whether assertion is governed by a truth rule, a belief rule, or a knowledge rule, together with the related distinction between violating such a rule and being blameworthy for violating it. I want criticism, not plot summary.

Start with Briony's identification of Robbie at the end of Part One. Suppose, as the novel seems to encourage, that she sincerely believed what she said at the moment she said it — that her conviction was assembled out of narrative need rather than evidence, but was nonetheless real. On the belief rule her assertion is then perfectly proper; she made the sort of mistake the rule is designed to tolerate. Yet nothing in the novel invites us to treat 1935 as an honest error of that kind, and the adult Briony does not treat it that way either. Is the novel's moral phenomenology therefore an argument in favour of the knowledge rule — the sense that she did something wrong *as an asserter*, not merely that she got it wrong? Or does the harshness of the verdict come from somewhere the norm-of-assertion framework does not capture at all: from how she formed the belief, from her indifference to whether she knew, or from the fact that the practice she entered was one of giving evidence rather than of telling? Please also take seriously the excuse route — that a child, in the dark, asked a direct question by adults, violates the rule without being blameworthy — and tell me whether that route can absorb the novel's verdict intact, and what has to give if it cannot.

Second, the 1935 sequence is not one bad assertion but a cascade: a studied non-denial by the actual victim, a silence from the man who benefits, an eager police and family, and, decades later, an adult retraction that no one accepts. Speaker-centred norms treat these as heterogeneous acts, while the novel treats them as a single mechanism. Does the pressure here push towards a norm on the hearer's side, or towards norms governing retraction and repair? Say what a speaker-focused framework systematically misses about how responsibility is distributed across that sequence.

Third, and most difficult: the closing, undated section reveals that what we have read is Briony's manuscript, tells us that the reconciliation narrated in Part Three never happened, that Robbie died of septicaemia during the evacuation from Dunkirk and that Cecilia was killed in the Balham tube station disaster, and yet insists that she has given the lovers their happiness in the book. On the standard view, fictional utterances are not assertions, so no norm of assertion governs them and her invention cannot be a lie. But my reader's verdict seems to decide between two theories. If we judge that she has lied — that conferring a happiness she knows never occurred is a wrong of the same family as her 1935 testimony — then we are treating the novel as assertion, and the knowledge rule reaches inside fiction after all. If we judge the act legitimate, the non-assertion view holds, but then her atonement is not the making-amends she takes it to be, since nothing she writes can be true or false in the sense her project requires. Work out which of these the novel endorses, and whether it can endorse either without self-undermining.

Finally, tell me where this reading strains. The book is commonly read as a novel about the failure of imagination, or about the impossibility of knowing other minds, and I want to know honestly where the assertion lens distorts rather than illuminates — including whether a child's utterances are governed by the same rules as an adult's, and whether the closing section is best described as assertion at all.

Two constraints on the deliverable. Please do not include a bibliography, a list of references, or any verbatim quotation from the novel; paraphrase the relevant scenes and locate them by part and circumstance so that I can find them in my own copy. I would like a continuous critical argument that tells me where the novel and the norm debate genuinely meet, where the honest critic must concede that the lens is imposed, and — by way of conclusion — what the novel contributes to the debate over the norm of assertion that argument on its own could not have delivered.
