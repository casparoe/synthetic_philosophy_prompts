I teach an upper-level undergraduate seminar on ethics and economics — about 20 students, one 90-minute session. The students have read a standard utilitarian text, Nozick's entitlement critique of end-state principles of justice, and enough Coase to state the invariance result roughly. I want you to design a classroom market game that makes them feel a specific philosophical problem rather than hear me describe it: the problem raised by the idea that wrongs and good deeds might be *transferable*, so that what matters is only the net balance on a ledger and not who caused what.

The contrast I want them to run into has two halves. First, a world in which every moral item is fully alienable produces better totals and, arguably, worse persons: the harm does not disappear, it just relocates to whoever is cheapest at bearing it, and the person with the best score may have been the worst actor. Second, once some items are made inalienable, the aggregate score falls but each player's score begins to track what that player actually did — and the price at which it becomes rational to reform yourself rather than buy an offset shifts visibly.

Here is the skeleton I have in mind. Please fill it out into something I can actually run, and correct the skeleton where you think it will not work.

**Round 1, the clear market.** Each player receives an endowment of harm cards and repair cards. Each card bears a point value and a provenance stamp — the badge number and initials of the player who first received it, written in permanent ink before play. Score at the end of the round is repairs held minus harms held, with any harm still in your hand counting double, so there is real pressure to shed. All cards are freely tradable against a notional currency of credit notes issued in fixed supply; a scribe keeps the single authoritative ledger and records a price series. Midway through, the facilitator deals a fresh tranche of harm cards to random players, so the market must absorb a flow and not just a static endowment. Players also have a conduct option available at all times: at a fixed cost in notes and forfeited trading turns, they may cancel one of the harm cards they personally issued, at face value rather than market price.

**Round 2, the marked market.** Identical endowments, identical scoring, identical conduct option — except that every harm card is now stamped non-transferable and must stay with its original issuer, while repair cards remain fully tradable. I expect the price of repairs to spike, and I expect some players to discover that self-reform has become the cheaper route.

**Optional Round 1b, the redeal.** If time allows, redistribute the initial harms entirely differently while holding the total constant, and let the class check whether the final score distribution survives the change in Round 1 and whether it survives in Round 2.

**What I need from you:**

1. A materials list and a printable card set: at least twelve harm types and twelve repair types, each with a point value and a one-line deed description. Keep the deeds abstract, small-scale, and free of real atrocity — the kind of thing one does to colleagues, neighbours, or shared stock. The values should be calibrated so that comparative advantage in *bearing* harms genuinely differs across players, and so that a price for a repair card can plausibly cross the cost of the conduct option partway through Round 1. Explain your calibration reasoning and tell me what happens to the game if I get it wrong.
2. A minute-by-minute timeline for 90 minutes, including the mid-round shock, the two scoring interludes, and the debrief.
3. Facilitator script lines for the single pivot moment I care about most: at the close of Round 1, having every player read aloud the provenance stamp on each harm card they are holding. Give me the exact question sequence that gets the class from "the total went up" to "the harms are all still in the room, and so is the person who did them," without my saying either sentence for them.
4. A debrief that maps specific moments in the play onto the underlying distinctions, and only *now* supplies the names and the literature — moral offsetting and moral licensing, the Coasean invariance idea and its moral analogue, end-state versus historical accounts, inalienability, agent-relative constraints, and the crowding out of moral motives by market norms. For each, tell me which observed moment in the game licenses the label, and flag any place where the label is being stretched.
5. An honest account of what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this, and what to do about it in the moment. I expect at least: a cartel forming in repair cards; one or two students refusing to trade on principle; forgery or a disputed transfer; deals that are elaborate but score-neutral; and a round that overruns. Tell me which of these I should permit, which I should rule on, and which I should convert into teaching material.
6. A frank statement of what this activity cannot reproduce and that I will have to supply myself — in particular, the fact that nobody in the room is actually harmed, so the reluctance the students feel is a reaction to bookkeeping rather than to wrongdoing, and the fact that the game assumes every repair card corresponds to a real repair, which is precisely the assumption that fails in actual offset markets.

Do not lecture in advance and do not let any technical term reach the students before the debrief; the design should be such that a student who arrived late and heard nothing I said would still be able to reconstruct the problem from the ledger alone.

Finally: tell me how confident you are that the two rounds will reliably produce the contrast I am after with twenty undergraduates rather than with idealized agents, and single out the one element of the design you are least sure of — the calibration, the pivot moment, the treatment of refusers, or something else — and say what you would change if that element fails in practice.
