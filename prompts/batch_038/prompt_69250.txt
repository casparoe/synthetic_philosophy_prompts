I run a small catering outfit — six of us, mostly weddings and office lunches — and I've realized I make maybe thirty actual decisions a day with no time to work out what the best answer really is. A friend of mine, Dev, mentioned at a barbecue last summer that there's a word for what I do, satisficing, from some guy called Simon, and that the idea is you don't go hunting for the best option, you take the first one that clears a bar you set ahead of time. He also said the phrase bounded rationality, which I liked because it sounds less like an excuse. That stuck with me. I've never read any of the actual books and I don't want a reading list or paper titles — I couldn't go look them up anyway and honestly I have no academic background, I just want someone to tell me straight whether the rules I've cobbled together for myself are any good.

Here's what I've accumulated over about nine years of this. Each one has a nickname because that's how I think of them:

1. Second cheapest — if there's more than four options for anything I buy, I take the second-cheapest one. The cheapest is always cutting something, and the top half of the price range is me paying for a logo.
2. The ten-minute clock — any purchase under $200 gets ten minutes of thought, and if I haven't decided by then I don't buy it that day at all.
3. Ask whoever has to live with it — anything about the rota or the kitchen layout, I ask one person, whoever happens to be on shift when I think of it, and then I just do what they said.
4. Coin flip with a peek — when I'm torn between two, I flip a coin and while it's in the air I notice which side I'm secretly hoping for, then I go with that side no matter what the coin does.
5. Three strikes and it's a rule — if I've made the same call three times in a row, I write it down as standing policy and stop deciding it. That's how we ended up never doing a delivery before 10am.
6. One night, not two — anything big gets slept on exactly once. I don't allow a second night, because that's how you lose the gig.
7. The supplier's pick — when I'm choosing among one supplier's own grades or products, I ask them which one they'd use themselves and I take that.
8. Better than last time — I never compare a new option to the perfect option, only to whatever I did last time. New venue versus the last venue, new temp chef versus the last temp chef. If it beats the last one, I take it and stop looking.
9. Leave slack — I never book the vans or the kitchen past about 85% of what they could hold, and I always order 10% more food than the headcount says.

What I want is a ranking, not a discussion. Sort all nine from most to least worth keeping, given my situation specifically: thin margins, decisions made fast and repeated constantly, no data analysis, and mistakes that usually show up as a bad review or a lost deposit rather than as an obvious disaster. For each one, I want to know how much thinking it actually saves me versus how likely it is to fail in a systematic way — meaning it keeps failing the same direction every time and I probably wouldn't notice, as opposed to just getting it wrong now and then like anything would. And tell me what feature of the world each rule is really getting purchase on, if any. Be blunt. Don't hedge, don't give everything a 3, and don't sandwich the criticism in compliments.

Please answer as a single JSON object with exactly these keys:

- ranked: an array of nine objects in rank order, each with rank (1–9), nickname (as I wrote it), tier (A, B, C, or D, where A is keep it and D is get rid of it), thinking_saved (1–5, 5 = saves me the most actual mental work), fails_systematically (1–5, 5 = will keep failing the same way without me noticing), exploits (one short phrase naming the feature of the decision or the world this rule is actually tracking, or null if you think it isn't tracking anything), and note (one blunt sentence)
- costume: an array of objects, one per rule you think is really just a bias wearing a rule's clothes, each with nickname and which_bias (name the bias in plain English, no jargon)
- overlaps: an array of objects with pair (two nicknames) and keep (which one I should keep and why in a sentence)
- conflict: an object with rule_a, rule_b, and winner — the two rules on my list most likely to give me opposite answers in real life, and which one should win
