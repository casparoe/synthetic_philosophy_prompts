So once a week I help out at a community centre near where I live, on one of those "difficult conversations" circles where people who've fallen out over some local thing come and have a natter with a facilitator. Ours opens every single session with the blind men and the elephant — six blokes who've never seen an elephant each grab hold of a different bit, then fight about what an elephant is like. Her line afterwards is never any different: we're all just blind men feeling different parts of the same elephant. Most of the time that's meant kindly, a way of getting people to stop shouting and admit they could be missing something. But last month it got used for something else entirely. A woman said that a campaign group's leaflet about a planning application was simply telling lies, and the facilitator did the elephant bit and said nobody's got the whole truth so it's not for any of us to say whose account is the wrong one. I sat there feeling like something had gone sideways and I couldnt say what, which is annoying because I'd happily told the same story myself a fortnight earlier.

I looked the parable up afterwards and it turns out it's a favourite in Jain philosophy, where it's used to illustrate something called anekantavada, many-sidedness, and there's a related habit of qualifying what you say with a word meaning roughly "in some respect" or "maybe", so you're never flatly asserting the whole truth off the back of one angle. That's about as much as I properly took in, and I've no idea how much of it survives the trip from a technical doctrine to a Tuesday night in a church hall.

Could you unpack that one sentence for me as a menu of the different precise claims it could be making, rather than settling on whichever reading you'd personally defend? Stuff I'm circling: is it saying every person's view is incomplete, or that no view can ever be completed by anyone; does it commit you to an elephant that really exists and really has all the features people are groping for, or can you run it without any elephant at all once you've listed the perspectives; and is it a claim about what's true, about what we're allowed to assert out loud, or only about how people ought to behave in a room together? For each reading you give me, say whether it's a platitude nobody would deny, whether it's substantive but defensible, whether it's just false, or whether it saws off the branch it's sitting on — I've got a vague suspicion that the boldest versions undo themselves, since the person saying it seems to be claiming to know something about the whole elephant that none of the blind men have. Then, and this is the bit I actually want, tell me which of the readings would have supported what our facilitator did that night and which wouldn't, and what the Jain version with its "in some respect" hedging would and would not license her to say to that woman. Please write the answer in American English, even tho I've written all this in my own scrappy British way.
