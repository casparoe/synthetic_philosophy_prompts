Write me a dialogue between two philosophers of language who disagree about how much work a speaker's intentions actually do. Here's the case they're arguing over: in a civil deposition, a lawyer asks a warehouse supervisor whether a certain shipment left on Friday. The supervisor is a very literal guy and answers that the driver was in the building all day and that he saw him eating lunch around noon. The lawyer asks no follow-up, then tells the jury in closing that the supervisor confirmed the shipment didnt leave. The supervisor is mortified — he says he never meant anything about the shipment, he just answered the question he was asked.

Character A takes a broadly Gricean line: you can't communicate that p unless you intend your hearer to recognise that you intend p, so nothing about the shipment was ever said or implicated — the lawyer did all the work. Character B thinks that makes communication absurdly mind-dependent: the inference was sitting right there in the exchange for any competent hearer to pick up, and whatever's going on privately in the supervisor's head doesn't get to veto it.

Both should be sharp and neither should be a pushover — they need to take on the actual best arguments, examples and diagnostic tests the other side has, rather than trading slogans. And I want the ending to leave things clearer about what would settle the dispute, or why nothing could.
