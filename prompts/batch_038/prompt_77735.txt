I want to stress-test a methodological claim about periodization, not relitigate any particular era. My claim has three separable parts.

First, labels such as the Middle Ages, the Renaissance, and the Scientific Revolution are retrospective constructions. Each was coined by later writers to cast their own standpoint as the destination of the preceding centuries, and each is now reproduced chiefly by institutional inertia — departments, survey syllabi, publisher categories, library classifications — rather than by anything the evidence compels.

Second, and more strongly, these labels do no independent explanatory work. Whatever a boundary appears to explain is already explained by local mechanisms operating on their own timescales: changes in scribal and copying practice, the cost and supply of writing materials, the career structures of particular institutions, the arrival or recovery of specific bodies of text, the reorganization of patronage, and so on. The period term is at best a summary convenience over these, and at worst a cause smuggled in where a description was wanted.

Third, I would hold that a period label deserves to be called real only if it passes what I will call the blind-sorting test: given an unlabeled, undated corpus of artifacts spanning a wide window on either side of an alleged boundary, an analyst working only from intrinsic features of the material — and with no prior acquaintance with the label — should sort the corpus into clusters whose edges fall within a tolerance narrower than the granularity the label itself claims. If the clusters land elsewhere, or nowhere in particular, the boundary is a habit rather than a finding.

What would have to be true for this composite view to be false? Please treat the three parts separately, because I expect their exposures to differ considerably; in particular, tell me whether part three is a criterion I could abandon without surrendering the first two, and what that abandonment would cost me. For each candidate defeater, say whether it would be decisive or merely awkward, and say what a historian working inside these periods would themselves concede as counting against me, as distinct from what an outside critic would allege. Address the possibility that the view has been insulated by qualification — for instance, by treating any successful clustering as an artifact of the analyst's own inherited categories, or by retreating to the weaker claim that the labels are useful fictions — and give an honest verdict on whether that insulation is a defect in a thesis whose target is a way of writing history rather than an empirical hypothesis about the world.

I would also like two things beyond the defeater map. State what my view predicts that a moderate realism about periods does not: where new dating and classification techniques should reproduce inherited boundaries, where they should dissolve them, and whether any such test has arguably already been run with a result unfavorable to me. And assess whether the blind-sorting test is itself fair — specifically, whether it is rigged, on the grounds that a boundary is defined by precisely those features an experienced historian has already learned to look for, so that no sorting procedure could fail to recover it and none could confirm it either. Rank your conclusions from fatal to me down to merely inconvenient.
