Hi — third-year philosophy of science PhD student here, co-teaching (well, "co-teaching", mostly marking) a masters-level course on scientific modelling. Next week's problem set is on unrealistic assumptions and idealisation, and I want a question that can't be survived by reciting Friedman's 1953 methodology essay or by dumping Musgrave's three-way taxonomy onto the page. My supervisor's advice was to bury the taxonomy inside a case where the classification is genuinly contestable, which is what I've tried to do below. Important: the case study is entirely made up by me — I invented the authors, the model and the numbers — so treat it as fictional and don't go looking for the real paper, there isn't one.

**The exercise, as I want it put to the students**

Kovač and Ferreira (2021) build a model of the spread of a carbapenem-resistant Klebsiella strain on a 40-bed hospital ward. Call it M. M makes three idealisations that the authors flag themselves:

- **A1 — perfect mixing.** Any colonised patient is equally likely to transmit to any susceptible patient on any given day. There is no ward geometry, no contact network, no clustering by bay.
- **A2 — closed system.** No patient arrives already colonised and no external reservoir exists; the ward is the only site of transmission in the model world.
- **A3 — unsystematic noise.** Hand-hygiene compliance varies around a fixed daily rate, and the day-to-day deviations are small and unsystematic.

Empirically: M is fitted to 18 months of surveillance data, incidence per patient-day. It tracks the series closely, R² = 0.91, and gets the timing of the two recorded outbreaks right to within a week. The authors argue that A1 in particular is vindicated, on the grounds that patients transmit as though contacts were random, and that the falsity of A2 is harmless because no colonised admissions were detected anywhere in the surveillance window.

Then a 12-bed step-down unit opens off the same corridor, drawing patients from a long-term care facility two miles away. Kovač and Ferreira keep the same fitted parameters and extend M to the 52-bed footprint. Over the next six months M under-predicts incidence by roughly a factor of four, and the two outbreaks it does predict come six to nine weeks late. Their response in a rejoinder: M was fitted and validated on the original 40-bed ward, the new wing is simply outside its intended scope, and nothing in the failure casts doubt on A1, A2 or A3 as applied to that ward.

*Question — three parts, 60 minutes, 25 marks.*

(a) Classify A1, A2 and A3 using Musgrave's distinction between domain assumptions, negligibility assumptions and heuristic assumptions. Say for each what kind of criticism it is vulnerable to and what kind it isn't — that is, exactly where the F-twist bites and where it doesn't. [8]

(b) The rejoinder says the out-of-sample failure is irrelevant to the status of the idealisations within the original scope. Give the strongest version of that argument, then the strongest objection to it that relies only on facts stated above. [9]

(c) Here's a distinction I've been developing in my own work and I want students to have to *use* it rather than have heard of it. Call a model's empirical success a **parasitic fit** when it depends on two or more false assumptions whose errors happen to cancel inside the calibration window, such that removing any one of them would make the model worse rather than better; contrast a **robust fit**, where success survives the removal of any one idealisation. Decide whether M's in-sample performance is best described as parasitic fit, say what evidence from the case would settle it either way, and explain why the answer changes what the rejoinder in (b) is entitled to claim. [8]
