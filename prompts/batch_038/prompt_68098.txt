I'm giving a talk in about three weeks at a physics institute's colloquium series — the kind where the room is mostly working physicists (a lot of phenomenologists, a couple of condensed matter people, and two who genuinely do foundations), plus a handful of philosophers who got invited under the "science and society" banner. I'm the philosopher of physics on the program. I'm going to argue something I expect both halves of the room to hate, and I want to walk in ready.

The thesis, roughly: the Weinberg / Hawking-and-Mlodinow / Feynman complaint is *true*, and the philosophers who answer it have been answering the wrong version of it. True in the sense that philosophy does not hand physics content — no philosophical argument has ever changed what a physicist calculated, and I'm not going to pretend otherwise. What I want to defend instead is that philosophy's contribution to a science is what I'll call the re-specification function: the activity of showing that a question a field treats as settled, or as merely technical, is not answerable in the terms the field has set, and of forcing the field to redo the specification of the question. On that account the output of philosophy isn't predictions, it's a change in which questions count as well-posed. Then I have a test I want to defend, call it the withdrawal test: a piece of philosophy has done that work only if, after the field has absorbed it, you can point to a specific class of problems the field now handles without stumbling, where the stumbling is documented, and where the unstumbling traces to the philosophical work rather than to the new math or the new data that came along with it.

My three exhibits. (1) The hole argument and general covariance in GR — the field had to be pushed into being explicit about when two mathematically distinct models count as physically the same situation, and the thing that resolves it is a criterion of individuation, not a calculation. (2) The measurement problem — for a few decades the official answer was effectively "don't ask," and the Everett / Bohm / collapse options got back into physics through work that was as much philosophy as physics. (3) The fine-tuning and multiverse business, where a probability-theoretic objection about what you're allowed to infer from the fact that this universe happens to contain observers got picked up by people who do this for a living. My claim is that the re-specification function is structurally invisible from inside the field, because by the time it works the work has been absorbed and the question no longer looks philosophical — so the physicists' complaint is guaranteed to feel true from where they're sitting, the same way an immune system never shows up on the org chart.

I'll end on the corollary: philosophers should stop claiming credit for content and start claiming credit for question hygiene, and should be willing to say out loud that most philosophy of physics fails the withdrawal test and deserves to.

So — build me the prep sheet. Specifically:

- the objections this particular audience will actually raise, in roughly the order they'll come, phrased the way a physicist would phrase them, which is not the way a philosopher would. Include the ones coming from the two foundations people, who will be far more dangerous than the phenomenologists because they know exactly which parts of my exhibits are theirs and which are mine.
- for each one: how much damage it does, what I should concede immediately and without squirming, what I hold onto, and a reply I can deliver in about ninety seconds standing up.
- tell me which of the three exhibits is the load-bearing weak one — where a single objection takes out more than just that exhibit and starts pulling on the account itself.
- the question I'm praying nobody asks. I have a guess: something like "if the function is invisible by construction, name one live question in physics right now that you claim isn't well-posed, and tell us what you'd re-specify it into." But tell me if there's a worse one lurking, and tell me honestly whether there's any answer to that one that isn't just bluffing.
- and where the philosophers in the room will come at me for conceding too much — I think they'll say I've accepted the enemy's criterion of usefulness and then tried to win on the enemy's field, which makes my whole talk a surrender dressed as a victory.

Don't give me a reading list or a bibliography, I know the literature and I can't look anything up in the room anyway. I want the moves: who says what, how bad it is, what I say back, and where my reply is soft.
