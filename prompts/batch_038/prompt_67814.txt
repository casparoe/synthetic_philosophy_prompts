I convene a graduate reading group in early modern philosophy, and we have a tradition of opening each session with a short joke that only works if the room has actually done the reading. This week's session concerns Thomas Hobbes's philosophy of geometry: his account of why geometry is the paradigm of scientia, the kinematic foundation he gives it in De Corpore (1655), and the pamphlet war that followed the purported quadrature of the circle included in that work and its refutation by John Wallis. Please write the opening joke.

The humour must be generated by the content of Hobbes's position, and specifically by what his own criterion of knowledge implies once it is turned back on his failed construction. The criterion is the one he works with throughout: philosophy is knowledge of effects acquired by reasoning from their causes, or of causes from their effects, so that we have scientia of a thing only insofar as we know how it is generated. Geometry qualifies as science in the strictest sense on this account because the geometer does not wait to be told how his objects came to be; he generates them himself, by motions he has stipulated, whereas in natural philosophy the bodies under investigation were made by someone else and their generation is hidden from us. Build the joke out of the trouble this creates for a man who claims to have generated a square equal to a given circle and is then shown by an opponent that he has not. The reflexive point is available in more than one direction — for instance, what it means for the author of a defective demonstration to be the only person positioned to give a full causal account of exactly how the error was generated, or what becomes of a discipline in which reasoning is nothing but the adding and subtracting of consequences when the consequence in question is that one's own figure is not equal to the figure it was meant to match. Choose the direction that yields the sharpest line and commit to it; do not attempt to use both.

Constraints on the joke itself:

- Three to eight sentences. Set-up and punchline only; the joke must not explain itself.
- No quotation of any text, no chapter or part numbers, no page references, and no Latin titles. The reader should need nothing beyond the ideas set out above.
- Hobbes and Wallis may be named, as may the Savilian chair or the Royal Society if you want a setting. No other historical figures, and nothing anachronistic.
- The punchline must not rest on Hobbes being old, stubborn, vain, mathematically incompetent, or unwilling to admit defeat. Those versions require no knowledge of his philosophy whatsoever and are precisely what I want to avoid.

After the joke, add a separate note of at most five sentences that identifies the single feature of Hobbes's view carrying the comic weight; says why that feature makes the joke available for this case and not for any other failed demonstration in the history of mathematics; and states what would be lost if the same joke were told about Wallis instead. Do not append anything further.
