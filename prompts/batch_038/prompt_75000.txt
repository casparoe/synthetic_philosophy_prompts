I am partway through a graduate seminar on historical explanation — we have moved from the covering-law debates into comparison, counterfactuals, and the logic of contrastive "why" questions — and my response paper has to be built out of a piece of popular writing rather than a journal article. I chose a best-selling popular history of the making of the modern world economy; I would rather not name it, since my interest is in the paragraph, not the author. In its opening chapter, where it states the puzzle the rest of the book claims to solve, it runs as follows:

> Every account of the modern world has to begin from the same puzzle, which historians know as the Needham question, after Joseph Needham, the Cambridge biochemist who spent decades documenting Chinese science and technology. By the fifteenth century China had been well ahead of Europe for the better part of a millennium: gunpowder, the compass, papermaking and printing, cast iron, the sternpost rudder, segment-gate canal locks, and an examination-selected bureaucracy, while Europe was still struggling back from the plague. Yet the Scientific Revolution and the Industrial Revolution both happened in Europe, and the second of them in Britain specifically. The usual answer is institutional. Europe was divided among competing states, so a ruler who turned away from an innovation simply handed it to a rival — when Portugal passed on Columbus, Castile took him up — whereas a unified China could decide for an entire civilisation, and did: the court abandoned the treasure fleets after 1433 and for long stretches treated private ocean-going trade as a capital offence. Revisionists have complicated this without overturning it. Kenneth Pomeranz and the others of the California School established that as late as 1750 the Yangzi delta was as rich and as commercialised as England, with markets and property rights differing from England's in no way that mattered, and that what Britain uniquely possessed was coal in accessible, well-sited deposits together with an American empire that supplied it with land it did not have at home. So the divergence comes down to coal and colonies. The honest form of the question is therefore contrastive: not why Europe industrialised, but why Britain and not China.

My seminar instructor read this whole framing as a way of not answering anything, and asked us to consider whether the question the paragraph ends on is well posed. My own instinct runs the other way: contrastive questions of exactly this shape are put constantly by working historians, and I suspect the paragraph is guilty of nothing worse than efficient compression of a live debate. I cannot yet say which of us is right, and I have a strong feeling it is doing two or three things at once that I am failing to keep apart.

Please work through the paragraph and tell me, claim by claim, what a specialist in this literature would accept as stated, what counts as fair compression, and what is wrong or seriously misleading. Three points in particular I want settled. First, whether the two puzzles it runs together from its first sentence to its last are in fact one puzzle, and what changes for the argument if they are not. Second, whether its account of what the usual answer is can be reconciled with its account of what the revisionists showed, or whether the paragraph is pulling in two directions without noticing. Third, whether the closing formulation can stand as it is or has to be restated — and if restated, into exactly what, including what the two terms of the comparison should be, given how the paragraph has been shifting between them. If a defensible version of the paragraph can be written at all, please write it, so that I can see how much of the original survives correction.
