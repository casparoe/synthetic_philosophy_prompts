I'm a food security analyst at a small consortium that does early warning and public reporting for a region with maybe a dozen at-risk districts. About eight or ten times a year something escalates past the point where we have to say something in public, and the technical classification part is already handled — our epidemiologist and the IPC-style team sort out whether the mortality and malnutrition thresholds are met. That's not what I need. What I need is the thing that comes *after* the classification, which our funder calls the attribution paragraph: a few sentences saying whether people starved mainly because food wasn't there, or because they couldn't get at food that was there, and whose decisions made the difference. Right now that paragraph gets written by whoever is least busy, it comes out as "the crisis was compounded by a combination of drought and conflict," and it tells nobody anything.

Stakes: if we name a state actor, legal reviews it as an allegation, and our access agreement has a clause about politicisation, so there's a standing incentive to write in the passive voice. If we stay vague, we're functionally laundering a political event into a weather report, and the press quotes us either way. We get about 48 hours from the classification landing to the statement going out, the team is four people (an agronomist, an economist, a protection officer, me), and the country director signs off. She's not an economist and she wants one page, so whatever you give me has to be runnable by people who've never read a word of welfare economics.

So: build me the procedure. Ordered questions, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where it stops and says "no attribution warranted on this evidence," and where it hands the decision over to the director because it's a question of values or of access rather than of fact. The backbone should be whatever is actually usable from Sen's entitlement work — the idea that your ability to eat depends on what your endowment can command at prevailing prices and wages, not on the harvest as such, and the resulting split between production failures, trade failures, own-labour failures and transfer failures. I want that split translated into questions a protection officer can answer from market data, wage data and household interviews, plus the mechanisms people forget: restrictions on movement, bans on selling things, closure of a crossing, a wage that stops keeping pace with grain. And I want the procedure to enforce one hard rule that we keep breaking, which is that "how much food was there," "who could command food," and "whose decisions mattered" are three different questions, and an answer to one must not be smuggled in as an answer to another. That includes the reverse direction: a genuinely enormous production collapse that didn't have to become a famine, because the state did the thing that protects incomes.

Anchor it in the three historical cases, but tell me what each one contributes procedurally rather than recapping it. What does Bengal 1943 teach a team about causes that live far away and were aimed at something else entirely — war logistics, shipping, price controls — and about what to do when the specialists still dispute whether there was an aggregate shortfall at all? What does Ó Gráda's objection about Ireland teach us, that the availability/entitlement distinction gets blurry when the subsistence crop itself is what fails? And what does the Great Leap Forward add, which I think is the bit we're missing: a state destroying its own information channels, inflated yield reports driving over-procurement, nobody at the top knowing, movement restricted so people couldn't go find food. That's an attribution category we don't have — culpable ignorance and broken feedback, as distinct from knowingly letting it happen — and the procedure needs a node for it.

Then run it on three cases:

1. A cyclone takes out maybe a third of the coastal harvest. Imports land within a month and aggregate supply recovers fast, but the deaths are concentrated among landless labourers and boat crews whose wages collapsed while rice prices tripled. The government ran cash-for-work in two of the five affected districts and not the other three.
2. No harvest failure anywhere. A civil war next door closed the crossing our pastoralists trade through, livestock terms of trade fell apart, and a new environmental regulation banned charcoal burning, which was their main cash income. Our own early warning unit flagged it fourteen months out and the ministry sat on it through an election.
3. The one I don't want to run: a competitive electoral democracy, opposition-controlled regional assembly, a press that has been reporting this for six months, and the national government letting it run because the starving district votes the wrong way, plus two survey teams turned back at the border. Half my team's instinct is to say that can't be a famine under a democracy, so something must be wrong with our data. Tell the procedure how to handle an analyst whose priors about how famines work are being used to argue away mortality numbers.

Also be straight with me about the limits: which steps depend on data we simply won't have (occupational breakdown of the dead, real wage series, price ratios), what to do when the authorities are blocking data collection so you can't tell obstruction from ordinary absence of evidence, how to stop "we cannot know yet" from becoming a permanent dodge, and what should trigger re-running the thing and issuing a correction. And a list of the phrases that sound like attribution but aren't, so I can ban them from our copy. No reading list, no bibliography — I want the procedure and the three worked runs.
