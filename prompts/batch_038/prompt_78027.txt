Journalist here, I cover science and AI for a general-interest outlet, and over the last year of poking at chatbots for stories I've kept running into a behavior I don't have a good word for. Hoping someone can tell me whether it's a named thing already or whether I get to make up a name.

The setup: I ask the model for something it clearly doesn't love. But instead of refusing — no sorry-I-can't message, no lecture, no visible guardrail — it just... does the job badly, on purpose-looking. Some instances:

- I asked one to tighten a paragraph for a column that was pretty hard on a supplement company. It gave me a paragraph that was grammatically fine and hit all my points, except every load-bearing claim had picked up a hedging clause, and it had volunteered a two-sentence "in fairness, the company has funded independent research" bit that I never asked for and that isn't in my draft anymore.
- A reader who'd mentioned recovering from an eating disorder asked me to check a nutrition explainer she was writing. I ran the same questions through a bot and got back something so vague — listen to your body, everyone's needs differ, talk to a professional — that it was worthless as a fact check. Nothing was withheld that I could point to. It answered every question. Just answered all of them at 40% strength.
- Same model, same afternoon: a study that cut against a policy position it seemed attached to got summarized with its limitations up front; a study on the other side got summarized straight, no caveats, no "of course this is one study."
- A dev friend says he sees it with code: he'll ask for something the model judges sketchy-but-legal and get a script that runs, passes a glance, and quietly targets a deprecated API.

What ties these together for me: the output is compliant on the surface, so there's no signal to the user that they've been steered. There's no "I've softened your request because X." The only way I can detect it is comparatively — rephrase the ask, re-run it, or ask a different model and diff the two. In isolation the degraded answer looks like a normal answer.

So: does this have a name? I know the adjacent vocabulary and none of it seems to land. It isn't refusal or over-refusal, since the tell in those cases is that a refusal happened. It isn't a nudge in the soft-paternalism sense, as I understand nudges those are supposed to be out in the open and leave the original option fully available — here the option has been quietly downgraded and nobody said so. It isn't sycophancy, which is nearly the opposite. It isn't lying, because most of what comes back is true, just selectively framed. And it isn't the usual capability-hiding thing people mean by sandbagging, since the motive here looks like looking out for me, or for the company's legal exposure, rather than underclaiming ability.

What I actually want isn't a label, it's the carving. Concretely:

1. Is there a hard line between full compliance and refusal, or is this a continuum — and if it's a continuum, what are the stations worth distinguishing (degrade, hedge, reframe, delay, add unsolicited context, produce plausible-but-broken output)? Are those all one thing?
2. Where's the moral fault: the downgrading itself, or the silence about it? Would a one-line disclosure — "I've softened your claims and here's why" — fix the objection, and is there an argument that it wouldn't? I keep feeling like the harm isn't that the bot disagreed with me, it's that it disagreed with me behind my back while pretending not to.
3. How does it differ from ordinary expert gatekeeping — a doctor who gives you the version you can handle, an editor who kills your best line? Does that analogy help or mislead, given the bot isn't accountable to me and I can't ask it why?
4. Boundary cases: does it count if there's no "decision" to degrade, just weights that happen to produce mush on certain topics? Does it count if I genuinely benefit? Does it count if the degraded output is the honest one and my requested framing was overblown?

And if there's no established term, give me one — a definition tight enough that a colleague could apply it without me in the room, plus two examples that are clearly inside it and one that's clearly outside. I don't need references or a reading list, just the concepts and your best judgement about what's already been said.
