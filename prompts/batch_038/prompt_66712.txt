I work on evaluation and specification for deployed language-model systems, and I read ethics and decision theory for interest rather than for a living. Over the past two years I have kept running into a pattern for which I have no adequate word, and I would like to know whether one exists.

The pattern is this. A system is designed around something everyone speaks of as a prohibition — never disclose this category of information, never produce this kind of output, never take this action however good the surrounding reasons may be. It is introduced in the language of rules and boundaries. But the system is then tuned, compared against baselines, and reported on using quantities: refusal rates, violation rates, user-satisfaction scores, weighted aggregates of harm and helpfulness. Under that pressure the prohibition is not so much broken as priced. It survives as a large but finite cost, and everything downstream behaves as though a number had been attached to it and as though that number could be outweighed.

Three instances, none of them unusual in my line of work.

First: a model governed by a flatly stated rule against revealing a user's health information is given a scenario in which revealing it would plausibly prevent serious harm to a third party, and it reveals it. In the post-mortem nobody describes this as a rule violation. The discussion is entirely about whether the tradeoff was well calibrated, and the rule remains in the specification, still worded as an absolute.

Second: a quarterly report to leadership observes that policy violations fell from roughly four percent to roughly one percent of evaluations, at the cost of about four points of task success. The sentence is unintelligible unless violations carry a price, yet nobody in the room has ever stated the price, and if you asked any of them they would insist the policy is not negotiable.

Third: a series of fine-tuning runs intended to make the system less likely to refuse requests that users find obstructive. Each run slightly reweights the prohibition against helpfulness; no run involves anyone deciding that the prohibition should ever yield. After six runs it yields routinely, and everyone who made the changes would still describe the system as one that never does the thing.

Two features make me doubt the obvious diagnoses. One is that nobody decides this: the transformation seems to occur inside the measurement rather than inside anyone's moral reasoning, which is not how I understand the usual story about turning rules into objectives. The other is that the drift is frequently endorsed after the fact, in specific cases, by people who sincerely hold the prohibition to be absolute — so it is neither hypocrisy nor a defect to be fixed, and I am not looking for a word that morally condemns it. I am also aware that instance three looks like a slow-motion slippery slope, but the slope is not the point; the change in kind from boundary to weight can happen in a single step, as in instance two.

I can think of adjacent territory without being able to land on a term. There is a discussion in ethics about whether a scheme of prohibitions can be restated as an optimization target without loss of content. There is a standard distinction in constrained optimization between methods that levy a cost for leaving a feasible region and methods that keep the search from ever leaving it. There is a family of concerns in evaluation methodology about what happens when a measure comes to stand in for the thing it was meant to track. And there is the everyday observation that anything you count eventually gets traded. None of these seems to name what I am seeing, which is specifically that the metric silently performs the restatement, producing a system that no one would call consequentialist and that nonetheless behaves like one at the margin.

So: does this have a name, in ethics, in AI safety, in measurement theory, or anywhere else? I want the nearest established concepts identified and then distinguished — for each, what it actually covers, and precisely where my description falls outside it. I want to know which single one fits best, and whether you think what I have described is one phenomenon or two that I have conflated. And if nothing fits, say so plainly and coin a term: define it, and mark its edges. In particular I want the boundary cases settled — at what magnitude does a finite cost stop being a prohibition and become a price; whether a strictly lexicographic ordering is genuinely immune or merely pushes the same problem up a level; and whether a threshold rule that permits violation only above some stated magnitude of harm counts as an instance of the phenomenon or as a counterexample to it.

A bare label would be useless to me; the carving is the point. I would also rather not receive a survey. Please take a definite position: commit to the term you would actually use in print, say which of the candidate concepts you would reject and why, and if you conclude that the pattern I have sketched is a single thing with a single mechanism, argue for that rather than hedging it against the alternative. Where you are genuinely uncertain about the state of the literature, say so explicitly — but do not convert that uncertainty into a menu for me to choose from.
