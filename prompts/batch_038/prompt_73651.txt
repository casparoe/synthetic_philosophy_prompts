I sit on a volunteer citizen advisory board in our county that has been asked to review the risk-assessment instrument our courts use at bail hearings. None of us has a technical background: the board is a retired school principal, two defense attorneys, a couple of people who work in city government, and one former actuary. For our last meeting we were assigned a chapter from a widely read trade book about algorithms in public life, and the chapter's centerpiece is an impossibility result, summarized in these terms:

"For most of the history of this debate the assumption was that a biased algorithm was simply a badly built one: clean the data, audit the code, and the bias disappears. In the wake of the 2016 controversy over the recidivism instrument used in Florida courts, that assumption was overturned by proof. Two research groups showed that no risk score can be fair, once fairness is spelled out in the three ways that matter: that a score means the same thing no matter who receives it (equal false-positive and false-negative rates across groups); that a score tells the truth about the individual it is attached to (calibration); and that people who actually go on to reoffend receive the same average score whichever group they belong to. These three requirements cannot all be met at once unless the two groups have identical rates of reoffending to begin with, or unless the score predicts perfectly — and perfect prediction is a fantasy, while identical base rates would require that we had already eliminated the injustice producing the gap. Because every real dataset is a record of a discriminatory society, no algorithm trained on one can ever be fair, and neither can the judge who relies on it. What the theorem settles, then, is that fairness here is not a technical property to be achieved but a political choice to be made; the honest conclusion is to stop presenting risk scores as objective, and in domains like pretrial detention, to stop using them."

I found that persuasive on a first reading, and I still half believe it, but two things happened at the meeting that unsettled me. One of the defense attorneys said the passage "makes the mathematics do a job it cannot do," and then refused to elaborate. Our actuary said something close to the opposite: that this is the old base-rate point in costume, that whenever a condition is more common in one group than another the same test will carry different positive predictive values in the two groups, that clinicians and epidemiologists have known this for decades, and that nothing about algorithms or justice is involved. I cannot tell which of them has the better of it, and I do not want to walk into the next session either reciting the book or waving it away.

So please go through the passage sentence by sentence and tell me what a statistician who works on this would accept as a fair statement of the result, what they would count as a legitimate simplification, and what is simply wrong. Specifically: are the three requirements the book lists the three conditions the theorem is actually about, and does each parenthetical gloss pick out the thing it is attached to, or have labels been swapped? When the book says no risk score can be fair, is that what was proved, or is it something narrower that has been widened? The passage concedes two exceptions and then dismisses them rhetorically — are those exceptions genuinely the ones in the result, and how much do they cost the argument? Is the sentence about the judge who relies on the score part of the theorem or an extension of it? Does the claim that discriminatory data make fairness impossible correctly identify where the tension comes from, or has a different source been substituted? And does the final recommendation follow from the mathematics at all, or does it require independent argument the passage never supplies? Lastly, tell me whether the actuary's deflationary reading holds: if this is a repackaged statistical fact about predictive values and prevalence, does that make it less significant, or is the repackaging where the significance lies? I am not looking for references — I want to know whether I should trust this chapter.
