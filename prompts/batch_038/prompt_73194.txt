I write about science and ideas for a regional daily, and I've been talked into giving the opening remarks at a one-day workshop in our state capital next month. It's co-hosted by a law school clinic and the state ag department, about 70 people, and the mix is the problem: roughly half are reporters and editors, several of them off livestock and commodity beats who think any story with a face on it is soft; a quarter are vets and animal scientists, two of whom spend their working lives in confinement systems and are not there to be moved; there's three philosophers, one of whom works on Descartes specifically and has been quoted in my own copy more than once, so she will hear every shortcut I take; plus legislative aides, and one industry lobbyist who live-tweets panels. There's a live bill in the state about laying hen housing, which is why ag bothered to show up at all.

I want to hang the speech on Descartes, but not on the version everyone in that room already has, which is: he thought animals were wind-up toys that only squeak when you step on them, that was monstrous and also simply wrong, science settled it fifty years ago, so all that's left is policy. I think that's bad history, and more to the point it lets everybody skip the question he was actually asking, which was an evidence question — how do you tell a mind from a machine that behaves exactly like one? His tests (no real language; no flexibility when the situation is genuinely novel) aren't tests I'd sign up for, but its the same question my beat now gets asked weekly by readers who want to know whether a chatbot has one. That's the hinge I want. Not "Descartes was evil about dogs," and not "look, we know better now."

Deliverable: about twelve minutes, so call it 1400 words, spoken register, one idea developed properly rather than six name-checked.

Ground rules. Nothing in quotation marks attributed to Descartes — paraphrase only, because I've been burned by models turning out lovely-sounding invented translations. If you want to gesture at the correspondence you can point at the Newcastle letter about training animals and the one to More late in his life, but no dates, page numbers, or edition references. Where he actually lands on whether animals feel anything is contested among people who know this far better than me, so don't fudge it: pick a reading, build the speech on it, and defend the choice. And please don't invoke the 2012 Cambridge declaration as though it closed anything — somebody in that room will, and I'd rather not be the fourth.

Don't make the ag people the villains and don't do uplift. Give the commodity-beat reporters something true they can use on Monday, and end on something the room can honestly disagree about in the afternoon session rather than on a feeling of agreement.

For the opening I have raw material, pick one and commit: a day I spent in a finishing barn this spring; a cuttlefish learning study I watched at a marine station; a poultry welfare auditor describing what he actually looks for when he walks a house; and the tickled rats from Panksepp's group with the 50 kHz chirps they called laughter, which is disputed and which half that room knows is disputed.

Then after the speech, give me a short note — five to eight lines, not a hedge — saying which reading of Descartes you committed to and why you think it's the better one, where the speech takes liberties or leans on something contested (including the machine parallel, which is obviously anachronistic; I'm fine with it if you tell me how much weight you're putting on it), and the single line in the speech you're least confident about.
