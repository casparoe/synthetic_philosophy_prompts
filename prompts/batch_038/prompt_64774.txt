I use the importance/tractability/neglectedness framework to pick causes, but I'm not sure my gut actually matches it. Where I land:

1. Pandemic/biorisk prep: enormous stakes, few people on it, real progress plausible within a decade → clearly great.
2. Advocacy for one specific rare disease: small population so only moderately important, very neglected, and each hour of work seems to move things → still feels great, better then I can argue for.
3. Mainstream climate policy modelling: important, probably tractable, extremely crowded → bad for me.
4. A far-future issue I think is huge and totally unworked, but where nothing I do will have any effect inside my lifetime → doesn't feel like a good cause, which surprises me.
5. A project where I'd be one of ~30 people and if I left someone else would do nearly identical work → bad. Same field, but if I leave the specific project just doesn't happen → good.

The pair I can't make consistent: in (3) the fact that so many smart people are already there is part of why I believe its tractable, but it's also why my marginal contribution is about zero. One fact doing opposite work. And neglectedness has the same problem backwards: I reward a cause for being neglected while also suspecting that if it were genuinely important *and* tractable, it wouldn't still be neglected. So neglectedness feels like evidence against the other two scores rather than something to multiply them by.

My tentative fix: score a cause by (how much better the world is if I work on it) minus (how much better it is if I do the next-best thing available to me instead, including letting someone else fill the slot). Is that an actual principle or just a restatement? Does it swallow all three factors? Tell me which of 1–5 it forces me to give up, and what it says about a case I left out: a cause that's important and tractable, but where I'm badly underqualified relative to whoever would take my place.
