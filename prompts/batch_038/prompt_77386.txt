Our department's graduate colloquium closes next Friday with a keynote on the boundary problem, and I have been handed the chair for the forty-five minutes of discussion that follow. The speaker defends an account she calls recursive self-constitution, and I want to press it properly rather than let the session drift into generalities about immigration. Her view, as I understand it from the introduction and one chapter, runs roughly as follows. She accepts the force of the circularity objection — that any democratic decision about who the people are already presupposes an answer to that question — but denies that it is fatal, on the ground that it treats legitimacy as a property a procedure either has or lacks at a moment of authorization. Legitimacy, she argues, can instead be acquired diachronically: a demos constitutes itself through a sequence in which each provisional electorate's framework is ratified by a wider electorate in the next round, so the structure is a spiral rather than a circle. To make this work she distinguishes authorization from validation, holding that the founding round is authorized by no one and is merely provisional, but is validated retrospectively when a later, more inclusive electorate endorses what it set up. That first round is deliberately over-inclusive: everyone within the jurisdiction, plus a random sample of outsiders who are plausibly affected by the polity's foreseeable decisions, sampling being her way of handling the unbounded reach of affectedness without narrowing the criterion. Inclusion is presumptive, exclusion bears the burden of justification, and the justification must be one the excluded could themselves accept. And no electorate is ever final: membership rules remain permanently revisable, which she counts as a virtue, since a frozen demos would tyrannize later arrivals. Her stock deflection, which I have seen her use twice in print, is to say that pressing on the founding round "presupposes a punctual conception of legitimacy," then to observe that every actual demos began in some species of usurpation and to redirect attention from origins to whether the sequence is inclusionary.

What I need is a run of ten questions I can deliver from the chair, ordered as a sequence rather than as ten independent topics, with each one keyed to the answer she is most likely to give and paired with a single follow-up that closes off that answer. The sequence must reach, at minimum: whether the founding round is a normative fiat dressed up as a method, and if so what the account adds beyond an exhortation to be generous about membership; whether retrospective validation can validate anything when the electorate doing the validating was itself constituted by the framework it is validating; the fact that revision is not monotonic, so a later, narrower electorate can disenfranchise those admitted earlier, and whether the ratchet she will invoke — presumptive inclusion plus the burden of justification — is not in the end a substantive constraint imposed on the demos from outside the procedure, which is exactly the move she claims to avoid; the sampling device, where the sampling frame is itself a boundary decision, so the circularity may simply have been relocated one level up; whether her account gives any different answer from a straightforward coercion-based all-subjected principle paired with generous transitional enfranchisement, in which case I want her to name a case where the two come apart; and the nested-demops question, which I would like posed concretely by contrasting the 2014 Scottish independence referendum franchise, which admitted sixteen- and seventeen-year-olds and resident EU nationals while excluding Scots who had moved elsewhere in the United Kingdom, with the 2016 EU referendum franchise, which was built on the parliamentary register and so admitted overseas British citizens within the fifteen-year rule, members of the House of Lords, and residents of Gibraltar while excluding most EU nationals who had lived and worked in the country for decades — two franchises at two levels, each defended as tracking the people concerned, tracking different criteria. Please also include one question on the German Constitutional Court's Maastricht reasoning that there is no single European people capable of forming a majority will, asking her whether, on her account, that is a claim about facts or a report that the spiral has not yet closed; one question on the interim, since binding decisions get made while the sequence is still running and someone must have authority in the meantime; and, as the final item, a falsifiability question that asks for a diagnostic — what observable feature would show that a given polity's sequence is merely self-reproducing rather than inclusionary — with something more specific demanded than the observation that contraction has occurred.

Two further constraints on the drafting. First, tone and audience: the room contains a faculty member who holds that the boundary problem is insoluble and that legitimacy must therefore be sourced somewhere other than democratic procedure, and a doctoral student who thinks the entire debate is a rationalization of the status quo, and I want the sequence to bite on both, so please include at least one question that concedes her spiral point in full and presses elsewhere, since a wholly adversarial run will get me nothing but a restatement of the thesis. Flag anywhere a question risks smuggling in a premise she would rightly reject, and mark the two or three questions that are load-bearing enough to survive a collapse of the clock and the one I should cut first. Second, usability: I have not read the book, so nothing may turn on passages or terminology I could not know, every question must carry its own setup in terms a graduate audience will follow, and each should be sayable in under thirty seconds of speech, in the second person, with no definitions of standard vocabulary, no requests for citations or further reading, and nothing resembling an inquiry into her intellectual biography.
