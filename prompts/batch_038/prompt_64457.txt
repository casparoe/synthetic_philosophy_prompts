I am in the second term of a political theory sequence, and this week's seminar was on constitutional entrenchment; the reading was Elster on precommitment. A classmate argued that entrenchment is undemocratic in a rather precise sense: it lets a majority that no longer exists defeat a majority that does, because the earlier one wrote in procedures the later one cannot undo. He offered two devices as paradigm cases — Article 79(3) of the German Basic Law, which declares inadmissible any amendment touching the principles laid down in Articles 1 and 20, and the proviso in Article V of the U.S. Constitution under which no state may be deprived of its equal suffrage in the Senate without its consent. The professor's reply, in roughly these words, was that Ulysses was freest when he was lashed to the mast. The seminar accepted this and moved on. I did not, but if I were pressed today the most I could manage is that the analogy simply fails, which is not a claim I can defend.

What I would like is a menu of the distinct theses that single sentence could be expressing, running from the ones that are safe because they say almost nothing to the ones that are ambitious and vulnerable. For each candidate reading, please:

1. State it as a precise claim, specifying whose freedom is at issue, freedom from what, over which interval, and by what measure — moment-by-moment choice, or the option set available across a lifetime, or something else.
2. Give a verdict: platitude, substantive and defensible, false, or question-begging against my classmate.
3. Say whether the reading requires treating the bound later self as the same agent as the one who did the binding, since I suspect that premise is where the objection will bite.
4. Say whether the reading survives the professor's own stated commitments: he announced in week one that he is a preference-satisfaction theorist and that talk of higher and lower selves is empty, so any reading that must separate considered preferences from currently felt ones may be unavailable to him even if it is otherwise attractive.
5. Say whether it actually answers the dead-hand objection, which concerns binding persons who never agreed, rather than binding oneself.

Please include at least one reading that is purely instrumental — something to the effect that closing off options can maximize expected preference-satisfaction over time, with no appeal to liberty at all — and tell me whether that reading is compatible with the word "freest." If it is not, the slogan may be smuggling in a conceptual claim about freedom that the professor has explicitly disclaimed, and I would like to be able to say so cleanly.

To close, two short items: first, which reading you judge is most likely doing the actual work when the sentence is deployed as a rebuttal rather than as a premise; second, a list of the specific ways the mast resists the transfer — the crew had wax in their ears and had been ordered to bind him tighter the more he begged, the rope was physical rather than normative, and it did not depend on anyone's continued willingness to treat it as authoritative — together with a note on which candidate theses each of these facts threatens and which it leaves standing. I am not looking for references or further reading, only the analysis laid out in numbered form I can bring to my tutorial.
