For some years now I have been trying to settle what I actually mean when I call a person unfree, and the only method that has made any progress is recording my verdicts on concrete cases and looking for the pattern. Below are eight of them. I would like to know what principle, if any, they add up to.

1. A man has been kept in a locked room for years and, through some mixture of adaptation and fear, no longer wants out. He says he is content and would refuse the key. Verdict: unfree; his contentment does nothing to remove the unfreedom.

2. The only road bridge for thirty miles is owned by one man. He charges nothing, has publicly promised never to charge, and no law prevents him from starting tomorrow. At present nothing he does stops anyone crossing. Verdict: the townspeople are unfree with respect to crossing, even though they cross unhindered every day.

3. The single employer in a one-industry town may dismiss any worker at will, without cause or notice. It has never done so capriciously and its managers are mild. Verdict: the workers are unfree in the same way as the townspeople in case 2, and the mildness of the managers is beside the point.

4. The state takes roughly a third of my income under rules published in advance, general in application, binding on the lawmakers themselves, which I voted on and may campaign to change. Verdict: this does not make me unfree, or not in the sense that matters.

5. The same state, under rules equally published, equally general, equally open to my vote, and administered by officials who genuinely consult my own interests as I report them, forbids me certain foods, fixes my hours of sleep, and caps what I may save. Verdict: here I am plainly unfree, and the fairness of the procedure does not cure it. Cases 4 and 5 are the pair I cannot reconcile; they differ, so far as I can tell, only in what is regulated.

6. A woman subject to nobody: no employer, no landlord, no official who need be asked permission for anything. Also no food, no shelter, no medicine, and no means of getting any. Verdict: I want to call her unfree, and yet I cannot identify anyone or anything that unfrees her. This is the case I keep returning to.

7. A man whose addiction defeats most of what he wants to do, whose principal obstacles are his own impulses. Verdict: unfree.

8. A member of a disciplined communal order whose director tells him what to want, what to work at, and what his deeper self would choose if he could see clearly. He reports being freer than at any point in his life. Verdict: not free, or at any rate his sense of freedom is no evidence of freedom. I cannot say what separates this from case 7, where an internal obstacle seemed to count.

My tentative principle is that a person is unfree to do X to the extent that whether X can be done depends on someone else's unconstrained choice rather than on a rule the person had a hand in making and that binds its makers too. That appears to deliver 2, 3, and 4, and to fail on 5, 6, 7, and 8.

What I am after is the following. State the best candidate principle, or the two or three genuinely competing candidates, precisely enough that someone who does not share my intuitions could apply it. Attach to each clause the case that forces it in, and say which clauses are load-bearing — what breaks if each is deleted. Then say whether the principle can be kept simple, or whether the price of covering all eight verdicts is a set of disjuncts so gerrymandered that it is no longer a principle; if some of my verdicts must go, say which and why, and do not try to rescue all of them. Apply whatever you settle on to three situations I have not considered: a warehouse worker whose every movement is scored by a system that adjusts assignments and pay by published formula, with an internal appeals process that in practice never overturns a result; a population under foreign military occupation whose occupier issues no orders to individuals, changes no local law, and has intervened at will twice in a decade; and a person who has contractually waived access to the courts in favor of arbitration conducted under rules the other party selects. Finally, propose one further case of your own that would discriminate between the competing candidates if there is more than one, and tell me whether the principle you end up with makes freedom a matter of degree, and if so, what the degrees are degrees of.

I am not looking for a survey of the familiar distinction between negative and positive liberty, or of the republican revival, or of the history of the idea; assume I know the standard moves and where my cases sit relative to them. Point out departures from those views where they matter, but the deliverable is the principle. No references, please.
