A colleague of mine is sceptical of the many-worlds interpretation and thinks he has a cheap way of dissolving quantum immortality: it is survivorship bias wearing a lab coat. His picture is the familiar one — a thousand people each flip a coin and leave the room when it comes up tails, and after ten rounds the handful still standing feel chosen, but only because nobody hears from those who left. Everettians, he says, are doing the same thing: there is always some branch containing a live observer, so of course that observer finds herself alive; nothing about first-person immortality has been shown, only a selection effect. Can you audit the analogy for me? Spell out the correspondences — the coin, the thousand players, the leaving the room, the survivor's sense of improbability, the audience that hears only from the winners — and then say where the mapping breaks, particularly in a theory where no branch is more real than another even though the branches carry wildly different Born weights, and where the live question is not what an outsider counts afterwards but what a person should expect to find herself experiencing once she has split. Close with your own verdict rather than a survey of the readings available: does the analogy license his deflation, does it cut the other way and help the Everettian, or does it simply fail to bear on the dispute — and if it fails, what analogy would do better? Please answer in continuous prose, as you would in a letter: no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables.
