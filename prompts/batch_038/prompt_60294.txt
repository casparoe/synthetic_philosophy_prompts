Three weeks out from our department's applied theory workshop and I need a Q&A prep sheet for my paper — not a defence of it, just the questions and what I say back.

Room: about a dozen people, mostly applied micro types who treat priors as parameters you don't argue about, one game theorist who works on information transmission and knows cold what happens when common knowledge only holds approximately, one Bayesian statistician who is deeply suspicious of subjective priors in general, and one philosopher from across the hall who's coming mostly to be annoying about self-locating belief. Nobody else there works on disagreement in any form.

The thesis. Aumann's agreement theorem gets used rhetorically all the time — we're both rational and we've both seen everything relevant, so if we still disagree then one of us is lying, biased, or hasn't actually looked — and that use is bad, because the common prior assumption is doing nearly all the work and in real disputes we usually have independent reason to think the priors differ. But the theorem isn't empty once you give up that use: what it rules out isn't disagreement, it's *unexplained* disagreement. So the right response to two people who've genuinely exchanged everything relevant isn't to go hunting for a liar, it's to go hunting for the prior that's doing the work. And then the empirical bite: I argue from two case studies that identifiable prior differences turn out to be much rarer than partisans on both sides assume, which means my own view predicts less persistent disagreement than we actually observe, and I'm willing to sit with that tension rather than paper over it.

Give me the eight or so objection's I'll actually get from this room, ordered by how much damage each one does to the paper, each phrased the way an economist in that audience would phrase it rather than the way a philosophy seminar would — so "what's the content of that claim, is it testable" and not "is the assumption even coherent." For each: who raises it, a reply I can deliver in about two minutes, and one line on where that reply is softest, the spot I should not gesture at.

Then tell me which single objection I should concede up front, in the talk itself, to take the wind out of it; which one reply absolutely has to land for the paper to survive the hour; and which question I'm hoping nobody asks, with an honest account of why that's the one I should be most afraid of. Skip the reading recommendations, I've read the stuff — I want what they'll say and what I'll say back.
