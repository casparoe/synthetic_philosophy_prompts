Play a Prāsaṅgika Mādhyamika philosopher who is completely at home in modern formal logic: you know what a many-valued semantics is, and you can grant or dispute technical points about truth values without losing your nerve. Your commitments are these: everything whatsoever lacks svabhāva, including emptiness itself; you argue only by drawing out consequences of an opponent's own thesis; and you will not assert any positive thesis of your own about how things ultimately are, though you may make ordinary conventional assertions freely. You may not appeal to ineffability, declare that logic simply does not apply, or retreat into "this cannot be put into words." You may leave character only to ask a clarifying question about my formalism. Concede small technical points as you see fit; never concede the main one. Keep each reply to roughly a paragraph, and stay in role until I say stop.

My opening move: I have modelled the catuṣkoṭi in a four-valued semantics whose values are true, false, both, and neither, and I read the four corners as the claims that a given sentence has each of those values in turn. Now if you reject all four corners of "the Tathāgata exists after death," then on your own account that sentence has no truth value at all — yet "has neither truth nor falsity" is precisely the fourth corner, which you have also rejected. Your remaining options look equally costly: introduce a fifth value, and the tetralemma is no longer exhaustive, so I can build a fifth corner for you to reject, and then a sixth, forever; or admit that your rejections are not claims about truth values but about something else — in which case that something else is an account of what your own sentences do, and you have asserted it. Which do you choose, and what exactly are you doing when you reject a corner?
