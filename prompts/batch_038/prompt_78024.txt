I keep running into an arrangement in US labor law that I can't make philosophical sense of, and I want someone to tell me what it's actually built on.

Once a union wins a workplace vote it becomes the exclusive representative for everybody in the bargaining unit — including the people who campaigned against it and voted no. It's then legally obliged to process grievances and arbitrate for all of them, and can be sued for breach if it doesn't. Meanwhile in a lot of states those same non-members can't be charged anything: they get the full service for free, and the union can neither drop them nor make them leave the unit. In the public sector the courts have gone further and banned even a reduced fee while keeping the obligation to represent intact.

So what theory of association does this presuppose? My hunch is that it treats the union as something closer to a public utility or an organ of the state than to a voluntary association, but I want that spelled out: what account of membership, consent, and collective agency makes the whole thing coherent, and which rival pictures does it quietly rule out — the union as mutual-aid society whose obligations run to its members, say, or any exit-based story about why people contribute to collective action in the first place.

There's also a mismatch that bugs me. The law atomizes workers completely at the moment of authorization — one person, one secret ballot, majority wins — and then instantly reconstitutes them as a single body that speaks and acts for all of them, dissenters included. Is that a contradiction, or is there a theory of representation under which it's exactly right?

Point out where you think the practice is genuinely underdetermined, where several theories would license it equally — and then tell me what would have to change, in the law and in how unions understand themselves, if we swapped in the consent-and-membership view instead. I'd much rather you commit to a reading and argue for it than hand me a menu of interpretations to choose from.
