I'm a junior in high school, and for the past year I've been permitted to sit in on a philosophy reading group run by a doctoral candidate I'll call Nadia. For several weeks we have been constructing cases about whether a person survives having her brain progressively replaced by synthetic parts, and Nadia has been ruling on each one. I have kept a list of her rulings, because they seem to me to have a shape I cannot quite state.

Her record so far, in the order we took them up:

1. A woman whose neurons are replaced one at a time by functionally exact synthetic equivalents over ten months, each removed part destroyed as it comes out, with no interruption of her waking life at any point: survives, and is literally the same person rather than a successor.
2. An entire hemisphere converted in a single continuous operation lasting three days, the patient awake and conversing throughout: survives.
3. The whole brain removed and destroyed and a functionally identical synthetic brain installed and started a few milliseconds later, with no overlap and nothing left running in between: does not survive. Her gloss was that "there was no one there to be continued."
4. A whole brain scanned, the original destroyed at the moment of scanning, and the resulting digital person started up elsewhere: does not survive; the digital person is a new person with her memories.
5. The same scan with the original left alive and well: the original survives, and the copy does not count as her, even though the copy would pass any test we could devise.
6. A conversion in which each synthetic part runs alongside the biological part it replaces for a month before that part is removed, so that there is a long period of duplication inside the head: survives, and she insisted the overlap made no difference to the verdict either way.
7. A woman who is now ninety-five percent synthetic, converted gradually over years: unambiguously survives. When someone suggested that the proportion of silicon must matter, she said flatly that it did not.

My case: the same procedure as the first, in the same order, one part at a time, each removed part destroyed as it comes out — except that the whole conversion is completed in forty milliseconds. At no instant is more than about two percent of her brain synthetic, her processing never stops, and she is in the middle of a sentence when it begins and finishes the sentence when it ends. The group has already agreed to stipulate away the physics, so the only thing at issue is the shape of the transition. My case therefore differs from a conversion she called survival only in how long it took, and from the one conversion she called death only in how much was synthetic at any given moment.

That is the point I want to press: her record has never separated those two features. The only conversion she rejected was both near-instantaneous and total, and every conversion she accepted was either slow or partial, so nothing she has said tells us which feature she is tracking.

Please answer as a numbered list of exactly five items, each of one or two sentences, with no introduction and no conclusion:

1. Your prediction of her verdict on my case, with a rough degree of confidence.
2. The rule that best accounts for all seven of her earlier verdicts, stated precisely enough that I could apply it to a case she has not yet seen, together with which of the seven is the closest analog to mine.
3. A different rule that also accounts for all seven but yields the opposite verdict on mine, and what about her record makes it hard to dismiss rather than merely implausible.
4. One further case, other than mine, that would separate those two rules more decisively than mine does, and why it is more decisive.
5. Whether the feature that decides my case is one the woman herself could notice while it was happening. My suspicion is that she could not, and that this is either a genuine cost to Nadia's position or a sign that Nadia is answering a different question than she believes she is answering; tell me which.
