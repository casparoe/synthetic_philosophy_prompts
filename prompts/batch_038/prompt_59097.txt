I am in the first year of a doctoral programme, and three weeks from now I am presenting at my department's graduate metaethics workshop — a twenty-five-minute slot followed by fifteen minutes of questions. The paper grew out of a seminar assignment I wrote for the idealization-in-ethics course I am taking this term; my seminar instructor thought the position was defensible but told me plainly that I do not know what is coming at me in the question period, and I suspect he is right. The room will be about a dozen people: two senior faculty who are non-naturalist value realists and who treat any account that locates normativity in a counterfactual deliberative process as either covertly realist or empty; one reductive naturalist who is broadly sympathetic to subjectivist idealization but thinks it has to be cashed out dispositionally or it is not a theory at all; three or four graduate students who came into philosophy from formal decision theory and will want to know about convergence, uniqueness, and cycles; and one person working primarily on Aristotelian virtue ethics who thinks the whole framing in terms of reasons-for-an-agent is a mistake worth naming out loud.

The position I am defending replaces the single idealization in full-information and full-rationality accounts — the objective-interests picture on which we ask what a better-informed, more vividly imaginative version of the agent would want her actual self to want, and the rationality-based cousin on which we ask what we would desire to desire were we fully rational — with an ordinal-indexed sequence of deliberations. Let stage zero be the actual agent with her actual attitudes and her actual epistemic position. At each successor stage the agent gets three things: the factual information that the previous stage's deliberation turned up about the objects of her evaluative attitudes, vivid imaginative acquaintance with the experiences the previous stage's outcome would have involved, and full awareness of her own deliberative history — she knows what she endorsed one stage back and why. She then deliberates afresh, and the reason-giving verdict is the limit point of the sequence: the first stage at which her output stops changing, or, if it never settles, the stable band through which it oscillates. I want to argue three things. First, continuity: the standard alienation complaint — that the idealized agent is a stranger whose endorsements give me no reason to act — targets a single large jump from the actual to the ideal, and my construction contains no jump, since every stage is defined relative to its immediate predecessor rather than to a global ideal. Second, non-monotonicity: everyone knows that supplying more information can destroy a desire rather than refine it, as in the case of the driver who mistakes mild dehydration for hunger and would, fully informed, no longer want the sandwich; on my account such a destruction is legitimate only if it survives being itself deliberated about at the next stage, which is a real constraint the one-shot idealization cannot impose. Third, agent-relativity: because the path depends on the starting point, the limit point need not be shared across agents, and I want to turn this into a reply to the convergence arguments my two realist audience members favour.

Please prepare me for the question period rather than defend the view for me. I want the eight or ten objections this particular audience will actually raise, ordered by the damage they would do to my position if I answered them badly — weighting likelihood and severity together, not severity alone — and tagged with the subgroup each comes from. Phrase each one the way that subgroup would phrase it: the realists will put their pressure as a dilemma about where the normativity enters the construction, the decision-theory students will ask about fixed points and whether I have any reason to think the sequence is well-behaved, the naturalist will ask what work the sequence does that a disposition could not, and the Aristotelian will object to the shape of the question. For each objection give me a reply I can deliver in about two minutes aloud, and then a separate sentence marking the softest point in that reply — the place where a follow-up would hurt and I should be ready to concede rather than bluff. Tell me also which of these I can defuse in the opening five minutes of the talk by conceding it myself, and identify the single reply that has to land for the position to survive the session at all. Finally, give me the question I should be praying nobody asks: the one for which I currently have no answer, an explanation of exactly why it is worse than the others, and the partial response that at least buys me the room's patience. I already expect to be asked whether the sequence converges and what I say to an agent who has to decide tonight; treat those as given and concentrate on the ones I have not thought of. Two housekeeping notes: I have the literature and I do not want a bibliography, a reading list, or citations of any kind, since what I am missing is not sources but objections; and please do not offer "clarify your terms" or "it depends on the case" as replies, because in that room either one is a surrender.
