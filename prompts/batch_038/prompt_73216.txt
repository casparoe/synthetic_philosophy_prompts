I cover behavioral science and ideas for a general-audience magazine, and I'm working on a piece about why the sunk cost "fallacy" keeps getting defended. At a panel last week a management professor made this case, roughly: refusing to throw good money after bad is like a general who leaves himself an escape route. The reason the money you've already sunk matters, he said, is that everybody else can see it — burning resources is how you buy the reputation of someone who doesn't quit, and that reputation pays you back later. He used it to argue the fallacy isn't a fallacy in the cases that actually matter, only in the toy ones philosophers reach for (the nonrefundable ticket, the movie you're not enjoying).

Before I let that into print I want the analogy audited. Can you lay out the mapping explicitly — what's playing the general, the boats, the watching enemy, the beachhead, the landing — and then seperate the disanalogies that actually damage his conclusion from the ones that are just surface noise?

A few things I specifically want pinned down:

- Is the general's fire doing prospective work or retrospective work? Meaning: does it change what other people expect him to do *next*, or is it about the value already spent? Those feel like different mechanisms and I suspect he's sliding between them.
- Does the analogy survive the fact that you could buy the same reputation more cheaply than by actually destroying resources — which would suggest the sunk cost isn't what's doing the signaling.
- The paradigm cases have no audience at all. What's left of his claim once you restrict it to situations where someone is watching?

Then tell me how much of his conclusion the analogy can carry versus how much was already loaded into it, and if it fails, whether there's a better analogy for the one situation where honoring a sunk cost does look rational — or whether the honest answer is that theres no such situation.

Give me the verdict plainly rather than hedging into "it depends," and then tell me what would change it: which argument or piece of evidence would make you say the general's fire really does rescue sunk-cost reasoning, and where you think your own reasoning here is most likely to break. American English, please.
