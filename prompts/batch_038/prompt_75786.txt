I can live with the regress bit at 132a — everyone in class could already recite that one and I know the general shape of it. What I can't get a handle on is everything around it, and it's three separate things. First, the sail. Socrates offers the day comparison precisely to show that one thing can be wholly in many places without getting carved up, and Parmenides answers with a sail — and Socrates just caves immediately, granting "a part over each." Is that concession actually forced on him, or is he throwing away the point for no reason? A day isn't a stretchy object with parts spread out over locations, and neither, you'd think, is an idea, so how does Parmenides get to leap from "it's like a sail" to "the ideas themselves are divisible into parts" as though that were established? Is he just assuming the analogy holds in the one respect that matters, or is there a real reason the whole-over-each option is supposed to be incoherent on its own — I'm thinking of that opening line about the idea being "separate from itself," which I don't understand either.

Second, the three little cases he runs after that don't look like the same argument to me, and I want them separated out. With greatness the complaint is that each great thing would be great by a part of greatness smaller than absolute greatness — but why is that unreasonable? I'm made great by whatever makes me great, and nothing obviously requires that the thing I'm great by be maximally great. Then equality gets handled differently, something about taking away a part of equality and so being less than equality while still being equal, which reads more like a flat contradiction. And smallness is where I get totally lost: "the small will be greater than this, since this is a part of it, and therefore the absolute small will be greater; but that to which the part of the small is added will be smaller." Please take that sentence apart word by word, tell me which direction the comparison is actually going and where the absurdity lands, and say whether the reasoning genuinely closes or whether the translator has mangled it into senselessness. Third, and smallest: when Socrates falls back on "each idea may be only a thought," Parmenides fires back "is each thought one, but a thought of nothing?" What premise is that leaning on, and why is it supposed to sink the move rather than just change the subject? (Also, in the English as I have it that sentence seems to be missing a semicolon — "which can exist only in our minds then each might be one" — does that matter for how the escape is meant to work?)

Finally, the question I'd most want settled before Tuesday: does any of this fragment business depend on ideas having proper parts at all, or is Parmenides only attacking a spatial way of picturing participation? Put differently, which of these moves could Socrates have blocked cheaply and in one line, and which ones bite even if he'd never mentioned the sail? I'd rather have a clear reconstruction of who is arguing for what at each step than a survey of what scholars think the whole dialogue is up to.
