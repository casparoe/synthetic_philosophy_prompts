In six weeks I present the central chapter of my book project to a working group that a state legislature has convened to consider redesigning its upper chamber. The group is eleven people: six constitutional lawyers (two have drafted ballot measures, one clerked on the state supreme court), three comparative political scientists who study legislative organization rather than deliberation, and two former legislators, one of whom lost re-election by fewer than a thousand votes. No one in the room works on mini-publics, and I expect the word sortition to be unfamiliar to half of them in its technical sense. They are not hostile to democratic innovation as such — one of the lawyers sits on the state redistricting commission — but they read proposals for what happens when things go badly, for enforceability, and for survivability under a state constitution, not for aspiration.

My thesis is deliberately not the epistemic one. I argue against the familiar claim that randomly selected bodies are wiser, calmer, or less polarized than elected ones. My own reading of the citizen-assembly literature is that those bodies are more agreeable chiefly because they contain no one whose livelihood depends on disagreeing, which is not the same thing as being better. My claim is narrower and, I think, stranger: the central vice of an elected legislature is neither corruption nor campaign money, but the structural necessity of seeking re-election, which forces every officeholder to price each decision by its effect on a coalition that votes in eighteen months. Sortition is worth having because it produces legislators who cannot be punished at the polls and therefore have nothing to gain by that pricing. Unaccountability is the design feature, not the price we pay for it.

The design I will put on the table: a single six-year non-renewable term, with no possibility of re-selection; selection by stratified lot from the voter file, drawing four hundred names and then permitting those drawn to decline, with vacancies filled from the same stratum's reserve list — I defend declination rather than compulsion, and this is a place I know I am exposed; no party labels, caucuses, or leadership offices inside the chamber, with committee assignments themselves drawn; the chamber forfeits the power to initiate appropriations and retains an absolute veto over redistricting, subpoena power, an amendatory power subject to supermajority override by the lower house, and sole authority over legislative ethics rules; and a permanent professional secretariat jointly appointed with the lower house, operating under an explicit prohibition on drafting the chamber's findings or reports.

My argument runs in three steps. First, that re-election dependence, not bad character, explains the systematic short-sightedness of legislatures, because the officeholder's own horizon is set by the electoral calendar. Second, that the institutions we already trust with long horizons — courts, central banks, agencies — purchase those horizons by purchasing expertise and insulation, and are captured by the organized interests that can afford to litigate and lobby them. Third, that a sortitioned chamber buys a long horizon more cheaply, since a member with no career to protect cannot be bought with a career, and its insulation is distributed rather than concentrated in a professional caste.

Prepare me for the question period. I want the objections this particular room will actually raise — not the ones I would get from deliberative democrats, political theorists, or a general civic audience — ordered by how much damage each would do to my case. Phrase each the way its likely source would phrase it: the way a lawyer who drafts remedies thinks, the way a legislatures scholar who works with roll-call data thinks, the way a former member who was voted out thinks. For each objection give me a reply I can deliver in roughly two minutes, and a blunt note on where that reply is thin, evasive, or resting on a premise I have not earned in front of this audience. Then tell me which two objections I should concede before I am asked, and in what precise form the concession should be made so that it reads as confidence rather than retreat. Identify the single objection that, if it lands cleanly, ends the presentation — the one I am hoping nobody raises — and give me whatever partial answer exists for it, including an honest statement of whether one exists. Finally, flag any case where several of their objections are really one objection in different clothes, and flag any place where a sound reply depends on an empirical claim I will not be able to verify in the room. I have my own list of the obvious ones — competence, democratic mandate, capture by the secretariat — so tell me if I am wrong about which of these are actually dangerous here, and do not spend space restating the standard defenses of sortition, since my argument explicitly disowns most of them.
