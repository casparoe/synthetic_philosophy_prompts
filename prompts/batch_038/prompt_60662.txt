Analyze the German shoot-down episode of 2005–2006, and its sequel in the 2017 ethics rules for automated driving, as a case study in the question of whether a system that decides about human life should reason as a utilitarian or as a deontologist. I am not looking for the trolley-problem gloss, which I know well, nor for a summary of the doctrine; I want a disciplined case analysis that runs in both directions.

The facts, so that nothing turns on your recollection of them. In January 2005 Germany enacted an Aviation Security Act whose §14(3) authorized the armed forces to shoot down an aircraft being used as a weapon against human life. In February 2006 the Federal Constitutional Court declared that provision void. Its reasoning, compressed: killing the innocent people on board would treat them as mere objects and as a means to the saving of others, violating human dignity together with the right to life; the court expressly rejected the argument that those people were doomed in any case and therefore not wronged, and it emphasized that the situation might yet develop otherwise, as it had on the fourth hijacked aircraft in 2001 and in a 2003 incident in which a disturbed man circled a light aircraft over Frankfurt's banking district before landing. The holding was limited to people not involved in the crime; the court separately found no constitutional basis for deploying the military with specifically military weapons inside Germany. Eleven years later a government ethics commission on automated and connected driving, chaired by a former constitutional judge, published twenty rules. Two matter here: automated driving is ethically required if the systems produce fewer accidents than human drivers, and in unavoidable collisions the system may neither offset victims against one another nor draw any distinction based on personal characteristics such as age or physical constitution.

Now the analysis, in this order.

First, do the mapping. For each of the three operative norms — the statutory authorization, the court's prohibition, and the commission's two rules — identify who the deciding agent is, what the admissible options are, and where the moral work is being done.

Second, classify them. Consider a trichotomy I want you to use and then pressure-test: a norm can enter a decision system as a term in the objective, so that the trade-off is computed and weighted; as a hard constraint on the action set, so that the trade-off is computed but certain options are excluded regardless of the numbers; or as a restriction on what the system is permitted to represent at all, so that the morally loaded fact is never available to be weighed. Which of the three is the commission's prohibition on distinctions by personal characteristics? I suspect it is the third, and that the third is not really a third thing but a constraint enforced by blinding. Argue the point.

Third, consider what I will call the standpoint reading: that the court did not forbid a state of affairs but forbade an institution from occupying the position in which lives can be netted against lives. Is that a deontological view, or is it a rule-consequentialist bet that any body permitted to do the arithmetic will eventually do it wrongly? The two readings diverge in their implications for a machine that has no standpoint to occupy, and I want that divergence drawn out: can an agent without intentions treat a person as a mere means, and does the commission's transposition of the court's reasoning into engineering rules quietly assume that it can?

Fourth, examine the level separation the two documents together produce — consequentialism about whether to deploy the system at all, constraint about how it may decide once deployed. Is that stable, or does the constraint feed back into the deployment calculus in a way that erodes it?

Fifth, set the United States contrast alongside: a standing shoot-down authority held in an executive chain of command, with no dignity-based prohibition, exercised on 9/11 in conditions of confusion and reached too late. What does the comparison show — that the two polities answered the same question differently, or that they were answering different questions?

Finally, tell me where the utilitarian/deontologist frame is simply silent about this episode, and say what the German trajectory implies for the framing of the question itself: whether real polities settle it by argument or by deciding who is allowed to do the arithmetic. If you are unsure of a legal detail, flag the uncertainty rather than supplying a plausible reconstruction.
