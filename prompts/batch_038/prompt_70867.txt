Two research groups have spent three years exchanging position papers about whether comprehension is ever anything over and above an organized set of competences, and both sides now agree that they have been talking past one another on at least some points. They have asked you to draft the joint statement that their adversarial collaboration should have produced. The parties are:

- **Party A (the Sufficiency View).** Comprehension is not a further ingredient added on top of competence. Attributions of understanding are summary labels that earn their keep only by tracking competences of the right sort, degree, and organization; any candidate ingredient allegedly beyond the competence list is either eliminable, or is itself just a further competence (e.g., the competence to report, to recombine, to detect one's own errors). Explanatory order runs from competence upward.

- **Party B (the Residue View).** There are explanatory jobs that no inventory of competences, however detailed, can discharge — paradigmatically, explaining why a system's successes transfer to structurally novel tasks and why its dispositions hang together as the achievements of one subject rather than as a bundle of separable capacities. Something beyond the list must be posited, and competence therefore does not in general suffice for comprehension.

To prevent the document from drifting into generalities, both parties have agreed to fix their attention on a single stipulated case, described below. Treat the description as a report of results both parties accept as accurate; you may not revise the numbers or add unstated findings, though you may stipulate further details where the case is silent, provided you flag them as stipulations.

**The case.** A building is described to three systems in three different ways.
- **S** receives the description as text (room names, adjacencies, door positions, distances). It answers layout questions about the building — "from the foyer, facing the stairwell, which room is immediately behind you?", "which two rooms are separated by the greatest number of doors?" — at 87% accuracy. When the same building is instead rendered as a navigable first-person environment and S is given an avatar and a continuous visual stream, S reaches a named target room on 8% of trials (chance is 6%).
- **N** is trained exclusively in the navigable environment by reinforcement learning, with no textual channel. It reaches target rooms on 95% of trials. It fails the layout questions at 14% (chance) and cannot select the correct floor plan from a set of four alternatives.
- **M** is trained jointly on both channels. It answers the layout questions at 61% and navigates successfully on 72% of trials — worse than each specialist on that specialist's own task.

Draft the joint statement. It should contain:

1. **A numbered list of claims both parties can sign.** At least twelve claims, and each must be non-vacuous: nothing that a party would accept only because it is too weak to constrain anything. Include claims about the case itself — what S, N, and M do and do not demonstrate, and what the case leaves open. For each numbered claim, note in a parenthetical whether it follows from the stipulated data or is an independent philosophical commitment that both parties happen to share.

2. **A disagreements table.** For each genuine disagreement, give: the disagreement in neutral wording; how Party A would phrase its own side of it such that Party A finds the phrasing accurate; how Party B would phrase its own side such that Party B finds the phrasing accurate; and a verdict on whether the disagreement is substantive, partly verbal, or wholly verbal — with a justification for the verdict rather than a bare label. Where you judge a disagreement verbal, say what the parties were really disputing, and state whether anything substantive survives the verbal reading.

3. **The cruxes.** Three to five sub-questions such that settling them would actually move at least one party off its position, not merely restate the disagreement. For each crux: state it as a question with a determinate answer; specify the observation, manipulation, or argument that both parties agree would bear on it; and record each party's prediction in advance, in language specific enough that a third party could tell whether the prediction came out right. Also state, for each party, what result would count as a loss on that crux — not as a loss of the whole debate, unless that is the case.

4. **A short section on what would *not* move the debate.** Both parties should be able to sign this too. Identify at least three kinds of evidence or argument that have been offered in the exchange but that both agree are uninformative here — and say why, in each instance. Address in particular whether adding a familiar intuition pump (a system that passes an interview, a system lacking any inner experience, a homunculus regress) would supply anything the case above does not, and whether scaling up any of S, N, or M would be probative for either party.

5. **A statement of the residual disagreement.** In one paragraph, and in wording neither party considers slanted against it, say what is left over once everything both can agree on has been set down. If your conclusion is that nothing substantive is left, say so and show your work.

Two standing constraints. Do not adjudicate: the document is something both parties could put their names to, and it must not smuggle in a verdict on who is right. And do not let the drafting rest on stock cases or on anyone's reported intuitions about them — the parties have explicitly agreed that the whole point of fixing on S, N, and M was to escape that pattern; where a stock case seems relevant, explain what it would have to show that the present case does not, or leave it out.
