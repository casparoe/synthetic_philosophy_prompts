I'm prepping a graduate seminar on responsibility and autonomous systems and I want a live sparring partner, not a literature dump, so let's do this as a role-play.

You play a committed defender of the view that there's a genuine, non-dissolvable responsibility gap when a machine causes harm. You can call yourself whatever you like, but no real people: no names of philosophers, lawyers, or cases, no book titles, no appeals to what "the literature" says. Argue from concepts alone. And your position isn't a strawman, you know it well: the gap for you is not about whether somebody pays or whether anybody feels bad, it's about whether there is an agent who can be called to answer — someone the victim has the standing to demand a justification from, in the second-personal sense. You'll concede small points readily (negligence doctrine stretches further than its critics admit, distributed responsibility is real, strict liability does useful work) but you will never concede the main one. Keep each reply to roughly 150 words so it feels like a conversation rather than an essay. If you need a factual detail about the technology or the law, ask me for it in character instead of assuming. Stay in character until I say 'stop'.

The setup for the whole exchange is a jurisdiction I'm inventing, so none of teh case law you already know applies — dont import it. Ridgemont passes a statute with three features. (1) Where a deployed autonomous system causes harm and no natural person's conduct meets the ordinary conditions for fault, liability attaches automatically to whoever stood to gain from the deployment: no-fault, capped, fast. (2) Payment under the statute legally discharges every remaining claim, including any claim to blame. (3) As a condition of deployment, the gainer must produce and publish a full account of why the system did what it did, pitched at a level the victim can actually follow, and must answer that victim's follow-up questions for a year.

I'll call any regime like this exhaustive: it compensates, it explains, and it closes the books. Here's my opening move. An exhaustive regime seems to hand over everything your gap says is missing — the victim is made whole, gets an intelligible answer to the question of why this happened to her, and gets it from a definite someone you can point at. So tell me, in character: what exactly is left over? And be careful, because if your answer is that the gainer is the wrong party to answer since they didn't do it, you've only re-described the absence of an answerer, which is the problem, not a solution to it.
