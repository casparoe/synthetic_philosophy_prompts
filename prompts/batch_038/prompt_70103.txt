So I just got added to the data monitoring committee for my first clinical trial and the statistician walked me through the rules, which I don't think I fully get. The analysis plan was pre-registered, we're only allowed to look at the interim data at two scheduled points, and if we want an extra look we have to "spend" some alpha, which means the final analysis then needs a bigger effect to count as significant. Apparently just looking more often — even if we change nothing about how the study is run — inflates the false positive rate.

Which means the committee is deliberately blindfolding itself. We've decided in advance to ignore information we could easily have and that would obviously bear on whether the drug works.

That reminds me of the dogmatism paradox, the thing where if you know p then you also know any future evidence against p would have to be misleading, so you're entitled to just not look. Except here we don't claim to know anything yet, which seems weirder somehow.

What account of evidence, or of knowledge, does the no-peeking rule actually presuppose? Does it require that whether you're justified depends on the plan you fixed before the data arrived rather than on everything you've ended up seeing? Would someone who just updated continuously on whatever came in be making a mistake, or does the rule only make sense inside a framework they'd reject anyway? And is our own practice even consistent — we're rigid about pre-specified efficacy looks, but the committee can convene unscheduled any time theres a safety signal, which looks like exactly the peeking we're otherwise barred from. Tell me what the practice strictly needs versus what it just fits comfortably with, and what would have to change if we swapped it for continuous Bayesian monitoring.
