I'm auditing a philosophy-of-mind seminar this term while, somewhat awkwardly, training small language models on the side, and the two commitments have finally collided. The seminar has been working through Hofstadter's strange-loop account of selves in *I Am a Strange Loop* — in particular the suggestion that a self is nothing over and above a pattern of memories, dispositions, values, and stylistic habits, and that such a pattern can be realized in part in the people who know it well; this is why he can describe the death of his wife as leaving a genuine portion of her still present in him and in their children rather than as a merely consolatory figure of speech. I find that attractive and wanted to make it precise enough to argue about, so I built a toy formalization, and the argument that came out of it now runs away from me.

Here is the toy quantity. For a person P and a carrier c — a brain, a notebook, a model trained on P's writings — let r(c,P) be the fraction of P's characteristic dispositions (the jokes, the verdicts on familiar cases, the ways of handling a recurring problem) that c would reproduce under counterfactual elicitation. Call r(c,P) c's share of P's self-mass. Two stipulations make it usable. Shares need not sum to one across carriers, since many carriers can reproduce the same disposition. And r(P,P) is not automatically maximal, since people drift from their own earlier patterns and sometimes the most faithful keeper of a pattern is someone else. My study group thinks the following is sound; I cannot accept it, and I cannot find the step that licenses my refusal:

1. A self just is a pattern of the sort r measures; there is nothing over and above its instantiations.
2. Patterns admit of degree and of multiple carriers: there is no fact about which carrier is *the* instance, only about how much of the pattern each realizes.
3. People who know me closely carry nonzero shares of my self-mass, so my self is literally distributed across brains and persists in them after my death.
4. A sufficiently faithful archive or trained model can have r(c,P) higher than r(P,P): my own discipline slips, the copy's does not.
5. So such a carrier is more me than I am — it realizes more of the pattern I am.
6. Nothing is smuggled in here: on (1) and (2), "being me" just means realizing the pattern, and realizing the pattern is exactly what admits of more and less.
7. Therefore a thing can be surpassed in being itself.
8. But a thing cannot be surpassed in being itself; at most a rival can be equally it.
9. Therefore (1) and (2) are false, and selves are not patterns admitting of degree.

My own suspicion is that something slips between (5) and (7) — that r measures graded resemblance and "is me" is doing identity work, and that I have quietly let a similarity metric stand in for an identity relation — but (6) was written precisely to block that complaint, and I cannot show it fails rather than merely dislike it. A friend in the group thinks instead that (8) is a bare assertion of the very thing at issue, since it presumes that being-me is all-or-nothing, which is what the argument is supposed to be examining. So: which step should I reject, why did it look unobjectionable when I wrote it down, and what does rejecting it cost? I want the alternatives priced, not listed. Suppose I insist on keeping (3), since the distribution claim is the whole reason I care about any of this: what has to go for (3) to survive, and is there a coherent selves-as-patterns view on which (4) is true and (7) is not, or does (4) genuinely entail it once (6) is granted? Tell me also which responses a Parfit-style reductionist — for whom identity is all-or-nothing but not what matters, and for whom psychological continuity comes in degrees — would happily sign and which they would dismiss as sentimental, and whether the Hofstadterian is entitled to (3) at all, since his own case is about grief and felt presence and I suspect my formalization has imported something from that context which the underlying view does not support.

Please be explicit about your own confidence as you go: which diagnosis you would bet on, how sure you are of it relative to the alternatives, and the single point in your answer you are least sure of — I would much rather have your uncertainty located than smoothed over. No bibliography or reference list, please; I will supply citations myself.
