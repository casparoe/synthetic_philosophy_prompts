Last night I had a long and rather fractious argument with a friend who works in internal audit, and I should like some assistance in determining what precisely he was claiming.

His phrase was 'you can't mark your own homework'. He began from cases that I accept as ordinary features of British institutional life: the external auditor of a company is appointed and paid by the company he audits; the press body that adjudicates complaints about newspapers is financed by the newspapers themselves; a complaint about a university's conduct of its examinations is dealt with by that university in the first instance, an outside adjudicator becoming available only once the internal process has been exhausted; allegations of misconduct against police officers are investigated initially by the professional standards department of the force concerned, with an independent body taking over only in the more serious cases. In each instance, he said, the arrangement is not merely risky but defective in principle, because a process cannot furnish evidence of its own trustworthiness.

When I pressed him, he produced what I take to be the general argument he wants to rest on. Suppose I wish to know whether my fuel gauge is accurate. I drive about, and each time I look at the gauge I record two things: what the gauge says, and — on the authority of that very reading — what is in the tank. After a hundred such pairs I observe that gauge and tank have agreed a hundred times, and conclude that the gauge is reliable. Every individual step appears to be a plain piece of knowledge and every inference appears valid, yet the conclusion is plainly not earned. He maintains that the same structure infects any attempt to assess one's own reasoning: to determine whether my judgement is sound I must exercise my judgement, so the determination is either empty or a disguised restatement of the thing determined.

My objection is that this proves far too much. I proofread my own prose; I re-derive an equation by a second route to see whether the same answer emerges; I test an estimate against a rough calculation done on the back of an envelope; I notice that I hold two beliefs that cannot both be true and revise one of them. None of this strikes me as empty, and if all of it were, I cannot see how anyone could ever come to know that they know anything at all. His reply was that these cases differ in kind because the second check is 'independent' — but when I asked what independence amounts to, he could only say that a check must not employ the same instrument, which appears to rule out precisely the cases I listed. I would add a difficulty from my own work: I deal with a system whose outputs are scored by a module drawn from the same trained weights that produced them, and those scores correlate respectably with human judgements. Whatever else is going on there, an instrument is being applied to the product of itself and something informative seems to result.

So I should like the slogan taken apart rather than adopted. Please set out the distinct precise principles it might be standing for, addressing at least the following candidates and adding any I have overlooked: a claim about the independence of evidence rather than the identity of the agent; a claim about the effect of a judge's interest in the outcome upon the judge's accuracy; a claim that reasoning cannot increase the warrant of its own premises however valid its steps; a claim about what could count as a reason, for the person relying on a source, to trust that source; and a claim that is institutional rather than epistemic, concerning how accountability should be distributed among separate offices. For each, state whether it is true, false, or true only under a contested reading of some term within it, and identify the term doing the work.

Then tell me which reading my friend actually needs in order to travel from the auditor, the press body and the police force to the general thesis about self-assessment, and which readings leave my counter-examples standing. In particular, I want to know whether there is a principled way to distinguish re-deriving an equation by a second route from reading the fuel gauge off the fuel gauge, and from having a module score the output of the weights that produced it — or whether the distinction is ultimately pragmatic, turning on how much the two applications could diverge. Finally, indicate where the slogan is performing rhetorical work that no precise version of it can perform: places where an audience's ready assent is secured by one reading while the conclusion being drawn requires another.

I should like the readings laid out as a list, each with a verdict, that I can bring to the next round of this argument, rather than a defence of whichever interpretation you happen to favour.
