I am defending a version of endurantism at a departmental metaphysics seminar next month. The audience is about eight people: four committed four-dimensionalists, two of whom are stage theorists and would rather dissolve the dispute than win it; one philosopher of physics whose sole criterion is whether a metaphysical thesis is Lorentz-invariant; one presentist who thinks both sides of my debate are arguing over a distinction with no reality; and two unaligned graduate students who know the standard arguments closely.

My position has three load-bearing commitments, and I want the objections aimed at those, not at endurantism in the abstract.

1. Ordinary material objects have no temporal parts: each is wholly located at every time at which it exists. I accept that this makes ordinary objects multilocated, and I deny that multilocation is a cost. On my view, nothing requires the location relation to be one-to-one, and the familiar paradoxes — an object encountering its earlier self, or a multilocated object appearing to have different parts at different locations — all depend on smuggling in a uniqueness constraint on location that I simply reject.

2. I handle temporary intrinsics by denying that fundamental properties come un-indexed. What an object instantiates, fundamentally, is being-bent-at-t, being-straight-at-t′, and so forth; change is one and the same whole object instantiating a series of such temporally indexed properties. I explicitly reject the relational analysis, the adverbial analysis, and any primitive notion of having a property at a time.

3. I accept a B-theoretic, eternalist background and unrestricted composition. I grant that this populates spacetime with strange fusions, but I maintain that none of them are temporal parts of ordinary objects, because I hold parthood to require that the part be located nowhere outside the region of the whole — a condition a fusion spanning disjoint regions fails.

Please prepare a Q&A sheet rather than a defense. I want the nine or ten objections this particular audience will actually press, ordered by the damage each would do to the position, and each phrased the way the person raising it would phrase it: the philosopher of physics will not put the matter the way a stage theorist will, and the presentist's objection will be aimed at my presuppositions rather than at my conclusions. For each, give a reply I can deliver in about two minutes while standing up, plus a candid note on where that reply is thin — what I would have to concede under sustained pressure. Then identify the two objections that are genuinely load-bearing, in the sense that if my answer to them fails the whole view collapses, and point out any weakness in the three commitments above that this audience is unlikely to notice but that I should nonetheless be ready for. Finally, name the question I am dreading: the one they are most likely to ask and I am least equipped to answer.
