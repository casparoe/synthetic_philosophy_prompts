I teach a grad seminar on philosophy of AI and the frame problem session always ends in a shrug. The logicist side says it's basically been solved — get your nonmonotonic formalism right, or in the situation calculus just write the successor state axioms, and there's nothing left to say — while the Dreyfus/Dennett side says that move is exactly the evasion, that formalizing it away just relocates the problem into the question of which facts get represented and which get treated as relevent in a novel situation, and no amount of axiom writing touches that. Nobody ever converts anybody and it's been this way for forty years.

My working hypothesis is that this isn't one stalled debate but two camps answering different questions under a single label: a formal-inference problem (which the logicists did largely fix), a relevance/salience problem about knowing what matters in an unforeseen situation (which nobody has fixed), and a real-time tractability problem that robotics mostly engineered around rather than solved. If that's right the impasse is a slide between questions, not a substantive disagreement.

So: diagnose it. Is my tripartition actually doing the work here, or is there a genuine substantive disagreement left over once you pin down the referent — and if so, what exactly are the two sides contradicting each other about? Give me reasons drawn from what each camp actually claims, not a history lesson, and then an honest answer on what would count as progress and whether any one of the three deserves to have kept the name.
