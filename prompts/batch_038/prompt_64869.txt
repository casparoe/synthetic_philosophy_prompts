The context. I am applying for a nine-month research fellowship at an institute with an applied AI remit, and the application calls for two pages. My half-formed idea is that current discussion of the frame problem — including a good deal of recent talk about whether large, learned systems suffer from it — has stalled because three distinguishable problems travel under one label: the ramification problem (what follows from an action, and how to represent that without enumerating every consequence); the qualification problem (how to state the preconditions of an action without an open-ended list of defeating conditions); and what I want to call the relevance-realization problem (how a system settles which of the facts available to it bear at all on the choice in front of it). Formal work has made real progress on the first; the second is partly managed; the third is typically assumed away or outsourced to a human designer. My suspicion, which I want the project to test rather than assert, is that no one has ever stated explicitly what would count as solving the frame problem, with the result that a solution is announced for one of the three and the difficulty migrates silently into another.

The proposal needs to contain the following, in your own organization rather than as a checklist:

1. The research question, formulated so that it has a determinate answer. Not whether the frame problem has been solved, but something like: what adequacy criterion must a proposed solution meet, and which families of proposals actually meet it. If you think my three-way split is mistaken or unhelpful, replace it with a better one and say why.

2. Why existing positions leave the question open. Identify the main families of response — nonmonotonic and circumscription-style logics, successor-state axiom approaches, dynamical and robotics-based replies, embodied and enactive critiques, and the recent appeal to learned representations — and say what each is genuinely responsive to and what it leaves untouched.

3. The method, month by month for nine months, and an argument for this method rather than the obvious alternatives: constructing a benchmark, writing yet another taxonomy paper, or producing an intellectual history of the debate.

4. What a successful result would look like as a deliverable — for instance, a defended criterion plus an audit of the solution families against it — and, just as concretely, what would count as failure. I want at least two specific failure modes, such as a criterion that turns out to be trivially satisfiable or one so demanding that nothing could meet it, and the possibility that the three problems cannot in fact be pulled apart in any interesting case.

6. A short paragraph answering the question the review panel will ask: why this needs a philosopher, and what an engineer building such systems could actually use.
