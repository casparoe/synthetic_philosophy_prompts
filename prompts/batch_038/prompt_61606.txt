I run the philosophy club at my sixth form and this week we're doing the Newcomb box thing — predictor, box A always has £1k, box B has £1m only if the predictor predicted you'd take just B, everyone knows how accurate it is. Half the room wants to take both boxes and half wants only B and they basically just shout at each other, so I want a decision tree that sorts them by what they actually believe rather than by what they call themselves.

You're a decision theorist who's taught this puzzle loads of times — think the space through properly before you write it, but only show me the tree plus short notes, not your working.

What I want in it:

- five or six branch questions max, each phrased so a club member could answer it without knowing any jargon. Not "do you accept dominance reasoning" — give them a case to react to instead.
- every leaf named with the real position, plus one sentence on the commitment that person probably won't like once they see it.
- these all have to show up as leaves: the plain two-boxer, the one-boxer whose main point is that one-boxers keep ending up richer, someone who thinks the whole puzzle is impossible because perfect prediction and free choice can't both be true, and someone who'd sign something binding themselves to one-box before walking into the room.
- use at least one of my own variants as a branch question: same setup, but the predictor is just a mate who's right about 60% of the time and box B only has £50 in it. I want to know whether that question seperates two people who looked identical on the standard version, or whether it changes nothing.
- say which combinations of answers nobody actually holds, and whether that's because they're incoherent, just unattractive, or simply unexplored.
- flag any pair of leaves your tree can't really tell apart.

Don't sit on the fence with it. Where you've had to make a judgement call about which question does the real sorting work, say so and give your reason for picking it the way you did, even if other orderings are defensible. Under 800 words for the whole thing, and in British English please since it's for a UK school.
