A philosophy-of-religion reading group contains two entrenched camps. The first holds that nonresistant nonbelief is decisive evidence against a perfectly loving personal God — the argument Schellenberg pressed in the 1990s, on which a God who loved us and wanted a relationship with us would not leave sincere, unresisting seekers unable to believe. The second holds that such a God may reasonably remain non-obvious, because an unmistakable divine presence would coerce assent and destroy precisely the independence and unforced moral development that love is supposed to cultivate. For a year they have re-litigated the same case: a sincere nonbeliever who prays, encounters only silence, and reports finding no resistance in herself. Draft the joint statement that year of argument should have produced.

It should contain: (1) a numbered list of claims both camps can sign, each substantive enough to be worth putting in writing, with any claim that one side signs only under an explicit qualification flagged as such, and none so weak that an uninvolved third party with no stake in the debate would sign it too; (2) a disagreements table in which each row states the issue in one neutral sentence and then gives each camp's own formulation of its position, in wording that camp would accept as accurate; (3) the cruxes — the sub-questions such that a settled answer would actually move at least one side. For each crux, name a case, argument, or kind of evidence both camps accept as bearing on it, and record each side's prediction in advance.

Two cruxes must be handled explicitly rather than deferred: whether a person's report that she feels no resistance is credible evidence that there is none, and what converts' retrospective reports of resistance they had previously denied imply about that credibility; and whether analogies to human concealment by a loving parent, teacher, or therapist support or undermine the autonomy reply. Close by stating what each camp would treat as evidence against its own position, and by identifying any portion of the dispute that turns merely on how "belief," "relationship," or "perfectly loving" is being used.
