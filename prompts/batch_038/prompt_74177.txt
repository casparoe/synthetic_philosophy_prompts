I am writing the term paper for an upper-level seminar on concepts and categorization, and I want a critical reading rather than a survey, so please be as argumentative as you can.

The seminar has spent the last two weeks on Douglas Hofstadter's Letter Spirit material, the work he did with Gary McGraw and others in the Fluid Analogies Research Group and later collected in Fluid Concepts and Creative Analogies. As we reconstructed it, the commitments are roughly these. The research domain is a gridfont: every lowercase letter of the roman alphabet is built only from straight segments on a fixed grid, so a given 'a' admits of many, many distinct realizations, some of them barely recognizable. Letter identity is therefore not a checklist of necessary and sufficient features; some deviations a letter can absorb and some are fatal to it, and which is which is itself context-sensitive. Style, on the other hand, is not a property of any single letter at all: it is a coherent way of making the same family of trade-offs across the whole alphabet, visible only when the letters are seen together, which is why the program's creative task is to take a few seed letters supplied by a human designer and complete the remaining letters so that all twenty-six share one spirit. Perception and creation are treated as the same capacity running in two directions.

My paper wants to read Borges's "Pierre Menard, Author of the Quixote" through that framework. The relevant content: a narrator eulogizes a French symbolist who resolved to produce Don Quixote not by copying it but by arriving at it as its author, and who leaves behind a couple of chapters of Part I and a fragment that are word for word identical with Cervantes's, yet which the narrator insists are a different and richer text because of who wrote them, when, and at what cost.

What I need from you is the following. First, decide whether this is the Letter Spirit thesis transposed from letterforms to texts — same letter, different font — or whether it is the exact inversion, since in a gridfont the marks vary while the identity stays fixed whereas in Menard the marks are fixed while the identity shifts; and say whether that inversion simply breaks the analogy or points at something the model genuinely lacks. Second, consider a case of my own devising: an independently designed gridfont, produced by someone who never saw the original, that turns out segment for segment identical to it. Is that the same font, and does the framework have any resources for answering? Does its struggle, if it struggles, illuminate Menard's? Third, on style: Menard produced a fragment of an alphabet, not a whole one, so can the notion of style the project relies on even apply to him, and is the narrator's talk of Menard's manner as affected and archaic the same notion of style at all? Fourth, set the reading against Goodman's autographic and allographic distinction and Levinson's account of works as types indicated by an artist at a time, and tell me which of those is on the side of the letter and which on the side of the spirit, and which one Borges's story actually endorses.

Please be blunt about where I should abandon the analogy. The narrator is an unreliable enthusiast, and it is entirely possible the story is ironizing the intuition I want to borrow from it. I do not need quotations, page references, or further reading — I have both texts in front of me. I would rather you end by saying what Borges contributes to the debate about categories, style, and essence that the computational model on its own cannot deliver.
