I am a postgraduate student in philosophy preparing the closing seminar of a module on the philosophy of action for third-year undergraduates. The week's topic is shared agency, and the reading consists of Bratman, Gilbert and Searle; my difficulty is that the students come away convinced the three are describing one and the same phenomenon in three vocabularies, and no amount of exegesis has disabused them. I want to hand them a single concrete case in which the accounts visibly part company, so that they can see the disagreement is substantive rather than verbal. As I understand the three positions — please correct me at the outset if I have any of them wrong, and build the case on the correction rather than on my error — Bratman analyses shared intention reductively, in terms of interlocking individual planning states (each intends that they perform the joint activity, and that they do so in accordance with and because of meshing sub-plans), together with mutual responsiveness and common knowledge of the whole structure; Gilbert holds that what constitutes a plural subject is a joint commitment, entered into when each party openly expresses readiness to be jointly committed with the other in conditions of common knowledge, and that such a commitment cannot be rescinded unilaterally; and Searle treats collective intentionality as primitive, locating it in each participant's own we-intention and, as I read him, denying that this requires mutual beliefs about one another's intentions.

The case I want is engineered so that Searle's account counts the two participants as genuinely acting together while both Bratman's and Gilbert's accounts refuse to. That direction matters: it isolates the mutual-knowledge requirement, rather than simply exhibiting a situation in which the requirement happens to be inoperative. The constraints are as follows. Two participants only, in an ordinary realistic setting: no science fiction, no neuroscience, no coercion, no manipulation, no deception. The activity must be a genuinely joint one in the pre-theoretic sense — the task cannot be completed by either alone, the participants' sub-plans mesh, and they succeed or fail together — so that the case cannot be dismissed as a pair of strangers doing similar things side by side. Each participant must be wholly and correctly informed about what the other intends to contribute and about the other's taking the activity to be a joint one: the failure must not come from first-order ignorance or false belief, nor from either party's uncertainty about the other's plans. What has to fail is the iteration, the higher-order knowledge, so that Bratman's common-knowledge condition and Gilbert's requirement that the expressions of readiness be common knowledge are both unmet while each participant's own we-intention remains intact. The mechanism that breaks the iteration must be an ordinary feature of the situation, not a stipulation to the effect that the participants happen not to know what they know; a case that is simply declared to lack common knowledge does not count as a success. Finally, the scenario itself must be statable in under two hundred words and in language a third-year undergraduate can follow without any technical vocabulary. A small workplace with a single shared deliverable would serve as a seed, but use it only if it earns its place, and prefer something more natural if you can find it.

After the scenario, show that it meets every specification rather than asserting that it does: go through Bratman's conditions one at a time, saying which are satisfied and exactly which fails and in virtue of what; then give Gilbert's verdict and Searle's, with the reason for each. Then report honestly where the intuitive verdict falls — whether most people would describe this as two people acting together, or whether the absence of any exchange between them makes that description feel strained, and whether there is a real risk that the case has been loaded in Searle's favour. Identify the single feature doing the work, remove it, and say what happens: does the case collapse back into uncontroversial symmetric shared agency that all three accounts accept, or does it cease to be joint action altogether? Close with how a Bratmanian and a Gilbertian would each attack the case, including the line that common knowledge is not, on the best reading, essential to Bratman's account and the line that what is really missing is mutual responsiveness rather than mutual knowledge, and say which of those attacks you think succeeds. Two further requests. First, please be explicit throughout about what is settled and what is genuinely disputed among people who work on this material: wherever the correct reading of a condition, or the question whether a theory is committed to it at all, is contested, mark the step in your reasoning as contested rather than presenting one side as the consensus. Second, if you are confident about where Tuomela's we-mode/I-mode framework would place the case, add a sentence or two on that; if you are not confident, say so and leave it out rather than guessing. I would rather not have any references, quotations or suggestions for further reading — what I need is the reasoning, since the students will not have the texts in front of them.
