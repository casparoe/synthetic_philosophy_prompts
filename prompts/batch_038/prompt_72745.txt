Our consciousness seminar blew up on Thursday and I still can't figure out who actually won, so I'm going to lay it out as faithfully as I can. Two people, R and M, went about nine rounds on whether heterophenomenology is a neutral method or a verdict in disguise. R kept insisting that what Dennett is rejecting is lone-wolf autophenomenology — treating the subject as self-certifying — and that the replacement is just data collection. Here's the exchange, roughly in order:

(1) R: The question-begging charge doesn't get off the ground. The method says: take the subject's utterances, treat them as sincere, build up the world of things they're thereby taken to be aware of — pains, afterimages, colored patches — and describe that world without endorsing it, the way an ethnographer describes a community's beliefs about forest spirits without saying whether the spirits exist. At that stage nothing at all has been decided about qualia.

(2) M: The ethnographer comparison is doing sneaky work. The ethnographer can stay neutral about forest spirits because spirits are the sort of thing third-person evidence could in principle bear on — a footprint, a witness, something. Heterophenomenology makes the subject's text the only doorway into the reported item and then says the item has to earn its keep by other evidence. So it's neutral about everything except the one thing under dispute. That's not neutrality, that's rigging.

(3) R: It's not rigging, it's just the ordinary treatment of anything unconfirmable in principle. If a posited item could never be confirmed or disconfirmed by anything beyond the reports that allege it, it explains nothing. Leaving it out isn't a hidden metaphysical verdict, it's the default: don't multiply entities.

(4) M: Then call it what it is — eliminationism with a polite preamble. A truly neutral method would leave open that the reports are about something real. Yours is constructed so that the only story you can finish telling is one where the reports get caused by processes that don't include the reported item.

(5) R: That's just false, and the counterexamples are the standard ones. Blindsight: the patient denies seeing anything and is forced-choice above chance. Change blindness: the subject swears they took in the whole scene in rich detail when the experiments show they'd miss a swap. The method lets us override the subject in both directions, and a framework that can override its own data source isn't pre-committed to that source being right or wrong about anything.

(6) M: Those cases prove my point instead. In each one the correction is supplied by discriminative performance — forced-choice accuracy, detection of the swap. So the method is only ever checking whether reports track what the subject can discriminate. Whatever in the report outruns what performance can confirm, the what-it's-likeness part, doesn't get corrected, it gets dropped off the books. That's not overriding the subject. That's deleting the residue and calling it neutrality.

(7) R: Nothing gets deleted, the residue is precisely the explanandum. You explain why the subject says there's something it's like to be them in terms of a brain that has to keep tabs on its own states without any clean inner picture of them. And the method doesn't require that explanation to succeed — if it failed, you'd be holding a datum your theory couldn't handle.

(8) M: It can't fail. Whatever comes out of a subject's mouth gets converted into "the subject believes they're in such-and-such a state," and that conversion is always satisfiable, for any input whatsoever. There is no possible heterophenomenological result that could indicate qualia are present. A framework where every outcome is compatible with its own presupposition isn't neutral, it's unfalsifiable in exactly the sense that matters here.

(9) R: By that logic every third-person framework is "unfalsifiable" about anything it can't measure, which makes the charge empty. And notice what happened: my claim was methodological — this is how you gather the data — and yours was metaphysical — this is what the data could be. From (4) onward we weren't even disagreeing about the same thing.

Half the seminar agreed with (9), the other half said (9) was a dodge. Please adjudicate this move by move rather than overall: rate each of R's and M's turns (right, partly right, or wrong) with a short justification, and say where the disagreement first goes wrong if it does. Specifically I want your call on three things. Does the forest-spirits comparison in (1) actually hold, and if it breaks, at which exact step does it break — (2), (3), or (6)? Is (6) a substantive objection or is M just redescribing the method's ordinary operation in loaded words? And is (9) a legitimate diagnosis of two people talking past each other, or a way of ducking (8)?

Also — and this is the part I care most about — is there something both R and M are granting that they shouldn't be? Like whether neutrality is even the right thing to demand of a method at a given stage, or whether they've both quietly assumed something about what the subject's report is supposed to be a report of. Tell me where the whole argument is built on sand, if it is.
