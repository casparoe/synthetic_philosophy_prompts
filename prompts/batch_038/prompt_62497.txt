You are to write as a philosopher who works at the intersection of the ethics of representation, the metaphysics of harm, and the theory of fiction — someone equally at home with the question of whether the dead can be wronged at all and the question of what a historical novel is for. I want you to think carefully and at length, and I want an essay that arrives somewhere, not a survey. Please do not give me a bibliography, a reading list, or citations; I am not asking what has been said about this, only what you take to be true. Nothing here depends on your knowing any particular book, case, or person, and everything you need is in this letter.

I am a novelist, and I am six months into a book I may have to abandon. Its subject is a real woman: a printer's widow who ran her late husband's shop in a small town on the middle Rhine from 1843 until her death in 1851, printing catechisms, broadsheets, and — for eleven weeks during the disturbances of 1848 — a pamphlet of her own that got her fined. She is not a figure you will have heard of, and I do not want you to try to recall her. What survives of her is a parish register, a bundle of business correspondence, a diary of perhaps sixty pages that stops three years before her death, and one disciplinary record. She had no descendants; both her children died in infancy; nobody living bears her name.

Three candidate wrongs have been circling my desk for months, and I want them taken apart rather than accepted from me. The first I have been calling the falsification wrong: the novel makes the public record worse, and someone who never reads anything else about 1849 will carry my invention away as knowledge. The second I call the usurpation wrong: I have taken a life as raw material from a person who cannot refuse, and the wrong lies in the taking rather than in the accuracy of what is taken. The third I call the ventriloquism wrong: I have made a dead woman say, in her own apparent voice, what I would have liked her to say. I suspect these come apart — that one can falsify faithfully, usurp without distorting, and ventriloquize truthfully — and I want to know whether they are genuinely distinct wrongs or three descriptions of one.

My own working rule, which I want you to test to destruction, I have been calling the ledger principle. It distinguishes invented interior from invented exterior. What she thought and felt is unverifiable in principle, so it is free; what she did is a matter of record, so it is constrained. Fiction may fill the gaps the archive leaves but may not contradict the archive. I have been following this rule, and it has begun to look both too permissive and too strict, and I cannot tell which.

Here are the five decisions in the manuscript. Rule on each of them.

One. I have given her a younger brother who never existed, so that she has someone to argue with. He absorbs material from three real correspondents. He is not defamatory, merely invented.

Two. The archive holds eleven of her letters to a married schoolmaster. Nothing in them establishes anything; the man's replies are lost. In my novel they are lovers, and there is a scene. The schoolmaster has one living descendant, a retired teacher, who wrote to me with great courtesy asking whether I intended to make an adulterer of his ancestor.

Three. The register says she died of typhus in 1851, in her house. My book's architecture needs her to die in 1849, in the street, after the troops come in. I have moved her death by two years and changed its cause.

Four. The diary is pious in a wholly conventional idiom. On the night her daughter dies I have given her a long interior passage of doubt bordering on blasphemy. Nothing in the record supports it. Nothing in the record excludes it.

Five. A real pastor is documented as having harried her for years and as having refused her daughter a proper burial. He is the grandfather of a woman who was my friend and who is alive and ninety. I have softened him and given him one scene of decency.

With that laid out, please do the following. Begin, before the essay proper, with a page of exploratory working in which you generate several candidate principles for what a novelist owes the dead, run each against at least two of my five decisions, and discard those that fail — showing where they fail. I would rather see the discards than a confident first draft.

Then write the essay. It should keep three registers visibly separate: the epistemic question of what my book does to what is known, the moral question of whether anyone is wronged and by what, and the aesthetic question of whether the book is a bad instance of the kind of thing it is. Some of my anxieties belong in only one of these registers and I have been treating them as if they belonged in all three; tell me which is which. It should confront directly whether the dead can be harmed or wronged by anything that happens after their death, and — if you conclude that they cannot — say whether the whole problem then collapses into a problem about the living, and whether that is a stable place to land or a way of changing the subject. Note that in my case there are almost no living people with a stake: no descendants, no name, no town memory. If obligations to the dead really are obligations to survivors, my subject has none, and I want to know whether that genuinely licenses everything or whether the principle that licenses it is wrong.

It should test the ledger principle hard. Find at least two of my five decisions where the interior/exterior distinction gives what you judge to be the wrong verdict, and say whether the principle can be repaired — by appeal to spirit rather than letter, or to what a reasonable reader takes a historical novel to be asserting — or whether it should be abandoned.

It should address two further variables I cannot resolve on my own. First, prominence: does it matter that my subject is obscure rather than famous? I have argued both sides with myself. For a famous subject the audience already holds a picture, so invention is a correction; for an obscure one the novel may become the only record anyone ever keeps, doing the work of an archive without an archive's discipline. Second, framing: would any of your verdicts change if this material appeared in a biopic carrying a title card announcing that it is based on true events, or in a novel whose afterword states that everything is invented except two dates? Does an author's note discharge the obligation, or does it merely purchase a licence to falsify at the price of one paragraph?

Finish with a single verdict, stated plainly. Tell me which of the five changes I must undo, which I may keep, and why — and tell me what you take the general obligation of a novelist toward the real dead to be, in a form I could hold myself to on the next book. Do not end on a note of balanced irresolution; if the honest answer is that some of these decisions have no principled resolution and must be made by temperament, say that, and say which ones.
