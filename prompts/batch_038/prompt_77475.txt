---

Machiavelli's *fortuna* is not a personal deity but a name for everything in political life that resists calculation: accident, the temper of an age, the succession of events no one foresaw. He compares it to one of those raging rivers which, when in flood, sweep across the plains, carrying off trees and buildings and moving the soil from one place to another; everything flies before it and yields to its violence without being able to withstand it. Yet, he adds, this does not mean that when the weather is fair men cannot construct defences and barriers, so that when the river rises again its waters may be channelled and its force less unrestrained and less dangerous. Fortune shows her power where resistance has not been prepared, and turns her forces where no barriers have been raised against her. The image states his central conviction: fortune is powerful but not omnipotent, and the space she leaves open is the space of politics.

That space is filled by *virtù*, which is not the moral virtue of the Christian tradition. Machiavellian *virtù* is the capacity to impose form on matter — energy, decisiveness, force of will — and it is what founds states and holds them. In substance it is practical wisdom in the Aristotelian sense carried over into politics: a settled disposition of character that chooses the mean between excess and defect. The prince of *virtù* cultivates mercy, good faith and integrity in due measure and departs from them only so far as necessity requires; *virtù* is the mean that governs his conduct, and the vices are simply what he avoids. Chapter 6 makes the same point about founders: Moses, Cyrus, Romulus and Theseus took nothing from fortune but the opportunity, which supplied them with the matter into which they could introduce whatever form they pleased.

The claim about halves is Machiavelli's way of refusing two errors at once: the fatalism of those who think nothing we do matters, and the confidence of those who think nothing can go wrong. If fortune governs one half and leaves the other, then a ruler who prepares in advance can reduce the share of the unpredictable in his state; the dykes are that preparation. This is why he insists that the prince must direct his actions according to the spirit of the times: cautious where caution is called for, impetuous where impetuosity is called for. Flexibility is the whole of Machiavelli's answer to fortune, and the prince who has it is secure, because he can meet whatever the times send.

His *fortuna*, moreover, is not really a pagan goddess. It is the fortune of the *Consolation* — the instrument of an order God governs but does not disclose — with the theological vocabulary removed and the structure retained. The river is divine will: unforeseeable, yet such that prudent men may build against it. Machiavelli secularises the language while keeping the framework, and that is the whole of his modernity.

There remains an unresolved tension. If the river cannot be stopped, the dykes are only a palliative; and the closing comparison of fortune to a woman who lets herself be won by the bold, and must be struck rather than courted, contradicts the earlier counsel of measured preparation. The two pieces of advice cancel one another, so that chapter 25 ends by offering the prince no practical guidance at all. Nor does his treatment of republics repair the difficulty, since a republic can adapt no better than a prince: it too must act through the fixed characters of its leading citizens.
