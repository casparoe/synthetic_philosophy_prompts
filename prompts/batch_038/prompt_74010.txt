I'm co-teaching a gen-ed seminar with the urban studies people this term, tentatively called "Who Owns the City?", and next week we hit Lorraine Hansberry's A Raisin in the Sun. I put it on the syllabus because it's the one text we're doing that stages housing exclusion from inside a kitchen rather than from inside a zoning hearing, and because I think my students are about to under-read it in a very specific way. They'll come in having seen Lindner as a cartoon racist and the ending as a triumph. What I want the reading to do is treat the play through the right-to-the-city idea: that what the Youngers end up asserting in act three isn't really a property claim at all, but a claim to belong to and to have some say in the urban fabric they already live and work inside. And note the wrinkle that makes this harder than the usual telling — Lena doesn't pick Clybourne Park as a desegregationist gesture, she picks it because the houses there are cheaper than in the Black neighborhoods. The moral claim doesn't arrive with the purchase; it arrives with Lindner's knock. So the play seems to be saying that a family becomes a claimant on the city only when someone tries to keep them out of it, and I want to know whether that's right, or whether I'm imposing it.

Two things I want the reading to actually wrestle with rather than wave at. First: is Lindner's argument a bad argument? I keep telling the seminar that the interesting version of NIMBYism is never malice, it's a claim about a community's right to shape its own character, and Hansberry gives Lindner a real speech instead of a slur — he's polite, he insists there's no prejudice involved, he frames the buyout as being for everybody's good including theirs. I've been giving my students a distinction that I think is useful and that I want tested here: call the weak form of the right to the city a claim not to be excluded from a particular parcel, and the strong form a claim to participate in deciding what the neighborhood is and becomes. Does the play answer the strong form, or only the weak one? Because on the weak form Walter's refusal of the money at the end is a complete answer — nobody gets to buy them out — but on the strong form the Youngers are still moving into a place whose character they had zero say in, and the play arguably celebrates exactly that. Second, the ending itself: through this lens it looks like a strangely proprietarian resolution, belonging cashed out as a deed bought with a ten-thousand-dollar insurance check, dignity expressed as an offer to be good neighbors. And then there are the two exits the play dangles — George Murchison's assimilate-and-get-money route, and Asagai's Nigeria, which is Beneatha withdrawing from the American city altogether. Does Hansberry endorse the purchase, indict it, or leave both live?

Some legal weeds I want folded in, because I think they change what the play is about. Hansberry's own father was the Hansberry in the 1940 Supreme Court case over the covenant on their Chicago house, and that case turned on res judicata and due process — whether a family could be bound by an earlier class action they weren't adequately represented in — not on whether covenants were constitutional. Then in 1948 the Court held that judicial enforcement of a racially restrictive covenant is state action and violates equal protection, while leaving private parties free to abide by one voluntarily. Which means that by 1959, when the play is set, Lindner can't get a court to do anything for him. So what exactly is the force of his offer? It seems to me this is NIMBYism proper rather than de jure segregation — private and social pressure, backed by money, with no law behind it — and if that's right the play is more current than it looks: swap the improvement association for a homeowner's association or a neighborhood coalition fighting a fourplex and the arguement is the same one my students will hear at a city council meeting in April. Does the play have resources to answer a Lindner who never once mentions race, who just keeps saying he's worried about how everybody will get along? And finally, the material side: the apartment's single window, Lena's plant that can't get enough sun, Travis on the couch. Is the sunlight doing philosophical work here — is overcrowding and light deprivation the injury that grounds the claim on the city — or am I over-reading a stage direction? One more thing to weigh if you know it: the Mrs. Johnson scene that Hansberry wrote but that got cut from the Broadway run and survives in print, where the neighbor cheerfully predicts the family will turn up in the papers as bombing victims. If you take that scene seriously, does the ending still read as vindication of the family's claim, or does the play know perfectly well what that claim is going to cost them?

Practical stuff: I've got the text in front of me, so don't bother quoting lines back at me and don't send me a reading list — I want the argument, not the apparatus. Write it so a nineteen-year-old who's never heard of Lefebvre can follow it, which means no jargon dumps, and please be honest where the play resists the lens instead of bending it to fit; a reading that ends with "and here's where Hansberry refuses the frame I brought to her" is more useful to me than a tidy one. If you think the right-to-the-city framing is simply the wrong tool for a 1959 Chicago play about a family trying to get out of a roach-infested apartment, say that plainly and tell me what you'd use instead.
