I am preparing a short paper for a departmental reading group on the equality-versus-priority debate, and the draft below has reached the point where I can no longer tell which of my moves are load-bearing and which are decorative. What follows is an abridged version (roughly a third of the eventual length); please treat the compression as deliberate and do not comment on exposition, transitions, or the absence of examples I have cut for space.

**Draft: "The Residue and the Premise: What the Leveling-Down Objection Actually Rules Out"**

**1. The set-up.** Parfit's distinction is between two views that coincide almost everywhere and diverge in a few revealing places. Telic egalitarianism holds that it is intrinsically bad when some people are worse off than others, so that an equal outcome is in one respect better than an unequal one even when the equality is produced by taking from the better-off and giving to nobody. Prioritarianism holds that the moral weight of a benefit depends on how well off its recipient already is: benefits count for more the worse off the person who receives them. Nothing in that formulation compares one person's level with another's. The divergence shows up in the divided-world case, where people's lives are wholly unconnected so that inequality between them could carry none of the social consequences egalitarians usually invoke, and it shows up most sharply in leveling down.

**2. The objection and the standard retreat.** Take a world in which half the population is at 200 units of wellbeing and half at 100, and a second world in which everyone is at 100, the reduction caused by something that benefits no one — a harvest failure confined to the prosperous region, say. In the second world nobody is better off and many are much worse off. The telic egalitarian is nonetheless committed to saying that the second world is in one respect better, since it is equal. Parfit's objection is that this is not a respect of betterness at all: an improvement ought to be an improvement in some assignable sense, and here there is no sense in which anything has improved.

The familiar reply, associated with Temkin, has two parts. First, "in one respect better" does not imply "better all things considered"; the egalitarian concedes the leveled-down world is worse overall and insists only that the inequality in the first world was a genuine bad, outweighed in this case by the losses. Second, the objection smuggles in a person-affecting assumption — that nothing can be better unless it is better for somebody — which is independently contentious and which Parfit's own work on the non-identity problem and on mere addition is largely an attempt to do without.

Grant both. Call what survives the first retreat the residue: equality's residual value once the egalitarian has stopped claiming that leveling down is in any all-things-considered way preferable.

**3. The residue has to be spendable somewhere.** A pro tanto value that could never alter a single verdict is not distinguishable, in principle, from no value at all. There would be no ranking, no choice, no act of endorsement anywhere in the theory's output that came out differently because of it. So the retreat is available to the egalitarian only if there remain cases in which the residue decides. There are: cases in which two distributions contain exactly the same total wellbeing, differently arranged, so that the egalitarian ranks them and the prioritarian is indifferent between them.

But notice where this leaves the residue. It has been confined to tie-breaking. Every case in which someone actually loses — which is to say, every case that generated the intuitive force of the objection — is a case in which the residue has just been declared outvoted. And the tie-breaking intuition is far weaker than the intuition it is being asked to replace. The leveling-down case asks how a change in which someone suffers and nobody gains could be good, and that question has real grip. The tie case asks only whether two worlds with identical totals but different spreads differ in value, and that question has almost no independent grip; where people feel confident about it, the confidence is usually inherited from what they already believe about the loss cases — precisely the ground the retreat abandoned. The residue is being spent on the strength of an intuition the egalitarian no longer has license to deploy.

**4. The premise the objection is really arguing for.** I think the debate has been mislocated. Both views are standardly presented as answers to the question of what the shape of a distribution contributes over and above how well individuals fare. But prioritarianism's answer is constructed entirely out of individual-level facts: a concave transform applied to each person's level separately, then summed. Neither the spread, nor the ranking, nor any comparison between persons enters the calculation. Telic egalitarianism's answer is not of that form: the badness of inequality is a property of the whole pattern and is not decomposable into contributions attributable to particular people.

So if the leveling-down objection has any force, its force comes from excluding non-decomposable properties from distributive evaluation. Call this the separability premise: the value of a distribution must be a function of what each person's level contributes taken on its own. Given separability, the objection has no work left to do — the leveled-down world is worse by arithmetic, and nobody needs an intuition about it. Without separability, the objection requires a substantive argument that pattern-properties are not the kind of thing that can bear value. Parfit supplies no such argument. He supplies a rhetorical question about how such a change could count as an improvement, delivered in a register that anticipates no reply, which is exactly what one would expect if the premise were being assumed rather than established.

**5. Why this costs the prioritarian more than he admits.** If the objection is in substance an argument for separability, its blast radius extends well past the target Parfit names. Relational or democratic egalitarians of Anderson's kind — for whom equality is about the social relations people stand in, matters of standing, deference, and domination, rather than about a pattern of quantities — are not separable in the relevant sense either, and are not obviously more so than the telic view. A prioritarian who treats the leveling-down objection as decisive ought, on pain of inconsistency, to reject those views as well; in practice he does not want to, and he cannot escape by saying that they are deontic rather than telic, since separability as I have stated it is a constraint on what may count as a reason at all, not merely on what may count as a better state of affairs.

**6. Conclusion.** Prioritarianism is usually sold as capturing everything humane in egalitarianism without the embarrassment. On the reading offered here, it captures the humane part by construction: it never permits distributive evaluation to consider more than one person at a time, and so it was never exposed to the objection in the first place. Whether that restriction is a virtue is a further question, and it is the only question the exchange has not addressed.
