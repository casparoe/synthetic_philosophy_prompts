I am a second-year philosophy PhD student with a previous life in machine learning, and I am applying to my department's interdisciplinary research fund for a four-month project that is expected to end in a workshop paper rather than a journal article. The half-formed idea I need turned into a proposal comes out of the literature on eliciting latent knowledge from large language models — the recurring suggestion that models carry information internally that does not surface in their default outputs, and the growing toolkit of techniques for drawing it out: linear probes over internal activations, oracle-style forced-choice formats, adversarial debate, self-report under hypothetical or fictional framing. My suspicion, which I want the proposal to test rather than assume, is that "latent knowledge" is currently being made to cover at least three distinct claims that come apart in practice: that some internal state carries the information; that the system can be brought, under some elicitation regime, to produce behaviour tracking that information; and that the system is disposed to act on it when nothing pressures it otherwise. The techniques then tend to be credited with establishing the first claim on the strength of the second. There is a sharper problem underneath, which a friend of mine doing interpretability work raised over dinner and I have not been able to shake: if the content recovered varies with the probe used, then what the model "knows" may be an artefact of the elicitation procedure rather than something the procedure discovers, in which case there is no determinate fact for the method to be right or wrong about — and the failures to elicit, which everyone treats as uninformative, would then contaminate the successes too.

Please write the proposal itself, at roughly 1,200 words, in a register that a general philosophy audience who has never seen a transformer can follow without being talked down to. It needs to state the research question precisely enough that it has a determinate answer, and to say why the three positions one hears — the intentional-realist view that treats these as genuine beliefs, the behaviourist view that says only input-output dispositions are real and the internal talk is decoration, and the deflationary view that this is all just prompt-sensitivity wearing a lab coat — each leave my question open rather than settling it. Then the method: what I would actually do month by month, including at least one concrete evaluation design that could in principle discriminate genuine retrieval from probe-induced construction, together with an honest account of the conditions under which no such design could be run at all. I want the proposal to take up a specific sub-question explicitly, because I think it is where the philosophical work lives: should a claim of latent knowledge be required to satisfy what I will call a guidance condition — that the information be available to steer the system's own behaviour in the absence of the eliciting probe — and can a system whose only behaviour is the production of text ever meet such a condition, or does meeting it collapse the condition into something trivial? State what a successful result would look like (a worked distinction, a diagnosis of which published claims trade on which sense, or something else), what would count as the project failing, and where you think four months is genuinely too little, rather than reassuring me that it is enough.

Do not include a bibliography, a literature review by citation, or any reference list; I will supply those myself, and I would rather you left the slots empty than filled them by guesswork. Refer to positions, arguments, and families of technique by description instead. One last constraint worth designing around: the review panel will include an experimentalist who thinks the whole question is settled by running more evals, so build in a short section that answers, without condescension and without appealing to the intrinsic value of philosophy, why this particular problem cannot be resolved by better instrumentation alone.
