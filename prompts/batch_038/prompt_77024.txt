I teach the opening seminar of a graduate course in the philosophy of computing. My students can describe a Turing machine but have never separated the different claims that travel under the name "Church–Turing thesis," and I want each of them to leave that first meeting able to say which claim they actually accept. Build me a decision tree for this: nine to eleven nodes, each node a question a student could answer from a concrete case rather than from a label, each leaf a recognizable position on the thesis and its epistemic status.

Two constraints on the nodes. No node may use the words "Church–Turing," "hypercomputation," "anti-mechanism," or "physical thesis"; phrase them instead in terms of scenarios — a mathematician working through explicit rules, a device whose settings are real numbers fixed to arbitrary precision, a computer dropped toward a rotating black hole and timed against a distant observer, a brain described at the level of individual quanta, a procedure allowed to revise its answer finitely many times before settling. And the tree must force a split between what is being claimed about effective procedure and what is being claimed about what nature permits, because I expect several of my students to have collapsed the two.

Whatever path reaches them, these leaves must appear: the thesis as a stipulation or definition rather than a hypothesis; the thesis as a falsifiable empirical claim about physical systems; the Gödelian anti-mechanist claim that mathematical insight outruns any formal system; the limiting or trial-and-error position; the real-number machine position; the relativistic-spacetime position; and one leaf for students whose answers come apart, with a sentence on the conflation most likely responsible.

Please organize the response in these sections, in this order: "The claims being sorted" (about 150 words, in plain language, before any branching); "The tree" (the nodes indented, each question followed by what each answer does); "The leaves" (roughly 40 words each: name, central claim, most awkward commitment); "Empty cells" (about 250 words on combinations of answers no one defends, each diagnosed as incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored); and "Pairs this tree cannot separate" (about 150 words on at least two pairs of leaves my questions will fail to distinguish, plus the further question that would distinguish them).
