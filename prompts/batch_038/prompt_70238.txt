I'm a backend engineer with no philosophy training. I'm in a reading group with a colleague who did computational neuroscience, and we're working through von Neumann's *The Computer and the Brain* — the Yale lectures published posthumously in 1958. Over the past few weeks he has committed himself to a set of verdicts about what von Neumann's comparison of the nervous system with a computing automaton does and does not license:

1. The nervous system is digital at bottom — the all-or-none pulse and the synapse behave like logical elements — but only superficially, since thresholds and chemical factors reintroduce an analog dimension.
2. A single neuron's arithmetic is nowhere near as precise as that of a modern processor.
3. That low precision is not a deficiency awaiting correction but a design condition, one that forces the whole system into a different organization: massive parallelism, and statistical rather than exact handling of information.
4. It therefore cannot be assumed to be a sequential stored-program machine; the number of successive steps any task is decomposed into is far smaller than in a computer, and its individual components are far slower.
5. Durable storage in the nervous system is not like addressable machine memory — there is no fixed location written once and read back, and capacity does not appear to saturate over a lifetime.
6. Short-term storage and the slower, more durable store are probably physically distinct.
7. Where the analogy finally fails is not in the hardware parameters but in the code: the nervous system may run a different language altogether, one weighted toward statistical and associative structure rather than precise sequential operations.

The case coming up next week is a large transformer-based model. Its weights are distributed rather than addressable; its forward computation is massively parallel; it is routinely deployed with 4-bit or 8-bit quantized weights and stochastic sampling; there is no fixed instruction stream; and what it knows was written in by training rather than stored at locations. Will he say this closes the gap von Neumann identified, or that it does not?

I care much less about the answer than about which of his commitments is doing the work. Please identify the rule or rules that are consistent with all seven verdicts, say what each candidate rule predicts about the transformer case and why, give me your best guess at his verdict with the reasons for preferring that rule over the others, and name the one further case that would most cleanly separate them. Two possibilities I have in mind are whether he would still call the gap unclosed if the same trained function were reimplemented sequentially in exact high-precision arithmetic, and whether he would concede that a machine built from components with neuron-level imprecision could reach arbitrary precision simply by iterating enough times.
