So — two thirds of the way through a comic novel set in a theoretical physics institute, and I've hit a wall on one chapter. It's the retirement dinner for the old string theorist everyone's built their career underneath, and the toast is given by his best student, who fifteen years ago went off and constructed the one serious rival theory anybody's ever managed to make work.

I need the joke he tells.

The hard part: it can't be the usual string theory gag. Nothing about it being unfalsifiable, or "not really science," or forty years with no predictions, no Schrödinger's cat, no physicist-walks-into-a-bar. My readers have seen all of those and so have I. I want the joke to run on the actual logic of non-empirical confirmation, which is the thing my characters genuinely believe in and which is genuinely weird: a theory can pick up support without any new data at all, because the absence of competitors is itself evidence. The reasoning is that if there were a workable alternative out there, the field would have stumbled onto it by now, so every extra year in which nobody produces one makes the incumbent theory better confirmed rather than less. There's a second leg too, where two bits of theory built for completely unrelated reasons turn out to mesh, and that unexpected coherence gets counted as support. If you can work that in as a middle beat, lovely — but don't let it smother the ending.

And the ending is where I need you to be clever, because the situation is already funny and I don't want to waste it. The man at the microphone is the alternative. Fifteen years of his life have been, from his teacher's point of view, a slow single-handed draining of the confirmation the old man's theory was recieving just from nobody beating it. So the toast should land somewhere around the recognition that the retirement is a relief in two directions — the emeritus gets to stop having his credence nibbled at, and the student finally gets to publish in peace. I'd love it if the last line made that flip explicit enough that a physicist in the audience groans and a spouse who's never heard of any of this still laughs.

Practical stuff: it's spoken aloud, so it has to be deliverable in about ninety seconds, and I want roughly 150 to 250 words for the joke itself. Keep it warm-ish — the student loves the old man, he's not assassinating him. Then give me a one-line version seperate from the bit, something I could stick on the page as the chapter epigraph.

Last thing, and I know it's un-fun, but add two or three sentences after telling me which beat of the joke is doing which piece of the argument. I've read enough about this to spot when a joke is waving its hands at a label instead of actually turning on the mechanism, and I don't want to be the novelist who gets taken apart by a reviewer with a philosophy of science degree.
