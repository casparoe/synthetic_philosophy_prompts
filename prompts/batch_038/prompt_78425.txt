So I watched that documentary about the Norwegian maximum-security prison that keeps making the rounds, and then my sister — she's a public defender in Ohio — spent an hour at dinner describing what release actually looks like in her county, and I've been stuck on the fact that these two systems can't possibly be running on the same idea of what a punishment is. I'm not a philosophy person, I do data analysis, so I want the underlying theory spelled out rather than the politics or a ranking of which system is nicer.

The Norwegian side, as I understand it. The place was built to resemble a small town: no barbed wire, no watchtowers, no bars on the cell windows, gaurds are unarmed and eat and play sports with the prisoners. Cells are small but have a TV, a fridge and their own shower, and every ten or so cells share a real kitchen with proper plates and knives, and people can buy groceries and cook for themselves. There's an official principle that the only thing the sentence is meant to remove is liberty, so everything else is kept as close to ordinary life as security allows — you keep the right to vote, and your doctor, dentist, school classes and library are provided by the same municipal and county agencies that serve everyone outside, so you don't drop out of the health system while you're in. The stated rationale is that the smaller the gap between inside and outside, the easier the transition on release. There's no life sentence; the longest term is 21 years, but a court can extend it in renewable five-year chunks if the person is still judged dangerous. Then the American side. In most states a felony conviction costs you the vote while you're inside and often long after; Florida passed a ballot measure in 2018 by a big margin restoring the vote to people who'd finished their sentences, and then it got narrowed so you also have to pay off all your fines, fees and restitution first, which by one count left hundreds of thousands of people who'd already served their time still barred, and by many accounts the state couldn't reliably tell them what they owed. On top of that, in a lot of places the incarcerated person pays for phone calls home at several times market rate with a cut going back to the facility, sometimes a co-pay for a medical visit, sometimes a slice of prison wages going to room and board; and after release there's a whole wall of collatoral restrictions — public housing bars, occupational license bars, that kind of thing.

What I want is the implicit theory in each case. For the Norwegians: if the sentence takes only liberty, what does the punishment actually consist of? What has to be true about why confinement is a harm — autonomy, severed relationships, being held somewhere you didn't choose, being cut off from your projects — for a comfortable prison to still be a punishment rather than a subsidized hotel? And is the principle in the background a claim about what offenders deserve, a claim about their standing as persons and citizens, or just a claim about future outcomes? I keep noticing that someone who cared only about recidivism could adopt every single feature of that prison on purely instrumental grounds, so tell me whether the normality idea strictly presupposes anything about the moral status of prisoners at all, or whether it's underdetermined and several rival theories would license it. For the Americans: what view of the political community makes the loss of the vote part of a criminal sentence — that a conviction forfeits membership rather than just relocating you? What view of punishment makes it sensible to bill the person for the phone call and the doctor and the bed, and to condition the vote on paying it, as though there were a debt that survives the sentence? Is that coherent with a retributive story where the time served settles the account? Then check the parts of each system against each other: Norway says the punishment is only the loss of liberty but then holds people indefinitely on the basis of dangerousness, so which logic is really doing the work there, desert or risk; and the US insists in principle that we punish the crime and not the conditions, while building conditions into the sentence and charging for them. I'd also like you to separate, for each practice, what it strictly requires from what merely sits comfortably with it, say which rival accounts each one rules out, and finish by telling me what would concretely have to change if you swapped in the rival presupposition — which statutes, which pieces of architecture, which invoices become incoherent if the US accepted that only liberty is taken, and which Norwegian features would have to go if Norway accepted that conviction costs you your standing as a citizen. Please skip the humane-versus-harsh summary, I want the concepts.
