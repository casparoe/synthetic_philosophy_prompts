I am not an academic and have never studied economics, but for the past eleven years I have been one of the people who run a shared community woodland: about forty acres held in common, with a rota for hedge-laying and path work, an agreed limit on how much firewood each household may take, and a small committee that settles disputes. We also manage, much less successfully, a shared car park for the twenty-odd households on our lane, where people routinely park inconsiderately and nobody will volunteer to enforce anything. The contrast between the two has got me thinking, and I have been reading around the subject on my own.

Last month I bought a paperback about how groups manage shared resources. One chapter sets out the standard story as follows:

'Garrett Hardin's 1968 article in Science established the logic of the commons with a simple model. Herders grazing stock on an open pasture each take the whole benefit of adding one more animal while sharing only a fraction of the resulting damage with everyone else, so each rational herder adds another animal, and then another, until the pasture is destroyed. Hardin's point was that this is not a moral failing but an inescapable mathematical result, which is why he called it a tragedy: appeals to conscience cannot prevent it, since the herder who restrains himself is simply out-competed by the rest. Hardin concluded that there are only two exits — selling the pasture off as private property, or having restrictions imposed by force — and he applied the same model to human population and to immigration, arguing that a wealthy country cannot keep its borders open without destroying the commons of its welfare state. The pasture example was not Hardin's invention: he took it from a lecture given at Oxford in the 1830s by an economist describing what had actually happened on English common land. For a generation this was treated as settled science, and it was used to justify enclosure, the privatisation of water, individual fishing quotas and the general principle that communal ownership fails. Then Elinor Ostrom went out and studied real commons — irrigation communities in Spain and the Philippines, mountain villages in Switzerland and Japan, groundwater basins in California — and found societies that had managed shared resources sustainably for centuries by means of locally made rules, monitoring by the users themselves and graduated sanctions, work for which she won the Nobel Prize in 2009. Ostrom's findings refute Hardin outright. The tragedy of the commons is a myth: communities do not need markets or states to look after what they share, and Hardin's real contribution was to give an academic-looking justification for taking land away from the people who were already looking after it.'

The ending reads to me as though it is arguing harder than the evidence warrants, and our own woodland is the reason I hesitate — but the first half sounds like a description of a proof, and I cannot tell whether that is fair. I would therefore like a careful audit of the passage. Please take it claim by claim and tell me what someone who works on commons governance would accept as accurate, what they would regard as a defensible simplification, what they would wince at, and what is simply wrong. In particular I would like to know whether Hardin's argument really is a mathematical demonstration or something looser than that; whether the 1830s pasture example was a historical account of English commons or a hypothetical illustration, and whether the two differ in a way that matters; whether "the commons" in this literature means the same thing as a public good, or whether something important is being run together, since our car park and our woodland seem to behave differently; whether Hardin himself restricted his conclusion to the two exits named, and whether the population and immigration material belongs to the same argument or to later work; and above all whether Ostrom's case studies contradict Hardin or examine a different kind of arrangement altogether.

Finally, tell me whether the chapter's conclusion follows from the parts of it that survive scrutiny, and whether anything accurate in the passage actually cuts against that conclusion. Please be explicit about which of your judgements reflect settled scholarly agreement and which are genuinely contested among people who know this literature — if, for instance, whether Ostrom refuted Hardin is itself a live dispute rather than a decided question, say so, and do not present one side of it to me as though it were the consensus.
