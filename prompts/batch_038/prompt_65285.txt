I run a small informal forecasting group — eight of us, two historians, two quants, and four people who mostly just like being right — and last month we picked a deliberately long-horizon question: will something recognisable as the current global technological order (world trade roughly at present scale, grid electricity, industrial agriculture, cheap long-distance travel) still be functioning in the year 2300? Predictably the group split not over the answer but over the method, and the argument has now gone on for three meetings and gotten worse, so I want an outside opinion, sorry, a more outside outside opinion.

My friend R. takes the outside view seriously. He built a spreadsheet with three nested reference classes — hegemonic great powers of roughly the last five centuries, complex agrarian-bureaucratic states of the last few millennia, and urban-literate societies of any kind going back as far as the record allows — and computed a survival distribution at each level. The thing that's bugging me: the median tenure rises monotonically as you widen the class. His story is that widening is the standard cure for a small n, and that it also happens to produce a rosier number because longer-lived kinds of thing are, well, longer-lived. My worry is that the cure for small n and the source of the optimism are the same move wearing two hats — that he's not extracting anything from history so much as choosing which history to be extracted from. There's also a measurement problem I can't get him to engage with: "the end" isn't one event type across those levels. A hegemon can be displaced while everything else carries on, a state can collapse and be replaced by something continuous in every practical respect, and a whole mode of life can fade without any date you could put on a tombstone. So it's not obvious the three rows of his table are even measuring the same quantity, let alone that ours is the same quantity as any of them. And underneath all of it, the case we actually care about has exactly one observed member, which makes me suspect any number that comes out of this is a laundered inside view.

So here's what I want. Give me your credence in this proposition: for questions about how long the current global technological order lasts, an outside-view estimate built from historical reference classes can supply genuine information — meaning it can rationally move a thoughtful person's credence — over and above what is already contained in the choices they made when constructing the reference class. I want a number and the full decomposition: name each sub-question as its own term, give it a probability and one line of justification, and say how they combine (multiplicatively, or some more honest way). Include at least one term for whether there's any non-arbitrary principle of reference-class selection that survives the n=1 case, one term for whether survival statistics transfer at all across the levels R. nested, and one term for whether a self-sampling style inference — the kind that says your own position in a sequence is itself evidence about the sequence's length — could rescue anything here in cases where the historical base rate can't. Then tell me which term you're least confident about and what would move it most, and separately: is the monotonic pattern (wider class, longer median tenure) an artifact of how the classes are counted, a genuine statistical effect, or simply unknowable from the data he has? Finally, R. is sitting at about 0.7 on the overall proposition and I'm at about 0.15 — tell me which single term you think accounts for most of that gap, because if I can't name it I'll be having this argument again next month. No reading list, no citations, no bibliography — I can't check any of that anyway and it isn't what I'm after. I want your reasoning with its weak spots marked.
