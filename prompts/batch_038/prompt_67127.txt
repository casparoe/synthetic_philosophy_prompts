I am a novelist — three published books, the fourth owed to my publisher in eleven months — and for two winters now I have been reading philosophy of mind the way some novelists read case law, as raw material rather than as a discipline. The new book is a first-person retrospective narration: a woman in her late sixties, a former hospice nurse, reconstructing a single summer, and in the later chapters I have drifted so far into free indirect style that it is frequently unclear whether a given sentence reports the character's judgment or the narrator's. The engine of the plot is a claim she makes repeatedly and calmly: that she felt nothing at her mother's death, and that whatever arrived afterwards was not grief. A friend who teaches cognitive science assured me that this is precisely the situation philosophers have in mind when they discuss epiphenomenalism — the view illustrated by the familiar image of a steam whistle that rides along with the locomotive while contributing nothing to its work, experience being caused by the brain but causing nothing in return — and that the paradox of phenomenal judgment resolves a craft problem I have been circling for months.

My understanding of the paradox, which may be too compressed to survive contact with the real thing: if a quale cannot enter the causal chain at all, then a person's judgment about that quale could not have been produced by anything except the quale itself, since any other producer would render the agreement between judgment and experience a standing coincidence; the paradox is that epiphenomenalism is unable to supply such a producer. What I took from this, and what I have been preparing to build the book's moral center on, is that a sincere report of one's own present experience enjoys a warrant no other kind of report has: it cannot be mistaken, because there is nothing in the vicinity available to be mistaken about. Unreliable narration would then be confined to errors regarding the world and to errors regarding one's own motives. My narrator could lie, could misread everyone around her, could remain blind to why she did what she did — but she could not be wrong about what she felt, and the reader would have no standing from which to doubt her.

So how am I to write a narrator who is wrong about her own grief without violating the guarantee I have just described? Two related difficulties. The first is that her central claim is retrospective and comparative: she asserts not that she feels nothing now but that she felt nothing then, and that what came later belonged to some category other than grief. I cannot determine whether such an assertion sits inside the protected class or outside it, or whether the boundary here is principled rather than a line philosophers draw when it happens to suit them. The second is that if the paradox does indeed dispose of epiphenomenalism, I appear to be left with a narrator whose felt experience is causally efficacious after all, which would demote her passivity — the conviction she has of observing her own life through a pane of glass — from a metaphysical condition to a mere opinion she holds, and I am unwilling to surrender that. I am not after a reading list, and I will not be citing anyone; what I want is to be told whether the inference I have drawn is sound, and if it is not, where exactly the fault lies: in my statement of the argument, in the step from the argument to a guarantee about reports, or in my assumption that the guarantee, if it exists, reaches far enough to cover the kind of sentence my novel is actually made of.
