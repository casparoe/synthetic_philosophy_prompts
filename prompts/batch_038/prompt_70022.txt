I'm a third-year taking a seminar on higher-order evidence — we've done the peer-disagreement material and the classic case where you're told a drug is messing with your arithmetic but you're still confident in the number you just arrived at. Alongside the seminar some of us run an unofficial study group, 14 people, 90 minutes next week, and I said I'd bring something for us to actually *do* rather than re-litigate the readings a fourth time.

Can you design an activity where they walk into the akrasia thing themselves? Roughly what I'm after: a run of short reasoning tasks with checkable answers, and higher-order evidence injected between them — in two distinct flavours that pull against each other on at least one round: generic claims about how the process tends to fail (this kind of puzzle trips most people up, and the wrong answer is usually X) and personal evidence about their own track record inside the room. Some of the injected evidence should be true but useless, some false but convincing. One round where they have to say their answers out loud before revising, so peer disagreement becomes yet another source. First answers need to be sealed or recorded somehow, so at the end you can put the agregate mismatch on the board: people who wrote down that their answer is probably wrong and then bet on it anyway, people who revised into a flag that turned out to be bogus. The label stays off the board until the debrief.

What I want them to come away seeing is that the mismatch isn't just a confusion about how to combine two bodies of evidence — it survives being pointed at.

Write out the actual tasks and the actual flags; don't tell me to find puzzles "of moderate difficulty." Materials: paper, envelopes, whiteboard, no real money, a points system is fine. Include a minute-by-minute timeline, the debrief mapping each moment onto first-order vs higher-order evidence and onto the question of whether the mismatch is irrational at all, a frank list of what will predictably blow up the first time I run it (my worry is that the two sharpest people work out the flags are unreliable, start ignoring all of them, and nobody feels any tension), and which feature of the real cases the activity flat-out can't reproduce that I'll have to supply by talking.

Don't hand me a menu of variants — commit to one design and defend the choices, especially which round is doing the most work. And if you think the thing as I've described it can't generate the feeling I'm after, say so outright and tell me what to change instead.
