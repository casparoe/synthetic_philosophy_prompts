My housemate Dara has lived in four countries and has strong views about when a visitor has to do as the locals do, and she applies them with anoying consistency. Her verdicts so far:

- A visiting researcher at a US university who thinks tipping is a racket still has to tip 18–20% at sit-down restaurants, including when the server was curt and the food was mediocre. Non-negotible, she says.
- That same visitor can cross against the light at 3 a.m. at a wide empty intersection in a city where locals wait religiously for the little green man. Fine. Also fine if a local is standing right there watching you do it.
- A foreign student who uses the laundry sign-up sheet everyone in the dorm maintains, but only signs it when it suits them and grabs a free machine when the room is empty: not fine. Her reason is that the sheet only does its job if people sign it on the mornings they'd rather not.
- A houseguest whose host says "please, shoes off — though honestly it doesn't matter, the rugs are ancient and I was going to replace them": shoes off anyway. She's explicit that this one isn't about the rugs.
- A guest at a wedding of a faith she doesn't share, asked to cover her head, finds the whole thing uncomfortable and doesn't believe any of it: cover it. Dara says the day isn't about you.
- A coworker who doesn't chip in for a farewell gift for someone he barely knew: completely fine, as long as he says he's not participating rather than pretending he forgot, and still signs the card.
- A visitor in a country where nobody drinks the tap water, who has read the water quality reports and knows perfectly well it's safe: drink it, fine — but she wants you to say out loud, in front of people, why you're doing it.

She swears none of that is inconsistent, and I mostly believe her, but I can't reconstruct the rule. Here's the case I actually care about.

Our department runs on a completely unwritten norm that nobody sends work email between 7 p.m. and 7 a.m. It's in no policy, nobody explains it to newcomers, and it isn't about hours worked — people are at their desks till nine, they just don't hit send. We're now hosting a visiting fellow for four months, from a place where midnight emails are normal and even expected of juniors. He sends them, always with a line at the top saying no reply needed before morning. Our grad students reply at 11 p.m. anyway, because they feel they can't leave a senior visitor hanging, and one of them told me she's stopped sleeping properly. Nobody has complained to him, nothing about pay or grading or authorship is affected, and when I finally mentioned it he was genuinely surprised — he said he'd happily schedule-send everything if we asked, but he also thinks the norm is silly and would rather be told it exists than left to guess. Its his last month here in eight weeks' time.

What's Dara's verdict — is he obliged to stop, obliged to do something less than stopping, or off the hook?
