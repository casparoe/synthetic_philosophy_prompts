Last spring I became the primary carer for my mother, whose dementia has progressed to the point that she accuses me of stealing from her nearly every day. I resent her for it, and then resent myself for resenting her — and I have become convinced that the framework I was trained in has no slot for this. It offers, in effect, a single switch: either she remains a participant in the moral community and my resentment is the fitting uptake on her ill will, or she is exempt and I adopt the objective attitude toward her as something to be managed. My half-formed idea is that there is a stable third stance: a reactive attitude retained while the demand for second-personal uptake is suspended, so that resentment keeps its character as a response to a person's conduct without entailing any expectation of apology, recognition, or reform.

I am applying for a one-year dissertation fellowship and need this turned into a proposal. Please state the question precisely enough that it could have a determinate answer; explain why the standard positions — the exempting reading, the quality-of-will reading, and the skeptical charge that the whole apparatus is naturalistic consolation — each leave it open; give the method, including what I would actually do month by month and which cases would be diagnostic; and say what would count as failure, for instance if the third stance turns out to be the objective attitude with a sentimental residue attached. No bibliography or citations; I will add those. Finally, tell me honestly whether the question is too tied to one clinical picture to be of general interest, and if so how to detach it without losing what makes it hard.
