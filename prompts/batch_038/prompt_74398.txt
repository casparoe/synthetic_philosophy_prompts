ok so this is for a paper in my gov class but it's also kinda personal — our city council passed an ordinance about short-term rentals and the state legislature just wiped it out with a preemption law a few months later. Half my class was like "well the state is bigger so obviously it should decide" and the other half was like "local control!!" and nobody could actually say why beyond vibes. So before I write anything I want the spec, not the answers.

Give me the list of requirements that any principle for deciding which level of government gets to decide a given thing has to meet to count as adequate — adequate to actually settle a fight like ours, not just to look principled in an essay. For each requirement I want the case behind it: a real-ish situation where a principle that fails it hands back an obviously bad answer. Not a reading list btw, my library wouldn't have it anyway, just the reasoning.

Some things I already suspect belong on the list but want sharpened or thrown out:

- it has to be comparative — "the lower level can do it well enough" isn't a standard, you have to say better than what, measured how, and by whose lights
- it has to handle spillovers, where the costs of a local decision land on people who had no vote in it
- it has to say something about exit and people voting with their feet, and I think that means it inherits whatever conditions make sorting work at all — mobility, lots of jurisdictions, no externalities — which is a lot to inherit
- it has to not collapse when the level it's protecting is the captured one. like our city council isn't automatically the good guys, and the state legislature isn't automatically the bad guys, and a principle that always sides with the smaller unit is just a bias
- it has to deal with a local majority doing something awful to a minority inside it
- it has to cover money and not just rule-making, because a level with authority but no revenue of its own isn't really competent to decide anything
- it has to answer who decides who decides, since that question is itself a decision that has to sit at some level

Then: which of these are non-negotiable and which are really just the preferences of one camp dressed up as requirements — decentralists vs centralists, or whatever you want to call them. And whether some subset can't all be satisfied at once. My hunch is that the comparative one plus the who-decides one is a trap, like the only body competent to judge whether a local unit can handle a problem is the one above it, which sort of defeats the whole point — but I could totally be wrong and I'd rather you tell me. Also flag which single requirement knocks out the most of the popular answers, and note that a principle has to say what counts as a level in the first place, because "local" and "regional" and "national" are lines somebody drew.

End with a short run of like three standard contenders against the list — the fiscal-federalism match-the-policy-to-the-benefit thing, the EU treaty version of subsidiarity, and the most enthusiastic decentralist version you can steelman. Keep that brief, the criteria are what I actually want.

Last thing: don't just sound confident. Tell me where your list is weakest, which requirement you'd most expect a smart person to argue isn't a requirement at all, what that arguement would look like, and what would actually change your mind about which ones are non-negotiable. Where's your reasoning most likely to break?
