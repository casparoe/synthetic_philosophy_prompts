So I'm in revisions on a novel set at a transplant center, and the spine of the thing is a hospital ethics committee chair I've renamed Bea. She's based pretty heavily on a real woman I spent about a year talking to while I was researching — long lunches, terrible hospital coffee, she let me sit in on two meetings and eventually handed me a binder of old minutes. She retired in the spring and I've been carrying her around in my head ever since. My editor says the committee scenes "feel like television," and my one beta reader is an ICU nurse, so I want to get this dead right. Here's Bea's track record, roughly as I have it in my notes:

- Woman in her fifties, catastrophic bleed, on a ventilator, no reflexes of any kind, failed her apnea test. Family refused to accept it because she was warm and pink and her heart was beating. Committee declared death anyway. The minutes say something to the effect that a warm body with a beating heart on a machine isn't evidence of a living patient, it's evidence that the machine is working.
- Guy with a shattered cervical spine from a pool accident. Totally aware, reads with his eyes, will never breathe on his own again. Somebody on the surgical service asked, half-joking, whether he counted as dead since his breathing had stopped permanently. Bea was genuinely offended. She wrote that the question is never whether the machines are doing the work, it's whether there's anything left that they're doing the work for.
- Total artificial heart patient, awake, complaining about the cafeteria eggs, not a pulse you could find anywhere. A local reporter called to ask if he was technically a corpse. She said no and hung up on him.
- Donation-after-circulatory-death case. Devastating injury but he was not brain dead — still had some brainstem function, still took a few breaths of his own. Family chose compassionate extubation, heart stopped, and Bea insisted on five full minutes of nothing before anybody laid a hand on him. A surgeon wanted sixty seconds. She refused, and then made a little speech in the minutes about the difference between "cannot be brought back" and "is not going to be brought back because we've decided not to try." She said the second one is what we actually lean on in this room and we ought to have the honesty to say so out loud.
- Teenager pulled out of a frozen lake, core temp in the mid-twenties Celsius, forty-five minutes of CPR with no pulse, pupils fixed, EEG flat as a pancake. She blocked the declaration. Nobody knows what's reversible in a cold body, she said. He walked out three weeks later.
- Older woman, four years past the point of recognizing anyone. Breathes on her own, sleeps and wakes, startles at a loud noise, and her daughters swear she sometimes squeezes back when you take her hand. A member wanted to rule her dead already, on the grounds that the person had left. Bea cut him off — the committee's job is to apply the standard, not to relitigate what the standard should be — and they answered the feeding-tube question on its own terms. But she left the phrase "the person had left" in the minutes. She could have struck it. She didn't.

Now, chapter 22. Nineteen-year-old, tear in the artery that runs up the front of the brainstem after a crash. The brainstem is finished: angiography shows no flow through it, no pupils, no corneals, no gag, nothing on cold calorics, positive apnea test, she's on the vent. But the carotids are wide open. The EEG isn't flat — organized background, some reactivity, an actual day-night rhythm. And the rest of her is doing things. Her hypothalamus is still managing her sodium and her water, she's making some of her own hormones, she's warm without a warming blanket, she's put on eleven pounds, she caught a pneumonia last month and beat it, her surgical sites healed clean. One neurologist tells the parents their daughter died on Tuesday. Another tells them she's alive and could stay that way for years if they keep going. Both of them have privileges. The parents want a ruling from the committee, and in my draft Bea has to give one.

So: which way does she rule, and how confident are you? Which of her earlier cases is the closest analog, and where does that analogy start to break down? What's the actual criterion she's been running this whole time — give it whatever name you want, then tell me which other criteria fit every single ruling above just as well and what each of those would make her do here. If her record genuinely doesn't decide it, I want to know what she'd be committing herself to by ruling either way, and what she'd write in the minutes to protect herself. And what's the one further case, the one I should have asked her about over lunch, that would have settled it beyond argument? Lastly, please tell me if the medicine in the setup is incoherent — if my ICU nurse is going to roll her eyes at some part of this, I'd rather hear it now than after it's typeset.
