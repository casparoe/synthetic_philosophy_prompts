ok so I'm putting together a reading-group session on Pascal's mugging and I want to make everyone argue a position that I think is actually right but that basically nobody states out loud. Here's the setup, and I'd like you to write the argument as if you were fully convinced by it.

The standard case: some entity shows up and says it'll torture or save some astronomically large number of people unless you hand over five dollars. Everyone's diagnosis is that the trouble comes from the tiny probability. You can't have a prior that small on a claim that outlandish, or utilities need to be bounded, or you should discount by description length, or testimony of that kind just isn't the sort of thing that moves a rational credence. The mugging gets treated as a puzzle about how small probabilities can be before they stop licensing big payoffs.

Now the variant I want to use. Suppose the mugger is *reliable*. Before asking for the five dollars, they run twenty small demonstrations you can check yourself — they make a hundred grains of sand vanish on request and put them back, they tell you things about your own past they had no way of knowing, they honor a series of cheap side bets at fair odds. So your credence that they can do the big thing isn't tiny, it's more like 0.6, and it's 0.6 because of accumulated evidence rather than gullibility.

The claim to argue: this version is worse, not better. Reliability doesn't rescue anything — it strips out every escape route people reach for and leaves the actual problem sitting there naked. Once the probability is perfectly respectable you can't blame the priors, can't blame unbounded utility, can't blame description length, can't say the testimony was inherently dubious. You're just left with an agent who is being rationally exploited, and who is being exploited *correctly*, by their own lights. The mugging stops being a technical puzzle about infinitesimal probabilities and turns into a demonstration that the theory licenses being a sucker, which is a much more damaging thing to have shown.

Write that argument. I want the positive case first — why reliability specifically makes things worse, spelled out rather than asserted, including what it does to each of the four standard fixes. Then handle the three or four replies someone in the room will definitely make: "fine, they should just pay, that's the correct answer, so where's the problem?"; "the twenty demonstrations support a high credence in the *mechanism*, not in the *intentions*, so the tiny probability sneaks back in through the side door"; and "if each of the twenty demonstrations already had the mugging structure then your case is question-begging, you've just stacked twenty muggings." End with what you think actually has to give if the argument goes through — name the assumption in the setup you'd attack and explain why none of the usual fixes can reach it.

Dont bother with references or naming papers or people, I'll hand out a reading list separately. Just the reasoning. Somewhere around 900–1200 words.
