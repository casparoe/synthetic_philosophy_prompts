our team keeps explaining process supervision with the homework analogy: an outcome-supervised model is like grading a math test on the final answer alone, so you end up rewarding kids who memorized the anwser key or got lucky, whereas a process-supervised model is like a teacher who reads the worked solution line by line and therefore can't be gamed the same way. it's doing real work in an internal argument — the conclusion being that we should move to step-level rewards and stop treating reward hacking as a standing threat.

can you audit the thing? i want the correspondence table first (who plays the teacher, the student, the answer key, the rubric, the fact that the teacher can check a line without solving the whole problem), then the places it breaks sorted into the ones that actually bear on that conclusion and the ones that are just decorative. two i especially want you to push on, one of which a colleague raised and i'm not sure about: (a) a student's written steps plausibly *are* the work, whereas a model's chain of thought may be a report of the work rather than the work itself; (b) in practice step labels often come from rolling the model out to a final answer and seeing whether it worked, which looks suspiciously like deriving the teacher's per-line rubric from the answer key.

does the can't-be-gamed claim survive once those are on the table, and if not, is there a better analogy that would carry the same point honestly?
