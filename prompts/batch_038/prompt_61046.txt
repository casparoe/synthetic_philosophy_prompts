You are, for this exercise, a philosopher who works both on nineteenth-century German thought and on the philosophy of history, and I would ask that you think carefully before answering rather than reaching for the first framework that comes to mind; it would help me a great deal if you opened with a few sentences of brainstorming in which you sort my cases into clusters before committing to any principle. I am a junior, and I sit on the student council of a fairly old high school in a small town, which means that this year our council has ended up arbitrating a run of arguments about what the school and the town ought to do with their own pasts. I read Nietzsche's essay on the uses and disadvantages of history for life over the summer, and I have been testing whether the three attitudes he describes there — the monumental, the antiquarian, and the critical — actually decide the cases I keep running into. They do not seem to. Please do not feel any need to quote the essay or to cite anything at all; I want your reasoning, and a careful paraphrase of him is worth far more to me than a quotation I have no way of checking.

Here are my verdicts, in the order the cases arose. The town's sesquicentennial committee proposed a pageant re-enacting the arrival of the first settlers, told entirely from the settlers' point of view; I voted against it. Our debate team keeps a binder of the arguments our strongest captains have used and reads it before every tournament, and it demonstrably helps them win; I think that practice is unambiguously good. The local historical society is spending two years digitizing every issue of the town paper from 1890 to 1940, society pages and livestock notices included, and no one is going to read them; when I was asked, I said it was worth doing even though I could not say what for, and I still hold that. When we learned that the benefactor our science building is named after made his fortune at a company that fouled the river, I supported renaming it, but I insisted that the old name remain on a plaque beside the new one, explaining why it was removed. A classmate wrote a well-sourced piece for the school paper showing that our beloved spring bonfire is no tradition at all but something invented in 1968; people were furious; I thought she was right to write it and said so publicly. That same classmate then did precisely the same thing to the town's claim that a Revolutionary War skirmish was fought on the ridge above the cemetery — and she is right about that too, there was no skirmish — but this time I wanted her to stop, and I did not want the piece published, and I have not been able to explain the difference to myself or to anyone else. When I am discouraged I read biographies of people who did hard things, and I sometimes fill in details about them that are not in the books; I would defend that habit as it stands. And the school has begun archiving every disciplinary record, anonymized and searchable, indefinitely; I said this was fine, nearly everyone else found it sinister, and I suspect they may be right without being able to say why. I have started calling the newspaper-digitizing and the disciplinary-archive cases *custodial* rather than monumental, antiquarian, or critical, on the grounds that in both the only aim seems to be that nothing get lost; I cannot tell whether that is a genuine fourth attitude or a failure of nerve on my part.

My tentative principle is roughly this: keeping a record is legitimate when the people keeping it can say what will be done with it; honoring the past is legitimate when there is something in it worth imitating; and attacking the past is legitimate when the attack lets someone get out from under it. The pageant and the bonfire fit, the binder and the biographies fit the second clause, but the digitizing project flatly contradicts the first clause and the two debunkings, which are the same act performed on the same town by the same girl, land on opposite sides of the third. So: is there a principle, or a small set of competing candidates, that captures these verdicts, stated precisely enough that I could read it aloud at a council meeting and be understood? For each candidate, tell me which of my cases motivated each of its clauses, and then tell me what it says about a case I have not considered — whether the incoming freshmen should be told the whole story of the cheating scandal from eleven years ago during orientation. Above all, be honest about which of my verdicts the principle cannot accommodate and would require me to surrender; I would rather have a simpler principle that costs me one or two verdicts than an elaborate one that saves everything, but I want to know which of the two you are recommending and why. Please keep the whole response under 700 words. Finally, tell me what would change your mind: which of my verdicts you would flip most readily, what argument or what further fact about the cases would flip you, and at which step in your own reasoning you think you are most likely to be wrong.
