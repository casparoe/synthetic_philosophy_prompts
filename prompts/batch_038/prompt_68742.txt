Our history teacher assigned us a two-sided debate on British rule in India, and I ended up preparing the version of the argument that is usually phrased as a balance sheet: on one side you set down the railways, a unified administration, English as a common language, the civil service and the rule of law; on the other you set down the collapse of Indian textile manufacturing, the famines including Bengal in 1943, revenue remitted to London, Amritsar, and Partition. The two books our teacher pointed us to as background are the well-known ones, a British historian's broadly favorable account and an Indian politician's indictment, and having read a fair amount of both I noticed something that has been bothering me for about a week.

The people on the two sides detest each other's entries but never once dispute the format. Both produce a list of credits and a list of debits, both insist their column is the larger, and both finish by announcing which side of the ledger wins; nobody suggests that placing a railway network on one line and famine deaths on another might be the wrong kind of operation altogether. Two details sharpened my suspicion. First, the standard reply from my side to the atrocity charges is that you cannot judge the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries by twenty-first-century standards, yet every credit we list — literacy, life expectancy, average income, the present-day usefulness of a rail network — is measured in exactly those twenty-first-century units, and I cannot see how the format survives giving one of those up. Second, the railway I keep seeing cited as a benefit was constructed by private British companies whose five percent annual return on capital was guaranteed out of Indian revenues, so I am not sure whose credit it is meant to be, or what settles whether a thing counts as a benefit to the people it was done to.

So I would like the ledger format itself treated as the object. Reconstruct the theory it presupposes as carefully as you can: what it must assume about the comparability of very different kinds of harm and benefit, about whose welfare is allowed into which column, about how the baseline against which improvement is measured gets chosen and about the date at which counting stops, and about whether the absence of any agreement by the governed registers as a debit, a credit, or nothing at all. Then identify the rival ways of understanding the wrong of colonial rule that the format forecloses before any evidence is examined — views on which domination is a wrong that no volume of good consequences can offset, views on which what is owed runs to identifiable descendants rather than to a net total, views on which some losses are not the sort of thing that has a magnitude. I am especially curious where you think this is underdetermined: is the balance sheet a substantive normative commitment, or merely a presentational device that a deontologist or a relational theorist could adopt just as happily without changing their view? Please address the inconsistency between the historicist defense and the present-valued metrics and say which of the two a defender of the format should abandon. Finally, if the ledger were replaced by a different structure for the same reckoning — one organized around claims and claimants, or around the character of the ruling relationship, rather than around totals — describe concretely what it would look like, what it would make visible that the ledger hides, and what it would in turn hide.

Lead with your bottom line in the very first sentence and then give the reasoning behind it, with no preamble and no restatement of the question.
