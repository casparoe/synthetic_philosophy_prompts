I TA an intro medieval philosophy section and we just got through the causality discussion in al-Ghazali's Incoherence of the Philosophers (the seventeenth one, fire meets cotton), and class went sideways on me. Half the room walked out thinking he says causation is fake and science is a cope, and one extremely confident pre-med kept saying "so he's just Hume but religious, right?" I tried to push back — the thing I keep insisting on is that he isn't denying fire has a nature that burns stuff, he's denying that the burning has to follow, because the nature is borrowed and could have been withheld — and I don't think I landed it. Afterwards I had a two hour arguement about it with my friend Nadia, who's finishing a kalām thesis, over cold shawarma at like 1am, and she thinks I'm softening him into something he'd have rejected, that for him the created thing genuinely does nothing and all the talk of natures is window dressing. We did not resolve it. I want to end the term with a poem I can read aloud in the last five minutes of section, and I want it to side with me, quietly.

So: a ghazal, because the form does exactly the work the doctrine does and I don't think the class will notice, but I will. Six or seven couplets, no enjambment between them — each couplet stands alone and owes nothing to the one before it, like one instant of creation sitting next to another with no glue in between. A radif: one short phrase repeated word for word at the end of every couplet, preceded by a rhyme word that changes, so AA BA CA DA and so on. Something in the neighborhood of "the burning isn't the fire's" or "and still it doesn't burn" is fine, you pick, it just has to be able to sit at the end of six different lines without getting tiresome, and it should be the habit itself — the thing that comes back not because the last couplet forced it but because it always comes back. Closing couplet wants a takhallus worked into the line; use something self-mocking rather than anybody's real name.

Content rules, since accuracy is the whole point. It has to be right enough that Nadia's advisor would recognize the argument and not just the incense: at least one couplet that gets the modal point (the effect doesn't follow from the thing's own nature, it follows because the custom has been kept); at least one that gives the created thing something real to do, so the poem isn't the caricature where fire is a picture and nothing happens anywhere; and one couplet where the refrain returns but the burning doesn't — the miracle without the word miracle. There's an obvious scriptural case for that one, the man thrown into the fire who walked out unscorched, and you can gesture at it, but please don't put quotation marks around any scripture or any translated passage, I don't want to be the TA who misquotes the Qur'an to fifteen undergrads. Also give the Hume kid a couplet, but not the one he wants: the issue isn't that we can't know, its that there's no necessity there to know. Keep it dry, no devotional swell, no "O Mystery," God named once at most. To the pre-meds it should read as a poem about a fire that behaves itself; to that one kid it should click as an argument. Title optional, and no gloss afterwards — I'll do the explaining.
