I am drafting a paper on gradual replacement and mind uploading, and I have assembled an argument that my coauthors insist must be unsound but no one has yet been able to locate the failure. It begins from premises I take to be close to uncontroversial and ends somewhere I cannot accept. Here it is, with each step marked.

(1) If conscious experience depends on the brain at all, it depends on it in a way that admits of no sharp cutoff: there is no smallest physical change sufficient to extinguish it, since for any candidate change a strictly smaller one can be specified that would have to make the same difference.

(2) Consider gradual replacement. Over the course of a year, each of my neurons is removed and replaced, one at a time, by a synthetic module that reproduces that neuron's input–output function and its connections exactly. I remain awake, and behaviorally unchanged, throughout.

(3) By (1), the replacement of any single neuron cannot suspend or destroy my consciousness; the difference that one substitution makes is smaller than any difference that could matter. This holds at every stage, for every one of the substitutions, since each is of the same kind.

(4) Therefore consciousness is present throughout the year and at its end, and the fully replaced system's experiences are qualitatively the same as those the biological original would have had.

(5) What secures (4) is not the biological material but the preservation of functional organization: at each moment the partially replaced system realizes essentially the same functional organization as the original, and at the end it realizes the same organization the original would have realized.

(6) Hence any system realizing my functional organization has my experiences, whatever it is made of and however it came into existence — including one assembled from a scan of me, built to specification, with no causal connection to me whatsoever.

(7) If such a scanned duplicate is constructed while I still exist, it and I are numerically distinct, though each realizes my functional organization and each is conscious. So what gradual replacement preserves, and what duplication preserves, cannot be numerical identity.

(8) What both preserve is instead whatever matters in survival: there comes to be a conscious person with my memories, character, and projects, standing to me in a relation that — as (2) through (5) show — does all the work that identity was ever supposed to do.

(9) But (8) then holds equally of the unconnected duplicate, of a duplicate that will be assembled from my specifications a thousand years from now, and of a duplicate that is merely possible, never built at all. So whatever matters in my survival obtains in situations in which I am annihilated at once and nothing succeeds me.

(10) Therefore either what matters in my survival obtains independently of whether I survive, and so does not matter, or the argument has gone wrong. I do not accept the first disjunct.

My colleagues are divided about what kind of trouble this is: some say the reasoning is valid and (10) simply forces me to abandon a premise I have been treating as obvious, while others say one of the inferences is not what it appears to be and the conclusion never follows. Please settle this. Identify the step at which the argument fails, or, if you judge it valid, the premise whose rejection is least costly; explain why that step looked unobjectionable when stated as I have stated it; and price the alternatives. In particular, tell me what I must give up if instead I reject (1) and allow a sharp cutoff, if I reject (3), if I reject (6) and deny that functional organization alone fixes experience, or if I reject the move from (7) to (8) and insist that survival requires identity after all. Note that (5) and (8) describe relations that may not be the same relation; say whether that matters.

Two further questions. A coauthor suggests that what (8) describes is nothing other than Parfit's psychological-continuity relation on its wide reading, where the continuity need not have the normal kind of cause; if that is right, what resource does Parfit have for stopping short of (9) and (10) that my argument lacks, and is it available to me? And if you conclude that the flaw is an equivocation rather than a false premise, state the two readings explicitly and show which premise is true on one and which inference is valid only on the other.

For what it is worth, I would most like to keep (2) through (4) — the claim that gradual replacement preserves consciousness is the premise I am least willing to surrender — and I am content to lose (9) and (10) entirely if the rest can be made to stand.
