We're rewriting my city's street-naming policy and the draft is a pile of rules that dont obviously go together: nobody who's still alive can get a street named after them, there's a five-year wait after death, no renaming an existing street unless the current namesake turns out to have done something awful that's newly surfaced, and the fire department effectively gets a veto on any change because dispatchers rely on the old names for 911 calls. Meanwhile we just named a bridge after a donor who is very much alive and gave us money for the library.

What theory of commemoration is this bundle actually committed to? I keep feeling like the rules point in opposite directions — the ban on living people treats a name like a final verdict on a life, while the fire dept veto treats a name like pure plumbing with no honorific content whatsoever. Is there a coherent picture underneath, or are we running two incompatible ones at once? Go rule by rule: what each one presupposes about what a commemorative name does, which rival accounts it quietly rules out, and where the practice is loose enough that several different theories would license it. And then tell me what we'd have to give up — in the rules, not just in the rationale — if we adopted the view that a street name is mainly an invitation for passersby to think about someone rather than a way of honoring them.
