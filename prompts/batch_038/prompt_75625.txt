My brother-in-law reads a lot of popular science and turns every holiday dinner into a seminar. Last year he made this whole speech about how the people who say "scientists used to think phlogiston was a real thing, so who knows what we're getting wrong right now" are missing something obvious — because science, he said, is like making a map. The old maps were crude and had sea monsters in the empty parts, but they weren't worthless, they were rougher versions of the same product, and every generation fills in coastline and adds detail. Newton's picture of the world wasn't false on this telling, just low resolution, and Einstein's is a sharper map of the very same territory. So we're not fools for trusting the science we've got, we're using the best map available and it keeps improving, and anyone who says we're getting closer to how things actually are is just describing what mapmakers do. Everyone at the table nodded, including me, and I've been uneasy about it ever since.

The reason is that I've spent fifteen years making maps for a living — I do charts and county-level GIS work, and before that I spent a while generalizing topographic sheets for a mapping outfit — and almost every step of his argument makes me want to object from inside my own job. There is no such thing as a flat map that doesn't distort. Squash a sphere onto a sheet and you have to give something up: you can keep local shapes right, or keep areas right, or keep distances right along particular lines, but never all of them at once, and which one you sacrifice depends entirely on what the map is for. Plenty of maps people rely on daily are wrong on purpose — a subway diagram barely cares about geography at all, it cares that the order of stops and the crossings come out legible. And a huge part of the craft is deciding what to leave out, because if you put every house and every fence on a small-scale sheet you get an unreadable mess. So from where I sit, "better map" is ambiguous between more faithful and more useful, and those come apart constantly.

So here's what I want: an honest audit of the map analogy for scientific progress, before I bring this up again and get steamrolled. Lay out what maps onto what — territory, scale, projection, the surveyor, the user, the sea monsters, "adding detail" — and then where the correspondence gives out, sorted into the breaks that actually matter for the argument he was making versus the ones that are just me being pedantic about cartography. Two things in particular keep nagging at me. First: I can always check my work against the ground, walk out there with a receiver and see whether the shoreline is where I drew it, and the whole idea of a map improving assumes that check is available. Can a theory do that, or does the analogy sneak in the assumption that the scientist has the same access to the territory that I have — which seems to be the exact thing under dispute when people ask whether theories really track the world. Second: adding detail to a map assumes the grid stays put, you don't re-derive the projection every time, you just add another layer. Is that how science works, or do theories sometimes get replaced in a way that's more like scrapping the projection and starting over, in which case "higher resolution" doesn't describe what happened at all. And while your at it — doesn't the same image get recruited for the opposite side? People always say the map is not the territory as a warning against mistaking your model for the world, and he's using it to say we're closing in on the world. Can one comparison carry both directions, or is somebody misusing it? Tell me whether his conclusion survives once the disanalogies are on the table, whether it has to be cut down to something more modest, and if the map picture can't do the work he wanted from it, what better image there is for theories getting nearer the truth — assuming there is one. I dont want a defense of him or of the skeptics, just how much weight the analogy can actually bear.
