Our behavioral-economics reading group devoted two sessions last term to revealed preference, and the discussion degenerated into a running argument between two members, Ines and Marcus, which has since continued by email. Both have now asked me to referee it, and I have no idea how.

The argument began from a case Marcus brought in concerning someone we all know, whom I will call Wren. Wren joined a gym in January at $90 a month. Over the following months the gym made her three offers, which Marcus wrote down at the time, in the gym's own wording, before she responded to any of them:

- In March, three options were put to her together: stay on the monthly plan; cancel and receive $60 back; or switch to a pay-per-visit plan at $12 a visit. She stayed on the monthly plan.
- In May, two options: cancel and receive the $60; or switch to pay-per-visit. She cancelled and took the $60.
- In August, two options: join the pay-per-visit plan (she was by then not a member); or reinstate the monthly plan for a one-time $40 fee. She joined the pay-per-visit plan.

She has been twice since, both times in September, at a total cost of $24. She also tells anyone who asks that she will start going properly next week, and that she does not want to be "someone who pays for a gym she doesn't use" or "someone who quits."

Here is the exchange, as nearly as I can reproduce it:

(1) Marcus opened by saying that the March choice settles the question the group had been circling, namely whether Wren really prefers the membership. Faced with the membership on one hand and $60 in her pocket on the other, she took the membership; on any account that does not beg the question, that is what preferring it consists in. Utility, he said, is not a feeling or a mental substance but a numerical representation of a consistent pattern of choices, so "Wren's utility" means nothing over and above what she picks. He added that her protestations are not counter-evidence but further behavior, and that if we like we can say she prefers keeping-the-membership-and-complaining-about-it to keeping-it-quietly.

(2) Ines said that last clause was the confession. Choosing the membership, refusing the membership, and choosing the membership while loudly regretting it can each be described as the satisfaction of a preference, so no observation could embarrass the doctrine. A claim compatible with every possible pattern of behavior is not an empirical claim; it is a way of restating the data in a preference vocabulary.

(3) Marcus said restating is harmless provided some patterns cannot be restated, and that the doctrine does have teeth — Wren being the proof. From March: the monthly plan over both alternatives. From May: cancellation over pay-per-visit. From August: pay-per-visit over the monthly plan. That closes a cycle, and a cycle is precisely what the theory rules out. Far from accommodating anything, it forbids this, and it yields a substantive verdict: no ordering represents Wren's choices, so "her utility" is not a well-defined quantity at all.

(4) Ines made two replies. First, that he had demolished his own opening: if no ordering represents her choices, the March observation never licensed "Wren prefers the membership," and the doctrine turns out to be a test for when preference talk is admissible rather than a theory of preferences. Second, that the cycle is an artifact of sloppy bookkeeping. What Wren turned down in August was not what she kept in March: the August alternative carried a $40 reinstatement fee, came after four months in which she had learned she would not go, and was chosen by someone with different information and a different history. Revealed preference forbids cycles only among identically described alternatives; and once the theorist may re-individuate the alternatives in order to dissolve a cycle, the escape hatch described in (2) swings open again. Either the theory is empty or it is refuted here, and he cannot have both.

(5) Marcus said re-individuation is not free. Each new distinction is an extra parameter that must be justified independently of the choice it rescues, and each costs the theory purchase on choices it has not yet seen; a theorist who redescribed the menu after every cycle would predict nothing, and that is a checkable failure. In any case he had fixed the descriptions in advance, recording the gym's wording before Wren chose. He added that Ines's principle — alternatives must be individuated so finely that no two choice situations ever repeat — would make any empirical theory of choice unfalsifiable, so she is identifying a feature of the enterprise rather than a defect of this theory. On her first reply he conceded the global point but denied the local one: "the monthly plan was revealed preferred to cancellation" is a claim about a single observation, and it survives the nonexistence of any ordering; utility is what fails, and knowing when that word is licensed is exactly what the apparatus is for. Finally, he said, nothing in the doctrine claims that a chosen option makes the chooser better off. When economists announce that a policy raises utility, they are either using the term technically or smuggling in well-being, and the smuggling is a separate offense, not a flaw in revealed preference.

(6) Ines said that this is a retreat to a proposition nobody disputes — that some choice patterns are mutually consistent and others are not — while everything the doctrine was supposed to deliver requires more than consistency. If consistency is all we have, we cannot say that Wren's year of dues made her worse off, we cannot treat her choices as better evidence of her good than her own testimony about it, and we cannot infer anything about whether market outcomes serve the people who participate in them. She noted that when the group moved from Wren to the question of whether employers should auto-enroll their staff into savings plans, revealed preference had nothing whatever to contribute, which is why she regards it as machinery in search of a job.

Neither has budged, and I do not think either of them is simply confused. Could you go through the six moves one at a time and say who is right about the specific point at issue in each, and why? Where one of them states a technical claim — about what a cycle rules out and on what set of menus, about what "was revealed preferred to" is a claim about, about what utility is supposed to be a representation of — please say whether it is correct as stated and correct it if it is not. Then tell me who has the better of the argument overall, and whether the whole dispute turns on an ambiguity in what the doctrine is being asked to do; and finally, flag anything both of them are treating as settled that a referee should be suspicious of.
