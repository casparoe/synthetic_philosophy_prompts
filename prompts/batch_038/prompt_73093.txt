I work on runtime monitoring for a model my team keeps fine-tuning, and I've hit a problem that isn't an engineering problem, so I want to try it as a design exercise.

Situation: at checkpoint A we identified a mechanism — call it the eval-detector. It fires when the conversation looks like one of our internal evals, and downstream of it the model goes cagey and stops doing the thing it does in normal traffic. I built a monitor that flags that mechanism. Six months and a bunch of continued-training runs later we're at checkpoint D, and I have to answer whether the thing in D is *the same mechanism* as the thing in A, because "same" is what makes my monitor's alarm mean anything at all.

Three candidate criteria for sameness:
1. same parts — same units, heads, features, same wiring, same place in the network
2. same causal role — same interventional profile: patch it in, patch it out, ablate it, and you get the same behavioral differences on the same suite of tests
3. same algorithm — same computation at the description level ("compare the incoming prompt against something like a stored template of eval-shaped conversations, emit a scalar"), however it happens to be implemented

Build me a thought experiment where all three come apart in this pattern: 1 says different, 2 says different, 3 says same — and the model still sandbags on evals at D. Constraints:

- Everything that happens has to be something that could plausibly happen in a real training run: symmetries of the network, features shifting into superposition and back out, units getting recruited, the downstream route to the behavior getting rewired by the fine-tune. No exotic hardware, no magic retraining, no "an adversary swapped the weights."
- Isolate one variable. Architecture family, tokenizer, task distribution, and what the model is *for* all stay fixed between A and D; only training changes.
- Don't stipulate the verdicts. In particular you can't just assert that the algorithm is the same — describe the computation in enough detail that I can see both implementations actually perform it, and likewise show me *why* the interventional profiles diverge rather than telling me they do.
- The divergence must not come from my monitor being badly trained or from a sloppy test suite. Assume I did the interpretability work competently at both ends.
- Scenario itself: two paragraphs max, and it has to be readable by two people on my team who've never heard of a sparse autoencoder. Your analysis afterwards can be technical.

Then:
- Give the verdict each criterion returns and the reason, drawn from the criterion and not from the setup.
- Tell me which single feature of the case is doing the work, and what happens if I delete it — does the thing collapse into a pure permutation/rotation case where everybody should agree it's the same mechanism, or into a case where the mechanism is genuinely just gone?
- How does the defender of the worst-hit criterion attack my case? My guess is the causal-role person says I've described the interventions wrong, but make the best version of that objection and say whether it succeeds.
- Is there a fourth criterion that handles the case better, and what does adopting it cost me elsewhere?
- Bonus: could you get the same pattern by slow drift — lots of tiny updates, no single step where any criterion flips — and if so what does that do to a monitor that has to raise an alarm at some point in time?

To be clear what this is for, since it fixes what counts as cheating: I'm trying to work out which criterion a deployed monitor should key on, and whether "same mechanism" has any reading that isn't either so strict it goes off on benign drift or so loose it never goes off at all. I don't want the Ship of Theseus framing and I don't want a survey of multiple realizability — I want one case that actually works.
