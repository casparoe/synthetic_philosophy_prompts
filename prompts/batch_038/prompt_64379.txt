ok so I've been chewing on the Buridan's ass setup — the ass between two identical haystacks, no reason to prefer either, dies of hunger — and rather than argue about whether it's even a real puzzle I want to stress-test the standard ways out against a case I cooked up. The ways out, phrased as claims about agents rather than donkeys:

1. **Ties don't happen.** Any two options differ in some respect — location, order of presentation, a stray thought — so a genuinely incomplete ordering is an impossible state, and the puzzle describes nothing.
2. **Randomize.** The agent lets a coin flip, or noise in their own motor system, settle it, and that's fully rational: it breaks the symmetry at zero cost and no theory should demand more.
3. **Second-order preference.** The agent can't rank the haystacks but can rank tie-breaking procedures, so the ordering is complete one level up and the tie dissolves.
4. **Choice for no reason.** Taking either is permissible; the choosing makes it the one you had most reason to take, and rationality never required that a choice be traceable to a prior discriminating reason.
5. **Explainability is required.** Rational choice has to be grounded in something favoring one option, so the ass really does starve — which is a reductio on the idea that all action needs such a grounding, not a problem internal to decision theory.
6. **Latent ranking.** Reports of indifference are a sloppy description of the agent's state; under pressure something surfaces, and anyone said to be facing a true tie hasn't been described accurately.

Here's my case. You're in a room with two sealed envelopes, identical in every observable way, and you have sixty seconds. Open one and you keep what's inside; open neither and you lose everything you own, so freezing is the only catastrophic outcome. You know from how the room was set up that exactly one envelope holds \$1,000 and the other holds \$1, and that the assignment was made by a process that's provably opaque: no amount of thinking, in any amount of time, by anyone in your epistemic position, will move your credence about which is which off one half. You also know the pair gets refilled by the same opaque process every day for the next year, same deal, and you're told afterwards which one you got — so you can keep records and form habits, it just cant help. Finally, for \$5 there's a machine on the wall that will select an envelope for you by a physically fair coin.

Put 1–6 into tiers by how well they handle this, with a sentence or two on why each lands where it does. I want to know which the case actually knocks out versus merely embarrasses, and why the difference. Also: which of the moves commit you to paying the \$5, and does the case's bite come mostly from indifference or from ignorance — give me a number between 0 and 1 for how much of it is still a Buridan problem at all. And last, for a bounded agent under the sixty-second clock rather than an idealized one, which single move would you bet on?
