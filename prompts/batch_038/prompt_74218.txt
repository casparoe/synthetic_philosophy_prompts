A friend and I spent most of a Saturday on the reductionist/anti-reductionist debate about testimony and ended it exactly where we started, which I found striking given that we agree on nearly everything else in epistemology. I want a diagnosis of the shape of that impasse rather than a verdict on who is right, so let me first set out the positions as we had them, in enough detail that your account has something to bite on.

The reductionist says that a hearer's belief formed on someone's word is justified only insofar as the hearer has positive reasons for taking this speaker, on this topic, on this occasion, to be likely to be right, and that those reasons must be drawn from something other than testimony itself — perception, memory, inductive track record. The anti-reductionist says that a speaker's word carries a defeasible entitlement with it, so that the absence of reasons for doubt is already enough, and that the onus falls on anyone who would withhold belief. Both, as far as my friend and I could tell, accept the same conditionals: if there are positive reasons, the belief is fine; if there are positive defeaters, it isn't. Where they part company is the region in which there are neither — and, as far as we could see, neither of them can produce a real, inhabited example of a case in that region. The reductionist's paradigms are the stranger's directions, the anonymous online claim, the unfamiliar specialist giving advice outside his field; the anti-reductionist's paradigms are a colleague's report of a meeting you missed, a friend telling you the time, a child acquiring a first language from parents whose reliability the child has had no prior opportunity to audit. Each side, when handed the other's paradigm, either insists it belongs in the populated region after all — the child does accumulate track-record evidence with remarkable speed, the stranger's directions are already embedded in a mass of corroborating background — or concedes the case and says it is peripheral. Nobody ever loses ground.

My working hypothesis, which I would like you to stress-test rather than accept, is that the dispute is therefore about the default: it is a disagreement over who bears the burden of proof, and burden-allocation is not the sort of thing that thought experiments can decide, because the very function of a default is to settle what happens in the region where the evidence runs out. On that reading the parameter each side is fighting over is never exercised in practice, which is why the debate has generated so much literature and so little movement. I want you to tell me whether that is right, or whether one of the following is a better account, and argue for your choice rather than listing them:

- that it is a verbal dispute, either about what "justification" means when a deontological conception and a truth-conducive conception are both in play, or about whether testimony is a source in the sense in which perception is a source, as against a transmission mechanism;
- that the two sides are answering different questions under one heading — one about the internal economy of an individual believer's reasons, one about the norms governing the practice of telling and the division of epistemic labour that practice presupposes;
- that the old debate has been quietly displaced rather than resolved, with its live parts migrating into work on epistemic entitlement in the wake of Burge, into the epistemology of trust and testimonial injustice, and into formal work on how an agent should update on the reports of others, leaving only a rump under the original name;
- that there is a hidden empirical question doing the work — about how hearers actually form, monitor and revise belief in others' reports — which developmental and cross-cultural research on children's trust and scepticism could in principle answer; or that such research is beside the point, because the question at issue is normative and no amount of descriptive psychology bears on it.

Two further things I want addressed squarely. First, my friend pressed a variant of my own hypothesis that I could not answer: that the choice of default is not a descriptive matter at all but a decision about which error is costlier — the gullibility of believing too much against the paralysis of auditing everything — and that once it is put that way, the debate is revealed as an argument about risk attitudes in belief-formation rather than about evidence. Tell me whether that makes the dispute merely pragmatic, and whether a pragmatic resolution counts as a resolution of the epistemological question the two sides took themselves to be asking. Second, on whatever diagnosis you end up giving, state what would count as progress: a distinction that would let both sides agree on a formulation whose truth they then genuinely disagree about, a kind of case that would discriminate, a formal result, a change of question, or nothing at all, with the reason why nothing should be expected. If you think the dispute is verbal or default-relative, do not stop at the label — say precisely which formulation of each side's thesis would come apart from which, and what would be left standing once the formulation was fixed.

I am after argument throughout, not a tour of the literature, and I would rather have a defensible diagnosis stated sharply than an even-handed catalogue of the candidates I have listed.
