I am setting a case-analysis exercise for a seminar on political aesthetics, and I would like you to write the model answer.

The theory is Rousseau's *Letter to d'Alembert on the Theater* (1758), written in reply to d'Alembert's article on Geneva in the *Encyclopédie*, which had suggested that the city might benefit from a playhouse. The episode is the six months from March to August 2020, when the world's theaters went dark and two very different substitutes appeared at once. Please take the following as given rather than reconstructing it: commercial and subsidized playhouses in London and New York closed in mid-March 2020; the National Theatre and other companies began streaming filmed recordings of past productions free of charge online; in early July 2020 a professionally filmed stage recording of *Hamilton* was released directly to a subscription streaming service some fifteen months ahead of its planned cinema release. Over the same weeks, beginning in Spain and Italy and then in Britain and elsewhere, households performed a scheduled collective gesture — applause at a fixed evening hour, singing from balconies and windows — addressed to health, transport, and delivery workers. The ritual was widely embraced, and within weeks widely attacked as a cheap substitute for protective equipment and, later, for pay.

I want five things. First, a disciplined mapping: in each of the three cases, who is the audience, who performs, what plays the role of the playhouse, and what, if anything, plays the role of the festival? Second, run Rousseau's actual arguments through the material — that drama cannot reform a people's morals and only intensifies what they already are; that the playhouse assembles atomized strangers into a purchased simulacrum of community, at the cost of time a working republic cannot spare; that the actor's trade is a public alienation of the self for money; and that the alternative is an open-air festival at which a people becomes its own spectacle. Say which of these do work on the 2020 material and which have no grip. Third, apply a distinction I am stipulating for the exercise: *spectatorial density* (how many people watch together in one place) against *spectatorial reciprocity* (whether the watchers are themselves visible and available to one another as spectacle). Locate the playhouse, the livestream, and the balcony applause on that grid; then say whether the distinction earns its keep or collapses under pressure, and whether adopting it changes Rousseau's verdicts. Fourth, be candid about where the framework is silent rather than forcing it to speak: Rousseau has no concept of asynchronous reproduction, of the camera as director, of an audience that is not a city, or of a festival that is itself organized and amplified by broadcasters and then attended by officeholders. Note too his own awkwardness — he wrote plays and an opera. Fifth, deliver a verdict in both directions. Does the balcony applause vindicate the festival ideal, or show that a festival is merely theater without a roof? Does *Hamilton* on streaming refute the access-and-scale argument (more people saw it in a month than could have filled the house in a decade) or confirm the passivity charge?

Length: 1,400 to 1,800 words, with section headings. No bibliography, no reading list, no citations. Paraphrase Rousseau rather than quoting him; where you are unsure of a formulation or of a detail of the 2020 events beyond what I have supplied, say so explicitly instead of supplying a confident invention.
