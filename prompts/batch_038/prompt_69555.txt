Some course context you will need, because the exam relies on apparatus that is ours rather than the literature's. Over three weeks we covered the claim that a competent person's advance directive can bind others after the person loses competence, and the family of objections that start from the condition of the patient who is now demented. In lecture we introduced a distinction that does not appear in the readings under these names, and students were told to use it on the exam. The *scope* of a directive is the question of which future conditions it speaks to, and whether the condition the patient is in falls under them. The *force* of a directive is the question of how much weight it carries against the interests of the person who now exists, once its scope is satisfied. Students were told explicitly that the two can come apart: a directive can plainly apply and still be weak, and a directive can be very weighty and yet not apply at all.

**Question as printed:**

> 2. (25 points) Alice wrote a directive at 62. It stated that if she reached a condition of advanced dementia in which she no longer recognized her children and could no longer follow a conversation, she was not to be given antibiotics for intercurrent infections, and was to be allowed to die of whatever came along. She gave no reason in the document beyond one sentence: that such a life would not be hers. Alice is now 79 and in exactly the condition she described. She is placid, hums along to the radio, enjoys being fed sweets, and does not resist care. She has developed pneumonia. Her daughter asks that the directive be honored. The attending physician proposes to treat, on the ground that the woman in the bed is not suffering, would not protest the injection, and has something to lose, namely the comfortable life she is now leading.
>
> (a) (6 points) Explain how Dworkin's distinction between experiential and critical interests is meant to resolve the dispute between the daughter and the physician. Say what each side is appealing to, in those terms.
>
> (b) (7 points) State the strongest objection that starts from Alice's present condition. Do not merely restate the physician's remark: explain what the objection has to establish in order to defeat the directive.
>
> (c) (12 points) Using the scope/force distinction as defined in lecture, say whether the objection you gave in (b), at its strongest, is an objection to the scope of Alice's directive or to its force, and argue for your classification. A bare classification earns nothing; the points are in the argument.

**Student's answer:**

> (a) Dworkin separates interests into two kinds. Experiential interests are interests in how moments feel: pleasure, the taste of something, music, the absence of pain. Critical interests are not about how moments feel but about the shape and value of a life taken as a whole; Dworkin talks about them in terms of narrative integrity, what makes a life a good story rather than a run of agreeable episodes. The physician is speaking the language of experiential interests: Alice now is comfortable, and treating the pneumonia buys her more comfortable days. The daughter is appealing to critical interests, and Dworkin's claim is that these are the deeper ones, so the person who was capable of framing her life as a whole speaks for the whole of that life, including its ending. It is worth adding that Dworkin's critical interests are basically what Frankfurt means by second-order volitions: reflective endorsement of a desire rather than the desire itself. Both are pointing at the same structural feature, that a person can stand outside her own motivations and evaluate them, and the directive is the written record of that evaluation.
>
> (b) The objection is not that Alice would mind the injection. It is that the being whose life is at stake no longer has critical interests at all. If critical interests require the capacity to conceive of one's life as a whole, and present Alice has lost that capacity, then nothing remains in her but experiential interests, and these are satisfied by continuing and frustrated by letting the pneumonia proceed. So the objection has to establish two things: first, that critical interests are capacity-dependent in this way, extinguished rather than merely unfelt; second, that the interests of the person now are the ones with standing to determine treatment, since it is she who will die. If both hold, honoring the directive sacrifices a real present interest to a defunct one, and the daughter's appeal is loyalty to someone who is gone. Note also that the usual way of blocking this — saying that demented Alice is literally a different person from the Alice who wrote the directive, so the directive never reaches her — is not available to Dworkin, since his whole point is that the earlier self's critical interests survive into the later condition and go on making claims. Others in the literature do run the identity argument, and it is tempting here.
>
> (c) The objection is an objection to scope. Scope asks which conditions a directive speaks to, and the objection says that the condition Alice is in now is not the condition she wrote about, because she wrote about herself and there is no longer a self of that kind to be written about. If the addressee of the directive has ceased to exist, the directive has nothing to apply to, and that is a failure of reach rather than of weight. The physician's point about the comfortable life she would lose looks like a question of force, but on reflection it is scope too, since it depends on there being someone whose interests count. So the objection in (b) undermines the directive's applicability.
>
> That said, the more interesting question is how much weight the directive would carry even if it did apply, and here the answer is: less than Dworkin assumes. The sentence Alice wrote — that such a life would not be hers — is doing a great deal of work, and it is not clear that it is a claim about her life as a whole rather than an expression of revulsion. The two are not the same and should not carry the same authority. A directive whose content is revulsion dressed as narrative judgment has force, but only as evidence of what the earlier person feared, not as an instruction about what the later person should be made to endure.
