I write about science and ideas for a general-interest outlet, and I am drafting a long feature on coordination: why some groups manage to act as a single agent while others are merely collections of people moving in compatible directions. A science writer whose work I respect has a forthcoming trade book with a chapter on this subject, and he sent me the key passage so that I could represent it fairly. It reads as follows:

'What separates a genuine joint action from two people who happen to be doing compatible things is a shared intention, and a shared intention is nothing over and above each participant's own intention, with the right meshing. On the dominant account in philosophy, for us to intend to paint the house together is for me to intend that we paint it and for you to intend that we paint it, plus each of us knowing that the other does, and each knowing that the other knows. It is that chain of mutual knowledge that does the work: it converts two private mental states into a single public plan. This is why philosophers can talk about intentions held jointly without invoking a group mind — the group's intention is fully analyzable into the intentions of its members, with nothing left over. Developmental psychology has confirmed the picture from the other end. Experiments with chimpanzees show that our closest relatives can coordinate, and can even recruit a partner to help with a task they cannot manage alone, but they cannot form shared goals of the human kind; that deficit, rather than raw intelligence, is why our collaboration scales up into institutions and theirs does not. The upshot is practical. When a team underperforms, the failure is almost always a failure of mutual knowledge: somebody did not know what the others were committed to doing. Fix the knowledge and you fix the agency.'

The chapter's endnotes cite two philosophers by name, Bratman and Searle, but I am not in a position to tell whether either would recognize his own view in this paragraph, and I do not want to print a confident synthesis that specialists regard as a mash-up of rival positions. I should add that I am inclined to think the mutual-knowledge story is broadly right, if only because it matches what I have seen in newsrooms: the disasters I have reported on almost always involve somebody who did not know what the others thought had been agreed.

Could you audit the passage sentence by sentence? Specifically: which claims would a philosopher working on joint action sign off on as it stands; which are defensible as a report of one position but are presented as though the field had settled on it; which conflate claims that the leading accounts take care to keep apart; and which are simply wrong. I would particularly like to know whether the middle sentences get the mechanism right. The author says first that meshing is what matters and then explains everything by mutual knowledge, and I cannot tell whether that is a slip in emphasis or a substantive mistake about the theory — and, if the latter, what the theory actually says does the work.

Please also give me your judgment on the comparative-psychology sentence, keeping it separate from the philosophy: is the chimpanzee claim a settled experimental result or one influential interpretation of contested data, and does the leap from that claim to an explanation of institutions follow, or does it skip a mechanism?

Finally, the last two sentences are what I most want to use, because they are the sort of claim readers can act on. Do they follow from anything true in the passage? If they do not, is there a better-supported version of the practical claim that a philosopher of agency or a researcher on team reasoning would stand behind, and what would that version predict about the cases that interest me most — teams in which everyone has accurate and mutually known information about what the others are committed to, and which nevertheless fail to act together? If the honest answer is that this body of philosophy licenses no management advice at all, please say so plainly; I would rather cut the paragraph than publish something a specialist would wince at.
