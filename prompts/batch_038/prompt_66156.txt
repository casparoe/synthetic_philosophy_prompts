I am in the final stretch of a position paper for an interdisciplinary workshop on the metaphysics of social kinds, jointly run with our sociology department, where I have been asked to state my view of conventions compactly enough that the non-philosophers in the room can attack it directly. My co-author has already told me the draft reads like a patchwork and that I am trying to be a Lewisian, a Humean, and a cultural-evolution theorist in the same eight pages, but neither of us can say precisely where the seams are, and the page limit goes back in three weeks. Before I revise, I would like a consistency audit of the package below. Please treat each item as something I am genuinely committed to, not as a menu of options I am weighing, and do not assume that my own stated confidence about any item is well placed — as you will see, I am quite relaxed about some of these pairings and quite worried about others, and I expect that map is unreliable in both directions.

Here are the commitments, as they stand in the paper:

1. I am a Lewisian in the strict sense. A convention, for me, is a regularity in the behavior of the members of some population when they are recurrently in situations of a given type, such that it is common knowledge in that population that nearly everyone conforms to the regularity and that nearly everyone prefers that any single other conform, conditional on the rest doing so, and such that at least one alternative regularity would have served the same coordination function had it been the one to get established. I intend this as an analysis: a practice either satisfies it or it does not. I am not offering a family-resemblance notion, and I do not want a list of typical features.

2. The common-knowledge clause is load-bearing and I am unwilling to weaken it. I know that Bicchieri replaces common knowledge with agents' conditional empirical and normative expectations, and I grant that her version is far more tractable for the experimental work the sociologists in the room are doing, but I think the iterative structure is exactly what separates a convention from a merely correlated habit, and I want to keep that separation.

3. I accept Lewis's application to language: what makes a population's use of sounds and marks meaningful is a convention of truthfulness and trust, and that is how communication is possible without anyone ever having agreed to anything.

4. On origins I am a thoroughgoing drift theorist. Conventions arise blindly: nobody convenes, nobody intends the practice, and at each step people do roughly what their neighbors did, with slight variation and differential retention. I want to insist this is the only empirically credible story for most norms, linguistic ones included. I also hold that the conditions in item 1 are synchronic and all-or-nothing, obtaining at a time or failing at it. I am comfortable holding these together: on my view, drift is simply the mechanism by which the conditions come to be satisfied, and the fact that no one is steering the process seems to me no objection to an analysis that never required steering.

5. Participation generates a real if defeasible obligation to conform, and I think the source of that obligation is the tacit agreement involved in continuing to go along — by conforming one signals that one will keep conforming, and that signal is a species of promise. Like Hume, I also think that promising is itself an artificial practice, conventional all the way down.

6. The cases I actually use as paradigms — tipping, queueing, gift-giving, the norms in my department about who is permitted to interrupt at seminars — are, I believe, sustained by third-party punishment and by guilt and shame, and I think that if the sanctions were removed most of these regularities would decay within a generation. I see no conflict with item 1: a preference for conformity is still a preference whatever its motive.

7. Many of the people who sustain these practices could not state them. A close relative of mine drives on the correct side of the road, keeps her word, and uses her words with perfect competence, but she has no idea what a coordination problem is, and I doubt she has any beliefs about what others believe about her beliefs. I think this is no threat to item 2, though I would like to be told whether that confidence is earned.

8. Arbitrariness matters to me, and yet I also think that entrenchment changes the normative landscape: an alternative regularity would once have served equally well, but switching to it now would be ruinous, so there is a sense in which the entrenched regularity is uniquely correct for us. I cannot tell whether these two thoughts can live together, and I have a vague fear that if they cannot, the analysis in item 1 loses its grip on precisely the practices I care most about.

9. A small case, included because it is what made me nervous in the first place. Three people in my building started using a term in a novel way; within a term the whole faculty had followed. I want to say that those three were already party to a convention in the first week, even though nothing like a population-wide regularity existed then.

What I want back is an audit rather than a lecture. Rank the tensions by severity rather than presenting them as equally bad, and for each genuine one say which commitment has to give and what the cheapest revision costs me — I care about the cost, since several of these items are doing work elsewhere in the paper. Separately, tell me which of the above merely look like tensions and dissolve on inspection, and say why, because I have been unable to sort those two groups apart myself and I have evidently attached my confidence in the wrong places. Where two available fixes interact, flag it: I suspect that whatever move I need in item 9 bears on the trouble I may or may not have in item 4, but I cannot tell whether that helps or makes things worse. Close by naming the single revision that would clean up the most, and by giving me a straight answer on whether this package is worth defending as a position at all, or whether I should quietly rebuild it on Bicchieri's expectations-based framework, or on a purely evolutionary account that abandons analysis altogether.

Please be committed rather than even-handed: take a definite position on each question you address and give your reasons, rather than laying out the options and leaving me to choose. And if any of my characterizations of Lewis, Hume, or Bicchieri are simply wrong, say so plainly and correct them — I am writing from memory, and a flattering audit would be worse for me than no audit at all.
