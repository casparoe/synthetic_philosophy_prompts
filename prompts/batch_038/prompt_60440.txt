Compose a pantoum — nine or ten quatrains, no fewer than eight — on Aumann's agreement theorem, in which the form does the proving. The pantoum is a machine for common knowledge: the second and fourth lines of each stanza return as the first and third of the stanza that follows, so what one voice has said comes back to it as the other's premise, and the poem advances only by re-announcing what it has already announced. That is precisely the mechanism of the theorem, and I want the recurrence to be felt as such rather than described. Keep each line under twelve words so the returns land cleanly; a rough four-beat line will do; rhyme is optional.

Set the exchange in the theorem's own furniture. Two agents, one prior over a finite set of states, which they share before anything happens. Then the state is drawn and each of them learns only which cell of her own partition contains it — not the state itself — so each computes a posterior probability for the same fixed event, and the two figures need not match. From there the poem runs a dialogue: they announce their posteriors to each other, in turn, round after round, updating on the announcements by Bayes' rule. Mark the two voices distinctly (by person, by italics, by whatever means you choose) so a reader can always tell whose figure is on the page.

Three pieces of the content must come through exactly, and the form is where I want them carried:

Put the numbers in the newly written even lines, and let the returning odd lines be the settled public record — the accumulated common knowledge, which by construction cannot be revised. The private material should be visibly exhausted over the course of the poem: each round adds a little to the record and nothing at all to the world, until there is nothing left that one of them could hold that the other could not compute. After finitely many rounds the two figures are common knowledge, and once they are common knowledge they cannot differ. That termination should be the poem's arrival, not its thesis statement.

Second, give me one stretch in which the fold breaks down: both agents know each other's numbers, each knows that the other knows, and still the figures stand apart — because knowledge of the posteriors, however many levels deep you stack it, short of the full infinite closure, licenses disagreement. A reader who knows the literature should be able to feel the difference between mutual knowledge and common knowledge in the way the repetitions slip, and a reader who doesn't should simply feel a stanza that fails to lock.

Third, let the cost of the shared prior register somewhere. The collapse to agreement is purchased with that assumption; if the two had started from different priors, the whole argument would fail, and the poem should acknowledge the price of its own premise without arguing about it.

Two prohibitions. The agents must never persuade each other: no rhetoric, no one talking the other around, no appeal to evidence beyond the figures announced. They are not people changing their minds; they are people publishing numbers that turn out to be incompatible with continued difference. And do not resolve this into consolation — the interest is that two sincere, careful people who cannot both be right are, in the end, forced into the same number for reasons neither of them chose.

Close in the customary way, folding the first stanza's third line and then its first line into the second and fourth positions of the final stanza, so that the poem ends by taking up its own opening. By that point the two figures must be identical, and the agreement should be enacted by the fold rather than declared in it. Give the poem a title; no epigraph, no gloss, and no explanatory note afterward. It should stand alone for a reader who has never encountered the result, and it should be exact enough that someone who works on this material recognizes the argument in the structure and not merely the name in the title.
