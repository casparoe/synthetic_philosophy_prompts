So, a bit of an odd request. I'm a philosophy professor — mostly action theory and responsibility, with a legal-philosophy seminar I've run for years — but I did four years as a litigator before I ever went back for the PhD, and honestly tort doctrine is still the vocabulary I think in: duty, breach, causation in fact, proximate cause, intervening versus superseding cause, eggshell plaintiff, strict liability as against negligence, conditions precedent, frustration of purpose, the whole apparatus. I can build one of those fact patterns in my sleep.

I've just had to pick the metaphysics of dispositions back up for a grad seminar and I've read through the fink/mask literature — Martin's electro-fink on the wire, Lewis's reformed analysis with the intrinsic causal basis, the masking cases (Johnston's sorcerer, the well-packed glass), Bird's antidotes, the reverse-fink business, and the various attempts to say masking is really just finking in disguise. I follow it. But my gut keeps telling me this is a rerun of problems the law already legislated its way through, badly or otherwise.

Here's what I want: restate the fink/mask problem and the main fixes on offer in the vocabulary I actually use. Take the standard cases one at a time — the wire that would conduct if touched but goes dead when touched; the fragile glass whose packing takes the blow so it doesn't shatter; the sorcerer who keeps the glass fragile and simply makes sure the breaking never happens; the poison neutralised by an antidote; a reverse fink, where the thing becomes disposed precisely because its stimulated. For each, tell me which tort notion it lines up with, how tight the fit is, and exactly where you had to bend doctrine to get it to line up.

Then the part I actually care about, the residue report. Three things. First, what has no counterpart at all in the legal vocabulary — I'm guessing the question of whether the disposition is still there once the manifestation is blocked, and also the categorical-basis stuff and the whole idea that a property is intrinsic to its bearer — and I want to know why the law never needed those notions and whether that's a sign of sophistication or of a blind spot. Second, be scrupulous about where you're not translating but importing: proximate cause is a policy instrument, not a piece of metaphysics, and it would be very easy to smuggle in the law's rules of attribution and hand them back to me as an analysis of dispositions. Flag every place that happens, or where you feel the pull. Third, given whatever's left over, tell me whether the residue shows my framework is genuinely missing a category, or whether it shows the metaphysical debate is less substantive than it looks — i.e. whether "still disposed, just masked" is doing the same work that "liable notwithstanding the intervening cause" does, and the whole argument is really about how to allocate a label.

One more thing I'd like you to test rather than accept: I'm not convinced the fink/mask distinction even survives translation. In tort terms both look like something coming from outside and defeating the manifestation, and the difference between the property being removed and the property being blocked may be exactly the thing legal causation refuses to track. If you agree, build that in; if you think I'm wrong, tell me where.

No bibliography, no references, and don't quote the papers at me — I've read them. Analysis only. And if the mapping breaks down somewhere, say so plainly and say where; a failed translation with a precise diagnosis is worth more to me than a smooth one.
