I'm restructuring the second chapter of a dissertation on deflationary accounts of consciousness, and I've realized I have been running together two positions that no reader will run together for me. The first is the user-illusion model: the thought, in the lineage that runs from Alan Kay's coinage for the desktop metaphor through Nørretranders' book-length appropriation of it to Dennett's later adoption of it, that conscious experience is a deliberately lossy, action-shaped summary of processes that are themselves nothing like experience — a dashboard rather than a picture. The second is illusionism proper, as defended in recent analytic work by Keith Frankish and others: the claim that introspection systematically misrepresents itself as presenting irreducibly phenomenal properties, and that phenomenal properties so conceived therefore do not exist. My committee's predictable objection is that the first is a claim about representational format and commits no one to the second. My own suspicion is that the two are much closer than that, but I want the closeness located precisely rather than merely asserted.

To force the issue, here is a distinction I have drafted and would like pressure-tested. There seem to be at least three different things one could mean in calling the dashboard an illusion. An illusion of content: the display misrepresents what is there, attributing to the system properties the system lacks. An illusion of process: the display is accurate as far as it goes, but it hides the fact that it is being constructed at all — the rendering is invisible, so its output presents itself as simply given, and it is that self-concealment rather than any error that generates the feel of immediacy. An illusion of audience: an actual graphical interface is an interface for someone standing outside the machine, so if the metaphor is applied to a mind supposed to be nothing over and above the machinery, there is no one left outside to be fooled, and the display is addressed to a user who does not exist. To make these concrete I have been using a pair of synthetic cases: two machine-vision agents with identical control architectures, except that the first is given a coarse but strictly faithful telemetry summary of its own states, while the second is given the same summary plus a decorative gauge corresponding to nothing in the architecture, whose readings it nonetheless consults when arbitrating behavior.

Please compare the two theories against this apparatus. Specifically: (a) which of the three readings does the user-illusion model actually require — can it survive on the illusion-of-process reading alone, which would leave it neutral with respect to qualia and hence not a version of illusionism at all? (b) which reading does illusionism require, and does the audience objection bite on one theory, on both, or on neither; in particular, can a Dennett-style multiple-drafts picture say who reads the display without smuggling in a homunculus, and can the illusionist do better with introspection as a faculty that has no audience either? (c) do my two synthetic agents differ in any consciousness-relevant respect, or have I merely described a difference in engineering hygiene that tracks nothing philosophically? And (d) which of the two positions is, all things considered, the more coherent package, and is there any empirical phenomenon — change blindness, confabulation, blindsight, motion aftereffects, the timing puzzles in the Libet literature — that could in principle discriminate them rather than merely illustrate both? I am not looking for a survey of the debate, and please do not append a bibliography or reading list; I want the argument itself, with the places where my tripartite distinction breaks down stated as plainly as the places where it holds. Somewhere between 1,200 and 1,500 words would be about right.
