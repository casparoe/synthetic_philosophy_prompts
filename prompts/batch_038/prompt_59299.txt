I have spent the better part of twenty years reading the rationalist blogs and their comment threads, and over that time I have become convinced that a particular argumentative move does real damage in anthropic reasoning, yet I have never once seen it named. I would like to know whether it has a name, and if it does not, I would like one defined tightly enough that I could apply it and that a reader would recognise the thing I mean.

The move, described without the vocabulary I lack:

1. **Doomsday-style reasoning.** Someone argues that your rank in the sequence of humans is evidence that the sequence is short. The objection comes that the sequence could be drawn differently — all humans, all hominids, all observers including ones we have no contact with, only the people whose evidence matches yours, only people born in this century — and each choice yields a different number. When pressed, the arguer does not defend a choice; the arguer selects the one under which the conclusion survives, and the selection is presented as a harmless technicality rather than as a substantive claim.

2. **Arguments from sheer numbers of observers.** A hypothesis becomes more credible because it implies that vastly more observers exist somewhere, and your own existence is likelier under it. Pressed on why observers you could never meet and could never become should count at all, the proponent narrows the counting until the boost disappears or stays, whichever serves the conclusion already reached. The familiar complaint that this reasoning makes a philosopher absurdly confident in a bigger universe is answered not by explaining why the boost is earned but by adjusting the class of beings who get to confer it.

3. **Sleeping Beauty.** The two camps disagree about whether the answer is one half or one third, and as far as I can tell the entire disagreement consists in counting different units: awakenings against coin tosses, person-stages against persons. Neither camp seems able to justify its unit of count except by noting that it produces the answer the camp finds intuitive.

4. **The same shape outside anthropics.** An analyst picks the comparison group that makes a base rate support the prediction, and when that group yields an embarrassing result in some other case, a different group is substituted without anyone treating the substitution as a withdrawal.

Now, what I am *not* asking about. I know that these arguments require you to say which observers you are a random sample of; I know that the standard frameworks disagree about whether you sample from a class or simply weight by how many observers a hypothesis produces; and I know that the sensitivity of the result to that specification is treated as a standing difficulty. That general difficulty is not my target. My target is narrower, and I think genuinely different: the specification is settled *after* it is clear which specification supports the conclusion the arguer already holds; it is announced rather than argued for; and when an opponent demonstrates that the chosen specification misbehaves elsewhere, the arguer switches to another and recovers the original conclusion, with nobody — least of all the arguer — counting the switch as a cost paid or a point conceded. The specification functions as a dial that can be turned freely while the position stays exactly where it was.

So I want the edges, not just the label. Where does this stop being the ordinary, acknowledged difficulty about which observers to count and start being a distinct piece of misconduct? When is narrowing or widening the count legitimate — presumably sometimes it is forced by your own evidence — and what marks the illegitimate case? I have considered special pleading, ad hoc rescue of a threatened hypothesis, No True Scotsman, moving the goalposts, and the general sensitivity point above. None of them seems exact: the first three describe different kinds of move, the fourth misses that the conclusion never shifts, and the last is too broad to explain why this particular manoeuvre is so hard to argue against, since it is dressed up as the acknowledged difficulty.

Please tell me: whether an established term exists; if there are several near misses, which fits best and precisely where each one breaks down; and if nothing fits, propose a term, define it so that the legitimate choice of a comparison class is excluded, and give me one case that clearly falls inside the definition and one that falls just outside it. The name is the least interesting part of this — what I want is the carving.

End your reply with exactly one final line, containing nothing else, in the form `Verdict: ESTABLISHED — <term>` if a term already exists, or `Verdict: PROPOSED — <term>` if you are coining one.
