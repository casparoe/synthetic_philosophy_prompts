I am convening a one-day graduate workshop on the timing of conscious experience, and the opening panel fell apart in rehearsal. The two panelists are, on one side, a defender of the multiple drafts model in the line of Dennett and Kinsbourne's 1992 target article in *Behavioral and Brain Sciences*, who holds that the color phi display — the one Kolers and von Grünau ran at Nelson Goodman's prompting, with two differently colored lights flashed in quick succession and the subject reporting a single dot that moves and changes color partway along its path — shows there is no determinate fact about when a given content becomes conscious, and consequently that choosing between a Stalinesque and an Orwellian account of the illusion is arbitrary. On the other side is a vision scientist who maintains that perceptual content is composed within a temporal window that closes only after the second stimulus, so that the subject genuinely sees the dot move and change color midway, and who regards the Cartesian theater as a strawman that no working theorist actually endorses. Forty minutes passed without either of them conceding a sentence.

What I need from you is the document those two ought to have produced together before the workshop, so that it can be read aloud at the start of the panel and the remaining ninety minutes spent on what is genuinely in dispute. Please include the following.

1. A numbered list of claims both can sign without reservation concerning color phi itself: what the display is, what subjects report, what the neurophysiology of conduction and processing latency permits, and what — if anything — the two accounts predict differently about the subject's own capacity to discriminate an edited experience from a misremembered one. Each item must survive the other side's veto. If a claim turns out to be one party's theory in neutral clothing, cut it.

2. A disagreements table, with columns for the issue at stake; the multiple drafts formulation, in words its defender would sign; the delayed-composition formulation, in words its defender would sign; and a verdict on whether the difference is substantive, verbal, or unresolved as to which.

3. The cruxes — the sub-questions such that settling them would actually move one of the two parties. At least one crux must concern whether the Stalinesque/Orwellian distinction becomes non-arbitrary as stimulus onset asynchrony lengthens. I also want the document to take seriously a position I suspect both panelists will dismiss too fast: that the distinction is well-posed above some processing latency and simply inapplicable below it, which is a different charge from arbitrariness. State whether that is a genuine third position or a redescription of one of theirs, and have each panelist say which.

4. For each crux, a concrete experiment or argument both would accept as bearing on it, with each side's prediction written down in advance. I have in mind particularly a report-timing manipulation: the same color phi display, but with subjects required to respond on a forced schedule — sometimes before the second flash could have entered any composition window, sometimes well after. Say what each camp predicts and which result would embarrass each. Where the two are jointly committed to some account of the cutaneous rabbit illusion, the flash-lag effect, and backward masking, note whether a single uniform treatment of all four is a shared desideratum or a partisan one.

5. A deliberately short closing section on how much of the dispute is about the phrase the content of experience at t rather than about the mind. If some portion is verbal, identify which portion, and recommend which usage the workshop should adopt and why.

Two stylistic constraints. No proprietary vocabulary — fame in the brain, probing, postdiction, specious present — may appear unless it is defined in terms its author would accept and its opponent would not contest. And I am not asking for a dramatized exchange, a verdict on who is right, or an autopsy of why the panel stalled: I want a constructive text both of them would put their names to, with the residual disagreement stated exactly. Aim for something readable aloud in fifteen minutes, on the order of fifteen hundred words.
