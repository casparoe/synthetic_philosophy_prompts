For several years I have taught an upper-division seminar on objective chance, indeterminism, and the special sciences. My own research is in epistemology — testimony and self-knowledge, chiefly — and so I have come to depend more than I would like on orientation materials prepared by colleagues whose home discipline is physics. This term a member of our statistical mechanics group, a thoughtful man who writes unusually well for non-specialists, produced a short note covering the reading for weeks three through five. The section on Lewis's Principal Principle has left me uneasy. I do not think there is anything embarrassing in it, and I would not dream of sending it back to him marked up on my own authority; but when the paragraph circulated at seminar last Thursday, one of my advanced graduate students remarked under her breath that the author had quietly deleted the proviso that does all the work, and that the refutation he offers at the end is exactly the case the proviso exists to exclude. I could not tell whether she was indulging in the sort of technicality that makes our discipline look defensive to outsiders, or whether she had found something real, and I would rather settle the question before the note goes into the course reader.

Here is the passage as he wrote it:

"Objective chance is not an elaborate way of describing our ignorance. To say that a given radioactive atom has a chance of one half of decaying within the next hour is not to report anybody's state of mind; it is to describe the world, and the world may be that way whether or not anyone takes an interest in it. The difficulty is to explain what such a fact could have to do with what a rational agent ought to believe. Lewis's Principal Principle is his answer to that difficulty: a rational agent's degree of belief in an event should equal the chance of that event, provided the agent knows what that chance is. Lewis took the principle to be about as basic as anything in the theory of chance, and he was prepared to say that a magnitude which failed to constrain rational belief in this way would not deserve the name of chance at all; in effect, then, chance is whatever stands in this relation to credence. Two consequences of the principle are worth putting in front of students. First, chance cannot be identified with frequency: in any finite run of trials the outcomes are perfectly free to come apart from the chances, and if they were not, the principle would be recommending a degree of belief that the facts contradict. Second, the principle cannot survive its simplest test case. Let the chance of the atom's decaying within the hour be one half, and suppose that I sit and watch and see it decay in the thirty-first minute. The principle still directs me to assign one half to the proposition that it decays within the hour, which is absurd; so the principle fails as stated, and anyone who wants objective chance owes us a different bridge between chance and belief."

Could you audit this for me? Specifically: (i) work through it claim by claim and sort its sentences into what a philosopher working on chance would accept as stated, what is a harmless simplification appropriate to an undergraduate note, and what is simply false or would be repudiated by Lewis; (ii) give me the principle correctly, in a form precise enough that I could hand it to my students without further caveats, and explain what work the qualification my graduate student had in mind actually does in that formulation; (iii) tell me whether the closing counterexample retains any force once the principle is properly stated, whether it depends on a further assumption about the time at which chances are assigned, and whether there is any residual lesson in it worth preserving; (iv) assess the frequency claim — the inference from the possibility of deviation to the failure of the principle — and say whether it trades on a confusion between two distinct relations; (v) take up the characterization of the principle as, in effect, an analysis of chance, saying what a defender would want to keep from that gloss and what he would reject in light of Lewis's larger program; and (vi) tell me whether there is a genuine difficulty in the neighborhood that the author has half-stumbled into and mistaken for the pseudo-problem he actually raises. I have a dim recollection of a worry involving chance statements that are themselves about chance, but it may be a confusion of my own, and I would prefer that you say so plainly if it is. Finally, to be practical: would you advise including the note as it stands, including it with a printed correction appended, or requesting a revision — and what is the smallest set of changes that would make the paragraph safe for students who will go on to read Lewis directly?
