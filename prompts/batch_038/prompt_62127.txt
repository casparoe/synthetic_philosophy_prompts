I am drafting the theoretical section of a paper on when market data counts as evidence, and I want to know whether the verdicts I keep arriving at add up to a single principle. I will state them as concretely as I can, together with the setting several of them presuppose.

Setting: the Tessera exchange is a single-unit sealed-bid double auction on a claim that pays either 0 or 100 at a fixed future date. Participants bid anonymously. Every executed trade is eventually published, together with the identities of both sides, but with a random lag of one to nine days drawn independently of everything else.

1. A block purchase by a firm widely known to hold proprietary models of the underlying outcome: strong evidence about value. I move my own estimate substantially.
2. A sale by an investor publicly known to be liquidating to meet a margin call: no evidence about value. I do not move, and I think it would be a mistake to move.
3. The same liquidating investor turns out also to have received a bad signal, and the sale cleared at a price well below what their own model would have supported: now I treat the trade as evidence, and as *stronger* evidence than in case 1, because two independent motives had to coincide.
4. A purchase by an algorithmic market-making program whose rulebook is fully published and whose trigger conditions I can compute from public data: no evidence, since I could have predicted the trade without knowing anything the market did not already know.
5. A purchase by a trader who has publicly committed to buying whenever a private signal crosses a threshold, where the threshold is known to me but the signal is not: evidence, and I can state exactly how much — my posterior jumps to the probability conditional on the signal having crossed.
6. A trade published after the nine-day lag: weaker evidence than the identical trade published immediately, even though the information it carries is the same. My reason is that a trader sitting on fresh, strong information would have found a faster venue, so the delay itself makes an informational motive less likely.
7. Two analysts, both of whom I regard as competent and honest, who have exchanged every piece of evidence they possess and still assign different probabilities: I think at least one of them must be going wrong somewhere — about the evidence, about their own inference, or about what the other believes.
8. A physician and a patient's spouse, both competent and honest, who have pooled everything they know and still assign different probabilities to survival: I think both can be entirely reasonable, and that nothing has gone wrong.
9. A market in which I am certain that all participants share my prior, are fully rational, and began from an efficient allocation: I think no voluntary trade should occur at all, and that if one did occur I should read it as evidence against my model of the participants rather than as evidence about the asset.
10. The same market, except that I hold my model of the participants with high confidence instead of certainty: I now think a trade *is* evidence about the asset, though its weight depends on how easily the model could be false.

My tentative principle is that an observed trade is evidence about value just in case it was caused by private information about value. I am fairly sure this is wrong, though I cannot say precisely how. Cases 2 and 4 come out right, but perhaps for the wrong reason; case 6 is caused by information yet its weight is diminished by something other than its informational content; case 3 is caused by two things at once and the principle gives me no handle on why the conjunction should strengthen rather than dilute; and case 9 is the one I find hardest, since a no-trade result of the kind I have in mind implies that under those assumptions no trade should occur, so an observed trade ought to be maximally surprising — yet I insist it is not evidence about the asset.

What I would like from you:

(a) State the principle, or the small set of principles, that generates these verdicts as precisely as you can, making explicit what the evidential weight of a trade is a function of — I want something I could in principle use to compute a weight, not a slogan.

(b) For each clause of the principle, name the case above that forces it in, so I can see which of my verdicts is doing the real work.

(c) Say which of my verdicts the principle cannot deliver, and which of them I should therefore abandon rather than accommodate by adding further clauses.

(d) Give the principle's verdicts on two cases I have not listed: a purchase by a firm known to be buying because a competitor's public filing implies good news about the outcome, and a purchase on Tessera by a trader whose identity is published but whose motive is unknown to me.

(e) Explain whether the principle can live alongside the standard result that agents with a common prior and common knowledge of one another's rationality and evidence cannot rationally persist in disagreement — and if it can, say what distinguishes case 7 from case 8, since the two look to me structurally identical.

I do not want a survey of the literature or a list of sources; I want the principle and its consequences.
