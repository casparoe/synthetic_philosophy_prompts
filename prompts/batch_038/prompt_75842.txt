I work in a small policy unit attached to our national statistics agency -- eight of us, half economists, half sociologists -- and we've spent a year fighting about whether to give the government a formal wellbeing target that's partly built out of survey questions asking people to rate their own lives. Our minister is keen because we sit decently in the international league tables and he wants to stay there. A colleague of mine, call her N, is dead against it, and after a long lunch she sketched an argument on the back of an envelope that I still haven't managed to answer. The problem isn't that its wrong about the league tables. The problem is that it keeps going, and it seems to prove way more then she wants -- she'd happily sign the first four lines and is appalled by the last two, but I can't see what entitles her to stop there.

Here's the thing as best I can reconstruct it. (1) A measure is a legitimate target for national policy only if a government cannot move the measure without improving the thing the measure is supposed to track. (2) The international tables rank countries by average answers to a ladder-style question -- rate your life from 0, worst possible, to 10, best possible -- and what they're supposed to be tracking is how good people's lives actually are for them, i.e. their happiness. (3) But a government can move that average without making anyone's life better: publicise the ranking so the question is salient on survey day; run up national pride so people score their country-life higher; shift the comparison group people use, by reminding them how much worse off others are or how much better off they were once told to expect to be; trim aspirations through schooling and media. And quite apart from anything a government does, those judgments are notoriously at the mercy of junk -- the old mood and priming studies where people report their whole life going better on a sunny day, or differently depending on whether they were just asked about their marriage. (4) So by (1) the ladder average is not a legitimate policy target, and a country trying to top the table is optimising a number rather than a good. Up to there I'm with her. (5) Except (1) doesn't care which measure you feed it, and everything anyone wants to put in a wellbeing index is partly made out of people's own judgements -- trust, sense of purpose, perceived health, satisfaction with democracy -- so all of it is gameable the same way, and no subjective measure is a legitimate policy target. (6) And the objective ones aren't safe either: you can lift measured life expectancy by changing how deaths get registered, or by steering resources to the places and ages where they buy the most measured years. So no measure at all is a legitimate policy target, and the countries at the top of the table have either been lucky or gotten good at moving a number.

So: where does it break, and what does breaking it cost? I'd rather keep (3) and (4) than (5) and (6) -- (3) is basically empirical and I don't want to hand her the whole argument -- but if the only way to save (3)-(4) is to reject (1), tell me what (1) should be replaced with, something that still licenses her conclusion about our league table without licensing (6). I also have a nagging feeling there's a premise that isn't on the list at all, something hiding inside the word happiness in (2), because a couple of the moves she names in (3) look to me like they would genuinely change how good people's lives are rather than just change the report of it, depending on what you think happiness is -- and if that's the real fault line I want you to say so bluntly and tell me which picture of happiness makes the argument valid and which makes it collapse. And if it turns out the argument is valid all the way through and my only option is to pick the cheapest premise to lose, say that too, and rank the prices from least to most expensive.
