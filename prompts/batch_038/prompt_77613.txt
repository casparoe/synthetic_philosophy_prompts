I'm a curator at a small natural history museum and I have to print a single number on a wall label — 96-point type, no room for caveats — saying when the Anthropocene started. I've been going in circles for weeks, so I sat down and wrote out my actual gut verdicts on the candidates, and I want to know what principle, if any, they add up to.

1. 1950, the plutonium from bomb tests, the Crawford Lake core in Ontario. Best marker on the planet: sharp, global, showing up in sediment and ice everywhere at once. And I think its the wrong date — by 1950 the thing had been under way for a long time. Worth noting the stratigraphers' subcommission voted the whole epoch proposal down in 2024, largely because the sedimentary record is too shallow and the start date too recent. I think they were right about stratigraphy and wrong about history, and I don't know how to hold both.

2. The CO2 minimum in the ice cores around 1610, the one people tie to the depopulation of the Americas after contact and forest regrowing on abandoned farmland. Morally this feels like the honest date. But it's a dip — the signal is the planet breathing out — and it was made by mass death. I don't want to pick a date because it makes the better story. As an exhibit object: yes. As the start: no.

3. British industrial soot in lake sediments, late 1700s onward. Right era, useless boundary: smeared across decades, regionally patchy, no year you could put a finger on.

4. Agriculture. Right story, impossible date — it happens at different times on different continents, over thousands of years, with no synchrony anywhere.

5. The broiler chicken. Somewhere around the mid-20th century chicken skeletons become recognizably one shape worldwide, a fossil a future geologist could dig up on every continent. This sounds like a joke and I keep coming back to it as the best single object in the whole debate. I can't explain why without sounding sentimental.

6. Here's the one I think breaks me. A lead-isotope shift in a peat bog: global, real, human-caused, but visible only to a mass spectrometer. My verdict: not a boundary — if a person with a hammer standing at the outcrop can't see it, it isn't a boundary. Except the plutonium spike in #1 also needs instruments, and I called that the best marker there is. So I'm being inconsistent and I want to be told which one goes.

7. The no-turning-back date: the last year in which holding the atmosphere near its pre-industrial state was still physically and politically possible. This is the most meaningful date on the list and it's completely ineligible — nobody could have known it at the time, and it can only be computed centuries later by people with better models than ours. A date no one alive can ever know doesn't feel like a date. But I can't tell whether that's a principle or just impatience.

8. Who decides. A commission of a few dozen stratigraphers voting by majority, versus some assembly of historians, Indigenous scholars and descendants of the colonized settling what the word means. My verdict: the commission's vote settles the golden spike and settles nothing else; the second body has no authority over rock, but I don't think it has less authority over the word. Two questions wearing one name. Tell me if that's a cop-out.

9. An accidental global layer — say a single reactor release depositing something detectable worldwide. Eligible as a marker, not as a start. This is where my tentative principle comes from:

The Anthropocene starts at the first globally synchronous trace of a change that human beings made on purpose and could not undo.

It handles #9 cleanly, and #4 (no synchrony), and #2 (it was an undoing). It doesn't handle #3 or #7 at all, and I suspect it just quietly crowns the chicken, which would be a strange place to be led by argument.

So: is there a principle behind all this, or am I running two seperate concepts and refusing to admit it? Test the tentative one, repair or replace it, tell me which of my verdicts it forces me to drop — and then tell me what to print on the wall.

Please use these sections, in this order:

- Bottom line — 3 or 4 sentences, including the number to print, or a defense of printing none.
- The principle — at most two of them, stated precisely. About 250 words.
- Clause by clause — for each clause, which of my cases forced it in and which of my verdicts it delivers. About 450 words.
- The pair I can't reconcile — plutonium versus peat, and whether the instrument objection survives at all. About 250 words.
- What I have to give up — an honest list of verdicts the principle overturns. About 200 words.
- Cases I didn't list — at least three new ones, with your verdict and a line of reasoning each. About 250 words.
- One question or two — the who-decides issue, and whether splitting it is legitimate. About 150 words.
