I came into philosophy from a math undergrad, so when I started my PhD I joined the department's philosophy-of-mathematics reading group pretty much on day one, and I've now sat through three semesters of the same argument about the epistemological challenge to realism. Nobody has moved an inch. The setup is always the same: the nominalist in the group (Field's program, roughly — math is a useful fiction, and the burden is on the realist to explain the reliability of our mathematical beliefs) says that a realist who thinks mathematical facts are about causally inert, mind-independent objects owes us an account of why our beliefs about those facts aren't a cosmic coincidence, and that every account the realist gives either quietly reintroduces some causal hookup that nobody believes in anymore or just asserts, in different words, that our methods work. The realist in the group (Maddy-flavoured, naturalised, wants to start from actual mathematical practice) says the challenge is rigged: it smuggles in a picture of justification the realist has every right to reject, and that if the standard were applied consistently it would sink our knowledge of quarks too. And then there's a third person who thinks the whole thing dissolves — either by deriving arithmetic from an abstraction principle, or by going modal-structuralist and giving up on a single fixed universe of sets — and who gets told by both of the other two that they've dodged the question rather than answered it. Every session ends with reciprocal accusations of begging the question, and its honestly the only thing the three of them agree on.

My working hypothesis, which I want you to attack as much as develop: there isn't one access problem here, there are two wearing the same coat. One is the original 1973 challenge, which leaned on a causal theory of knowledge and mostly evaporated when people stopped being causal theorists — but it still gets invoked rhetorically, so that the realist spends their energy defending against a demand nobody is actually making anymore. The other is the reliability challenge, which asks why the correlation between our beliefs and the facts shouldn't count as a fluke, and which survives the death of the causal theory untouched. The reading group keeps answering the first when pressed with the second, and pressing with the second as if it were the first, and thier respective allies in print do something similar. If that's right, the deeper disagreement might not be about mathematical objects at all but about what an epistemological challenge is even permitted to demand — a mechanism, or a demonstration that things could not have gone otherwise — plus a side disagreement about whether a priori intuition counts as evidence in the same currency as observation. Or maybe I'm wrong and there's a genuinely substantive disagreement buried in there that the two-challenges framing hides.

So: diagnose this for me. Is the stall a verbal one about what 'access' or 'reliability' is supposed to mean, a real substantive disagreement that both sides are just bad at articulating, two camps answering different questions under a single heading, or a sign that the question is ill-posed and neither side will admit it? Is there a hidden empirical or formal question doing the work — for instance, whether our mathematical beliefs actually are reliable sounds checkable until you notice you can't check it without using mathematics, and there's the further claim that mathematical belief-forming methods are somehow insulated from the usual debunking moves? Ground the diagnosis in what each side actually says rather than giving me a survey of positions, which I've read enough of. Then tell me what would count as progress given your diagnosis: a distinction, a change of question, a formal result, a shift in what counts as data about practice — or an honest argument that no progress should be expected and why not. I need to turn this into a paper topic by the end of term, so if your diagnosis implies one narrower question worth writing about, say what it is. And please flag how confident you are in the diagnosis overall and single out the one step in it you're least sure about, because I'm going to be defending this to people who think the problem is perfectly well-posed as it stands.
