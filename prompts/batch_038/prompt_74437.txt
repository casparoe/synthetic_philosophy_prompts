I am preparing a contribution of roughly 8,000 words for an edited volume on the philosophical foundations of cause prioritization, due at the end of the academic year, and I want a research proposal to work from — not the chapter, but the plan for it.

The idea I want to develop is this. The familiar heuristic ranks candidate causes by multiplying importance, tractability, and neglectedness, treating the three as independent scalar scores whose product tracks the expected marginal value of one more unit of effort. I doubt the independence, on two counts. First, neglectedness is not a monadic property of a cause but a relational one: it is indexed to a counterfactual baseline of what other agents would otherwise do, and that baseline moves depending on who is doing the counting and from inside or outside which community. Second, and more consequentially, the sign of the interaction between your effort and the effort already deployed is a substantive parameter that the heuristic assumes away. Where additional entrants substitute for existing work — a fixed pot of funding, a fixed number of policy windows, a queue of applicants for the same posts — neglect is a genuine discount on marginal value. Where they complement it — shared infrastructure, a shared conceptual vocabulary, credibility thresholds that are crossed only collectively — a cause that everyone has abandoned may be precisely where an extra worker is worth most, and the neglect discount then points the wrong way. My working hypothesis is that there is a threshold in that interaction parameter at which the multiplication stops being merely imprecise and starts inverting the ordering, and that much of the framework's intuitive appeal comes from tacitly restricting attention to the substitutive case.

Please turn this into a proposal containing: (i) the question stated precisely enough to have a determinate answer, ideally in one sentence; (ii) an account of why the two standing responses leave it open — defenders who treat the framework as a rough rule of thumb that owes no semantics, and critics who dismiss it as pseudo-quantification, neither of which can say what would show the product order-preserving or order-inverting; (iii) the method, with an explicit choice among building a small formal model of an allocation problem with an interaction term, arguing conceptually from the structure of marginal value, and working through a comparative set of cases — say which, and why not the other two; (iv) what a successful result looks like, which I expect to be a conditional claim of the form "the heuristic tracks marginal value if and only if…", together with what would count as failure, including the failure mode in which the answer reduces to a parameter nobody could estimate; and (v) an honest assessment of scope: what is achievable in 8,000 words and one year, and what I would have to cut to achieve it.
