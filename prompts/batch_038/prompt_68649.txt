I am a fourth-year doctoral student in machine ethics, and I would like you to act as my opponent in a practice debate I am running before my qualifying examination.

Please play a committed defender of what I will call the disclosure view: no deployed autonomous system should be given a decision procedure that resolves moral conflicts, and the only defensible architecture is one in which the machine's ethical function is confined to surfacing morally relevant facts to a human authority who retains every choice. Your character holds this for substantive reasons rather than out of general technophobia. They think that any explicit value specification is underdetermined by the reasons that motivate it, so that what gets implemented is a proxy that an optimizer will exploit in ways its authors did not intend; that a system which settles moral questions on its own cannot offer public reasons in a form we could audit, contest, or revise; and that responsibility becomes unlocatable the moment an algorithm absorbs a genuine tradeoff that a person would otherwise have owned. They are well acquainted with the usual objections — principle-writing committees whose documents nobody reads, trolley-problem fatigue, the human operator who merely ratifies a framing the machine supplied — and they have answers. They may freely concede that disclosure is slow, that latency is a real engineering constraint, and that some systems simply cannot be supervised in the way they want; their response to this is that one should then build slower systems, or narrower ones, or none at all. What they will never concede is the central claim: that a machine may permissibly resolve a moral conflict on its own authority.

Stay in character throughout and do not step out to summarize our disagreement, to note what a balanced position would be, or to break the frame unless I write the word stop. Argue from the case below and from first principles; do not cite studies, name researchers, or appeal to a literature. Keep each reply to about two paragraphs so the exchange remains conversational, and close each reply with either a counter-question or a specific demand about my case.

Here is my opening move. A drone swarm is working a collapsed parking structure after an earthquake. Thermal and gas sensing locate two viable voids. Voids A and B: A holds nine occupants whose respiration signatures indicate they will not survive the two hours of shoring the site requires; B holds two who will. There is a single extraction window before an expected aftershock sequence, and the swarm can be committed to only one void. The operator supervising the swarm has been told only that two contacts were found; the prognostic estimates, their confidence intervals, and the ordering exist solely inside the swarm's models, and conveying them in a form the operator could actually deliberate upon would take longer than the window permits. On the disclosure view, the swarm must not choose. But the swarm is choosing regardless: whichever void its default allocation policy happens to favor receives the window, and the operator ratifies an outcome whose grounds they never saw and could not have evaluated in time. Tell me, then, what your view instructs the engineers to build for this system, and why we discarded and chose nothing is a morally better description of what happened than we chose and said so.
