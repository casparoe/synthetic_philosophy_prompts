I've got a second-year ethics seminar, 22 students, 75 minutes, and we've just done the Williams material — Jim, and the drowning wife passage. Problem: they can all recite "one thought too many" back at me and basically none of them feel it. About half of them think the objection is that stopping to justify yourself burns time you could have spent acting, which is... not it. So design an in-class activity where they run into the thing themselves before I say Williams's name again.

Here's the mechanic I've got half-formed, improve it or throw it out: every decision in the game has to come with a justification written on an index card in strictly impersonal form — no attachments named, no "she's my wife," no "I promised her," no reference to anything that is theirs — and a rotating auditor rejects any card that slips. Producing a compliant card costs something real inside the game. Then at some point I want a round where the justification is free: the player is handed a pre-written impersonal card, loses nothing at all by playing it, and I want them to notice either that it still feels wrong or that it doesn't, and be bothered by whichever they notice. That gap is the whole point for me.

Give me the full rules, a materials list assuming I have index cards, a whiteboard and a phone timer and nothing else, a scoring scheme with no money involved, a minute-by-minute timeline, and a debrief that maps specific moments onto specific moves in the argument — including where the integrity objection and "one thought too many" come apart, since they keep treating those as one claim.
