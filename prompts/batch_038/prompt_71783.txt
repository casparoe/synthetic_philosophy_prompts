I have been working through the literature on low-impact AI, in particular the proposals that penalize an agent for reducing the number of states it can subsequently reach (relative reachability) and those that penalize it for diminishing its capacity to attain some specified set of auxiliary objectives (attainable utility preservation). There is an objection that seems to me to dispose of the whole program, and I would like to know what its defenders could say.

The objection is this. Any such measure must in effect assign a cost to the loss of each state, and where no costs are assigned it treats the loss of any two states as equally costly. But an assignment of costs over states just is a utility function, or at any rate the skeleton of one — and it is only on some such moralized reading that "impact" could be the thing we actually care about, since sheer causal divergence from a baseline is normatively inert. So it appears to have been shown that "side effect" cannot be characterized without already having characterized, in full, what the system is to value; the impact-measure project is then covertly a project in value specification, and the advertised independence from ethics is illusory.

Granting that, what follows? Is the honest response to abandon the project and work directly on preference aggregation, or is there some principled restricted class of value commitments — a partial ordering, a small set of auxiliary goals — that the low-impact theorist could adopt without conceding that she was doing normative ethics all along? I would also want to know whether the objection as I have stated it is one that practitioners of this approach would recognize as an objection at all, or whether I have misconstrued what these measures claim to be doing.
