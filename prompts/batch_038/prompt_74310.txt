I'm a second-year PhD student in a cognitive science department — not philosophy; I've never taken a decision theory course — and my lab is designing an online coordination game in which a large anonymous audience is told that a prize will be paid only if a very small number of people enter. Hunting for precedents, I read Douglas Hofstadter's superrationality columns from his early-1980s *Scientific American* run, collected in *Metamagical Themas*, and I've gotten stuck on something that isn't the mathematics. He first poses the Platonia dilemma as a thought experiment: twenty people isolated from one another, each permitted to send a single telegram, a billion dollars for the sender if exactly one telegram arrives and nothing for anybody if none arrives or more than one does. Since each reasons in the same way and knows the others do, either everyone sends or no one sends, and both are ruinous; so the superrational move is to randomize, sending with probability one in twenty, which leaves each of the twenty roughly a two percent shot at the billion. Then he doesn't stop at the thought experiment. He announces an actual Luring Lottery — a real posted offer in which replying is individually attractive but the value of replying shrinks as more people reply — and reports back on who replied and what they wrote in explanation. I'm working from memory of the columns and may have details wrong (I believe only a handful replied, nine if I recall the follow-up correctly), so please analyze the structure as I've described it rather than trying to reconstruct the history.

What I want is an account of what that practice presupposes: the practice of mailing a normative decision theory to real people and treating their replies as evidence about the theory. Four things bother me and I don't have names for any of them, so please supply the vocabulary as you go. First, the Platonia argument appears to require a closed group of exactly twenty, each of whom knows that the others are superrational, yet the lottery went out to an audience of who knows how many, and I cannot tell whether the operative group was the people he wrote to or the entire readership. What has to be true about the relation between a stipulated thought experiment and a performed experiment for that to be legitimate rather than a confusion of two different things? Second, the argument starts from "we will all arrive at the same answer" and ends by instructing each person to flip a coin, so the sameness has become sameness of rule rather than of outcome — what is the sameness premise actually carrying at that point, and what does the practice assume about when two people's decisions count as one decision rather than two? Third, he wrote to people he had already identified as superrational thinkers, which seems to make the result unloseable, since anyone who replies can simply be reclassified as not one of them; what conception of testing a normative theory does that require, and how does it compare with the way we treat a moral theory whose adherents fail to live up to it? Fourth, the whole intervention is prose in an envelope, which appears to assume that correctly describing a decision problem to someone is sufficient to install the reasoning the problem licenses.

So: reconstruct the implicit theory as carefully as you can, and keep three things separate throughout — what the practice strictly cannot do without, what it merely sits comfortably with, and where it is underdetermined, so that several incompatible accounts of rational agency would license exactly the same mailed lottery. Tell me which rival conceptions of what rationality is (a standard of correctness, a psychological disposition, a property of the situation rather than of the person, a criterion for evaluating institutions) the design quietly rules out, and whether its parts are pulling in the same direction or against each other. Then tell me what would have to change — in what gets sent, to whom, in what a reply counts as, and in what would count as a failed result — if the presupposed view were replaced by the most serious rival you identify. My own game will have an audience of several thousand strangers who do not know one another and will never learn how many entered, and I want to know whether I would be borrowing an argument that only works in a room of twenty.
