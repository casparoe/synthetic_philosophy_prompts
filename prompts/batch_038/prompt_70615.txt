In the recurring forum arguments about whether the targeted killing of a dictator can be justified, Aquinas keeps getting wheeled out as the authority for the claim that a private individual may kill a tyrant when there is no human superior left to appeal to — the reading being that his treatise on kingship makes recourse to God a last resort that leaves the individual free to act on his own initiative. I want to reconstruct the permission properly before citing it, and two things puzzle me. First: is the permission genuinely individual, or does it run through the community that installed the ruler, and what is left for the lone agent in the no-superior case? Second: whatever the answer, how does it sit with the question on sedition in the Secunda Secundae, where sedition is a mortal sin by its very genus, and yet disturbing a tyrannical regime is said not to be sedition — and the tyrant himself is called the real seditious party? If I have the permission wrong, say where it actually resides, and tell me whether his resistance to private action is jurisdictional (no one has the standing) or consequentialist (the odds of making things worse are too high), since those two readings support very different modern conclusions.
