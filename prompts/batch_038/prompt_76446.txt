A review essay in a general-interest magazine tries to explain microhistory to non-specialists in a single paragraph. It reads, roughly:

"For a couple of decades now, a school of historians has been pushing back on the idea that the past is best captured in aggregates. The classic example is Carlo Ginzburg's study of Menocchio, the sixteenth-century Friulian miller who told the Inquisition that all had originally been chaos, that out of it a mass had formed the way cheese forms from milk, and that the worms appearing in it were the angels. The method is straightforward: take one small, otherwise unnoticed life and follow it through whatever documents survive, in exhaustive detail. Because that evidence is few in number and intensely personal, the microhistorian can recover what ordinary people actually thought, more or less in their own words, whereas a social historian working from parish registers or tax rolls can only ever see them as numbers. The payoff is a rejection of generalization itself: since every case is ultimately unique, claims about how societies work are just novels dressed up as analysis. Which is why microhistory delivers narratives rather than arguments, and why literary critics have embraced it."

I found this persuasive, partly because I distrust the view that the only rigorous historical questions are the countable ones, and then a friend who does quantitative social history dismissed it as a caricature without saying where. Could you go through the passage claim by claim and tell me which parts a working microhistorian would sign off on, which are fair simplifications, and which quietly reverse or overstate the position? I am most unsure about the sentence on recovering thought in the subject's own words, about whether the case is being chosen as an ordinary specimen or for some other reason, and about whether the hostility to generalization is really the school's own commitment or something the essay has imported from a neighboring debate. Finish by saying what remains of the claim that these books offer narrative instead of analysis once the corrections are in.
