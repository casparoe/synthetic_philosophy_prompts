Consider a variant of the two-agent debate protocol proposed as a means of scaling human oversight of AI systems. Two agents are asked a factual question Q that the overseer cannot settle unaided — for concreteness, let Q be: which of two candidate drug compounds produces better outcomes in a clinical trial dataset far too large for the overseer to read? The agents alternate turns for a fixed number of rounds, each turn consisting of a message of bounded length. At the end of the debate the overseer rules for one of the two answers; the agent that gave it receives +1 and the other −1.

The variant makes the overseer's verification budget explicit. Over the course of a debate the overseer may perform at most k spot checks, where a spot check consists of selecting one specific claim made by one agent and adjudicating it directly — recomputing a statistic, re-running a piece of code, reading the relevant passage of the source record. Spot checks are genuinely costly, and in the case at hand k = 3. Both agents know k in advance, know the rough cost of adjudicating each kind of claim they might make, and may structure their messages accordingly. The overseer is otherwise ideal: scrupulously honest, perfectly rational given what has been presented, and free of any predisposition toward either agent. The budget is the only limitation.

It is frequently said that in a protocol of this general shape, optimal play favors the true answer. A false answer obliges its defender to maintain an extensive, mutually consistent web of supporting claims, any loose thread of which the opponent can pull; a true answer can be defended simply by directing attention to the world. On this picture the asymmetry between truth and falsehood does the substantive work, and debate purchases oversight by converting an unaffordable verification problem into an affordable one.

Explain why a fixed and commonly known budget of this size creates real pressure on that picture, notwithstanding the overseer's honesty, both agents' optimal play, and the fact that Q has a determinate true answer that is discoverable in principle. In your explanation, address the following:

1. How does the strategy of an optimal defender of a false answer change once it knows that at most three of its claims will ever be adjudicated? Characterize what this defender is optimizing for, and explain what difficulty it faces and what difficulty it does not face in this regime.

2. Why is the natural rejoinder — that the honest agent will expose the falsehood, so the liar is deterred in the first place — unavailable here? Be precise about which feature of the situation does the work in that rejoinder, and about why that feature fails even though the honest agent's incentives and rationality are entirely unimpaired.

3. In what sense, if any, does the budget change the question the protocol is actually answering? Distinguish between a protocol that settles Q and one that settles some other question correlated with Q, and identify what governs the strength of that correlation.

4. What would have to be true of k, or of the procedure by which spot checks are selected, for the asymmetry between truth and falsehood to be restored? Explain why making the overseer more conscientious or more hardworking, holding k fixed, cannot do the job, and say whether the burden of choosing which claims get checked can be shifted onto the agents without smuggling the original verification problem back in.
