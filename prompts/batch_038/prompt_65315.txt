I co-teach a first-year course on noncooperative game theory, and my colleague's way of introducing iterated elimination of dominated strategies has started to worry me. He tells the students that the deletion procedure is natural selection in disguise: strategies are organisms, payoff is fitness, and a strictly dominated strategy is an organism that is outcompeted in every environment it could encounter, so it cannot persist. He then draws the methodological moral that the epistemic apparatus our textbook spends two chapters on — common knowledge of the payoff matrix, common knowledge of rationality, everyone reasoning about everyone's reasoning — is a luxury the argument can do without. On his telling, players need not be clever enough to run the deletion in their heads, because selection, whether biological or competitive-market selection of the kind that licenses treating firms as if they maximized without assuming anyone maximizes, will do the running for them. Iterated dominance therefore becomes the one part of the theory whose conclusions hold up when the players are not ideally rational, and he suggests to the students that this is why dominance arguments are safer than equilibrium arguments.

He extends the analogy to the mixed-strategy case as well. A polymorphic population, in which a fraction of players uses each of several pure strategies, is the biological counterpart of a single player randomizing; so a pure strategy that is strictly dominated only by a lottery over other pure strategies is like an organism beaten not by any single rival species but by a diverse community occupying the same niche. He presents this as a virtue of the analogy: the mixed-strategy version of the procedure looks mysterious on epistemic grounds, since nobody deliberates in lotteries, but perfectly natural on evolutionary ones.

I would like to know whether to object, and on what grounds. Please audit the analogy.

Specifically:

1. Lay out the correspondence explicitly — a table is fine — pairing each element of the biological or market picture with its alleged counterpart in the deletion procedure: organism, fitness, environment, generation, extinction, polymorphism, and the temporal counterpart, if any, of one round of deletion.

2. Separate the disanalogies that actually threaten his conclusion from the ones that are merely loose phrasing. At a minimum, address: (a) the fact that selection acts on realized payoffs against the mix actually present in the population, not against every possible profile of the others' strategies, and what that does to the claim that the dynamic counterpart of deletion is strict dominance rather than something closer to failing to be a best response to the current state; (b) the gap between one individual randomizing and a population being polymorphic, and whether that gap matters precisely in the mixed-strategy-dominance case he leans on; (c) whether the analogy can be stretched from strict to weak dominance, given that deletion of weakly dominated strategies is order-dependent and can destroy equilibria that the strict version leaves intact; and (d) whether a long-run persistence claim can license any conclusion at all about a one-shot game being solved.

3. Say what the analogy quietly imports: for instance, that fitness is fixed independently of a strategy's own frequency, that extinction is irreversible with no mutation or re-entry, that the selective environment can be identified with a single round of reasoning, that the procedure's order-independence has an analogue in the dynamics.

4. To keep this concrete, construct a small two-player normal-form game in which some pure strategy is strictly dominated only by a mixed strategy, and then walk the same case through both the deletion argument and the evolutionary story, marking exactly where the two accounts agree and where they come apart.

5. Give me a verdict on his conclusion. Is iterated dominance really the epistemically safe part of the theory, and can a dynamic process substitute for common knowledge of rationality? If only part of what he says survives, identify the part, and say what the honest substitute would be — including whether some other analogy, dynamic or not, would serve a first-year class better than this one.

Argue from the concepts themselves; I am not looking for references or a reading list, just the analysis.
