I'm a fourth-year graduate student in moral philosophy, TA-ing an upper-level undergraduate ethics seminar: eighteen students, one 75-minute section, third week of our consequentialism unit. They've already worked through the drowning-child case, the integrity objection, and the fair-share response to non-ideal conditions (the view that under partial compliance you're required to bear only what you'd bear under full compliance, no more). The problem is that "that's just too demanding" has become a reflex — they deploy it to swat down conclusions rather than to argue for anything, and they haven't noticed how much work it's doing or how unstable their own thresholds are.

Design an in-class activity that makes the demandingness objection bite as a problem they generate themselves rather than one I announce. The mechanism I want built around: each student receives an endowment of tokens redeemable for something they'd genuinely rather keep — a small amount of extra credit, or minutes off a required reading log — so transfers are real costs, not pretend ones. They then face a sequence of individually trivial transfer requests. Half the group sees a running cumulative ledger of what they've already given; the other half sees only the current request. Before the sequence begins, every student privately writes down the cumulative total at which they think the demands would become excessive, and afterwards they compare that number with what they actually did.

Give me the exact rules, the token scheme, the request sequence, and a minute-by-minute timeline. The debrief should map the two arms onto the philosophical options — what the ledger asymmetry would show for the fair-share theorist, versus for someone who reads it as either a framing artifact or evidence of a genuine integrity cost — and should press them to say what would count as evidence here rather than as mere psychology. Finally, tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how to contain it, and state honestly which feature of the objection the activity structurally cannot reproduce.
