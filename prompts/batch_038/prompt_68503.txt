so I'm working through a Leibniz text on justice — one of the unpublished essays where he pushes the line that justice is basically the charity of the wise man. It's an old scanned translation and here's a para I'm stuck on, in my own loose words (definitely not verbatim):

Justice is nothing other than the charity of the wise man; and charity is a universal benevolence, a habit of loving all. But to love is to be pleased by the happiness of another, that is, to make another's happiness a part of one's own. The wise man therefore loves all, so far as reason permits, and does not prefer the nearer to himself without cause; wisdom being the science of happiness, it directs this love so that it is proprotioned to the worth of the person and to the degree of perfection at stake. Thus both what the jurists call strict right and what they call equity fall under this one notion, and piety toward God is only the highest instance of it, since God is that being whose happiness is most worthy of being loved.

three things I can't figure out:

(1) how is "love" doing any normative work here at all? if to love someone just is to take pleasure in their happiness, that's a report about an affect, not about what's owed to anyone. where does the obligation come from — is he quietly redefining justice as a virtue/disposition and just giving up on the "owed" part?

(2) does the wisdom qualifier actually answer the obvious objection, namely that universal benevolence can't tell you to help this person rather than that one? or is "proportioned to worth and perfection" just the answer smuggled in — i.e. you need a prior standard of worth for wisdom to consult, and then that standard, not charity, is doing the real work.

(3) the last clause has piety toward God as the top case of the same thing. how does that sit with his insistence that the good isn't good merely because God wills it? if justice is loving God's happiness above all, but God's will isn't what fixes the good, then what does fix the "worth" that love is supposed to be proportioned to — and does that leave God's happiness special for reasons that have nothing to do with morality?

mainly interested in whether (2) and (3) are the same problem in different clothes, or genuinely separate worries.
