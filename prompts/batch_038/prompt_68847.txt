I am a senior in high school taking an independent study in classical Chinese philosophy. Next week I am supposed to hand in a short written statement of where I have landed on the Mencius–Xunzi debate about human nature, and my teacher and the two other students in the seminar are then going to attack it. Before I commit anything to paper, I would like a consistency audit of the package I have in my head. Here it is, stated as carefully as I can:

(a) I accept Mencius's thesis that human nature is good, but I read it teleologically rather than descriptively. What makes it true is not that we are born virtuous, or that we reliably behave well, but that we are born with the four sprouts — the heart of commiseration, the heart of shame and dislike, the heart of deference, the heart of approval and disapproval — and that the normal, unforced development of those sprouts just is the four virtues. Development requires favorable conditions, the way the trees on the bare mountain needed to be left alone.

(b) I read Xunzi's thesis the other way around, descriptively: it is a claim about what untrained people are actually like before anyone has shaped them. I accept that claim as well. Most people behave badly most of the time, and an honest inventory of ordinary human conduct looks far more like his picture than like Mencius's.

(c) I hold that moral education works chiefly through practice and correction from outside: learning the ritual forms, imitating a teacher, being corrected repeatedly until the right responses become habitual. You cannot argue a sprout into existence.

(d) I also hold that nothing external can supply the motivation to submit to that training. Unless the student already cares about something, unless there are sprouts available to be recruited, the training produces compliance rather than virtue, and the compliance ends when the supervision ends.

(e) I do not think the sage is a different kind of being from the rest of us. Anyone could become one. So whatever the first sages drew on when they made the rituals, they got it from the same nature everyone else has.

(f) I distrust the sprouts as guides to particular judgments. Spontaneous compassion tracks vividness, kinship, and proximity, and I expect a morality built on it to be parochial. So I want impartial rules and institutions to override our spontaneous responses across a wide range of cases.

(g) On a concrete case: I think Mencius is right that someone who watches a child topple toward a well and feels nothing is missing something constitutive of being human. But I also think a clinical psychopath is not thereby less human, and I do not think we blame people for capacities they never had.

(h) Ordinary adults, though, do seem blameworthy to me when their sprouts wither. The farmer who hauls on his seedlings to help them grow is at fault, but so is the one who leaves them alone entirely; forcing and neglecting look to me like failures of the same kind.

Please work through these and tell me the following. Which pairs or clusters genuinely pull against one another, ranked by severity rather than presented as a flat list of worries. Which ones only look like conflicts and dissolve once they are stated more precisely. For each real tension, what would have to give, what the cheapest available revision is, and what that revision costs me elsewhere in the package. I have no attachment to any individual item: if (a) is the one that must go, say so plainly. Finally, tell me which single change would repair the most damage at once, and whether anything on my list is not really a Mencian or a Xunzi position at all but something else I have mislabeled.
