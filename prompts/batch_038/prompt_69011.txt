In three weeks I am giving a talk at an interdisciplinary workshop on the measurement of wellbeing. The room is roughly a third value theorists who work on population axiology and the impossibility-theorem literature, a third philosophers of mind and measurement psychologists who work on pain, and a third health economists who do cost-effectiveness analysis and priority setting. No one in it is a negative utilitarian, and no one is likely to be converted by me.

My thesis is that suffering is not univocal in the way axiology assumes. Below a certain intensity threshold, pains and pleasures are commensurable at a finite exchange rate and ordinary aggregative reasoning applies; above it, no quantity or number of pleasant lives can offset a single instance. The distinctive move is that I do not treat the threshold as a moral stipulation. I locate it at the point where the psychological machinery that makes suffering comparable at all — the capacity to rank one's own experiences against one another, to trade them off, to report them comparatively — ceases to function. That is supposed to buy three things: an escape from the extinction-style objections to pure negative utilitarianism, an answer to the no-sharp-boundary worry, since the discontinuity is in the nature of the state rather than in my stipulation, and action-guidance, since nearly every real policy question concerns only sub-threshold items.

Prepare my question-period sheet. I want the eight or so objections this particular audience will actually press — not the ones I would field before a general ethics crowd — ordered by how much damage each does to the view, each in the vocabulary of whoever raises it, with a reply I could deliver in about two minutes and a frank note on where that reply is softest. Mark any objection whose force depends on a premise my own sketch already commits me to. Tell me what I should concede in the opening ten minutes to disarm the room, and which single exchange must go my way if the thesis is to survive the afternoon. End with the question I am hoping nobody asks. No references or reading lists — I know that literature; I want the arguments.
