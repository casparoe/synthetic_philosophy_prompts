I'm a documentary filmmaker, and I need you to act like a philosopher who specializes in consent but who has also actually sat through film ethics panels and knows how these things go wrong on a real production. Treat this as a practitioner document, not a seminar paper.

Here's the situation. I'm two years into a vérité film about a small-town hospital being shut down by the health system that owns it. I've got maybe 40 people on camera, most of whom consented in a hallway or at a kitchen table with the camera already rolling. There's no lawyer on the project and the funder's compliance person only cares that the releases hold up, not that anybody was really consenting. I want a consent procedure my crew can actually run — in the field, in about five minutes, with no theory in it — but grounded in what the philosophy of consent actually says about when consent counts: what has to be disclosed, what counts as pressure, what happens when comprehension or competence is shaky.

A few things I've been chewing on that I want the procedure to take seriously instead of papering over:

- I don't think consent on a project like this is a one-time authorization. It's more like a standing waiver the subject can pull back at any point, including after the footage exists and after they've seen themselves on screen. But if it's revocable at will, what normative work is it doing at all? Tell me where the procedure should treat a withdrawal as binding and where it can't, and why.
- The film has an argument. I know roughly what it is; the subjects don't; and spelling it out would change how they behave on camera. Is that deception or just normal? Where does the line go?
- Nobody's holding a gun to anybody, but a lot of these people are frightened — of loosing their jobs, of the town turning on them, of me packing up and leaving. Is fear that isn't coming from me still coercion, and if so how do I separate it from ordinary nerves?
- We give some people a $200 honorarium for travel and lost shifts. When does money stop being reimbursement and start being an offer they can't refuse?

Then write the procedure: the questions to ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where it just stops with a no, and where it hands the decision to a human because it's a values call rather than a facts call. Flag the judgment calls as judgment calls — don't dress them up as algorithm. It has to be runnable by a 24-year-old assistant producer at a kitchen table, and defensible by me if the film gets written up somewhere unflattering.

Apply it to three situations, and for at least one of them tell me straight that the answer is a no I'm not going to like:

1. A woman in her sixties with early dementia is the emotional center of the film. She's clear most days, she loves being filmed, her daughter is fine with it but won't sign anything herself.
2. A hospital tech agreed to be filmed the night the closure was announced, crying in a parking lot. Eighteen months later he wants that scene out.
3. A nurse still employed by the health system will talk on camera only if we promise the segment can never be used against her in a disciplinary proceeding — a promise I can't actually guarantee.

Think carefully, and do two or three lines of brainstorming first about how consent goes bad on a production like this — I want to see that you've got the failure modes, not just the textbook categories.
