At a reading group last week a senior colleague waved my proposal away with Carroll's tortoise and the line people always reach for: "you can't argue someone into accepting a rule of inference." He treated that as settling the matter. My suspicion is that the sentence is doing several different jobs at once, and that most of them never touch my question.

Could you lay out — as a menu, not a single favored gloss — the distinct precise claims it might be making: from the infinite-regress and question-begging versions, through the claim that a rule of inference is not the sort of thing that could figure among the premises of an argument, to the claim that accepting a rule is a commitment or disposition rather than a belief, plus any readings I've overlooked? For each, say whether it is a platitude, substantive and defensible, or false. Then tell me which, if any, would actually license dismissing a reductive account, and where the familiar reply — that the tortoise conflates entertaining a premise with being moved by one — belongs in that taxonomy. Keep it tight enough that I can put it in front of him next week.
