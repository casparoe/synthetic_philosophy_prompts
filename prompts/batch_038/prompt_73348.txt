Longtime LW reader, first time I've wandered this far from AI risk, but I've been in a philosophy-of-biology rabbit hole for a couple months and hit a debate where more reading has stopped helping — so I want an argument, not a survey. The topic is what makes something a biological function. One pole is the causal-role/dispositional view in Cummins's line: a function is whatever a component contributes to some capacity of a containing system, where the capacity in question is picked out by the explainer's interests. The other pole is the etiological/selected-effect view from Wright through Millikan's proper functions: the function of a trait is the effect that got it there by selection. There's also the goal-contribution middle ground that gets mentioned as a compromise, and I'm fine with you landing there, but I want the two poles actually weighed against each other first.

I already know the stock objections and I don't want the essay spent on them: causal role looks like it overgenerates (the heart also makes a thumping noise, and that is a causal contribution to the containing system) and looks like it can't handle malfunction (a fibrotic heart that can't pump has no pumping causal role); selected effect looks like it undergenerates (a brand-new beneficial mutation apparently has no function on the day it appears, which sounds crazy to anyone who works with real genomes). Fine, whatever. What I actually care about is three cases a friend and I invented after we'd had the same arguement three separate times, because I think these are where the views genuinely come apart and I don't trust my intuitions on any of them:

**Case A, the repatriated enzyme.** A lab takes a soil bacterium whose enzyme has, by everyone's agreement, a selected effect of cleaving a plant toxin found in that soil. They run several hundred rounds of directed evolution in a chemostat where the only usable substrate is a synthetic pesticide that has never existed outside that building. End state: ~500x better on the pesticide, effectively dead on the original toxin. Then they put the lineage back into the original soil, where the pesticide is absent and the toxin is everywhere. What is the function of that enzyme, in that organism, in that environment, right now? Selected-effect seems pushed toward pesticide-cleaving, causal role toward toxin-cleaving or nothing, and both answers feel wrong to me in different ways. Sub-question I care about: does it matter that the selection was imposed by humans rather than by the wild environment? I suspect a consistent etiological view has to say no, and I want to know whether you think that's a bug or a feature.

**Case B, the de novo ORF.** In a freshwater fish, a previously noncoding stretch picks up a promoter and starts making a small peptide. Knocking it out costs the fish nothing in its home river, but in brackish water the knockouts die at roughly three times the rate. The species has been in that river for a very long time and there's no signal of selection on the locus at all. Does the peptide have a function? My worry is that the answer depends entirely on which environments you're willing to count in the reference class, and if so, whether function talk is doing any work beyond recording our choice of reference class.

**Case C, the dead duplicate.** A gene duplicate is transcribed, translated, folds correctly, and is catalytically dead because of a single active-site substitution; the organism is mildly worse off for making it, since it burns ATP and amino acids. Etiological says it has a function it's failing at, which I like and want kept. But causal role, pushed honestly, seems forced to say its function is consuming resources — which turns waste and parasitism into functions, and I can't see how to block that without smuggling selection history back in.

My friend is a bioinformatician and keeps insisting both sides are right about different timescales. His proposal, which he calls the recency-weighted account: a trait's function is the effect it has reliably produced over a window short enough that the current population could still respond to selection on it, so functions fade over generations instead of switching instantly, and a trait can have a dying function and an incipient one at the same time. I find this weirdly appealing but I suspect it's a redescription rather than a theory — "the window over which the population could still respond to selection" is doing all the work, and I can't tell whether that window is set by generation time, by strength of selection, by effective population size, or by whatever the person explaining happens to find convenient. So: is it a genuine third option, a special case of one of the poles, or a verbal patch?
