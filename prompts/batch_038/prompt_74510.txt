I am the senior reliability engineer at a 120-person manufacturer of safety components whose entire value proposition is a disposition claim: a self-sealing liner for an aircraft hydraulic reservoir, a thermal cutoff inside a battery module, a sacrificial coating on a marine fastener. Our datasheets and qualification reports assert sentences like "the liner closes a 4 mm breach within 200 ms," and our customers — and the notified body that audits us — read those as counterfactuals about what the part would do in service, not as summaries of what it happened to do on our bench. Six months ago we had a field event: parts that had passed our accelerated corrosion program by a wide margin corroded within a year in service. During the post-mortem a friend of mine who teaches metaphysics — I mention her only because she is how I found the relevant material — suggested that some of our plans defeat themselves rather than merely under-sample. Roughly, her diagnosis was that our bath contained a corrosion inhibitor absent from the service environment, so the coupons never manifested a disposition they still had; and that in another program the fixture's own protection circuitry would have removed the stimulus before the part's response could occur, so we were certifying parts that were never given the chance to fail. I have since read as much of the literature on dispositions, finks, and masks as an engineer can stand, and I now think several of our standard plans are confused in a way that has nothing to do with statistics.

Please write me a review procedure that a test engineer with no philosophy background can run against a draft qualification plan in about half a day and that yields a written verdict on whether the plan licenses the disposition sentence we intend to print. It should be an ordered set of questions, each with a stated sufficient answer: how to pin down the claim itself — the stimulus conditions, the manifestation, and the causal basis in the part that would explain it — to a precision the plan can actually be checked against; whether anything in the fixture, the preconditioning, the instrumentation, or the protection scheme would remove or alter that basis before the manifestation could occur; whether anything would block the manifestation while leaving the basis intact; whether the manifestation could be produced by something other than the basis, including the rig itself; and whether the dispositions the part has inside the fixture are even the dispositions it will have in service. I want the translation made explicit at the point where the standard repair to the naive conditional analysis requires that the basis retain its intrinsic character, undisturbed: tell me what laboratory action — which measurement, which before-and-after comparison, which post-test sectioning or non-destructive inspection — corresponds to that clause, because that seems to be where the theory offers something we are not currently doing. For each step, say what evidence discharges it, what probe the plan must add when the answer is unclear, and which outcomes force the verdict that the plan cannot support the sentence. Mark clearly which steps are matters of judgment that the procedure can only discipline rather than settle, and which decisions are not factual at all and belong to a human being — residual risk acceptance, the cost of a re-test, whether an undetectable failure mode is tolerable.

Then run the procedure on three of my plans. First, the battery module thermal cutoff: the harness includes a monitoring board that interrupts current within 30 ms of detecting an overtemperature condition, and our test drives current upward while recording whether the cutoff opens; many units never open, and we have been scoring that as a pass on the grounds that the board did its job. Second, the corrosion coupon program: the bath is buffered and refreshed on a fixed schedule, while the service environment is tidal splash with no renewal and long wet-dry cycling. Third, the self-sealing liner: the rig's dump valve establishes the pressure differential that drives sealant into the breach, so the assembly seals in the fixture largely regardless of the liner's formulation, and we qualified two different supplier formulations under that plan with both passing. One of the three — tell me which — should come out with a verdict I will not like: we have shipped against that plan for six years, and if the procedure concludes the datasheet sentence was never licensed, I want it said plainly, together with the minimum re-test that would license it. Finally, state what the procedure cannot do. I suspect two limits and would rather see them written down than papered over: that a passing test can show the absence of a manifestation under those conditions but never the absence of a disposition, and that where we have never characterized the causal basis — several of our coatings are legacy formulations nobody fully understands — a number of the steps have nothing to operate on. No reading list, no citations; assume I cannot look anything up and need the reasoning inside the document. I would rather have the honest failure modes of the procedure than a tidy flowchart that conceals them.
