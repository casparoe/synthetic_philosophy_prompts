This semester I convene a sixty-minute interdisciplinary reading group for a department that is nominally philosophy but in practice mostly machine learning: five ML PhD students, two computational linguists, and one applied statistician. None of them has taken a philosophy course. Next session's topic is Lewis's 1976 triviality result for indicative conditionals — the theorem that no proposition's probability can be guaranteed to equal the corresponding conditional probability, on pain of collapsing the probability space.

Their starting position is the one I most need you to help me plan around. They know Bayes' rule cold, they have read a few popular write-ups announcing that Lewis "showed you can't put probabilities on if-statements," and their default diagnosis is that Lewis must have confused notation: obviously P(if A then C) = P(C|A) is a definition, so if a philosopher thinks it can fail, he is either talking about material implication or he has made an error. They will not find the result interesting until they see that their own reflex is exactly what the theorem forecloses.

The room has one whiteboard, no handouts, and no projector. I have also made myself a standing rule: no possible-worlds diagrams, no selection functions, no modal notation of any kind, and every step of the derivation must live inside a single finite sample space small enough that they could enumerate all of its regions by hand in under a minute.

I am not asking you to explain the result to me. I am asking you to plan how I should explain it, and in particular to settle a structural problem I have not been able to solve. The derivation needs the premise that the thesis holds not merely for my current credence function but for every probability function on the space, including the ones I would get by conditioning on further evidence. My instinct says that premise has to be on the board before anything breaks, but I cannot motivate it before they have seen a failure, and if I introduce it as a standing assumption they will dismiss the whole result as an artifact of an unreasonably strong hypothesis about rational agents. Tell me where it goes, what earns it, and what I lose by placing it anywhere else.

Please structure your response in exactly these sections, in this order:

**Bottom line** (about 80 words). The single sentence I want the room to be able to say on the way out, and the one dependency in the argument that everything else hangs off.

**Sequence** (about 550 words). The order in which the pieces must be introduced, with rough time markers. For each step, say what it depends on and what specifically goes wrong if it is moved earlier or later. I want the dependency structure, not a transcript.

**The carrying example** (about 250 words). The one finite chance setup I should build the entire session around — a die, an urn, a two-draw process, whatever you judge best — together with the features it must have, the features it must lack (zero-probability antecedents? nested conditionals? equiprobable outcomes?), and the precise move — the reweighting, the conditioning, the counting of available values — that turns it from an illustration into a contradiction they can check themselves on the board.

**Misconception map** (about 350 words). The errors they will bring in and the errors they will form mid-session, listed in the order they will actually surface. For each, state the point in the sequence where it must be headed off rather than answered afterward, and why answering it later fails. At minimum I expect to see the material-impulsion reading, the "it's just a notation confusion" reading, and the belief that weakening the thesis to an inequality or to a single agent rescues it.

**The objection worth stealing** (about 100 words). Identify the one moment where the room's naive reaction is not a misconception at all but the seed of a genuine live response in the literature, and tell me how to acknowledge it in a sentence without turning the session into a survey.

**The closing check** (about 120 words). One question I can put to the room in the last five minutes that separates people who understand the theorem from people who have merely concluded that material implication is a bad model of "if." Include the wrong answer most of them will give first, so I can recognize it.

**What I will cut** (about 150 words). The topics I should refuse at this level, each with the reason it does not survive the sixty-minute constraint, and the sentence I should say out loud when cutting it.

Do not send me readings, a bibliography, or suggestions for further study; I am not assigning anything beyond what I have described, and my students will not read it.
