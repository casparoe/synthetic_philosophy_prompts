I spent thirty-eight years in isotope geochemistry, mostly measuring things that decay very slowly and arguing with people about mass spectrometers, and since I retired I've fallen down the philosophy-of-mathematics hole. It's a strange change of pace because nobody asks me for error bars any more. The thing I'm stuck on is the continuum hypothesis. I've done the usual homework: I can follow the shape of Gödel's constructible universe argument, I have a rough feel for what forcing is doing, and I get that independence is a result about one axiom system and not automatically a result about the question. I also understand that the big axioms everyone hoped would settle it don't — they decide a lot of other stuff about definable sets of reals and leave this one wide open, and they're compatible with either answer. What I can't figure out is the philosophical argument that's been running alongside the mathematics for sixty years, and the fact that it doesn't look like it's going anywhere.

As far as I can tell there are two camps and they barely share a language. One says there's a single intended universe of sets, that the continuum hypothesis is flatly true or false in it, that our axiom system is simply too weak, and that the job is to find new axioms — justified either intrinsically, by extending the iterative conception or making the picture of V hang together, or extrinsically, by being fruitful and settling lots of other questions. Woodin is the interesting case here for me: he went from a sophisticated technical program that pointed toward the hypothesis being false or undecidable in the platonist sense to a different program built around an inner model he thinks implies it's true, and both times he took himself to be tracking the same fact. The other camp says that's the confusion — forcing lets you move between universes where it holds and ones where it fails with no way to priviledge any of them, the notion of "all subsets of ω" isn't sharp enough to have a determinate extension (Feferman's worry, as I read it), so the honest thing is either to talk about a multiverse of set-theoretic universes à la Hamkins or to admit the question was never definite in the first place. And here's what bugs me: both camps agree on every single piece of the actual mathematics. Nobody disputes a theorem. They just draw opposite conclusions from the same results, and as far as I can tell not one person has changed their mind in decades.

So what kind of disagreement is this, really? My hunch — and please push on it rather than flatter it — is that the two sides are answering different questions under one heading, one about what the word "true" attaches to in set theory and one about how much determinacy a mathematical concept needs before there's a fact at all, and that each keeps mistaking the other's answer for a bad answer to its own question. But I can see at least four readings and I don't know how to choose between them: that it's a purely verbal fight about the phrase "the universe of sets"; that it's substantive metaphysics which is nevertheless epistemically inert, because no conceivable evidence discriminates between the pictures; that the real disagreement is about standards of evidence — what would even count as justification for a new axiom — with the ontology talk as decoration; or that it's bedrock intuition, one side unable to live with an incomplete totality and the other unable to live with a picture nobody has ever exhibited, and neither able to argue for its starting point. Sort out which of these, or which combination, actually fits, using what each side says rather than the labels people apply to themselves, and tell me plainly where my own hunch is wrong. Then the part I care about most: what would count as progress, given your diagnosis? Would a new axiom that decides the question and gets broadly accepted settle it, or would the other camp just say "true in that universe, so what"? Could a theorem do it — something showing that no axiom system meeting such-and-such conditions can decide it, or that any system that does must be either incomplete or arbitrary? Is there a distinction someone could draw that would dissolve the whole thing? Or is the right verdict that it's ill-posed and should be dropped, and if so, what would make it ill-posed rather than merely very hard — I want to know what separates those two verdicts, because in my old field I'd have called something "merely hard" plenty of times when it turned out to be badly posed. One ground rule: no survey of the positions and no list of who holds what, I've read all of that, and I'm not after citations or a reading list.
