I've been chewing on one question for maybe four years now and my intuitions have started visibly disagreeing with each other, so I want someone to either hand me the principle that unifies them or tell me exactly which ones I have to throw out.

The question is about information that's doing causal work inside a system but isn't represented in that system's ontology — and, conversely, about representations whose referents aren't in the world at all. The alignment crowd calls one half of this the ontology identification problem, with the diamond maximizer as the stock illustration; the LessWrong flavor of the other half is ontological crisis. But I keep tripping over it in places that have nothing to do with superintelligence, and my verdicts stopped lining up.

Here's what I actually say, off the top of my head:

1. Diabetes-alert dog that wakes it's owner before a hypo. The dog has no concept of blood glucose, not even a crude one. I say: it knows, or at any rate it has the information, and I'd bet on it. High confidence.
2. Self-driving car that slams the brakes for a plastic bag drifting across the road because the bag's pixel signature looks pedestrian-ish. Behaviorally the same shape of reliable-but-unrepresented sensitivity as the dog, arguably better calibrated. I say: no, it does not detect pedestrians, and I wouldn't trust it to.
3. Chess engine playing a positional principle no human has ever stated, plus the grandmasters who can't say why a move is good but can reliably pick it out of three candidates. I credit both with knowing the principle.
4. Same test, an LLM: nails the citation format in the register it trained on, then falls over when you paraphrase the underlying question. I say it doesn't know the thing, it knows the surface of the thing.
5. Thermostat. No. Obviously no, and I can't tell you why this one feels different from the dog without waving my hands.
6. A phlogiston chemist with a fully worked-out theory who predicts combustion correctly for a century. I want to say he knew nothing whatever about phlogiston, because there's no phlogiston, but that he knew a great deal about oxidation while calling it the wrong name.
7. An agent whose utility is defined over souls, which then discovers there are no souls. I want to say the goal survives — it gets relocated onto persons — and that a good successor agent should perform that relocation rather than shut down or start maximizing soul-shaped proxies.
8. A diamond maximizer whose world-model has no diamond node, only low-level physics. I want to say the reference is fixed, it's about diamonds and nothing else, but the identification fails and there's nothing to relocate onto.

Look at 7 next to 8. I'm doing surgery on the goal in one case and refusing to do it in the other, and the only reason I can give is that souls dont exist and diamonds do — which feels like it should be load-bearing but I can't get it to do any work. And 1 vs 2 is the pair that actually keeps me up, because there I'm not even splitting reference from identification, I'm just flat-out contradicting myself about whether reliable unrepresented sensitivity counts.

So: is there a principle, meaning a small number of clauses and not a laundry list, that gets as many of these as possible? For each candidate you come up with, tell me which of my cases motivated which clause, then run it on two or three cases I havent listed — I specifically want the ones where it delivers an answer I'd hate — and then say plainly which of 1–8 it forces me to give up. I'd rather lose two verdicts than accept a nine-clause principle, but if the honest price is that 1 and 2 can't both survive, tell me which one goes and why that's the one.
