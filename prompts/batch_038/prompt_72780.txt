I am putting together a two-page handout for a first-year graduate metaphysics seminar on possible worlds, and what I need is a diagnostic decision tree rather than another survey of the options. The problem is specific: my students can recite the positions but cannot tell you which one they would actually defend, and when pressed they tend to reproduce whichever view they read most recently. I want an instrument that sorts them by their commitments.

Fixed entry point: the reader arrives having already accepted that some modal claims are literally true and are not reducible to claims about the powers or arrangements of actual things — for example, that some things that do not actually exist might have existed. Everything downstream of that must be a real fork; no node may simply restate the entry commitment.

Build the tree out of the logical space rather than out of a list of views. Begin with the axes you take to be load-bearing — I expect at least these: what worlds are made of; whether "actual" picks out one world absolutely or merely the world the speaker inhabits; whether individuals are confined to a single world or span several; whether the full range of worlds requires an explanation or may be taken as brute; whether a world must be internally consistent; and whether "possible" is itself primitive, with worlds derivative on it. State the answer space each axis allows, and only afterward place the positions within it.

Requirements for the tree itself:

- Between seven and nine question nodes. Each must be phrased as a concrete case or a plain question that a student can answer without knowing which leaf the answer leads to. No node may contain the name of a position or its proprietary vocabulary.
- Between ten and fourteen leaves. Each leaf gets the standard name of the position if it has one (if it has none, say so), one sentence stating what, on that view, a world is, and a single bullet it has to bite — the most awkward commitment the view carries, stated so that anyone who accepts the leaf is visibly accepting the bullet.
- At least three leaves must be varieties of the view that worlds are abstract objects, distinguished from one another only by what worlds are made of (recombinations of actual materials, maximal sets of truths, maximal states of affairs involving unexemplified essences, or any further distinctions you think belong there). Because these differ in only one respect, the tree must contain a node whose sole job is to separate them, and you should say whether that node can be answered by someone who has not already picked a side.
- At least one leaf must dispense with worlds altogether.
- At least one leaf must confine each individual to a single world while still making cross-world claims about that individual come out true, and at least one must let the very same individual exist in several worlds.

Use at least four of the following test sentences as the content of nodes, presenting them as things a student is asked to react to; add others if the tree needs them:

- Humphrey might have won the election. Is that true of Humphrey himself, or of something suitably like him?
- Something might have had a property that no actual thing has and that no actual thing could have acquired.
- There might have been two universes bearing no spatiotemporal or causal relations to each other, which together would not constitute a single world.
- Sherlock Holmes might have existed.
- Two worlds might have differed only in which individual plays which role, everything else being qualitatively identical.
- There might be things that could not have failed to exist, so that what varies across worlds is only what is exemplified.

After the tree, give me three things:

1. A path key: for each leaf, the sequence of answers that leads to it, so that I can check that the leaves are mutually exclusive and that the tree is exhaustive of the answer space you laid out.
2. At least three combinations of answers that your axes permit but that no position occupies. For each, diagnose the vacancy: is the combination incoherent, is it available but unattractive because nobody wants to pay its costs, or is it merely unexplored? If you think one of them is a live option, say what would have to be true for it to become attractive.
3. Any pair of leaves your tree cannot genuinely separate, with an explanation of why not, and what further question would separate them if you think one exists.

Constraints on form: no bibliography, no reading list, no citations, and no quotation from any philosopher; where a position has a standard name, use it, but the handout must remain usable by students who have not read anyone. An indented outline or a flowchart rendered in text is fine as long as it fits in roughly two pages. Write for a reader who is philosophically trained but has never had to decide between these views.
