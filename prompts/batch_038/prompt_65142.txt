Retired physical chemist here, forty-odd years of kinetics and instrumentation, and I've only recently got round to all the philosophy I kept meaning to read. I came at it through Gödel, Escher, Bach, which I half understood as a undergraduate, and then I Am a Strange Loop, which landed much harder now — especially the pages about his wife Carol dying, and his claim that loving someone means running a working copy of them inside your own head, so that an I isn't walled in by one skull but is smeared out across everyone who carries a copy, some faithful, some badly distorted.

My daughter made me watch the Cloud Atlas film after I'd read Mitchell's novel, and between the two I've got properly stuck. In the novel each story reaches the next one's world as an artefact: half of Adam Ewing's journal, which drives Frobisher mad because he can't finish it; Frobisher's letters, which Luisa Rey turns up; the Luisa Rey mystery Cavendish reads; Cavendish's own ghastly ordeal, filmed, which Sonmi-451 watches on her way to waking up; and Sonmi's recorded orison, which ends up as the sacred text of Zachry's people, who worship her as a goddess. The film instead puts the same actors across all six timelines and gives them a comet-shaped birthmark, which pushes a quite different idea — the same soul coming round again.

So I'd like a real reading of Cloud Atlas through Hofstadter's distributed self, and I want it to cut both ways. Specifically:

- Which of those transmissions, if any, is a case of a person actually persisting in another head, and which is only information about a person travelling? Does his account have the resources to tell them apart, or is it committed to saying that Zachry's goddess just is Sonmi-451, living on, however mangled?
- He's candid that his own copy of Carol is loving and therefore distorted. If distortion doesn't disqualify a copy, what does? Is a farce about a dodgy old publisher a vehicle for a self? Are the Valley Folk's deity and Meronym's archived testimony copies of a person, or copies of stories about a person?
- The book keeps handing you degraded copies — a journal missing its second half, a life reduced to a comedy, an orison whose meaning Meronym holds back from Zachry. Does the theory need a fidelity threshold, some minimum bandwidth of person, and can his suggestion that souls come in different sizes do that job, or is that a seperate axis?
- The fabricants are the inverse case: maximal similarity, no shared history at all. Do the clones show that pattern-matching alone isn't enough, and does the novel know that — or does the film's casting and birthmark quietly trade on exactly the thing the novel refuses?
- Where does my lens break? Is the one-soul-recurrence reading something Mitchell earns, or something readers and the filmmakers imposed on him? And am I turning a book that is mostly about predation and how the strong devour the weak into a metaphysics seminar puzzle? Say so plainly if you think I'm forcing it.

Also tell me which scene you'd point a reading group at, and what the book gives this debate that Hofstadter's argument on its own doesn't. The Cloud Atlas Sextet strikes me as a case he never uses: a pattern that survives only in performances by people who weren't there when it was made, and whose own structure is nested interruptions.

Assume I've read it — no plot summary. British English, please, and hard cap of 900 words for the whole thing. Finish with a line on how confident you are in the reading overall, and name the single point you're least sure of.
