I ran a physical chemistry lab for thirty-odd years and only started reading philosophy properly since I retired, so bear with me if I'm mixing registers. I've been going back and forth between two ideas about how cooperation can happen without trust, and I can't tell whether they're really the same idea in different clothes.

The old friend is the folk theorem for repeated games. In an indefinitely repeated prisoner's dilemma, if the players are patient enough, pretty much any payoff that's feasible and leaves each player better off than what the other could force on them by punishing can be held up as an equilibrium — grim trigger, tit for tat, whatever you like. Cooperation isn't goodwill, it's the shadow of the future: I don't defect on you today because you'll defect on me next round and I care about next round.

The newcomer is program equilibrium. Two players are programs that get to read each other's source code before moving, and there's some stipulated rule — a "matching" function — for working out what happens when each program's output depends on the other's code. Now the one-shot dilemma has profiles where both sides run "cooperate if and only if your opponent's code cooperates," which yields mutual cooperation, and neither side gains by swapping in a different program, since always-defect against that kind of opponent just trips the opponent's defect branch. Cooperation with no future at all, no discounting, no reputation, no second round.

My gut says the second is the first with the repetition deleted and "history" swapped for "source code": in both, my move is conditioned on yours and the conditioning is what makes cooperation self-enforcing. But the analogy itches at me, so here are three spots where I think it strains and I'd like to know whether it actually snaps.

One, bounded reading. A's code is millions of lines and B can only inspect the first chunk before it has to commit. Under the folk theorem, sloppy monitoring weakens cooperation without necessarily destroying it — you get a worse payoff, not total collapse. Does the same go here, or is program equilibrium brittle in a different way, where a partly-read opponent is just an unknown opponent and you're straight back to defect-defect?

Two, mutual recursion. A's behaviour depends on what B will do and B's behaviour depends on what A will do. My gut says there are profiles where no outcome is even defined, the matching rule just spins. The repeated game has nothing like this: histories are always well defined, there's no fixed point that might fail to exist. If that's right, is program equilibrium a well-defined equilibrium concept at all, or only one under extra assumptions I haven't spotted?

Three, exploitation. In the repeated game a defector gets caught and punished. Here, what stops a cleverer program from running something like "cooperate with cooperators, but defect against any program whose behaviour is conditional on mine"? Is that a deviation the profile has to be robust to, and does mutual-conditional-cooperation survive it? My instinct is that there's an arms race in both games, just spelled differently.

There's also the old Schelling picture I keep coming back to: the driver in a game of chicken who throws his steering wheel out the window so the other guy can see he can't swerve. Program equilibrium looks like a world where drivers can not only see the thrown wheel but read each other's wiring diagrams. Does that extra transparency make commitment strictly better, or does it open new ways to lose?

So give me your verdict, argued for: is program equilibrium a special case of folk-theorem logic — the shadow of the future replaced by the shadow of transparency — or a genuinely different mechanism with its own failure modes? If you had to bet on one as the more robust account of how self-interested agents end up cooperating, which is it, and does the answer flip between a world where agents are transparent to each other and a world where they're opaque but meet over and over? And please tell me if one of my three cases is a dud — I'd rather find that out now than defend it.
