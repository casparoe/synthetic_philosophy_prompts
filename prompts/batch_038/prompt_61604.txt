Take the case of jade. In China and in Mesoamerica, independently and over millennia, a single term named what was treated as a single substance: a stone too hard to be worked with metal tools, tough enough to survive being shaped with abrasives, capable of a polish and a translucency no other stone matched, and valued accordingly. In 1863 Alexis Damour established by chemical analysis that the material answering to that description was in fact two minerals, jadeite and nephrite — a sodium-aluminium pyroxene and a calcium-magnesium amphibole, differing in composition, crystal habit, and hardness, with nephrite the tougher of the pair and jadeite the harder. Each tradition had predominantly used a different member of the pair without knowing it, and the distinction could not have been reached by inspection or by handling the stone.

Analyze this episode through the thesis that the sciences carve nature at its joints, running the analysis in both directions. First the mapping: identify the candidate kind, the stereotype that fixed the term, and what precisely the discovery does to the picture on which a natural-kind term's reference is settled by an underlying structure to which lay speakers defer. Then apply a test I have been using, which I'll call the doppelgänger test: a candidate kind is joint-carving only if anything matching its complete observable and functional profile would have to share its underlying nature. Jade appears to fail it. Tell me whether that failure is fatal to the natural-kind reading or whether it relocates the joint — in particular, whether jade is better construed as a functional kind whose multiple realization is no more damaging than multiple realization is supposed to be in the philosophy of mind, and whether the analogy holds up under pressure or merely sounds tidy. Close by saying plainly where the joints vocabulary runs out and the episode stops being about that question at all.
