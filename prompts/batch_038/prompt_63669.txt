I am writing a seminar paper on Henry Sidgwick and have run into an interpretive dispute that I cannot settle by myself, so I would like your help arguing it out properly. Here is the material I am working from, which you may treat as reliable and use as premises without checking it. Sidgwick co-founded the Society for Psychical Research in 1882 and served as its first president; by his own account, his interest in the subject was driven mainly by the hope of finding evidence bearing on survival after death. That hope was not a private eccentricity but grew out of his ethics: in The Methods of Ethics he argues that rational egoism and universal hedonism each rest on premises that look self-evident, that they can genuinely conflict, and that no further principle available within ethics can settle the conflict — the "dualism of the practical reason." He thought the dualism could be overcome only if one could suppose a moral government of the universe in which duty and happiness ultimately coincide, and he did not believe philosophy could demonstrate that supposition. So he went looking for it empirically. The results were modest: he chaired the statistical survey of apparitions published in 1894, which canvassed some seventeen thousand people and collected about seventeen hundred reports of hallucinatory experiences, testing whether the subset coinciding with a death exceeded what chance would predict; he attended sittings in 1895 with a celebrated Italian physical medium at which he and his colleagues detected conjuring; and he ended up accepting telepathy as a real phenomenon while refusing to treat mediumistic communications as proof of survival. Historians disagree about how to file all this. One camp reads the psychical research as an embarrassing lapse: the great rationalist's failure of nerve, a man so frightened by his own conclusion about the fragility of morality that he went ghost-hunting. The other camp reads it as strictly continuous with his system — the empirical wing of a philosophy that had already conceded it could not settle the question from the armchair.

I want to propose a third reading, and I want you to tell me whether it holds up. My suggestion is that the programme was aimed at the wrong target, so that it could not have repaired Sidgwick's ethics even in the most favourable possible world. To make the charge precise, distinguish three propositions that the literature constantly runs together: (S) at least some persons survive bodily death in some form; (J) whatever survives is subject to a morally ordered administration in which happiness is proportioned to virtue; and (K) a finite inquirer can reasonably believe (J) on empirical grounds. Sidgwick's dualism requires something in the neighbourhood of (K) — not merely the logical possibility of posthumous compensation, since a bare possibility does nothing to dissolve a conflict about what one has most reason to do, but a probability high enough to make moral action non-imprudent. Psychical research, however well it went, could deliver at best (S): apparitions, trance communications, and census statistics are evidence that experience continues, not evidence that the continuation is justly administered, and no séance protocol can supply the missing premises about the character of the post-mortem order. My seminar partner objects that this is anachronistic, that it judges Sidgwick by decision-theoretic standards he never held, and that a defender of the continuity reading has two decent replies: first, that repeated communications exhibiting memory and continuity of moral character would provide defeasible inductive support for (J), not proof but enough to render belief reasonable rather than wishful; second, that Sidgwick's aim was perhaps never to establish (K) but only to prevent the empirical question from being dogmatically closed, which is a much cheaper goal that the Society plainly achieved. There is also a sharper version of my own charge, which I am less sure about: a methodological inversion objection, saying that the only kinds of evidence psychical research could ever produce — private, testimonial, contaminated by expectation, vanishing under controls — are exactly the kinds that Sidgwick's own epistemology treats as unfit to carry any weight, so that even a maximal positive result would have been inadmissible by his own standards. On this view the project failed twice over: it sought the wrong proposition, and it sought it with the wrong instrument.

Acting as a professional philosopher who is also a careful historian of nineteenth-century British thought, please write an essay that adjudicates this dispute. Begin with a short planning section — half a page at most, labelled as such — in which you brainstorm the argument space: map the possible verdicts, list the moves each side can make, and identify what you think the decisive question is. Then draft the essay itself. In the draft, define the dualism of the practical reason in your own terms rather than assuming I have it right, state clearly which of (S), (J), and (K) you think Sidgwick actually needed and why, and evaluate my gap argument, saying whether it succeeds wholly, partially, or not at all. Take seriously the two replies on behalf of the continuity reading and the methodological inversion objection, and add at least one further objection of your own devising that you judge stronger than any I have offered; also consider whether your verdict shifts depending on whether Sidgwick is assessed by the evidential standards he professed or by the needs of the ethical system he built. Weigh both sides honestly and construct the strongest version of the position you end up rejecting, but do not finish on a non-committal note: I want an explicit verdict on whether his psychical research was a rational instrument of his ethical project or a betrayal of that project's own standards, and if your verdict is qualified — for instance, rational as a research programme yet incapable of supplying the premise it was meant to supply — say which of the two descriptions carries more weight and justify the qualification rather than hiding in it. Aim for roughly fifteen hundred words in the essay proper. Please do not include a bibliography, reading list, or footnoted citations, and do not invent or reconstruct quotations from Sidgwick's published works; if you want to gesture at a passage, paraphrase it and flag it as your paraphrase. I have the biographical facts above and do not need them retold, so spend your words on argument rather than context.
