I'm 71, retired four years ago from an analytical chemistry lab. Thirty seven years, mostly mass spec, and the last fifteen of those really amounted to teaching other people's students how to read a spectrum. Since retiring I've been reading philosophy, way more than I expected to, and I've tried to write down what I actually believe about perception so I can check it the way I'd check a method before trusting the numbers it gives me. Here's the list. No names attached, because most of it I arrived at from the bench rather than from books.

(1) The visual system is a guessing machine. It takes a crappy signal and fills in — edges, continuity, the whole object. Most of what I "see" is a construction, and it gets built whether or not I endorse it afterwards.

(2) But you cannot decide to see something. I have lain awake willing myself to see a peak that wasn't in the trace and nothing happens. What I believe and what I want sit on the other side of a wall from what I see. Wishful thinking changes what people report, not what they see.

(3) Expertise changes seeing, and I mean literally, not just "makes you quicker." A first-year student sees a hairy baseline with bumps on it. After enough hours I see the contaminant. It took me years to get there, and every student I ever trained described the change as the picture changing, not the thinking speeding up.

(4) When what you expect gets into an experience, its worth less as evidence. That's why I've always distrusted data from the one person in the group who needed the result. Seeing-what-you-want-to-see isn't a weaker kind of seeing, it's a corrupted measurement.

(5) Science works because looking is the court of appeal. Two people can be told to go look at the same plate and settle it there. I grant that no observation comes with zero interpretation attached, but I don't buy the strong version of the story where people inside different frameworks are literally seeing different worlds and can never check each other.

(6) Knowing how to read an instrument is not a pile of facts you could write down. I had brilliant students who could recite all of it and still couldn't see it, and I couldn't tell them what they were missing — I could only put more spectra in front of them.

Does this hang together? I have a hunch that two, three and four can't all stand, but that's a hunch from a man who reads spectra and not an argument, and I'd be pretty annoyed if you just confirmed it for me. Give me an audit, not a lecture, in these sections in this order:

- **Bottom line** — three or four sentences. Does the package stand or not, and if not, roughly how much of it survives.
- **The real fights** — worst first. For each: which of my commitments are involved, why they genuinely can't both be held, and which one has to give. About 150 words each. If two of what I think are separate fights are really one fight in different clothes, merge them and tell me.
- **The false alarms** — pairs that look like they clash and don't. About 100 words each. Name the distinction that dissolves it, and tell me where the dissolve leaks, because I'd rather know that than be handed a clean answer.
- **Repairs and prices** — for each real fight, the cheapest fix and what that fix costs me elsewhere on the list. About 100 words each. I don't want the fix that turns me into a different person, I want the smallest edit.
- **If I could only change one** — about 100 words. The single revision that buys the most coherence across all six, and what I lose by making it.

Nine hundred to twelve hundred words for the whole thing. No bibliography and no name-checking unless a name is doing actual work — I can't go and look things up, and I'd rather you spent the words on the argument. And if one of my six is just badly stated rather than wrong, tell me how to restate it instead of counting it as a casualty.
