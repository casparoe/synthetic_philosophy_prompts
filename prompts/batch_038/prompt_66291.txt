I cover science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and I have spent the better part of two years on a long feature about the founding generation of the Society for Psychical Research — less about the séances than about Henry Sidgwick as a judge of evidence. I have been working through the Society's record case by case, and I now want to test my reading of that record against a file the Council never actually had to decide. Please take the role of a historian of Victorian philosophy and experimental psychology who knows this material well, and think slowly rather than thoroughly: I would much rather have a considered judgment about a few real forks in the road than a survey of the whole terrain.

Here is the track record I have assembled, stated as verdicts rather than as events.

1. Two sisters who performed convincingly at guessing concealed cards and objects in the Society's first years, under conditions the investigators believed excluded signalling, were favourably reported; not long afterwards the same sisters were caught operating an agreed code, and Sidgwick accepted the reversal without hedging or salvage.

2. In the mid-1880s a member of the Society staged slate-writing séances that faithfully imitated those of a well-known professional medium, and the written reports of respectable, attentive sitters were then compared with what had actually occurred and found to be dense with confident errors. Sidgwick treated that demonstration as establishing something general about testimony under séance conditions, not as a curiosity about one set of sitters.

3. Richard Hodgson's investigation in India, reported to the Society in 1885, concluded that Madame Blavatsky's claimed correspondence with the Mahatmas was fabricated. Sidgwick sat on the committee and accepted the finding; the number and social standing of the witnesses who had vouched for the phenomena did not soften it.

4. The Census of Hallucinations, begun in 1889 and reported in 1894 with Alice Johnson, Frederic Myers, Frank Podmore and Eleanor Sidgwick, asked some seventeen thousand people whether they had ever, while awake, had a vivid impression of a figure or voice not attributable to any external cause. About one in ten said yes. Among those, a subset tallied with distant events unknown to the percipient, and a large share of these involved deaths. Taking the daily probability that any given person in the country would die as roughly one in nineteen thousand, the committee found death-coincidences running at roughly one in forty-three, and concluded that chance could not carry the excess. Sidgwick endorsed that conclusion.

5. On the American trance medium the Sidgwicks brought to England and sat with over a winter, he allowed that she produced information she had no normal means of obtaining, and then declined for years to say where it came from, holding telepathy among the living and communication from the dead as both live and neither established.

6. On the paid Italian physical medium brought to Cambridge in the mid-1890s, whose hands were repeatedly found free at the moment the phenomena occurred, his verdict was that the case for genuine physical phenomena had not been made out; he was unmoved by the fact that respected investigators working under looser control in Naples and elsewhere reported otherwise.

7. Throughout, he insisted that telepathy, if established, would be a natural law of mind continuous with hypnotic suggestion and the rest of psychology, and therefore would not by itself establish survival of death — the thing he most wanted evidence for.

Now the file I want you to rule on. I have constructed it out of period-plausible materials; nothing like it was ever formally adjudicated, so I am not asking you to recall a verdict, only to predict one. Suppose the Council had been handed, in 1897, the following.

An amateur who takes no fee, but whose household is maintained by subscriptions from an admiring circle and who has published a book of her experiences. Over three years the Council ran proxy sittings with her: the sitter was unknown to her, chosen by a third party, and given nothing but a surname, while the facts about the deceased had been collected in advance and sealed. In nineteen of forty-four sittings she produced specific correct statements — a cause of death, a childhood illness nowhere recorded, a name known to no living person. Twenty sittings produced nothing usable and five produced confident statements that were simply false. All of it, failures included, was written down at the time and none of it was published before the sitting. Because the sitter did not know the deceased, telepathy from a mind present in the room seems excluded.

Separately, and never under Council control, at six evening sittings in her own house a lamp was seen to move and raps answered questions; one competent observer, writing afterwards, could not swear that her hands had been free.

Her own account is that the communications come from a brother who died eleven years earlier, and who also gave her in advance the date of a cousin's death, which matched. Finally, the committee's minutes and transcripts were kept by an assistant who was a convinced spiritualist and who, it emerged, tidied the wording of some transcripts before filing them.

What I want from you:

- Begin by brainstorming. List the candidate decision rules that would generate verdicts one through seven — at least four, and please include one you think misdescribes Sidgwick but that fits the record just as well as the others. For each, say what it implies about the 1897 file.
- Identify the pair of rules that diverge most sharply on this file, and say which feature of the file does the separating.
- Give me your best single prediction of what Sidgwick would have ruled: on the proxy-sitting component, on the uncontrolled physical component, and on the file as a whole, together with your reasons for preferring your rule over the runner-up.
- Say whether he would have treated the assistant's tidying as contaminating the whole file or as quarantining only the affected parts, and what that choice would reveal about which rule he is in fact running.
- Name one further file, differing from this one in exactly one respect, that would settle the matter between your two leading rules, and say which way each rule comes out on it.
- Then tell me what would change your mind: which consideration, if I produced it from the archive, would flip your prediction, and where your reasoning here is most likely to fail. In particular, which of the seven verdicts above would you most expect to read differently if you had the documents in front of you rather than my summary of them?

Two housekeeping points. No bibliography, no reading list, no citations — I cannot check them and I do not want invented ones; if you think a detail in my summary is off, flag it as a doubt rather than silently correcting me. And keep the whole thing to roughly a thousand words, written so that a section of it could survive into print.
