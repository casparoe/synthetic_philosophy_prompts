I'm in an undergrad seminar on the ethics and safety of machine intelligence this term (mostly philosophy people, I'm the one who actually codes) and this week we read the off-switch game paper — Hadfield-Menell et al., the one where the human can press the robot's off switch and the robot can decide whether to disable it. I followed the setup fine but I got stuck on the result and my TA kind of hand-waved it, so I want to understand the mechanism instead of just the theorem statement. Don't point me at more papers btw, I already have a pile I'm not going to read.

What confuses me is that the fix seems to come in two steps that pull in opposite directions. Step one: an agent that takes its reward function for granted has an incentive to disable the switch, except in the special case where the human is perfectly rational — a dead agent collects no future reward, so it pays to remove the risk of dying, and the only reason not to is if the press reliably means you're doing badly. Step two: the proposed repair is to make the robot uncertain about its own utility function and to treat the human's action as evidence about what that utility function is. But then the robot's willingness to be switched off still tracks how rational/informative it takes the press to be — turn the dial on human error and the deference drains back out. So I dont get what the uncertainty actually *did*. If being unsure about your objective is what makes you tolerant of shutdown, why does making the human noisy undo it? What's the real difference between "I don't know what I should be maximizing" and "I don't know whether the person with their finger on my button is a trustworthy source of information about what I should be maximizing"? Both are uncertainty, but only one of them seems to generate deference, and I can't articulate why.

Connected thing that blew up in our discussion section: suppose the press carries no information whatsoever about the robot's performance — somebody leans on the button by accident, it gets hit during a scheduled maintenance window, an attacker forces it, a coin flip settles it. If deference is downstream of treating the press as evidence, then in those cases the robot should resist, and it should resist in exactly the way we were trying to prevent. Is that a failure of corrigibility, or does it show that corrigibility was never really about resisting-shutdown-as-such but about something else? And relatedly, doesn't a robot whose compliance depends on its model of the human have an obvious incentive to go work on that model — to make sure the human is the kind of person who presses the button for good reasons — instead of making sure the button stays pressable? Feels like that reopens the whole problem through a different door and I want to know if I'm reading too much into it.
