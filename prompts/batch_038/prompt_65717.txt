I work in platform engineering at a mid-sized SaaS company in Berlin, where I maintain the billing and consent-management services — the bits that decide who is enrolled in what, on whose authority, and how they get out again. I have no formal training in philosophy, so please treat me as an intelligent outsider rather than as someone who knows the literature. What has got me stuck is the German church tax, which I encounter professionally as much as personally. As I understand it: certain religious bodies that hold public-law corporation status may have their dues collected by the state revenue authorities as a surcharge on income tax, at eight per cent in Bavaria and Baden-Württemberg and nine per cent in the other Länder; the state retains a collection fee for the service; liability is determined not by belief, practice or any act of agreement by the payer, but by an affiliation recorded at address registration and matched against the data held by the tax office, which is why someone baptised as an infant and never inside a church since pays it for forty years; and the only way out is Kirchenaustritt, a formal declaration made in person to a civil authority such as the Standesamt or an Amtsgericht, for which several Länder charge a fee. In my own work I would never be permitted to ship a billing relationship with those properties: enrolment triggered by a third party's record, no consent artefact the payer ever sees or signs, an exit path that costs money and requires physical attendance, and an intermediary that keeps a percentage. I am aware that EU data-protection law takes the opposite view of exactly these features — that withdrawal must be as easy as giving consent — and yet that same regime contains a carve-out allowing churches that already had their own comprehensive data-protection rules to continue applying them under their own supervision. So the legal order seems to hold both principles at once.

The comparative detail is what makes me think there is something to reconstruct rather than a mere historical accident. In England, as far as I can tell, the established church has no state register of members and no procedure with civil effect for leaving it; belonging is ascriptive through baptism, and parishioners hold legal rights to be married or buried in their parish church irrespective of what they believe, while bishops sit in the upper chamber and the sovereign must be in communion with the church. In France the 1905 separation applies nationally except in Alsace-Moselle, which was German at the time and where the state still pays the salaries of clergy of the recognised faiths and provides religious instruction in state schools. And the United States apparently does the reverse of Germany again: no establishment, but churches are treated as exempt without ever having to apply for recognition or file returns. My reading of that spread is that these are not four implementations of one idea but possibly three or four different ideas of what a religious body is — a membership association, a status conferred by the polity, a public utility, a private conscience the state declines to look at — and that the German arrangement in particular does not fit any of them cleanly, since it taxes people for a belonging they never chose and then sells the exit at a price.

What I want from you is a reconstruction of what the church-tax arrangement, in its German form and read against the English and Alsatian variants, actually presupposes philosophically: what view of religious belonging, of the person as a member of communities, and of the state's competence in matters of belief it only makes sense under, and which rival views it quietly forecloses. Please be careful to separate what the practice strictly requires from what it merely happens to sit well with, and tell me plainly where it is underdetermined — where several incompatible theories would license the very same administrative machinery. I would also like the sharpest inconsistency between the national variants identified rather than listed, and a concrete answer to what would have to change in the practice if the presupposed view were replaced by a straightforwardly consent-based one. Deliver this as exactly five numbered items, in this order: (1) the single most fundamental presupposition; (2) the rival theory the arrangement looks superficially compatible with but in fact rules out; (3) the point at which the practice is underdetermined; (4) the sharpest inconsistency between the German, English and Alsatian arrangements; (5) what would have to change under a consent-based replacement. Each item should run to one or two sentences only, with no introduction and no conclusion, and no reading list, no bibliography and no name-checking of philosophers — I want your own analysis. After the five items, add one further line stating how confident you are in the reconstruction overall and singling out the specific claim you are least sure of. Please write in British English.
