I am in the second half of a third-year epistemology module whose final two weeks have been given over to self-knowledge, luminosity and the KK principle, and for the closing seminar each of us has to bring a case of our own construction rather than a summary of the reading. Mine is meant to test a question our tutor raised in passing and nobody followed up: whether the denial of luminosity and the denial of KK amount to one thesis wearing two hats, or to two separable theses that can come apart in either direction. I would like help building the case to specification, because my own attempts have either smuggled in an infallible introspective faculty or simply asserted the verdicts I wanted.

The specification is this. Design a single thought experiment featuring one ordinary subject, S, and one condition C, such that all four of the following hold at once:

1. C is luminous for S in the sense at issue in Williamson's anti-luminosity argument: in every case of the relevant series in which C obtains, S knows that C obtains.
2. C is not disqualified as trivial from the outset. It must not be a condition whose obtaining is already guaranteed by S's existing, or by her thinking anything whatsoever; it must obtain in some of her cases and fail to obtain in others, including cases that are in the ordinary sense very close to ones in which it obtains. I want an explicit example of such a near-miss case.
3. KK fails for S with respect to that very knowledge at that very time: S knows that C obtains, and does not know that she knows it.
4. The failure at (3) must not be manufactured out of S's being generally muddled about knowledge, her lacking the concept of knowledge, any stipulation that iterated knowledge attributions are simply unavailable, or any device that impairs her cognition. She is a competent, alert, ordinarily reflective adult.

Constraints on the setting: realistic rather than science-fictional — a bedroom, a library, a commuting train, a waiting room. No demons, no vats, no perfect predictors, no introspection-enhancing technology, no idealised or specially gifted subject, and nothing that confers privileged access on S by definitional fiat. The scenario itself should run to a single paragraph of no more than about 150 words; the demonstration that follows may be as long as it needs to be.

Then show me that the case meets the specification, rather than merely describing it as though it did:

- State C precisely, and state the series of cases over which you are running the argument.
- Luminosity: explain why S knows C in every case in which C obtains, and set out the margin-for-error premise in the form your own case requires, identifying the exact step of the inductive argument at which that premise ceases to apply. Formulations of the premise vary, so give me yours explicitly.
- KK: identify what blocks the second-order knowledge, and explain why whatever blocks it does not also block the first-order knowledge. This asymmetry is the crux of the whole exercise; if you cannot account for it, the case has failed and I would rather be told so.
- Say which single feature of the construction is doing the work, and what happens if it is removed: does luminosity go, does KK come back, or do the two collapse together?
- Close with two attacks and a comparison of them. First, how a defender of KK would attack the case, including whether retreating from a principle about actual iterated knowledge to one about being in a position to know rescues it. Second, how a defender of the anti-luminosity argument would deny that C is luminous in the sense the argument targets, notwithstanding (1). Say whether these are one objection in two sets of words or genuinely distinct ones, and what my conclusion for the seminar ought to be if only one of them succeeds.

Assume I have read the standard material on the anti-luminosity argument and on Hintikka's defence of KK: I need neither a survey, nor a history of either debate, nor any references. What I need is a construction that works and an honest assessment of how much it proves.
