ok so my school runs this discussion group on philosophy of science and there's an argument about DNA that's been going for like two months and nobody has moved an inch, and I want you to figure out what kind of argument it actually is instead of just picking a winner.

The question: is DNA literally a code, or is "genetic code" just a metaphor that got stuck? Both sides think the other one is being sloppy, which is what makes it so annoying.

The people who say it's a real code (my bio teacher is one of them) go like this: a code in the strict sense is an arbitrary mapping between symbols and what they stand for, plus something that can read the mapping. Codons and amino acids fit that exactly. Nothing about the chemistry of the three bases UUU forces it to come out as phenylalanine — you need tRNAs and the ribosome and the synthetase enzymes to do the translating, and that machinery could in principle implement a totally different table. And different tables do exist: inside our own mitochondria, a codon that means "stop" for the rest of the cell gets read as tryptophan, and one that means isoleucine gets read as methionine. So the table is swappable, like a cipher key. They stress that this puts DNA in a different category from "blueprint" and "recipe" — nobody thinks a recipe is a code — so it's not just the same tired metaphor with a fancier name.

The people who say it's a metaphor (my friend, who's pretty good at this) go like this: there's no sender and no message about anything. DNA doesn't represent the organism or describe it, it's a molecule in a causal process, and saying the cell "reads" it is exactly as loose as saying a thermostat "reads" the temperature. They also say the arbitrariness point is a dodge — "arbitrary" here just means "we haven't found the chemistry yet" — and worse, there's no independent key, because the tRNAs and the ribosome are themselves built out of gene products, so it's a closed causal loop, not a symbol system standing apart from the thing it encodes. And they claim the information talk never does any work that plain causation couldn't do.

heres my own contribution, which nobody in the group liked. I made up a case: suppose we find a rock formation where the mineral layers happen to line up with a table that a machine could read out as instructions for assembling something, and we can establish for certain no mind ever made it. Is that a code? If my teacher says yes, then his criteria has nothing specifically to do with being alive, and calling DNA a code stops being interesting — it becomes true of half the universe. If he says no, he's smuggling in a sender, which is the one thing he insists he doesn't need. I also noticed both sides use "arbitrary" in two different senses — one means "not forced by the chemistry," the other means "you could actually go reassign it" — and a codon table is basically not reassignable inside a living organism without wrecking it, so on the second sense DNA looks nothing like a cipher. My working theory, which I'm not married to, is that most of this fight is verbal and there's one real empirical question buried under it about how much recoding a genome can actually survive, since people are already making organisms with rewritten codes in labs.

So diagnose it for me. Is this a substantive disagreement, a verbal one about the word "code" or about "information," two different questions getting answered under one heading, a hidden empirical question, or bedrock intuitions neither side can argue for? Use what each side actually says, not a survey of positions. Then tell me what would count as progress given your diagnosis — a distinction, an experiment, a formal result, changing the question — or argue that no progress should be expected and say why. And does coming down on either side change anything about the biology you'd actually do?
