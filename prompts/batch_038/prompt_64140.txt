I have no academic training in philosophy. I spent my working life in property insurance, reading loss reports after earthquakes, floods and windstorms, and what always struck me was how little the size of the event had to do with the number of dead: the toll is mostly a function of how densely people are housed, how the buildings were put up, who could afford to live where, and how fast anyone could get out. Turning that over for years, I ended up at the Lisbon earthquake of 1 November 1755 and the argument it set off, and I have been working on it by myself ever since. What I would like is a careful analysis of that episode as a test of Leibniz's claim that this is the best of all possible worlds.

The facts as I have them, so that nothing here depends on your looking anything up, and I am not asking for references or a bibliography at the end: Lisbon was destroyed on the morning of All Saints' Day by the shaking, then by a tsunami up the Tagus, then by fires fed by the day's candles, and tens of thousands died; the Marquis of Pombal afterwards rebuilt the lower city on a grid with construction meant to resist shaking. Leibniz had been dead nearly forty years. Voltaire wrote a long poem on the disaster, composed within weeks and published in 1756, directed at an axiom of the general form that all is well, an axiom that ran Leibniz's optimism together with Pope's; in 1759 he put the same target in the mouth of Pangloss in Candide. Rousseau answered Voltaire in a letter of 18 August 1756 defending providence, arguing among other things that nature did not construct twenty thousand houses of six or seven storeys on that site, and that if the inhabitants had been more evenly spread out and more lightly lodged the damage would have been much less, perhaps of no account.

First, do the mapping honestly. Set out what the optimality claim actually commits Leibniz to — a choice among whole compossible worlds rather than among episodes within one; a criterion involving the greatest variety of phenomena together with the greatest order or simplicity of laws; the permission of evil rather than its willing; the insistence that the world be judged as a whole and not from one vantage point inside it; the point he presses against Arnauld, that you cannot subtract a single event from a world and keep everything else. Then say which of these resources does real work against Lisbon and which is decoration.

Second, I have a test of my own and I want you to use it, attack it, and tell me if it is broken. Call it the minimal-tweak test: an event counts against the optimality claim only if there is a nearby possible world — same basic laws, same people, broadly the same history — in which that event does not occur, or occurs with far less suffering, and in which nothing that makes this world good is lost; if every such world is worse overall, the optimist has an answer. My worry is that Leibniz's own argument about not being able to subtract a single event means there are no nearby worlds at all, so the test can never be satisfied and no finite disaster can ever count against the claim. Is that fatal — a doctrine that nothing could endanger — or is it simply what a claim about whole worlds ought to look like? Does it turn optimism from a thesis into a posture?

Third, where does Voltaire's poem actually land? My suspicion, which may be quite wrong, is that it is not a counterexample but a change of subject: from whether the total is optimal to what a sufferer may be told while standing in the ruins. If so, does that make it philosophically weaker and rhetorically stronger, or is the consolation optimism offers itself a substantive claim that can be false and, in Voltaire's view, cruelly false?

Fourth, Rousseau is the part I care most about. Does his letter supply the strongest available Leibnizian reply — relocating the death toll from God's choice of laws to human arrangements, so that what remains is the kind of evil the theodicy was always entitled to? Or does it concede the crucial thing: that a world with the very same physical laws could have contained vastly less suffering, which sounds like an admission that this world is improvable and therefore not the best? Note that modern disaster practice is Rousseau's argument turned into an industry — Chile in 2010 absorbed a larger shock than Haiti did the same year and lost a few hundred people, while Haiti lost well over a hundred thousand. Does that make his letter the durable half of the exchange and the optimality claim the loser by default, or does it show that the claim was never about anything that loss data could touch?

Fifth, run the episode in the other direction, as evidence about the theory. Did Lisbon do to optimism what a genuine anomaly does to a hypothesis — forcing restriction, revision, or an auxiliary assumption such as the free-will relocation or the observation that the simplest laws entail exceptionless processes, catastrophic ones included? Or was the claim already immune, so that the earthquake altered only its social standing, with Pangloss's caricature doing more damage than any argument? And is there a version of the claim that survives Lisbon honestly, one that says something a person in the ruins could accept without being insulted?

Close with a one-line verdict: does the episode defeat, weaken, or leave untouched the optimality claim, and why.

Then tell me how far to trust your own analysis, and what would change your mind. Specifically: state which argument, distinction, or fact would move you from Rousseau rescues the claim to Rousseau concedes it, or the reverse; identify the step in your reasoning you think is most likely to fail; and flag any place where you suspect you are reading Leibniz or Rousseau more or less charitably than their texts deserve. If you think I have mischaracterized either of them above, correct me rather than building on my error.
