I am a seventeen-year-old high school student, and for the last two years I have been the lead administrator of a mid-sized Minecraft server — about forty concurrent players at peak, three hundred accounts on the whitelist. My real job is the economy. A currency plugin mints coins through mob drops, a daily vote reward, and admin-shop buybacks of farmed crops; it burns coins through a fixed-price gear shop, land-claim fees, and seasonal cosmetic crates. Next to that sits a player-to-player auction house where prices float. Our recurring crisis is that veterans accumulate balances they never spend, so the supply is effectively frozen, nobody new can outbid anyone, and every proposed fix — another sink, a partial wipe, a season-only currency, a tax on idle balances — gets argued in a vocabulary I know cold: faucets and sinks, turnover rate, hoarding, inflation, coins per active player, whether the economy is "dead." I can read the plugin configuration and query its transaction log directly. I have one year of AP Macroeconomics and no philosophy training.

For an independent-study seminar this year I read Berkeley's *Querist*. The line I underlined, query 23 in the edition I was given, is: "Whether the true idea of money, as such, be not altogether that of a ticket or counter?" Around it he argues that money is useful only insofar as it stirs up industry and lets people share one another's labour; that a smaller quantity circulating quickly is equivalent in effect to a larger quantity circulating slowly; that gold and silver have no virtue beyond setting people to work; that a nation is enriched by the opinion and will of its people exciting them to industry; that the creation of new wants is the likeliest route to making a people industrious; and that arbitrary tampering with the denomination of coin is a cheat on the public. He also pressed a plan for a national bank issuing paper to employ the Irish poor. Those are my paraphrases, so treat them as approximate.

Restate Berkeley's monetary position, claim by claim, in the vocabulary of server economy design — minting and burning, faucets and sinks, turnover, hoarding, float, admin authority, auction-house prices, retention metrics — as faithfully as you can manage. Then give me the residue report. For each claim, say whether it translates cleanly, whether it translates only with distortion, and whether it has no expression at all in my framework. Keep the translation honest in both directions: do not simply dress my plugin configuration in eighteenth-century language and announce that Berkeley anticipated it, and do not silently substitute my admin team for his sovereign or my players' choices for the necessities his labourers faced. Where you have to make a substitution to keep going, name it.

On the basis of that report, tell me which of the following the residue indicts. Is my vocabulary missing something — sovereignty and legal tender, credit creation, taxation as a reason to need money at all, subsistence, a genuine collective will rather than an administrator's config — such that Berkeley's claims are inexpressible in it? Or does the failure of translation show that his claims were less determinate than they sound, doing their real work through institutional facts he left implicit? I will add one test case: a server currency is about the purest instance I can construct of money with no intrinsic value whatsoever, coins behaving exactly as tickets and counters; if Berkeley is simply right there, then something other than the ticket claim must be carrying the weight, and I want to know what. Note also which of his claims my server appears to contradict — an idle community can be rich, and hoarding seems able to break an economy whose supply is adequate — and whether the one place an external exchange rate exists for our coins, the real-money trading we ban as cheating, gives his view of a money's value as internal to a community's will any foothold in my framework or none.

Please do not send me a bibliography or a reading list; I want your own reconstruction and your own verdict.
