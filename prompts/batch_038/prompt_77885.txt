I spent 35 years in field ecology — mostly seed dispersal and how plant populations creep across a landscape — so "fitness" was always a number I could actually measure. Retired four years ago and, to my wife's mild alarm, I've fallen into philosophy. I now run a Tuesday-evening ideas group out of the back room of our public library: about 14 of us, mostly retirees, two ex-teachers, three engineers, one nurse. Ninety minutes, long tables, no projector, budget roughly whatever I can carry home from the office-supply place.

Lately I can't stop thinking about the meme idea — Dawkins in The Selfish Gene, 1976 — and about the more ambitious versions people have tried since (Blackmore wanted to turn it into a real scientific theory in 1999, predictions and everything). Half of me finds it intoxicating, the way any tidy formalism is. The other half, the half that spent decades arguing with modelers, suspects its an analogy wearing a lab coat: I can't point to the unit, I can't tell copying apart from reconstruction, and the objection that culture simply doesn't come in discrete packages seems to me to have real force.

So I want an activity, not a talk. No framing lecture up front — they should walk into the problem and only get the vocabulary afterwards.

Here's the half-formed thing I've been sketching on napkins; improve it or throw it out. Two seperate transmission chains running at once in the same room. A copy channel, where each person copies a drawing from the previous person's output and never sees the original. And a retell channel, where each person hears a short account and writes down what they'll pass on. Then a round where I seed the retell chain with a variant that is false but arresting, and we watch whether it displaces the accurate one. Then a round where I deliberately raise the fidelity — notes allowed, diagrams allowed — to see whether anything cumulative turns up that no one in the room designed.

What I'd like back:

- a minute-by-minute plan for the 90 minutes;
- a materials list plus what I have to prep in advance versus what I can do that afternoon;
- the actual words I should read aloud to each chain, since instructions passed around verbally will themselves get mangled, which is sort of the point but not the point;
- a debrief that ties specific moments in the game to specific parts of the idea — variation, differential retention, whether a variant can win against its host's interest, and the unit problem;
- an honest list of what the game cannot show, so I know what I have to supply out of my own mouth afterwards. I already suspect a few things: one chain isn't the population-level process the theory is about, the piece of paper isn't the replicator, and they know they're being watched.

Also tell me what predictably goes wrong the first time I run it. People peeking at the original, a chain collapsing because link four is terse, the engineers turning it into a competition. And the one I dread: Ray, who has read all of this and will announce halfway through that it's just the telephone game and proves nothing whatever about culture. Give me something to say to Ray that isn't a dodge, because I think he's partly right and I don't want to bluff.

No references or reading lists, please. I'm not going to chase anything down, and I'd rather you not guess at titles or pages.
