I'm giving the guest paper at our department's joint methods workshop next month, and I want to walk in prepared. Its a room that's mostly historians — early modernists plus a few twentieth-century political historians who basically live in the archives — along with three comparative politics people who do process tracing, one economic historian who's the only other person in the building who has ever run a regression, and a couple of grad students. Nobody's hostile exactly, but the default assumption in that room is that history is about understanding particular events, and that anyone importing methodology from the social sciences is about to flatten something important. Thirty minutes plus whatever the questions turn into, whiteboard only, and I've been told in advance that a slide with formal notation on it would read as a provocation.

Here's the claim I'm defending, and I want it attacked in the room's own terms rather than in mine: most published historical explanations are overfitted, in a sense that is close to literal rather than metaphorical. The historian picks the case after the outcome is known, then goes into the record looking for a mechanism that makes that outcome intelligible; and the features that make the result a satisfying story — one through-line, actors whose intentions carry the plot, a reversal that pays off at the end — are the same features that make it unauditable. What I'm proposing is a discipline-level reform. A historical explanation should have to ship with something like a pre-registration: before you open the archive on a case, you commit in writing to an explanandum that doesn't already presuppose the outcome you know happened, to two or three rival accounts you'd count as genuine competitors, and to what you'd expect to find in the record if each of those rivals were true. Plus held-out cases — you develop the account on the case you know best, then run it against the ones you didn't choose, and you report what happens to it. I'll be leaning on the analogy to backtesting a trading strategy on the same data you selected it with, and I'll use three running examples, a financial crisis, a revolution and a firm's collapse, all chosen because the standard explanations of them are famous.

For each one: how much damage it does, a two-minute reply I can deliver out loud without notes, and an honest line on where that reply is soft. Tag each objection by whether it's aimed at the analogy or at the proposal itself, because I think I can give up the analogy and keep the proposal, and I want to know which ones survive that trade.
