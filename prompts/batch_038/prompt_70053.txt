Two members of our department — call them the Dissolver and the Residualist — have spent a decade arguing about Zeno's paradoxes of motion, and a weekend workshop has finally been convened with the express aim of producing a document both will sign. You are the rapporteur. Draft that joint statement. It is not an adjudication: we do not want a verdict on who is right, and any passage that quietly settles the argument in one party's favor should be struck.

The two positions, stated as each party would recognize them:

- The Dissolver holds that the Dichotomy and the Achilles are answered by the modern treatment of convergent infinite series and of the continuum: infinitely many intervals can sum to a finite distance, and infinitely many traversals can be performed in a finite time, and nothing further — metaphysical or otherwise — needs to be said. Whatever is left over is at most a physical question (is spacetime discrete at some scale?), not a philosophical one.
- The Residualist holds that this resolution addresses a question Zeno did not ask, and does so by assuming the very conception of a continuum that the paradoxes put under pressure; and that it leaves untouched the substantive issues about what it is to carry out an infinite sequence of acts with no last member, what it is to be in motion at an instant, and whether the state of a system at a limit time is fixed by its states at all earlier times.

The statement must contain, in this order:

1. Agreed claims, numbered. Include agreements about the arguments themselves — bearing in mind that Zeno's own words do not survive and the arguments reach us in paraphrase through Aristotle and much later commentators — about what the mathematics of limits and convergent series does establish, and about what is not in dispute at all. No agreed claim may be one that merely looks like agreement because it is empty; where a claim is trivial but worth recording, mark it as trivial and say what work it is doing in the document.

2. A disagreements table. For each live disagreement: its precise formulation; a column giving the wording of the dispute that the Dissolver could sign; a column giving the wording the Residualist could sign; and a column stating what would have to be shown for each party to give up their view.

3. Cruxes — the sub-questions such that settling one would actually move at least one party. For each crux, name the case or argument both parties accept as bearing on it, and record each party's prediction in advance: not a hedge, but a committed answer to a specified question. At least four cruxes must engage the following stipulated cases, and for each the statement must say explicitly whether the two parties regard the case as relevantly like or unlike Achilles' run, and on what grounds:
 (a) a lamp toggled on and off at intervals halving toward a limit minute, where the earlier states leave open whether it is on or off at that minute;
 (b) the staccato runner, who covers each successive halving interval and then rests for a progressively shorter pause before beginning the next;
 (c) a device that performs its nth step in 1/2^n minutes and so, after one minute, has executed infinitely many steps and displays an answer that no finite number of steps could yield;
 (d) the arrow: whether anything in the resolution gives content to being in motion at this very instant, or whether motion must be analyzed away into the occupation of different positions at different times.
Where a disagreement turns on a distinction — for instance, between two senses in which an endless task might be said to be completed — state the distinction and specify which sense each party's argument requires.

4. A short section identifying any part of the dispute that is merely verbal, in particular whether the two parties mean different things by saying a paradox has been solved, with an argument for that diagnosis rather than a bare declaration of it.

5. A closing paragraph, jointly signed, saying whether the parties believe the residual disagreement is resolvable by argument at all — and if not, what it is a disagreement about.

Throughout, no loaded vocabulary in Sections 1 through 3: no begging the question, pseudo-problem, or obviously. Every formulation must be one its target party could sign without feeling caricatured, and the interesting cases are the ones where achieving that forces a genuine reformulation of the position rather than a dilution of it.
