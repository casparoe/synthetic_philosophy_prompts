I work in freight logistics, and some years ago someone mentioned to me that North American time zones were invented by the railroads rather than by any government, and I have been chewing on it ever since. Here is the version I have assembled; correct me where I have it wrong. Before 1883 there was no shared civil time: cities kept local solar time, each railroad kept its own, and a long trip meant resetting your watch several times. Rather than wait for Congress, the heads of the major railroads met in Chicago and agreed among themselves to divide the continent into time zones; William Allen, secretary of the railroads' General Time Convention, pushed the plan specifically so that the government would not impose one of its own. Astronomical observatories agreed to send the time signal by telegraph, four zones took effect on Sunday, November 18, 1883, towns and cities were free to adopt them or not, many did and some held out, and Congress did not write time zones into federal law until the Standard Time Act of 1918.

Analyze this episode through Winner's question about whether artifacts have politics. Do the mapping carefully first: which of Winner's two senses, if either, does a time zone fall into — a technical arrangement whose design settles an issue in a community, or a technology that is political in itself because it requires a particular form of authority? Does the railroad timetable have politics, or is it merely a coordination device that any government would have arrived at anyway? Then run it in the other direction and say what the episode does to technological determinism, given that the technical problem was genuine while the solution was contingent, privately arranged, and unratified by any state for thirty-five years. Be explicit about where Winner's categories go silent or misdescribe what happened rather than forcing the case to fit. I want a definite verdict you would argue for, not a survey of the positions with the choice left to me. Please answer in American English.
