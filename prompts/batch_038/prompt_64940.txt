I am a junior, and for the past two years I have run the puzzle and machine-learning club at my high school. Once a month I write a small set of Bongard problems for the members: twelve little diagrams per problem, split into two groups of six, where the task is to say what concept puts the first group together and keeps it out of the second. The format comes from Mikhail Bongard's 1967 book on pattern recognition and became widely known through Hofstadter's discussion of it in *Gödel, Escher, Bach*. I mention this only so that we are working from the same picture of the thing; I know you cannot look anything up, and I am not asking for sources, references, or a reading list. I am asking you to build something.

The club has drifted into an argument that I would like to convert into a diagnostic instrument. Members who solve the same problem describe what they did in ways that strike me as different in kind, not merely in wording. One insists she tests each box against an explicit rule and keeps whichever rule survives. One says the difference between the two groups "pops out" and that he could point to it correctly long before he could state it, and that demanding a statement is a category error. One argues that most of my problems have several correct answers and that I am only pretending otherwise. One says the actual skill is guessing which kind of rule a Bongard designer would choose, so that the answer is fixed by a convention among puzzle-makers rather than by the twelve diagrams. Another says all of these people are confused, because there is no deep question here at all: these are puzzles, not windows onto how concepts are acquired.

What I want from you is a sorting quiz of nine items, plus a scoring key, that places a member on this question: **when someone solves one of my problems, what exactly have they got hold of, and what makes their rule the right one rather than another rule that also separates my two groups?**

The instrument must satisfy the following.

1. Nine items. Items 1 through 3 must be my own problems, given below. Invent items 4 through 9 yourself in the same style. Since neither of us can attach figures, every item must be stateable in words using only a small vocabulary: circle, square, triangle, line, dot, outlined, filled, count, size, position, touching, inside.

2. Each item is a concrete case or scenario with two or three answer options. No item may name a position, and no item may use terms such as *underdetermination*, *Gestalt*, *simplicity*, *description length*, or *concept*. A member must be able to answer every item without having any idea what the labels are, so that the quiz sorts people by what they actually believe about the cases.

3. At least one item must concern a solver who reliably picks the right boxes but cannot say why. At least one must concern what the negative group contributes — whether six positives alone would have been enough. At least one must ask what should be said when two rules fit all twelve boxes and are equally easy to state.

4. Build the space before you name it. Lay out first the questions and the combinations of answers they permit, and only then locate actual positions in that space. I expect at least six leaves. One must be the deflationary position that there is no interesting question here. At least one must be a cell that I believe nobody actually occupies; if you think I am wrong about that, say so and name the view. Give every leaf a name — or mark it unnamed — and one sentence on its most awkward commitment.

5. Supply a scoring key that maps *patterns* of answers onto leaves, not individual answers onto individual views. Say explicitly what to do with a profile that is internally inconsistent, since several of my members will produce one, and what to conclude about someone who picks the same option nine times.

6. Flag any pair of leaves that your items cannot really separate. Do not paper over it; tell me which further case would be needed, or say that no case could do it.

7. Note the unoccupied combinations and diagnose each: is the view incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored?

8. My own draft taxonomy has four buckets — counting rules, position rules, "you just have to see it" rules, and rules about how the pictures were drawn. I suspect this is wrong in a way I cannot articulate. Dissolve it rather than preserving it, but tell me which of my four buckets survives intact and which turns out to straddle two of your leaves.

Here are my three problems. I use "outlined" for a figure drawn as a line only and "filled" for a solid black figure.

**Problem A.** Positives — I: a large outlined square with a small filled circle inside it, touching nothing. II: a large outlined circle with a small outlined triangle inside it, touching nothing. III: a large outlined triangle with a small filled square inside it, touching nothing. IV: a large outlined circle with a small filled circle inside it, plus one filled circle outside. V: a large outlined square with two small filled circles inside it, touching neither each other nor the square. VI: a large outlined triangle with a small outlined circle inside it, plus one filled circle outside. Negatives — 1: a large outlined square and, separate from it, a small filled circle. 2: a large outlined square with a small filled circle straddling its right edge, half in and half out. 3: a large outlined circle with a small filled square resting exactly on its boundary, touching but not crossing. 4: a large outlined square and a large outlined circle overlapping each other, neither inside the other. 5: two small filled circles and one filled circle, all separate. 6: three small outlined squares in a row, each touching the next. The rule I intended was that some figure lies wholly inside another figure without touching it.

**Problem B.** Positives — I: two filled circles and one outlined square. II: three filled triangles, one outlined circle, one outlined square. III: a single filled square. IV: four filled circles and three outlined triangles. V: two filled triangles, one filled square, and two outlined circles. VI: five filled circles and four outlined squares. Negatives — 1: one filled circle and one outlined circle. 2: one filled triangle and three outlined squares. 3: two filled triangles and two outlined circles. 4: one filled square and two outlined triangles. 5: three filled circles and six outlined triangles. 6: a single outlined square. The rule I intended was that the box contains more filled figures than outlined figures. I should note that a member proposed "an odd number of figures," which fits every positive and is killed by negatives 4, 5, and 6.

**Problem C.** Positives — I: a triangle above a circle. II: a square above a triangle. III: a circle above a square. IV: a triangle above a square. V: a circle above a triangle. VI: a square above a circle. Negatives — 1: a circle below a triangle. 2: a triangle below a square. 3: a square below a circle. 4: a triangle to the left of a circle. 5: a circle to the right of a square. 6: a square overlapping a triangle. The rule I intended was that one figure is above the other, but I can no longer tell that apart from "the two figures are vertically aligned," or from "the line joining their centres is vertical," and I could not have said which of the three I meant when I drew them.

Finally, for each of A, B, and C, tell me whether it is well-posed on your own account, and if not, what I would have to add or remove to make it so. The whole thing has to fit on two sides of a page as a club handout, so keep the items short even where the scoring key is not.
