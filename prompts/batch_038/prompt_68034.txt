I'm a second-year graduate student in a seminar on abolitionist political thought, and my seminar paper concerns Frederick Douglass's break with Garrison over the meaning of the Constitution. Over the holidays I read a long essay in a general-interest magazine about the prehistory of living constitutionalism, which offered the following account of that break:

> "For most of his career as an agitator Douglass repeated William Lloyd Garrison's verdict that the Constitution was a pro-slavery compact, and he was right to: the clauses on representation and on the return of persons owing service were written to protect slavery, and the men who drafted them said as much in private. What changed in 1851 was not Douglass's view of the historical record — he never seriously disputed what the framers intended — but his politics. Once he concluded that the Union could be turned into an instrument of emancipation rather than a covenant with slaveholders, he needed a constitution that could be used that way, and he promptly found one, announcing that the document's preamble promised justice and liberty to everyone and that not a single clause ever names a slave. That last argument is the tell, and the weakest link in his case: it mistakes the drafters' euphemisms for their absence, since the three-fifths and fugitive provisions are transparently about slavery whatever they are called. The mature Douglass is therefore best understood as an originalist manqué — he kept the originalist frame, in which meaning is fixed by what the framers meant, and simply substituted a friendlier set of intentions. The general lesson is the interesting one: constitutional meaning tracks political need, and Douglass is proof that a determined advocate can argue a document into almost anything."

Much of that matches what I thought I knew, and the essay is clearly written by someone who knows the period. But two things make me uneasy, and I lack the grounding to say whether my unease is justified. First, I have a vague recollection, from his later lectures and from his second autobiography, that Douglass was pointedly dismissive of the private purposes of the drafters — that he treated what they secretly hoped to accomplish as legally irrelevant and insisted the instrument be construed by the ordinary rules applicable to any written text, which would make the label originalist manqué not a simplification but a reversal. Second, I believe I was told somewhere that he argued one of the representation provisions operated against the slaveholding states rather than in their favor, which is not a move the essay's summary leaves any room for; I may be misremembering, and I would want that checked rather than assumed.

So please audit the passage claim by claim. For each, tell me whether a specialist would accept it, treat it as a fair popular simplification, or treat it as a misrepresentation — and where it is a misrepresentation, say what Douglass actually held and why the difference matters rather than amounting to a quibble about labels. In particular: was his later interpretive theory originalist, textualist, purposivist, or something without a modern equivalent, and what did he take to fix the meaning of a written constitution? How did he handle the clauses the essay says he waved away — did he deny that they concerned slavery, or offer narrow readings of them, and how much weight can those readings bear? Was the conversion strategic, sincere, or can the two even be separated, and is the essay entitled to treat the political motive as showing that the legal argument was mere rationalization? Does the closing moral — that Douglass demonstrates you can argue a document into almost anything if you have reason to — survive once the summary is corrected, or was it resting on the very conflation the correction removes? I would also like you to keep apart two things the essay runs together: what Douglass said the Constitution meant, and what he said about how the government was in fact conducting itself under it. Where historians genuinely disagree about any of this, say that they disagree rather than silently adopting one side; and please mark your confidence as you go, singling out the one point in your audit you are least sure of — especially if it concerns what Douglass's own later writings actually claim rather than what a modern label implies.
