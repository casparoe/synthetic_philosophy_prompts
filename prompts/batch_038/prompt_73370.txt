You are a professional decision theorist and formal epistemologist, and I would like you to audit a position of mine before I defend it in public. Please think the matter through carefully first: rather than reasoning from labels, construct small numerical examples for yourself — a pair of bets, the same pair taken jointly and taken separately, and a sequential version of both — and test each pair of my claims against them.

Here is where I have landed on risk:

1. The Allais pattern is not a bias. Faced with a certain £1 million against a gamble offering 10 per cent of £5 million, 89 per cent of £1 million and 1 per cent of nothing, I take the certain million; faced with 11 per cent of £1 million against 10 per cent of £5 million and 90 per cent of nothing, I take the latter. I do not think anyone who chooses that way has made a mistake, whatever becomes of the sure-thing principle.

2. The right explanation is risk-weighted expected utility: a rational agent has a risk function alongside her credences and her utilities, and it weights the cumulative probability of the act as a whole rather than the probabilities of the individual outcomes. Because the weighting is global, no amount of concavity in the utility function can reproduce these preferences — risk attitude and diminishing marginal utility are different things. (If you think I have garbled the view here, correct me before you audit me.)

3. Which options an agent faces is fixed by the world, not by her description of it. Whether she treats a batch of bets as one decision or as a series of separate ones cannot alter what she ought to do; a theory on which framing changes the verdict is not yet finished.

4. Two agents with identical credences and identical utilities may differ in their risk functions and both be fully rational. There is no fact of the matter as to whose risk attitude is better.

5. Even so, an agent who turns down a bet that would transform her life for the price of a pound has her attitudes in the wrong shape, and I would criticise her for it — not as one criticises a bad inference, but as one criticises a bad temper. Criticism, yes; irrationality, no.

6. My risk function is a stable trait that does not flex with the stakes: I am not one person at the casino and another at the hospital. (This sits alongside the fact that I am cautious about the surgical option and quite happy to buy a raffle ticket.)

7. Giving up the sure-thing principle does not leave the agent open to a money pump, because she can settle on a plan in advance and follow it through rather than choosing afresh at each node.

Tell me whether this hangs together. Which of these commitments genuinely pull against one another, and which only appear to? Rank the real tensions by severity rather than reporting them all as equally bad, and for each say precisely what would have to be given up and what the cheapest revision would cost me. Then tell me which single revision would clean up the most.

Please be committed rather than even-handed: take a definite position on which of my claims cannot survive, and give your reasons for it, instead of setting out the options and leaving me to choose. Where you judge a tension to be merely apparent, say so plainly and give the resolution; where it is real, do not soften it. I would rather be told which of my commitments is untenable than be shown both sides of each.
