I write a fortnightly column on science and ideas, and I am drafting a piece on path dependence. Before I commit anything to print, I want a distinction I have been leaning on argued for as hard as it can be.

I separate two things that the literature tends to run together. Call the first **narrow lock-in**: a practice persists because switching is costly and no individual can move alone, even though everyone involved would prefer to move if they could move together — the familiar increasing-returns story. Call the second **broad lock-in**: a practice persists because the alternatives have ceased to be available as options at all, in the sense that the relevant agents can no longer formulate them, weigh them, or want them; the formation of preferences has itself been foreclosed by the path taken.

Please argue that only the second deserves the name "path dependence": that narrow lock-in is a coordination problem in historical costume, and that much of what is confidently asserted about lock-in in economic and institutional history either collapses into ordinary equilibrium talk or becomes untestable once the two are told apart. Make the case rather than survey it, and work through at least one technological and one institutional example — the institutional one should turn on foreclosed imagination rather than on foreclosed budgets. No citations or references, please; just reason from cases you know well.

Write in British English, and use these sections in this order:

- **The distinction** — about 150 words, sharpening the two notions and giving a test for telling them apart in a case one has not seen before.
- **The argument** — about 500 words, your positive case.
- **The three best objections** — about 300 words, each stated at full strength and then answered rather than waved off.
- **Bottom line** — about 75 words, ending with the single sentence I could lift into the piece.
