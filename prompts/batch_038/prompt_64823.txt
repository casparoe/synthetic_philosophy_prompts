For each, give me a number 0–10 on axis A, "how much of what I originally wanted when I started caring about this is still standing," and a separate number 0–10 on axis B, "would survive contact with a real 60-person org that has factions, grudges, and a slack channel." Define axis A's weighting yourself but say in a clause what you're weighting, and give each score with one or two sentences naming exactly which condition got weakened and what breaks as a result. The routes:

1. Domain restriction — assume preferences are single-peaked along a left-right line, at which point the median-voter rule is genuinely strategy-proof and onto (Moulin's version of the move).
2. Randomization — give up determinism; random dictatorship, which per Gibbard's later mixing-voting-with-chance result is basically the only strategy-proof and ex-post-Pareto random rule anyway.
3. Change the outcome space — stop picking a single winner, start allocating bundles plus money, i.e. VCG. Strategy-proof in dominant strategies, but arguably only because you smuggled transfers in.
4. Weaken strategy-proofness to "obviously strategy-proof" in Li's sense — the claim isn't that lying never pays, it's that no ordinary human can see how lying would pay.
5. Weaken it to Bayesian incentive compatibility under some belief about the others — and then eat Myerson–Satterthwaite, which says even that collapses for bilateral trade with private values.
6. Give up onto-ness — precommit publicly to a shortlist and let people vote only within it, so the range of the rule is smaller than the space of conceivable alternatives.
7. Keep everything and just measure instead — run score voting, concede it's manipulable in principle, and argue the achievable gain from lying is empirically tiny.

Then rank the seven overall, and tell me three more things. Which two of these are secretly the same move wearing different clothes.
