I volunteer at a small nonprofit that runs after-school tutoring for kids who are behind in reading. Last month the board met to decide whether to keep funding it, and two board members got into a long argument that I've been turning over in my head ever since. Neither of them is a scientist. Ray owns a hardware store, Ana is a middle-school councelor, and both are sharp. I'm not taking sides, I just want to know who was right about what.

Here's how it went, roughly in order:

(1) Ray said reading ability is something like 60–70% heritable by the teenage years, and that twin studies show the effect of the home and school environment kids share basically fades out to nothing once people grow up. So a tutoring program is pushing against something pretty deep, and the board should put the money somewhere it can actually move the needle. He said he wasn't claiming the kids can't improve — just that the population-level lever is weak.

(2) Ana said that mixes up two completely different things. Heritability is about how much of the differences between kids inside one group come from genes, given the environments that group happens to live in. It says nothing about whether changing the environment changes the average. Her example: plant genetically mixed seed in two fields, give one bad soil and one good, and nearly all the variation within each field can be genetic while the entire gap between the fields is caused by the soil. She also brought up a genetic condition — I think it's the one where you can't break down something in your food — that is about as heritable as anything gets and is basically handled by changing what the kid eats.

(3) Ray said her field example actually helps him, because tutoring turns out to be good soil for some kids and does nothing for others, and if kids differ in how much they respond, then further down the line those response differences will look heritable too. So heritability isn't beside the point. And he said the twin findings do hand you a real prediction: if what matters is environment that isn't shared, then one-on-one, kid-specific help should beat a blanket program — which is a claim you can go test.

(4) Ana said he was just relabeling. "Environment that isn't shared" in those studies is mostly measurement noise plus stuff nobody has managed to pin down, and non-shared doesn't mean not-shared-across-a-classroom — it only means not shared between two kids in the same family. She also said the home-environment-fades-out result is about adults who chose their own situations, so using it to argue tutoring won't stick turns things backwards, since kids are stuck in whatever situation they get put in. And she said the whole argument is beside the point anyway: you find out whether a program works by running it and measuring, not by reasoning about variance ratios.

(5) Ray said fine, measure it — but they've been measuring it, sort of, for eleven years, and the scores have never budged, which is exactly what he predicted. And he said that if her line were right, that null result wouldn't count as evidence for anything at all, which struck him as a strange thing to be committed to.

Then we ran out of meeting.
