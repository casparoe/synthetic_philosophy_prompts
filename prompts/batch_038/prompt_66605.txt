Been going round in circles on a draft for a while now and I could do with someone telling me whether it actually hangs together, or whether I've just dressed up a banal point in a fancy coat. It's a piece about the epistemic status of the Church-Turing thesis — not the mathematical results, the philosophical claim. Roughly 900 words of argument below; happy for you to be blunt.

---

Everybody agrees there's a difference between what got proved and what got asserted. The proved part is closed and exact: the class of functions computed by a Turing machine is the same as the class definable by any of the standard formalisms — general recursion, lambda calculus, register machines — and this is a theorem you can teach in a term. The asserted part is that this class is also the class of what a human being, following rules with paper and pencil and no insight, can work out. The gap between those two is where all the philosophy lives, and it's a much stranger gap than people tend to admit.

Here's the strangeness. A normal empirical generalisation earns support from surviving tests it could have failed, and it can be undercut. CT does neither. Every putative counterexample since the 1990s has been met with the same two moves, and the two moves pull in opposite directions, which is what I think nobody has quite said. Move one: the proposal isn't really physical — the accelerating machine needs unbounded energy in bounded time, the Malament-Hogarth spacetime needs an observer who can receive a signal from an infinite proper-time worldline, the real-valued analogue network needs weights to infinite precision, which is to say it has smuggled the answer into the setup. Move two, which sometimes arrives in the same paper as move one: even granting the physics, the procedure wouldn't be reliable or noise-robust or finite-resource in the way anything we'd be willing to call a computation has to be. Move one says the objection fails as physics. Move two concedes the physics and then adds a condition on what counts as computing at all — and that condition is never argued for independently of the thesis it protects.

That's the structure I want to press on. There is no pre-theoretic, theory-independent criterion of effective calculability lying around to confirm or disconfirm CT against. The concept was constituted by the formalism; the formalism now adjudicates the concept. So the thesis can't be confirmed (any survey of successful computations is circular: you already decided which procedures were computations before you tabulated them), and it can't be refuted either, because refutation needs a criterion to fail.

Notice this isn't just the tired underdetermination point. Underdetermination says the evidence doesn't pick between rivals. Here there aren't rivals of the right shape. Hypercomputation proposals are the only candidates, and each one gets absorbed rather than rebutted — either reclassified as non-physical, or reclassified as non-computational. The thesis behaves like something that is doing structural work in a practice rather than sitting inside it as one of its claims.

The way I want to put it, and this is the bit I'm least sure of: CT functions as a hinge. In the last stretch of On Certainty Wittgenstein's point is that some propositions are exempt from doubt not because they're too obvious to doubt but because doubting them would take the surrounding language-game with it; they're the scaffolding against which testing happens, and they can migrate — what was an empirical claim in one era becomes a rule of grammar in another. If CT is a hinge, three things follow. First, the familiar charge that CT is unfalsifiable is beside the point, since hinges aren't supposed to be falsifiable; unfalsifiability is a defect in hypotheses and a job description in scaffolding. Second, the mathematical version isn't evidence for the philosophical version at all — it's the hinge made precise, and a hinge made precise is a different object, not a better-supported one. Third, the debate in the hypercomputation literature stops looking like a disagreement about physics. When a critic says a proposal is unbuildable or too fragile to be taken seriously, they are not making a physical objection, they're performing the hinge: marking the boundary of what the community will allow to count as a computation, which is to say as something checkable, repeatable, and open to correction by an ordinary person with a pencil. That boundary is a normative choice about a practice, and it can be argued about — but it can't be settled by building a machine.

So my answer to the question in the title is deliberately deflationary: the evidence for CT is that we have never agreed on what would count as evidence against it, and until we do, the thesis is neither well-supported nor in trouble. Which raises an obvious worry about my own piece — if that's right, the question of the thesis's epistemic status is the wrong question, and the interesting question is who gets to police the boundary and what they're protecting when they do. I gesture at this in the last section but haven't worked out whether it's a real position or just a mood.
