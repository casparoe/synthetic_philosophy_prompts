Everyone points at Harsanyi's aggregration theorem, and the commentary I'm reading gives me whiplash: one camp says it shows social welfare has to be a weighted sum of individual expected utilities and that's the whole ballgame; a second camp says a weighted sum isn't utilitarianism unless the weights come out equal, and the theorem doesn't hand you that; a third says the whole thing is close to circular, because to even state it you've already assumed that seperate people's utilities sit on one common scale.

Can you untangle this for me? I want to know what the theorem actually assumes versus what it actually delivers, where the equal weights are supposed to come from if not from the theorem itself, and how much work that comparability premise is doing — is the Arrow contrast (he banned interpersonal comparisons and got an impossibility, Harsanyi allowed them and got a representation) the right frame, or does that undersell what Harsanyi did? And does it matter that the Pareto condition is being applied to prospects rather than to sure outcomes? Tell me which of these three camps, if any, are just talking past each other. Conceptual lay of the land, not a proof.
