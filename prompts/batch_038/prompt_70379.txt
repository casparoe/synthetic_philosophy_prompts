I retired three years ago from a national measurement laboratory, where most of my working life was spent on artefact standards: the physical objects that once defined the units, and which have since been superseded by definitions anchored to constants of nature. In retirement I have taken up philosophy more seriously than my family expected, and I have been reading my way through the recent decision-theoretic literature on what its authors call acausal interaction — the thought that agents who can model one another accurately, but who can neither signal nor influence each other by any ordinary means, may nonetheless have reasons to bargain, to threaten, and to keep their word. Two ideas from that literature have lodged in my mind. The first is the surrogate goal: an agent may deliberately adopt a goal it has no independent reason to care about — arbitrary in content, modestly costly, and above all easy for an outside modeller to verify — in the hope that any threatener or bargaining partner will take aim at that goal rather than at the things the agent genuinely values, on the principle that a threat is useful only if the threatened party will truly flinch. The second is the acausal threat: a demand backed by a conditional harm that the threatener has already resolved upon, that cannot be deterred, negotiated away, or verified, and whose entire leverage consists in the target's own reasoning about whether such threateners exist at all. I have a suspicion, formed from long habit with instruments, that a commitment adopted purely for the impression it makes on observers is a commitment that fails at the moment of testing; but I cannot yet state the argument properly, and I should like to see the problem dramatized rather than expounded to me.

Would you therefore write me a short story — say two thousand to three thousand words — along the following lines? The setting is a small endowed institute for reference standards in a university town, which for most of a century performed an annual ritual: over three November weeks its technicians compared nine hand-forged test masses against one another on a beam balance and printed the results in a slim volume that perhaps forty people read. The ritual lapsed, and was then revived ten years ago by the board on the explicit recommendation of a young philosopher it had appointed, whose memo argued that the institute should adopt the Comparison as a surrogate goal — costly, arbitrary, publicly verifiable, and just the sort of thing a modeller it could never contact would find convenient to threaten. The institute's real work, meanwhile, is a low-temperature noise laboratory that occupies a quarter of the staff. The story should begin on the morning a sealed note is found on the balance table in the vault, where the access log says no one was. The note demands that the laboratory be permanently closed, its equipment dismantled, and a notice published in a specified journal stating in specified wording that the closure was made in response to the note; it explains that its author neither can nor claims to affect the institute causally, has already acted, and offers no demonstration of any kind, so that the institute's decision cannot rest on evidence about the author but only on its own prior reasoning about whether such authors exist; and it observes, unkindly, that a hostage which its keepers selected from a menu and revived by vote is no hostage at all. It then reproduces, close to verbatim, several sentences from the closed minutes of the board meeting at which the Comparison was revived — minutes to which only seven people were party, one of whom is a former trustee who resigned two years ago in a sour temper and now lives abroad.

Please populate the institute with a director, the senior technician who has kept the Comparison alive, the philosopher whose memo revived it, and at least one person who thinks the whole note is a leak and a hoax; let them argue it out properly, and make the case for compliance as formidable as the case for refusal, since the loss in question is concrete and bounded while the reasoning on the other side is speculative about entities nobody has met. I would ask that the theory surface only in what people say and do — no narrator stepping in to explain surrogate goals or acausal threats to the reader, and no character who functions as a mouthpiece for the correct view. Note that the note's demand forecloses the easy escapes: the closure must be real and the published notice must be true, so a feigned or secret compliance is not available even in principle. Then choose an ending. I want a definite decision and definite consequences — I am not looking for a piece that holds both readings in suspension and leaves the question where it found it, and I would rather you did not resolve matters by having someone discover who wrote the note. If you judge that one of the two courses is what a rational institution would actually take, commit to it and let the story make that case through what happens; if you judge the whole edifice of reasoning to be nonsense, commit to that too, but show me the nonsense doing real damage rather than simply telling me it is nonsense. I am not asking for sources, references, or suggestions for further reading.

After the story, please add a few sentences of commentary in your own voice: identify the two or three points where the underlying ideas are genuinely contested — for instance, whether a goal adopted for strategic reasons can ever be valued in the way the strategy requires, whether complying with an unverifiable threat is self-defeating or merely expensive, and whether an acausal threatener is distinguishable even in principle from an ingenious hoax — and state plainly which side of each your story took and why. Say also where you have taken liberties with the concepts as they are actually argued, and where you have let a character make a point better than the literature manages.
