Okay so — six weeks from now I'm giving the keynote at a closed-door session of our national statistics office's wellbeing advisory group, and I need a Q&A prep sheet, not a defense of my paper. Here's the setup.

I'm a measurement philosopher seconded to the agency. My thesis has three parts. First: the headline international happiness ranking is built off a single life-evaluation question — respondents picture a ladder where the top is the best possible life *for them* and place themselves on it — so what it reports is a judgment about one's life as a whole, not the quality of one's experience, and those two can come apart in both directions for the same person without either report being false. Second: because the top of the scale is defined by each respondent's own sense of what a best possible life looks like, cross-national differences in the score partly encode differences in what people treat as within reach, which makes the ranking a map of aspiration and reference classes at least as much as a map of how lives are going. That's why, on my reading, the evaluation numbers track GDP per capita much more tightly than experienced-wellbeing measures do, and the top of the table is close to a rebranding of material living standards. Third — and this is where I get in trouble — I argue the government should NOT adopt a rank target, and should keep publishing the evaluation number under an honest label (a life-evaluation indicator, not happiness), add an experienced-wellbeing panel alongside it, and refuse to build a composite. Three numbers, side by side, no weights. My reasoning for the no-target part: a rank is relative to other countries so it moves for reasons nobody here controls, and targeting a self-report is a worse-than-usual Goodhart problem because you can move reports without moving lives.

The room, and this is the part I need you to get right: four or five health and welfare economists who do wellbeing valuation — they monetize mental-health gains, they like WELLBY-style cost-benefit work, and they think the evaluation question is the only measure that has actually earned its place in appraisal guidance. Two survey methodologists who work on cross-national comparability, differential item functioning, response styles, and anchoring-vignette corrections — one of them will absolutely say my point two is an empirical claim that has been tested and that the corrections turn out to be small, and I need to know whether I survive that. One academic positive psychologist who defends subjective wellbeing as a construct with real convergent validity and thinks experience sampling is a psychometric mess of mood contamination and attrition. A Treasury policy lead whose ministers have told her to get the country into the top ten by 2030 and who needs something to say back to them on Monday. And a chair who is a former head of the agency, mostly worried about public trust in official statistics and about whether publishing three numbers reads as the government admitting it doesn't know what happiness is.

What I want: eight to ten objections this specific room will actually raise, ordered by how much damage they'd do to the proposal if unanswered, each one phrased in that person's own idiom the way they'd say it out loud — the economists will talk about valuation and appraisal, the methodologists will talk about invariance and scale use, the Treasury person will talk about deliverability. For each: a two-minute reply I can give without notes, and a blunt line about where that reply is soft. Then flag which objections are interpretive (we're reading the same numbers differently and no new data will settle it) versus which ones are empirical bets I could genuinely lose, and for the empirical ones say what finding would sink me. Tell me what to concede in the first five minutes so I don't spend the whole hour defending things I don't need, and name the single reply that has to land for the three-number proposal to survive the meeting. And include the question I'm hoping nobody asks — my own guess is that it's whether refusing to weight three numbers is just a composite index that's afraid of itself, but you might see a worse one.
