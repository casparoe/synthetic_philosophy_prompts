I keep seeing American exceptionalism thrown around like it's one thing when it's clearly at least three, and I want to test that on an actual episode instead of on slogans. Take the fight over keeping the Phillipines after 1898: McKinley's side argues a duty to uplift and educate the Filipinos, says you can't hand them back to Spain or let Germany have them or just leave them to anarchy; the Anti-Imperialist League -- Carnegie, Gompers, Twain -- argues that governing people without their consent rots the republic from the inside, that empire turns America into the thing it revolted against. Both camps sound exceptionalist, which is exactly what bugs me. So sort the arguments for me: which are exceptionalism as a historical thesis about what America actually is, which as a normative claim about what it owes other peoples, which as ideology -- self-description doing work as justification? Then tell me whether the frame earns its keep. Once markets, naval stations, race science and the 1900 election are in the picture, does exceptionalism explain anything the material interests and party politics don't, or is it mostly an academic way of repeating what the actors already said about themselves? And where does it break: the Insular Cases a couple years later have the Court holding the Constitution doesn't automatically follow the flag and inventing a category of territory that's ours but not part of us. Is that exceptionalism working as intended, or a case the concept never anticipated?
