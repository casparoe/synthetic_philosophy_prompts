Our lab's reading group has spent three weeks on an argument I no longer know how to resist. It was offered to us as a reductio of interpretability talk — specifically, of the claim that a trained network has representations of this or that — and its author freely concedes that the argument is not original, which he takes to be precisely why it must be answered. The steps, as we have them written up:

(1) For a state s of a system S to represent property F, two conditions must be met: s must covary with F, and that covariance must matter to what S does.

(2) Given any F satisfying (1), there are further properties F* that satisfy it just as well. In our own running case, one direction in a vision model's activation space responds strongly both to animal faces and to the front ends of cars. Alongside the obvious candidate — roundish frontal object with two symmetric dark spots — we may substitute the property of being an animal face or being the front of a car photographed in or after 2015, and more generally any of indefinitely many variants obtained by re-indexing the training inputs to properties via an arbitrary function. Each covaries with the activations exactly as the others do, and each figures in exactly the same explanation of the model's outputs.

(3) Where two candidate attributions cannot be separated by any fact about S's own operation — neither by covariance nor by causal-explanatory role — nothing makes one of them correct rather than the other.

(4) Therefore no attribution of a specific property to s is correct: either s represents nothing, or it represents arbitrarily many things at once, and a claim that it represents one of them is false or empty.

(5) Nothing in the construction at (2) depends on S's being silicon; it requires only covariance facts and behavioral facts, which we also have for neural populations in animals and people.

(6) Therefore no state of any system represents anything.

We all find (6) unacceptable, and I cannot locate the exit. Premise (2) looks like a trivial consequence of the fact that properties admit of re-description; (3) looks like the minimum commitment of anyone who wants representation to be a natural relation rather than a projection onto the world; and refusing (5) looks like special pleading for wetware. If I am forced to choose, I would keep (3) — it is the premise I would want to defend in print — but I do not know what keeping it obliges me to.

So: which step do you reject, and on which reading of it? If you think the argument is valid and that some premise must simply be given up, say which is cheapest. Explain in each case why the rejected step looked unobjectionable from the inside, and state what its rejection commits me to — in particular, name the further fact or criterion that is supposed to break the tie among the candidates in (2), and say whether an interpretability paper could establish that fact with the tools we actually have (ablation, activation patching, linear probes, counterfactual inputs) or would have to assume it as a precondition of using those tools at all. Then apply your criterion to the animal-face/car-front direction, and to the superposition cases, where a feature's direction is recoverable only statistically across many contexts rather than as a single interpretable axis. Finally, tell me whether your diagnosis is meant to work the same way for brains as for networks; if it does not, say why the asymmetry is not arbitrary.
