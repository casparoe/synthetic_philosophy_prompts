I am a junior, and for the past year I have been running an independent reading group at my school on probability and the philosophy of language; we meet on Wednesdays and I usually write up a short summary of what we have covered. This term we took up the debate over indicative conditionals. Because I could not get hold of the primary literature, I worked mostly from a chapter in a recent trade book about the logic of everyday reasoning, which summarizes Lewis's triviality result as follows:

'Lewis proved that indicative conditionals have no truth conditions. His target was the attractive equation associated with Ernest Adams, on which the probability of a sentence of the form "If A, B" is simply the conditional probability of B given A. Lewis showed that this equation can hold for a given A and B only if A and B are probabilistically independent of one another, unless the language is so impoverished that it contains little more than two sentences. Since antecedents and consequents are obviously not generally independent, the equation must be false; and if there is no proposition expressed by an indicative conditional, there is nothing for a probability to attach to. Conditionals are therefore not truth-apt at all, and to assert one is merely to disclose a conditional degree of belief. This is why the result is the definitive dissolution of the paradoxes of material implication: once truth conditions are withdrawn from conditionals, there is nothing left for the paradoxes to be paradoxical about.'
