Been kicking around a lens I want to pressure-test on a real case, so tell me if it holds up. Call it the standing problem: restitution arguments nearly always get framed as a question about who should own the object, but there's a prior question that almost nobody argues about — who gets to speak for the community the thing was taken from. In practice the answer defaults to the postcolonial nation-state, mostly because that's the entity with legal personality and a foreign ministry, and the default rarely gets examined.

Now run the German Benin Bronzes transfer through it. In 2022 Germany handed ownership of its holdings over to the Nigerian state, and that got sold widely as the model everyone else should copy — while the British Museum stayed put behind the 1963 Act's bar on disposal, and the institutions that had already returned objects (Aberdeen, Jesus College Cambridge) could move precisely because they weren't state bodies. Since the German handover, who actually holds and decides about the returned pieces inside Nigeria — federal commission, the Oba's palace, Edo State, the private trust set up for the museum — has been openly contested.

So: what does the standing problem predict going into a case like this, which features of that episode does it explain that ownership-framing can't, and where does it just go silent? Main thing I want to know is whether the custody fight inside Nigeria confirms the view or shows it's just relabelling an ordinary property dispute — and whether it bites differently where the standing question can't even be reached.
