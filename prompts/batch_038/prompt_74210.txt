I'm a second-year graduate student working through dominance-based solution concepts, and I've hit a knot I haven't been able to untangle by rereading definitions. Take it as given that I have the standard definitions of strict dominance, weak dominance, best response, and Nash equilibrium; I don't want them restated. What I want is the conceptual connective tissue, worked through on one concrete example.

Player 1 chooses a row, player 2 a column. Payoffs are listed (player 1, player 2):

```
            L         C         R
   U     (4, 3)    (0, 3)    (2, 1)
   M     (0, 3)    (4, 3)    (2, 1)
   D     (1, 0)    (1, 3)    (0, 3)
```

Four features of this game are puzzling me, and I suspect they are four faces of a single issue.

1. D is strictly dominated, yet no pure strategy of player 1 strictly dominates it — the equal mixture of U and M does the work, as does any mixture putting strictly between a quarter and three quarters of the weight on U. Explain why the mixed dominator is not a mere technical convenience. In particular, explain the equivalence between being strictly dominated by some mixture and being never a best response to any belief about the opponent's play, and say why one direction of that equivalence is the substantive one: what does establishing it require, and what does the required machinery tell us about what a mixed dominator represents from the point of view of the opponent's beliefs?

2. For player 2, C weakly dominates both L and R, and the whole of C's strict advantage over L comes from the D row — a row that point 1 has already ruled out as never a best response. Explain why this is precisely where the order-sensitivity of iterated weak-dominance elimination comes from, and identify the two different survivor sets that two legitimate elimination orders yield here. Then contrast the strict case: why is iterated strict-dominance elimination order-independent while the weak version is not? I want the answer phrased in terms of what each elimination step entitles you to infer about the other player's beliefs, not merely in terms of whether the relevant inequality is strict.

3. Eliminating L appears to require player 2 to assign positive probability to D, that is, to a strategy that common knowledge of rationality has already excluded. Spell out this tension. Is the positive-probability assumption doing something illegitimate, or is common knowledge of rationality simply too strong a premise to be combined with weak dominance in the first place? Say what the weakest assumption on beliefs is that would license the elimination of L, and whether that assumption is consistent with the reasoning in point 1.

4. Both (U, L) and (M, C) are pure Nash equilibria, and one of the orders in point 2 destroys (U, L). Work out whether (U, L) survives trembling-hand perfection, and explain what your answer shows about the relation between weak dominance and trembles. Does the tremble vindicate the order that looked unjustified in point 3, or does it reproduce the same difficulty one level up?

Close by telling me what the example implies about whether iterated weak-dominance elimination is a well-defined solution concept at all, and what a theorist who wants to retain it would have to supply — an ordering convention, a restriction on admissible beliefs, a refinement — in order to make its output unique. Where you invoke a general theorem, state what hypotheses it needs; you need not reproduce proofs. No citations, please, I'll track down the references myself.
