My advisor is making me write out my actual position on free will and moral responsibility as a numbered list before he'll let me bring a single argument or case to my work-in-progress talk — he says he wants to see whether the package is even coherent before we start litigating parts of it. Fair enough, except now that I've written it down I can't tell whether it hangs together, and my friends in the reading group all told me different things about which item was the problem, which is its own kind of evidence. So here's the list:

1. Frankfurt cases do what Frankfurt said they do. Jones is blameworthy for shooting Smith even though Black was sitting there ready to force him to do it if he'd shown any sign of deciding otherwise, and Black never actually has to lift a finger. So blameworthiness doesn't require the ability to do otherwise, and the principle of alternative possibilities is false.
2. But what was ever attractive about that principle is a fairness intuition — you can't be blamed for what you couldn't help — and I don't want to lose it. I think ought implies can, roughly, and I think any account of blame that violates the spirit of that is doing something objectionable.
3. I'm a reasons-responsiveness person about blame: what makes an agent blameworthy is that the mechanism that actually issued in the action was her own and was at least moderately responsive to reasons. Actual sequence properties do the work, not the alternatives.
4. Which is why I think the determinism question is beside the point. I'm an agnostic about whether determinism is true and I'm proud that my account doesn't need to take a side — that's supposed to be the selling point, the thing that gets me out of the metaphysics of causation.
5. On the other hand I find the Pereboom-style manipulation cases genuinely compelling. If neuroscientists are covertly tweaking an agent so she ends up committing a murder, and her psychology as it unfolds looks from the inside like an ordinary reasons-responsive one — same dispositions, same responsiveness, no defect you can point to in the actual sequence — I think she is not blameworthy. Manipulation kills responsibility even when nothing is locally wrong with her.
6. And I don't think there's a principled line between that covert manipulation and plain deterministic causation. If the manipulated agent isn't responsible, then the determined agent in the same shape of story isn't either.
7. Separately, I'm pretty confident the Libet stuff doesn't threaten anything, since the readiness potential isn't a decision and doesn't have the content of one, and I get irritated when people wave it around like a refutation.

So: audit this for me. Which of these genuinely pull against each other, and which ones only look like they conflict until you're clear on what exactly is being claimed — I suspect at least one pair in here is a fake problem but I can't tell you which, and I don't want to spend the talk defending a tension that isn't there. For the real ones, rank them by severity rather than treating them all as equally bad, and say what would have to be dropped or weakened and what it costs me if I drop it — specifically, whether the thing I care about most survives the surgery. If a fix is available that keeps most of the list intact, say what the fix is and what it silently commits me to. Please don't lecture me on the literature or hedge everything into maybe-you-could-say-thats; I'd rather be told flat out that two of these can't both stand. And don't guess which one I'd cut, because I genuinely dont know and I'm probably wrong about at least one of them.
