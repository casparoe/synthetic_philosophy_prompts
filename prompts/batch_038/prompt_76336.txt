My friend, who does software licensing for a living, and I spent most of a long dinner last night arguing about whether the law has already settled the question of whether ideas can be owned. He thinks it has. His exhibit was the Supreme Court's opinion in Baker v. Selden, 101 U.S. 99 (1879), which he reads as a court declaring, on metaphysical grounds, that ideas and methods are simply not the kind of thing that can belong to anyone. I argued the opposite: that the opinion says nothing of the sort, and is instead making a narrower point about what one particular statutory instrument — copyright in a book — can and cannot reach. He said I was splitting hairs, since the Court expressly calls the truths of a science the common property of the whole world. We did not resolve it, and I have come to suspect we were both reading past the sentences.

Here is the passage I have been working from. For context: Selden had obtained copyright in a book called Selden's Condensed Ledger, or Book-keeping Simplified, which explained a system of bookkeeping and annexed ruled forms and headings illustrating it; Baker produced account books achieving the same results by a different arrangement of columns and different headings. The opinion of Bradley, J.:

"It is contended that he has secured such exclusive right, because no one can use the system without using substantially the same ruled lines and headings which he has appended to his books in illustration of it. In other words, it is contended that the ruled lines and headings, given to illustrate the system, are a part of the book, and, as such, are secured by the copyright; and that no one can make or use similar ruled lines and headings, or ruled lines and headings made and arranged on substantially the same system, without violating the copyright.

. . . Where the truths of a science or the methods of an art are the common property of the whole world, any author has the right to express the one, or explain and use the other, in his own way. As an author, Selden explained the system in a particular way. It may be conceded that Baker makes and uses account-books arranged on substantially the same system; but the proof fails to show that he has violated the copyright of Selden's book, regarding the latter merely as an explanatory work; or that he has infringed Selden's right in any way, unless the latter became entitled to an exclusive right in the system.

. . . And this is really the question to be decided in this case. Stated in another form, the question is, whether the exclusive property in a system of book-keeping can be claimed, under the law of copyright, by means of a book in which that system is explained? The complainant's bill, and the case made under it, are based on the hypothesis that it can be.

. . . There is no doubt that a work on the subject of book-keeping, though only explanatory of well-known systems, may be the subject of a copyright; but, then, it is claimed only as a book. Such a book may be explanatory either of old systems, or of an entirely new system; and, considered as a book, as the work of an author, conveying information on the subject of book-keeping, and containing detailed explanations of the art, it may be a very valuable acquisition to the practical knowledge of the community. But there is a clear distinction between the book, as such, and the art which it is intended to illustrate. The mere statement of the proposition is so evident, that it requires hardly any argument to support it. The same distinction may be predicated of every other art as well as that of book-keeping."

I would like help reading this closely, and then help deciding which of our two positions the text actually supports. Specifically:

1. Give me a sentence-by-sentence plain rendering of the excerpt, including the nineteenth-century legal locutions I am unsure of — what it means to say a bill is "based on the hypothesis," what is being "predicated of every other art," and what force "as such" carries in "the book, as such."

2. What kind of distinction is the one between the book and the art? Is it an ontological claim about two sorts of object, a claim about two sorts of human activity (describing a practice versus engaging in it), or a claim about the scope of two different legal instruments? Point to the wording that decides the matter.

3. The Court says the mere statement of the proposition is so evident that it requires hardly any argument. But the complainant's contention, quoted in my first paragraph, is precisely that the separation cannot be performed: no one can use the system without using substantially the same ruled lines and headings. Does the passage answer that contention, or does the appeal to obviousness bypass it? If it bypasses it, is that a defect in the reasoning or an artifact of what a court needs to establish in a case like this one?

4. Take apart the sentence beginning "Where the truths of a science or the methods of an art are the common property of the whole world." Note that it grants a right not only to express and explain but to use. What is the status of the premise — a description of how ideas are, a statement of positive law, or a normative commitment about what must be left open for others? And does "common property" mean unowned, or does it mean owned in common, which is not the same thing? This sentence is the one my friend leans on hardest, so I want it read against him and against me, whichever way it falls.

5. The Court concedes that the book may be a very valuable acquisition to the practical knowledge of the community, and that it may explain an entirely new system, then denies the claim anyway. What conception of copyright does that concession presuppose, and why is the book's value or novelty beside the point on that conception?

6. Then adjudicate. State the strongest version of my friend's reading and the strongest version of mine, and say which the excerpt better supports — or whether the text supports a third reading neither of us had. If it turns out he is right, say so plainly.

7. Finally, tell me where the distinction comes under pressure and whether the passage contains resources to handle it. My own candidate pressure points are cases where the art cannot be practiced except through its embodiment, and where the embodiment is itself the thing used rather than a description of it — though I do not want you to accept those candidates merely because I offered them.

Two constraints. Reason from the text I have given you and from general argument; I am not looking for a bibliography, a list of authorities, or citations to later cases or commentary, and I would rather you not supply them. And since I have excerpted rather than reproduced the opinion, if any of your conclusions depend on material I have left out, flag that dependency explicitly instead of reconstructing what you think the rest of the opinion says.
