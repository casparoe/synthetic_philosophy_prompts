I teach an upper-level seminar on Aristotle's practical philosophy — sixteen students, a single 75-minute session, all of whom have read *Nicomachean Ethics* V.1–5 and *Politics* I.8–10 but have not yet discussed either. I want a simulation that makes them run into Aristotle's problem about money and exchange before anyone names it, rather than sitting through my reconstruction of the argument. Please design the whole thing so that I can run it from your document with no further preparation beyond printing and buying supplies.

So that the design is not built on a shaky foundation, here is what I take the target claims to be; tell me if you think I have any of them wrong. From the reciprocity chapter of the *Ethics* (roughly 1132b–1133b): that exchange requires the things exchanged to be made commensurable; that what actually holds exchange together is need, which is not one and the same for all parties and is not stable over time; that money comes to stand for need by convention, which is why its name is tied to convention or law and why Aristotle holds it to be within our power to alter money and render it useless; and that he nonetheless concedes that in truth things so different cannot be made commensurable — so that money reads less like a solution to the problem than like an expedient that lets people act as though the problem were solved. From *Politics* I.8–10: that every thing has a proper use and a use in exchange; that the acquisitive art belonging to household management has a limit because need is finite, while acquisition directed at money-making has none; and that the most unnatural variety of the latter is the one in which money is set to begetting more money instead of serving the exchange it was invented for.

The mechanics should carry the argument, not my commentary. In particular: (i) each participant's needs must be private and merely ordinal — no one may state or write down a number for how much they want anything, and you should tell me what to do when someone inevitably tries to price their preferences anyway; (ii) the exchange ratios in the opening rounds must be genuinely underdetermined by the goods themselves, so that disputes over ratios cannot be settled by arithmetic; (iii) any medium of exchange must arise from precedent among the players, never from an instruction by me, and it should be arbitrary in the sense that a different item would have served equally well; (iv) need must shift mid-game by some exogenous mechanism of your devising, so that whatever the players have been using as their measure turns out not to track the thing it was standing in for; (v) at some point a token that satisfies nobody's intrinsic need must be introduced, together with a rule permitting it to be lent at a markup, so that unlimited accumulation detached from use becomes available and attractive; and (vi) there must be a moment at which I exercise the power to change the money and make it useless. Tell me when in the timeline to spring that last move, what it will do to the players' trust in the rest of the session, and whether it is worth the cost.

The stakes should be real but small and must not touch grades — you decide what the accumulated tokens convert into at the end and specify it precisely enough that I can procure it. Materials must be cheap, obtainable in a day, and printable on ordinary paper; write out at least four of the role cards in full so I can see the pattern, and state how many distinct roles are needed for sixteen players and how to double them up without letting the duplicates simply collude. No lecture before or during the play, and none of the Greek vocabulary should be spoken until the debrief.

Deliver a minute-by-minute timeline for the 75 minutes; a debrief that maps particular moments in the run onto particular claims, with the questions I should ask rather than the conclusions I should announce; and a candid account of what the simulation cannot reproduce — I expect at least the teleological framing in which exchange serves the self-sufficiency of household and polis rather than the satisfaction of preference, and Aristotle's own concession that commensurability fails in truth, since in the game commensuration will visibly succeed. One case I want handled explicitly in the debrief script: what to say to the student who spontaneously proposes that the ratios should be fixed by the labour or cost that went into the goods, which is a recognisable modern reflex and not Aristotle's account.

Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this — the hoarder who breaks the later rounds, the player left with nothing anyone wants who disengages in minute ten, the ratio dispute that slides into an argument about fairness and never returns, the mathematically quickest student who reverse-engineers the implied prices and destroys the experience for everyone else — and give me a rule or an intervention for each.

Finally, I want you to be explicit about the standing of your own design choices. State what would change your mind: which observation in the room would persuade you that the activity is teaching something other than Aristotle's point, and which competing reading of these chapters would require you to redesign it rather than merely adjust the debrief. Flag every place where you have leaned on a contested interpretation — for instance, taking the concession about commensurability to mean that money is a fiction people act by, or taking the two uses of a thing to be a normative limit on acquisition rather than a neutral taxonomy — and say what in the activity turns on that reading being right.
