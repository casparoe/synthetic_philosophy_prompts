I work in refugee admissions policy, and I have been trying to pin down a pattern I keep running into without having a term for it. A state adopts a measure — interdiction at sea, offshore processing, a deliberately slow determination system — that produces a large backlog or a large population stuck in limbo. When it is then asked to admit people or to regularize those already present, it answers that the numbers are now too great, its system too strained, the disruption too severe, and that admitting anyone would encourage further arrivals. The strain it cites is a product of the policy it chose. What strikes me is the direction of travel: each year it declines to comply makes compliance costlier, so the excuse strengthens rather than weakens, and a state that has ignored the obligation for a decade is in a better defensive position than one that ignored it for a year.

I know the general neighbourhood. The principle that ought implies can is obviously in play, and there is a doctrine in contract law, as I understand it, barring a party from pleading an impossibility it brought about itself. Neither seems to capture the escalation — the way the cost of doing the right thing is manufactured by not doing it, round after round, until the obligation is argued out of existence.

Is there an established concept for this? If so, what is it called? If several concepts sit near it — in ethics, in law, in the economics of incentives — please say what each covers and where the thing I have described diverges from it. If nothing fits, define it for me: give it a name, state the conditions under which it applies, and say what it excludes. Please answer in continuous prose, the way you would write a letter or an email — no headings, no bullet points, no bold, no tables.
