I cover science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and I have been commissioned to write a piece in which I stake out my own position on contingency in history. Before I commit to print, I would like a frank audit of whether the position I intend to defend is internally tenable. My commitments are these:

1. Historical processes are sensitive in the way chaotic systems are: a detained messenger, a storm in a narrow channel, or one survival at a bottleneck can cascade into large-scale outcomes that would otherwise never have obtained.
2. This does not make me a partisan of great individuals. The structural frame of a period — ecology and disease environment, geography, demography, the available state of technique — sets what is possible, and where a single figure looks decisive I take it that a comparable figure would have surfaced from the same conditions.
3. Counterfactual reasoning is a legitimate historical method rather than a parlour game. Some claims about what would have happened had a given event not occurred are answerable, and we can hold some of them with real confidence.
4. Explanation must be causal all the way down. I do not accept chance or accident as an explanation of anything; every event has a causal history, and citing contingency is a way of ending an inquiry, not of completing one.
5. I reject every doctrine of historical inevitability or built-in direction, including the secular theodicies in which progress functions as a law.
6. I also think formal forecasting — models of political instability, state failure, or conflict onset — can be genuinely informative at horizons of five to ten years, and I want to defend that work against the objection that my first commitment makes it hopeless.

Please tell me which of these actually pull against one another and which merely look as though they do; for each genuine tension, identify what in my wording generates it, say which commitment would have to be weakened or abandoned, and say what would be lost by that revision. Rank the tensions by severity rather than presenting them as equally damaging, and tell me at the end which single change would buy the most consistency for the least cost.
