I run a Thursday-night beginner pottery class, ten adults, nobody's studied philosophy, but they talk while they work and I've steered us onto something that's been bugging me for years. I've got 90 minutes and there's wet clay on every table, which feels like an advantage.

The thing I want them to actually get: I make a jug out of a lump, and then I want to know how many objects are on the table. My gut says two — the lump could survive being squashed flat, the jug couldn't, and one thing can't survive what the other can't. But a student says "that's just two words for the same thing" and I've never had an answer for her. I also have a sourdough starter that's been fed weekly since my grandmother had it and now lives in four jars in four kitchens — I want to say all four are the same starter and also that they're four different starters, and I don't know which half of that I'm meant to give up. Might be a different problem entirely, I honestly can't tell.

So plan the session for me, don't run it. What has to land before "how many things are there" is even a question they can hear, whether the jug is the right example to carry the whole thing or whether the starter does better work and why, the answers they'll reach for and the exact point in the sequence where each should get headed off — I'm braced for "it's all just atoms so the jug isn't real" and "identity's relative, you're both right" — one question I can ask at the end that shows whether they've got the problem or just a slogan, and what I should flatly say I'm not covering.

Then tell me where you think I'm the confused one: which of my own verdicts you'd overturn, and what consideration would make you order the session differently from how you just did.
