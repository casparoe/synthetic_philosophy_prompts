I spent thirty-four years as an atmospheric chemist, most of it measuring sulfate and ash horizons in polar ice, and the last decade of that career working within the probabilistic framework my field developed for asking whether a particular heat wave or flood had been made more likely by anthropogenic forcing. I retired three years ago and am now in the second year of a part-time master's programme in the history and philosophy of science, where I must submit a research proposal for a thesis to be completed over the following twelve months. The provocation comes from reading environmental historians, who write with apparent seriousness about the eruption of Tambora in 1815, or about the pathogen behind the sixth-century Mediterranean epidemic, as protagonists of their narratives — things that acted, that changed what human beings were able to do, that had roles. Colleagues in the humanities object that this is environmental determinism in disguise. What strikes me is that neither side seems to notice that my old discipline abandoned the very form of causation the objection presupposes: we stopped asking whether a forcing caused an event in a but-for sense, because that question has no answer for an unrepeatable event in an open system, and asked instead how the probability of the event shifted relative to a counterfactual world that never existed and that we could model but never observe.

My half-formed idea is this. When a historian calls a non-human thing an actor, at least three separable claims are being run together: a claim about causal contribution, a claim about explanatory salience (what the narrative treats as worth emphasising), and a claim about agency in the sense that carries goals or interests. I suspect that the determinism objection is nearly always aimed at the second, is answered or simply evaded at the first, and only rarely has any purchase on the third, and that the tangle persists because historical explanation has no analogue of the likelihood-ratio reframing. So the question I want the proposal to pose is roughly: can the conceptual structure of probabilistic event attribution be transposed into historical explanation of non-human causes, and if it can, does the transposition dissolve the determinism objection, merely relocate it, or introduce something worse — for instance, that the counterfactual reference world a historian would need is not even definable rather than merely unobservable, or that the reframing erases the distinction between a cause and a standing condition, which is the very distinction that makes narrative history possible at all.

Please turn this into a two-page thesis proposal with the following parts: a precise statement of the question, formulated so that it admits of a determinate answer rather than being a matter of temperament; an account of why the positions currently on offer — those who want strict symmetry between human and non-human actants, those who treat the vocabulary of agency as a harmless metaphor, and those who see determinism as a political rather than a conceptual error — each leave my question open; a method section saying what I would actually do, month by month, including which cases I would work through (I want at least one volcanic-climate episode and one epidemic, and the proposal should acknowledge that in the epidemic case even the identification of the pathogen is contested); an explicit statement of what a successful result would look like and, more importantly, what would count as the project having failed; and a frank assessment of whether this is feasible for one person in twelve months with no fieldwork and no archives, together with a sentence or two on why this is a philosophy-of-history project rather than a piece of historiography. Do not include a bibliography or any list of references; I will supply the citations myself once the framing is settled. Please begin directly with the proposal document itself, with no preface, no commentary, and no restatement of what I have asked for.
