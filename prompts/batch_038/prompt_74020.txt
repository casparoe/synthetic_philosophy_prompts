I coordinate a peer accountability program at a mid-sized nonprofit: small facilitated circles in which staff raise and work through complaints about one another's conduct. A skeptical member of our board has asked me to put in writing the normative picture the program rests on, and in particular to explain why one employee is entitled to criticize another at all. Before I submit it, I want an audit of the commitments I have actually ended up holding, because I suspect that at least two of them cannot both survive and I do not know which.

Here is the set. I use "standing" for a precondition on the entitlement to blame someone and "license" for the all-things-considered permission to voice the blame; on my view standing is necessary for license but not sufficient.

1. Blame is not essentially an address to the person blamed. To blame someone is to revise one's own attitudes and intentions toward them in light of how their conduct has impaired the relationship; whether one says anything to them is optional and secondary.

2. What forfeits standing is not the critic's own fault but the implicit self-exemption in the blaming. In holding another to a norm, the hypocrite commits herself to the view that the norm binds them and not her. The forfeiture is a matter of that commitment, not of the underlying behavior.

3. Because the forfeiture is a commitment, it can be undone by one. If the hypocrite first blames herself — acknowledges the same fault in herself, in the same terms and with the same seriousness — the implicit exemption is withdrawn and her standing to blame the other person returns. No apology to that person is required for this.

4. Standing is all-or-nothing. Either the implicit exemption is present or it is not; there is no such thing as having sixty percent of the standing to blame someone for lying.

5. Nonetheless I want to say, and want our program's language to be able to say, that critics differ in how badly they are disqualified. Someone who denounces a trivial fault while guilty of a grave and unrelated one is worse off than someone whose own fault is of the same kind and comparable weight.

6. A victim always has standing to blame her wrongdoer. Her own record, whatever it contains, is irrelevant to that.

7. The circles depend on participants who are neither victims nor complicit nor otherwise involved in the conduct at issue, and I defend their participation on the ground that moral norms are held jointly, so any member of the community is in principle a proper critic of any violation of them.

8. One case verdict I am attached to. A colleague, R., publicly reproaches a junior staffer for padding an expense report. R. has been quietly doing the same thing for years and has no idea that she does it; if it were shown to her she would be appalled and would stop. I want to say that R. lacks standing to blame him, and also that she has done nothing wrong in voicing the reproach and that what she does is not hypocrisy in the culpable sense.

What I want is an audit rather than a defense or a lecture. Begin your answer with a single sentence stating your overall verdict on whether the package hangs together, then give the reasoning. Identify which of these commitments genuinely conflict and which only appear to conflict and dissolve under inspection; rank the genuine conflicts by severity, most damaging first; and for each, say what has to give, what the cheapest available revision is, and what that revision costs me — including whether the revised position can still support the circles described in item 7 and can still deliver the verdict in item 8. Please do not assume I would rather keep any particular item; I have no prior about which one goes. Write in American English, with no preamble and no restatement of the question.
