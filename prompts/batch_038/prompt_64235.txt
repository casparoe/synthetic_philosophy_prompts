I'm writing up a position paper for my distributive justice seminar (we've just finished Parfit's "Equality and Priority") and I'd like an audit of the package I've landed on before I commit to it in print. My commitments:

(1) I'm a prioritarian in Parfit's sense: the moral weight of a benefit to a person depends only on how well off that person already is, with diminishing marginal moral value as one moves up the scale, and not at all on how their position compares to anyone else's.

(2) I take the leveling-down objection to be decisive against telic egalitarianism: equality is not intrinsically valuable in any respect, so a change that makes some people worse off and no one better off is in no way an improvement.

(3) Well-being is cardinally measurable and interpersonally comparable, but only up to an arbitrary choice of origin. Differences between levels are meaningful; I don't think there's any fact of the matter about a zero level of well-being, or about levels below it, and I'd rather not have my view rest on one.

(4) Since we can't measure well-being directly, I apply the concave weighting to income. I justify progressive redistribution with two arguments that I treat as distinct but mutually reinforcing: the diminishing marginal utility of income, and the extra moral weight owed to those at the bottom.

(5) Prioritarianism is a thesis about the goodness of outcomes, and I think the justice of institutions is a function of the prioritarian-weighted well-being they generate.

(6) A classmate says all of this is just egalitarianism with the word "equality" scrubbed out, because in every two-group case I've discussed, the distribution I prefer turns out to be the more equal one. I'm inclined to think he's wrong, but I can't tell whether that's an argument or loyalty to a label.

Tell me which of these pull against each other and which only look like they do. I don't want a survey of the prioritarianism literature — I want to know where my own set breaks.

Please use these sections, in this order. "Bottom line" (three or four sentences: is the package jointly tenable as it stands?). "Genuine tensions" (the bulk of the reply, roughly 400–500 words, ordered by severity; for each, why the conflict is substantive rather than verbal, which commitment would have to go or be weakened, and what that loss costs me). "Only apparent" (about 150 words: which worry I've listed dissolves on inspection, and what exactly does the dissolving). "Cheapest repair" (about 150 words: the single revision that resolves the most, and what it gives up).
