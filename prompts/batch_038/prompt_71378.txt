Consider the record of numerical weather prediction: Richardson's hand-computed forecast, which took him months and missed badly; the first machine forecasts on ENIAC; and the decades since, in which skill has climbed by roughly a useful day per decade while the ceiling has refused to move past about two weeks. Analyze that record through the lens of deterministic chaos, keeping three claims apart: that the atmosphere is deterministic; that finite-precision initial data combined with sensitive dependence fix a predictability horizon; and that forecasts past that horizon are worthless. Which of these does the history actually support, and which does the popular butterfly-effect story collapse into one another? Then say what the lens explains and what it leaves unexplained about the operational response — the shift from single trajectories to ensembles, from predicting where the flow will be to predicting its distribution — and where it falls silent, both on why skill kept improving inside the horizon and on why tropical phenomena like El Niño are usefully forecast months out. Finally: does the two-week ceiling embarrass Laplace's demon, and would adding molecular-scale quantum uncertainty to the story change the verdict?
