Three weeks ago I started as a research engineer at an AI safety org — mostly evals scaffolding and interpretability-adjacent tooling, nothing exotic — and in my second week my manager asked me to write a scoping memo on whether the team should spend any of next year's compute budget on defensive work against acausal attacks, with surrogate goals being the main proposed defence. She gave me three weeks and said, helpfully, just read the discussion and come back with a recommendation. The problem is that I've read the discussion, twice, and I can't tell whether there is an actual disagreement in it, which makes writing a memo very hard because every draft I produce is just me picking a side by vibe and then dressing it up.

Here's what I think I'm seeing, tell me if I've garbled it. On one side: take decision theory seriously and you get agents that can build accurate models of other agents' decision procedures without ever interacting with them, and once that's on the table, threatening someone no longer requires a causal channel — the attacker doesn't have to be near us or able to reach us, it just has to be the sort of agent that would carry the threat out, and we have to be able to figure that out about it. If that works, then an advanced system we build is exposed to a population of such attackers we know nothing about, and the obvious defensive move is to install something cheap that an attacker can threaten instead of the things we actually care about, so that complying costs us almost nothing. On the other side: this is a philosophy puzzle that misfires, for reasons that get stated in a bunch of different voices. The attacker is being assumed to have a very precise model of our decision procedure, which is a huge empirical capability claim smuggled in as a logical possibility. A threatener that correctly predicts we'll fold will threaten us and one that correctly predicts we won't fold won't bother, so the whole thing is unstable and there's no equilibrium to point at. The priors over how many such agents are out there and how hostile they are are pulled out of the air, and doing expected-value arithmetic on invented priors with unbounded stakes is exactly the reasoning that makes you pay a mugging, so a theory that recommends paying is broken, not deep. And no observation could ever confirm or refute any of it, which for some people is the end of the conversation. There's also a third mood that I can't place: people who say the interesting thing isn't blackmail at all but the cooperative version — the same machinery gives reasons to trade and cooperate — and who think the threats debate is downstream of that one rather than separate from it.

My working hypothesis, and I want you to attack it rather than flatter it, is that this is three different arguments wearing one coat, and that the one doing all the load-bearing work is the one nobody is explicitly having. There's a dispute about priors over an empirical question nobody can gather data on; a dispute about what an argument is even allowed to establish when no possible observation bears on it, i.e. whether decision theory in this domain outputs action guidance or only coherence constraints; and, underneath both, a dispute about what we want our decision procedure to be like under pressure — whether the design target is to never be reliably exploitable by anything that can model you, or to maximize expected value and eat the exploitability where the numbers say to. That third one sounds like a value judgement about what kind of agent you want to build, not a technical claim, and my suspicion is that it's why people who agree on all the mechanics still don't budge. If I'm right, the memo should probably say so, and I'd like to be able to defend that.

Could you write this up with headers, in this order:

1. **Bottom line** — two or three sentences I could paste into the memo, saying what kind of disagreement this is and what follows for whether to fund the work.
2. **What each side is actually committed to** — about 200 words. Strip out the rhetoric and give me the load-bearing commitments, including the ones the two sides share. I especially want to know whether the third mood I mentioned (cooperation instead of blackmail) is a separate position or a re-description of one of the other two.
3. **The diagnosis** — about 450 words, this is the part I care about. Take my hypothesis seriously but don't just accept it: if the split is somewhere else, say where and why. Be explicit about which parts are verbal, which are about standards of evidence, which are hidden empirical questions with no way to get data, and which are bedrock value commitments neither side can argue for. Name the specific step where each side would have to change its mind and say whether that step is available to them.
4. **What would count as progress** — about 250 words. A distinction, a formal result about whether the threat scenario has a stable equilibrium at all, some empirical finding about how good models of other agents' decision procedures can get, or a change of question. If your honest answer is that no progress should be expected, say that and tell me what the team should do instead given that — including whether installing surrogate goals is still worth it as cheap insurance even if the threat case is unresolved.
