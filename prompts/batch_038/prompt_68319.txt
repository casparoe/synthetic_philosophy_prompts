I teach an intro phil of mind section and I've been leaning hard on the desktop/GUI line to explain the "consciousness is a user illusion" family of views — experience is the interface, not the machinery; the icons aren't little pictures of the files, the trash can isn't where the bytes go, and the whole display is shaped by what helps you get things done rather than by what's actually true down below. Students eat it up. Last week a colleague sitting in on the lecture pushed back afterwards and said the analogy doesn't just break down, it breaks down in a way that undoes the view I was using it to sell: a GUI only makes sense if there's a real user sitting outside the machine who could in principle open a terminal and go look, whereas on this story there's nobody home and no terminal either, so either I'm smuggling a homunculus back in through the front door or I haven't described the view I think I described. I didn't have an answer for him on the spot and now I'm worried my favourite bit of pedagogy is a trap.

Can you do a proper audit for me? First the mapping, laid out explicitly — what plays the role of the desktop, the icons, the mouse click, the file system, the kernel, the user, and the fact that somebody designed the interface on purpose. Then the disanalogies, but sorted: which ones actually threaten what I use the analogy to do (which is basically two things — dissolving the "what is it like" question, and making it comfortable that experience doesn't resemble neural activity in any structural way) versus which ones are decorative griping about computers. I especially want you to push on three points: whether the interface picture needs a user and, if it doesn't, what's left in the analogy doing the explanatory work once the user is deleted; whether "interfaces are tuned for fitness, not truth" can even be asserted by a mind that is, on its own account, nothing but an interface, since the claim itself is supposed to be a truth-tracking insight about the machine; and whether anything at all plays the role of opening a terminal — some route by which the system inspects its own sub-personal machinery from the inside — because if nothing does, I want to know what that costs the view and what it costs the analogy separately.

End by telling me straight: does my colleague have it, does the user illusion view survive the audit even if the GUI framing doesn't, and what should I use in class next semester instead — including the option that the honest answer is to drop analogies of this shape entirely and say something less vivid. No need for citations or a reading list, I'm not assigning anything, I just want the reasoning worked through.
