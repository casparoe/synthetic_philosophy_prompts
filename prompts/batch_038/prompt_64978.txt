I'm teaching a senior ethics seminar in the spring — 18 students, 75 minutes, movable tables but no projector and I don't want to use one. They'll have read the bits of Scanlon on what could reasonably be rejected, and about half of them think they already get it. Design an activity that makes them physically run into the thing that actually makes contractualism weird: the restriction that you only ever compare one person's complaint against one other person's, so a million small burdens never get to pile up against one big one. They should hit that wall before anyone names it — no mini-lecture up front, and the phrase 'individualist restriction' shouldn't be uttered until the debrief.

Mechanically I want the constraint enforced by the setup rather than by me policing talk. So give me the dossiers, the roles, exactly how a comparison gets staged at a table, what a student is allowed to say and what gets ruled out of order, and some small real stake. I'm happy for the tribunal to actually decide something for the class — a take-home extention, which film we screen at the end of term, whatever you think will hold — but no money, no candy, no participation points.

Three cases, in this order: a warm-up where everyone's harm is identical and the numbers somehow manage to count; the transmitter room one (Jones pinned under equipment, getting him out means cutting the broadcast for fifteen minutes and a vast audience loses the end of a World Cup match); and a nastier one of your own devising where strict pairwise comparison commits them to something they'll find repulsive.

Here's the bit I most want built in: the order the pairs get heard should matter. Give me a mechanism for fixing that order, and have them run one case twice under different orders so they watch the verdict flip and then have to explain why it did. I want them leaving suspicious of the procedure, not reassured by it.

Then the debrief: map specific moments from the run onto the restriction, onto the tie-breaking move Scanlon reaches for when the harms are the same, and onto the difference between reasonably rejecting a principle and merely not liking it. Also a blunt list of what will go wrong the first time I run this — I'm guessing at least one student will try to speak for a crowd; how do I handle that without breaking the fiction? — and an honest note on which feature of the view the game just can't reproduce and I'll have to supply by hand. Finish with a compressed 50-minute version and a fix in case enrolment lands on an odd number.
