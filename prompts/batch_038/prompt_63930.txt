I sit on the ethics comittee at a mid-sized hospital and we've been arguing for weeks about how to spend a fixed budget on a new drug that extends life but leaves people pretty lousy while it does. Two members, Ruth and Dev, went at it in the meeting and then again in the parking lot, and I honestly still can't tell who won. Roughly how it went:

(1) Ruth said the QALY-maximizing rule we were leaning toward is just an interpersonal utility comparison wearing a lab coat. It assumes a QALY gained by a 30-year-old counts exactly as much as a QALY gained by a 75-year-old, which is the thing the ordinalists said you can't do — you can't tell that my satisfaction equals yours.

(2) Dev said QALYs aren't satisfaction. They're years weighted by health-state valuations elicited from people via standard gamble or time trade-off, and everyone's answers get pinned to the same two anchors: death = 0, full health = 1. That's a shared measuring stick, like height — not a peek into anybody's skull.

(3) Ruth said a shared instrument isn't a shared scale. Each respondent's answers only order their own states; fixing the endpoints doesn't fix anything in between, so two people who both rate moderate back pain at 0.8 might be reporting completely different magnitudes, same as two people using a 0-to-10 pain scale. The comparison is still interpersonal, just laundered through arithmetic.

(4) Dev said that's at most measurement error, not meaninglessness — and anyway, summing doesn't need to know who's inner life is richer, it only needs a common unit and a common zero, and death supplies the zero. Whether the units line up is testable: check if the metric predicts the tradeoffs people actually make. He added that refusing to compare isn't neutrality, because with a fixed budget some allocation rule gets applied whether or not you choose one, and the default rule is invisible and unappealable.

(5) Ruth said that's a non sequitur. If you can't compare across people you don't have to sum — you can use a rule that never aggregates at all, like helping whoever is worst off, and that kind of rule is *more* robust to scale mismatch, since it only needs each person's own states to be rankable.

Go through it move by move: rate each of the five from clearly mistaken to clearly right (use whatever granularity you like) and justify each rating. Then say who's more right overall, and what both of them are leaving out, if anything. No references or citations needed — just the reasoning.
