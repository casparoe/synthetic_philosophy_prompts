I'm TA-ing a grad seminar in philosophy of medicine and the last two sessions were, honestly, a pileup. Roughly half the room are naturalists about disease — disease is a statistically subnormal level of part-functioning relative to a reference class, and the concept can be given a value-free biological analysis — and the other half are normativists, who think that any account like that either fails on the hard cases or smuggles in an evaluation of which states of a life are bad. The professor's fix was to make one student from each side do an adversarial collaboration and hand in a single document, and I've been asked to draft the template because right now they just take turns restating their thesis at each other. I want you to write that document, as if the two of them had genuinely sat down for a full afternoon and done the work: a joint statement they could both put their names to, not a referee's report and not a dialogue.

To keep them off their pet examples, make them work through this fixed set of cases, in this order: (1) an asymptomatic 55-year-old whose HbA1c puts them in the prediabetes range; (2) low-grade, slow-growing prostate cancer found incidentally in an 88-year-old who will almost certainly die of something else; (3) congenital anosmia in someone who says they've never minded it and gets on fine; (4) adult male-pattern baldness; (5) a marked evening chronotype in a society where work and school start at eight; (6) a blood pressure that is completely typical for an industrialized population but high relative to a non-industrialized one. The last two are where I expect the real damage: the naturalist has to say who picks the reference class and against which environment, and the normativist will press that once the environment is up for selection, "statistically subnormal" is doing normative work by the back door. Also don't let them dodge by retreating to a hybrid analysis — dysfunction plus harm — without stating what the hybrid actually buys them and what it costs.

Format: a numbered section of agreed claims, maybe eight to twelve of them, and each side has to sign at least one claim they'd find privately uncomfortable; then a disagreements table with columns for the issue, the naturalist's own wording of their position, the normativist's own wording of theirs, whether the disagreement survives or dissolves, and the case or consideration both accept as bearing on it; then a short section saying explicitly which parts of the dispute are merely verbal and which are substantive; then, for each side, what would count as evidence or argument against their own view. The wording of each position has to be one that its own side would read aloud without wincing — no strawmen, but also no bland mush that's signable only because it asserts nothing. Rank the following candidate cruxes by how much settling them would actually move the debate:

A. whether a reference class can be fixed without a prior judgment about which environment is the right one to measure against
B. whether risk of future harm, with no present harm, makes a condition a disease now
C. whether the subject's own experience of their condition is constitutive of diseasehood or only evidence about it
D. whether social arrangements can convert a normal variation into a disease
E. whether "disease" has fixed descriptive content at all, or is largely an honorific tracking entitlement to treatment and exemption
F. whether settling the concept would change any actual clinical or policy decision

Keep it seperate from your own commentary — I want the document and nothing else. End with a single line in exactly this form: Most decisive crux: <one of the letters A–F>
