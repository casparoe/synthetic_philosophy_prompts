ok, working novelist here, four years into a book about Alfred Russel Wallace, and I've hit a structural problem I can't think my way out of. Hoping somebody with more philosophy-of-science in their head than me can help.

background: my third act is built entirely on the 1858 business. Wallace on Ternate with the fever, writing the essay, sealing it up and posting it to Darwin; then Lyell and Hooker arranging the joint reading at the Linnean Society so nobody gets scooped outright. I've written it as a tragedy. Wallace is the man who could have been first and wasn't, and the ache of the act is supposed to be that he loses the one thing that would have made his life make sense — and loses it partly to his own decency.

but I've been reading around the history-of-science side and I keep runnning into this multiple-discovery stuff: Newton and Leibniz, Darwin and Wallace, oxygen, non-Euclidean geometry, the telephone, whatever. And the argument that seems to travel with it is that when the same idea turns up in two heads independently in the same decade, the idea was in the air. Its going to happen regardless. It's a product of its moment rather than of a person, so strictly speaking nobody's entitled to much credit for it. And once I actually swallowed that, my third act collapsed on me. Because if natural selection was going to get written down in the late 1850s no matter who did it, then Wallace didn't lose anything — the slot he lost would have been filled by someone else anyway, and there's nothing there to mourn. My tragic guy is just a man who happened to be standing a bit further from a door that was opening all by itself.

so my question is craft advice with the philosophy load-bearing: how do I write an act whose whole emotional engine is inevitability without it turning into a shrug? Do I lean in — make the reader feel the flatness, the particular horror of a world where it doesn't matter who does the thing — or is there some way to hold onto it-was-coming-anyway AND still make Wallace's loss a real loss? And if the honest answer is that I have to make the idea less inevitable in order to save the book, tell me, because I'd much rather hear it now than in draft eleven. I'm most stuck on the last bit: how a reader is supposed to care about a man losing a race that, on this account, was never actually run.
