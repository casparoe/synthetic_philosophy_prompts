I am in the third year of a philosophy PhD, working between philosophy of biology and philosophy of mind, and I would like a critical assessment of a thesis I have been developing rather than a general survey of the literature on machine understanding.

The thesis concerns the two inversions that Daniel Dennett discussed under a label he took from one of Darwin's nineteenth-century critics: the idea that a process can produce a beautifully adapted organism without knowing how to make one, and the parallel idea, drawn from Turing, that a machine can carry out arithmetic operations impeccably without knowing what arithmetic is. I call my view the single-inversion thesis. It has three commitments.

First, that these are not two analogies that happen to rhyme but one principle discovered twice: comprehension is never a causally operative ingredient in the production of competent performance. It is at most a high-level description of a pattern that such performance happens to instantiate, and the description can be applied or withheld without any change in what the system does.

Second, that the principle is symmetric in its reach. It does not license a new boundary between systems that merely simulate understanding and systems that possess it; it dissolves the older boundary, because human understanding is itself a retrospective gloss laid over competences produced by processes that understand nothing. On my reading, talk of comprehension is best treated as a stance-relative attribution rather than as a claim about a grade of mechanism, so there is no determinate fact of the matter about where comprehension begins. I take this to go further than Dennett himself, who treats comprehension as graded and as genuinely, if partially, present in artificial devices; I want my version assessed on its own terms, not as an interpretation of his.

Third, that arguments of the Chinese Room family are consequently not so much false as malformed. They require a determinate threshold between genuine and simulated understanding in order to generate the intuition they trade on, and the single-inversion thesis denies that any such threshold exists to be crossed.

With that set out, please give me a map of the view's exposure:

1. Identify what would count against the single-inversion thesis. Rank the defeaters, and for each say whether a defender should classify it as fatal, seriously damaging, or merely awkward, and why it belongs in that category rather than a neighbouring one.

2. For the most serious entries, describe the shape the evidence or argument would have to take. I am interested in concrete kinds of result — something from comparative cognition, an interpretability finding about trained networks, a formal result about what simulation does and does not preserve, a development in the metaphysics of realization or grounding — rather than in the mere existence of rival positions. What should I be watching for?

3. Distinguish which defeaters a committed defender would concede as counting against the view and which she would dismiss, and say explicitly where you think the dismissal is too cheap. I have one worry I want examined directly: that the asymmetry between the two inversions is my weakest point. The Darwinian inversion concerns the process that produces a design and leaves entirely open that the resulting organism harbours genuine internal models of its environment, whereas the Turing inversion is supposed to concern the operations of the system itself. If that is right, my inference from one inversion to the other may equivocate between the level at which designing happens and the level at which performing happens. Assess whether this worry is decisive, or whether the thesis can be reformulated to survive it, and if it can, say what the reformulation costs.

4. Give me the strongest reason to think the thesis is insulated from refutation — that its stance-relative treatment of comprehension absorbs every counterexample simply by re-describing it — and then judge whether that insulation is a defect in a thesis of this kind or a legitimate feature of it. I would rather be told plainly that my view risks nothing than be handed objections it can swallow.

5. Finally, say what the thesis predicts that its rivals do not. I want at least one place where accepting it, rather than a comprehension-first alternative, should change how one expects things to turn out — in the conduct of some science, or in how artificial systems are built and evaluated. If you can find no such place, that is itself an answer I would want to receive, and I would want to hear what you take it to show.

Please organise the response around these five tasks, and resist padding it with background on positions I have not named. I am not looking for sources or further references; I am looking for a defended judgement about where this view is most vulnerable and how much of it could survive.
