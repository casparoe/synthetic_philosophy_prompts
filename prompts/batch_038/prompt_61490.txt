I am in the final weeks of an upper-level course in normative ethics, and our last seminar is devoted to the separateness-of-persons objection: the charge that a theory which adds up gains and losses across different lives has treated a many-personed world as though it were a single person deciding, and has thereby let the question of who bears what drop out of the moral description entirely. Our discussion of it never converged. Roughly a third of us thought the objection decisive; a third thought it proved far too much, since if the boundary between persons is what forbids adding across it, then the same reasoning seems to forbid adding across the boundary between my Tuesday and my next year, which would outlaw the toothache I accept now for the sake of the healthy mouth later; and the rest thought it merely restated, in louder language, the very intuition it was supposed to explain. The department's student reading is the following evening, and I have offered to close it with a poem that keeps all three positions alive at once.

Please write that poem as a strict sestina: six sestets plus a three-line envoi, unrhymed, in the standard rotation of end-words (if the first stanza ends 1-2-3-4-5-6, the subsequent stanzas run 6-1-5-2-4-3, 3-6-4-1-2-5, 5-3-2-6-1-4, 4-5-1-3-6-2, 2-4-6-5-3-1), with the envoi gathering all six words, three of them closing lines and three buried mid-line. The form should be chosen deliberately rather than decorated: three of my six end-words should belong to the vocabulary of the particular — the first-person singular, the individual who cannot be averaged away — and three should belong to the vocabulary of net arithmetic, and I want the assignment of words to slots to be worked out in advance so that the permutation itself carries the argument. At minimum, arrange the end-words so that the strongest aggregate term and the word "who" fall in adjacent lines in several stanzas, so that the substitution of a total for a person is literally heard as the poem turns; and so that the poem's final word is "who."

The six sestets should between them do the following work, though you are free to interleave and reorder it: the picture of a household, a crew, or a body that decides as one and is thereby asked to stand in for many; the thought that nobody is ever situated at the net, that the sum is not anyone's afternoon; the picture of persons as places where good and bad happen rather than as the ones to whom things are done; the intrapersonal counter-argument, given its full weight rather than waved away, since the dentist's trade is the objection's hardest case; the pressure from the other direction, that the line between one person and two may be less sharp than the objection needs, so that it cannot simply lean on that line; and the reply that the objection has only renamed the intuition it set out to vindicate. If you can also fold in the thought that what the objection really delivers is not a differently shaped total but a prohibition — certain things may not be done to this person at all, whatever the arithmetic — put that pressure into the stanza that answers the objection rather than into the envoi. The envoi should refuse to settle the matter without shrugging at it.

No proper names, and no argumentative vocabulary: the words utility, utilitarian, consequentialist, aggregation, and the phrase separateness of persons should not appear, since the ideas must be carried by the images instead. Keep the register domestic and physical — a kitchen, a queue, a sickbed, a shared bill, a hand — and avoid the emblematic props this kind of poem usually reaches for: no scales, no ledgers, no abacus, no coins. Someone who has read the debate should be able to recognise every move in it without a single name being dropped, and someone who has never heard of the debate should simply find a good poem about a family counting. Lines should be long enough to hold a whole thought, close to pentameter but never padded to reach it.
