I'm an SRE and I write incident postmortems for a living, and last week I got into it with a teammate who insists a good postmortem is nothing but the facts. Trying to argue with him I stumbled onto some historiography stuff: an early 1800s German who thought the job was just to show how it actually happened, and then later a British one and an American who wrote about narrative who basically said the events don't arrive with a plot attached — you pick a story shape and lay it on top, and the american's shapes were something like romance, comedy, tragedy, satire.

Write me a poem that stages the argument instead of explaining it. Here's the constraint I care about: first give me six bare facts from one outage — timestamp of the deploy, the metric that moved, the alert that got silenced, the wrong first guess, the actual fix, recovery time — and then four stanzas that each retell all six of those facts, in the same order, with none added, dropped, or altered. One stanza plays it as a romance, one as a comedy, one as a tragedy, one as a satire. Keep the stanzas short, like 6 lines each max, so it's obvious the facts are identical and only the shape changes. Then a two-line coda that lands on the german's slogan, and it should read differently after the four versions than it would have at the start. Don't name any of the three guys anywhere in the poem — I want to read it aloud at our retro without it sounding like a lecture.
