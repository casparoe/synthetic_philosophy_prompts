So I TA a second-semester intermediate micro section, and to keep my own head straight I've started writing out my actual commitments about dominance reasoning in normal-form games — mostly because one of my students (a philosophy major, annoyingly sharp) told me after section that my position is "a mush of two different solution concepts wearing one hat," and I want to know whether he's right before the final exam. Here's the list, roughly as I'd defend it:

(1) A rational player never plays a strictly dominated strategy, where "dominated" includes dominated by a mixture — in one of the problem sets I set this term, an action of mine was worse than a 50/50 mix of two of my other actions against every pure choice the opponent could make, even though neither of those two beat it on its own, and I deleted it without qualms. What I like about this kind of step is that it only uses my own payoff numbers. It doesn't matter what I think the others will do, the action is just bad, so it can't be rational. That's why I trust it more than equilibrium reasoning, which needs everyone's beliefs to line up in a particular way.
(2) I won't help myself to correlated beliefs in a noncooperative game. If A and B can't talk and are choosing seperately, my belief about the pair (what A does, what B does) has to factor into a belief about A times a belief about B. Handing me an arbitrary joint distribution over their choices is smuggling in a signalling device the game doesn't contain.
(3) In a three-player game from my own problem set, one of my actions was never a best response to any combination of independent mixtures by the other two, but no mixture of my own actions dominated it. I deleted it anyway and I take that to be exactly the same kind of step as in (1) — one more round of iterated dominance, nothing fancier.
(4) Order doesn't matter when you iterate. I also delete weakly dominated strategies, in whatever order is convenient, and I read the surviving set off as the game's solution.
(5) Whatever survives has to contain all the Nash equilibria of the original game. If a procedure throws away a Nash equilibrium, I don't count it as a procedure about rationality, I count it as a procedure that has smuggled in an extra assumption.
(6) I'm not willing to sign up for infinite stacks of "I know that you know that I know" — that's my main complaint about the epistemic foundations stuff — but I'm perfectly happy running iterated dominance, because in a finite game it stops.

Does this hang together? My student's charge, specifically, was that (3) isn't the same animal as (1) at all and that (4) and (5) can't both be true, but I honestly don't know if he's right about both, or right about one and bluffing about the other. Please don't just tell me everything is in tension with everything — rank the problems by how bad they actually are, and for each one say what specifically has to go and what the cheapest repair is, including what that repair costs me elsewhere on the list (I suspect fixing one thing wrecks something I like more). Also flag any pair that only looks like a problem and isn't, and say why it dissolves. Where you can nail a problem down with a tiny game you make up yourself, do that — but if you can't actually produce one with the property you're claiming, say so rather than inventing a matrix that doesn't work. No citations and no re-explaining the definitions to me, assume I know perfectly well what strict and weak dominance mean.
