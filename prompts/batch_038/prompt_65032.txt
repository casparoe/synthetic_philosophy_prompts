I've spent twenty-two years as a non-life underwriter, and for the last nine of those I've worked in capital modelling: setting exposure limits peril by peril, deciding how much reinsurance to buy, arguing at committee about whether a loss curve nobody can validate should still drive a capital add-on. I have no philosophy training and no intention of acquiring any; what follows is something I've assembled for myself over a lot of evenings with articles I found online. Twice in the last decade my firm had to make a call that I now think was philosophical rather than technical — whether to keep writing property in a coastal county after a storm whose severity we had no basis for anticipating, and whether to touch wildfire exposure on the urban fringe at any price at all. In both cases we ended up declining business we could have sold profitably, and we described the decision to ourselves as prudence. Reading about the precautionary principle afterwards, I had the persistent feeling that my profession already runs on some version of it, and that the arguments about it are conducted in words I don't need.

So here is what I'd like you to do. Restate that debate, as faithfully as you can manage, in the vocabulary I actually use at work. I mean specifically: expected loss and loss distributions, tail events and the probability of ruin, risk appetite and exposure limits, the difference between pricing a risk and refusing to write it, diversification and the way correlations collapse precisely when you need them not to, reinsurance as a means of moving a risk onto somebody else's balance sheet, capital held against model risk rather than against estimated losses, adverse selection and moral hazard, and the distinction we draw between a parameter we estimate badly and a process we cannot model at all. Take the main positions one at a time. First, the strong formulation, on which a novel activity should not proceed until it has been shown to be harmless. Second, the weak one, which I believe descends from a United Nations declaration adopted in Rio in 1992, and which holds that unsettled science is not by itself a reason to postpone measures proportionate to the seriousness of the threatened damage. Third, the objection that the principle is either empty or self-defeating, since inaction carries risks of its own and can therefore always be turned against itself, producing paralysis rather than protection. Fourth, the reply that this objection misses the point, because some harms cannot be undone, some losses fall on people who never agreed to bear them, and the burden of demonstrating safety should sit with whoever proposes the change rather than with whoever would be exposed to it. And fifth, the version I've seen described as maximin reasoning: a rule for choosing when you decline to attach probabilities at all, taking instead the option whose worst outcome you could survive.

For each of those five I want an honest ledger rather than a paraphrase. Which claims map onto something my profession genuinely does, and would it recognise them as the same thing once translated? Which can be carried across only by quietly changing the subject — and be precise about what got changed in the carrying. And which have no expression whatsoever in an underwriter's toolkit? I have two suspicions of my own, and I want you to test them rather than flatter them. The first is that irreversibility does such heavy lifting in the philosophers' debate partly because my framework has only three boxes — a risk is priceable, reinsurable, or unwritable, and those three exhaust the space — so a harm that can be neither undone nor shifted onto a counterparty is not a category I can even form; that might mean my vocabulary is impoverished, or it might mean the category is doing no real work. The second is that the whole disagreement about acts and omissions, about causing harm being worse than failing to prevent it, simply dissolves in my world, because a loss is a loss on the ledger regardless of whose name is on the file; but I cannot tell whether that dissolution is a genuine insight into the debate or an artefact of a bookkeeping convention I've never questioned. Tell me which it is. Please don't smuggle my framework's assumptions in and present them as translations: if a position only makes sense on the premise that risks can be pooled, priced, or declined, say so rather than translating it into that premise. And finish with the verdict I actually care about — for each piece that failed to carry across, whether the failure exposes a hole in the way I think or exposes a claim that was never as clear as it sounded.
