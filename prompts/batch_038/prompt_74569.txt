I serve as secretary to the Joint Standards Panel of a privately held machinery manufacturer with a plant outside Stuttgart and one outside Cleveland. The panel has six members, three drawn from each site, co-chaired by the German operations director and the American quality lead. It hears two kinds of matter: prospective requests for authorization to depart from a documented procedure, and retrospective reviews of departures that have already occurred. Its holdings run to a paragraph or so each. Below is the substance of the first six, which I have transcribed faithfully but put in my own words.

1. Cleveland. A technician could not complete a mandatory lockout verification because the only certified verifier was on leave, and the machine had to be isolated that day for an unrelated hazard. He performed the verification himself, working from the documented checklist. The panel ratified the act, required the file to record that verification had been performed by an uncertified person, and directed that the certification rule be amended within sixty days to name a deputy.

2. Stuttgart. A line supervisor would not sign a batch record he took to understate a defect rate, and offered to have a colleague sign in his place. The panel upheld his refusal, declined to void or amend the record, declined to compel his signature, and required an independent second measurement. It noted that the signature rule and the accuracy rule could not both be satisfied by him on these facts.

3. Cleveland. A logistics team routed shipments outside the documented routing standard to save two days and a substantial sum; the reroute succeeded. The panel refused to ratify, entered the deviation as unauthorized, imposed no penalty, and forwarded the team's data to the rule owner with a recommendation that the standard be reconsidered prospectively. Its reasoning observed that the routing standard exists to hold volumes to a contracted carrier and to keep a single auditable trail, neither of which the saving served.

4. Stuttgart. An engineer followed a written procedure to the letter. The procedure required a step that degraded a safety margin, and the degradation was discovered before anyone was hurt; the defect was not visible on the face of the procedure but required knowledge of the material's fatigue behaviour. The panel declined to find fault with the engineer, held that a person who follows a documented procedure in good faith does not bear the consequences of that procedure's defects, and ordered a rewrite on a thirty-day deadline. It added that the separate rule requiring personnel to report known defects was not in conflict with the procedure and that the duty to report had attached independently.

5. Cleveland. An employee sought exemption from a client-facing appearance standard on grounds of personal conviction, with no religious or medical claim. The panel denied the request. It recorded that it did not doubt the employee's sincerity, that the standard's purpose was one the employee's own conduct would directly defeat, and that granting the request would oblige the panel to weigh the force of a conviction against the force of a rule's purpose, which it said it was not constituted to do.

6. Stuttgart. Two documented standards assigned approval authority over changes to a shared system to different offices. An engineer chose one path and proceeded. The panel ratified, holding that where the standards contradict one another the person who must act may choose, provided the choice and its grounds are recorded and the unchosen office is notified. It recorded that its holding rested on the contradiction and did not address whether the burden imposed by a single, internally consistent standard could ever justify departure from it.

The matter now before the panel is this. A test engineer in Cleveland was to run a pressure-cycling test under an in-house procedure transposed, years earlier, from a manufacturer's specification written for a lower-rated fixture. Executed exactly as written, the procedure would drive the rig past its rated limit; from the fixture's nameplate and the current manufacturer's manual she judged that a technician at the flange could have been injured. The in-house procedure is unambiguous and does not contradict any other standard in the company's document set; the rated limit appears only in the manufacturer's literature and on the nameplate, neither of which is incorporated by reference. She halted the test, ran it to the manufacturer's parameters, finished a day late, missed a client milestone, notified her manager the same day, and logged her reasoning contemporaneously. She now asks the panel for two things: ratification of the deviation, and standing authorization to use the manufacturer's parameters whenever the in-house procedure and the equipment rating disagree. No one was hurt and no rule was violated other than the procedure itself.

I would be grateful for the following.

First, set out the candidate principles that survive all six holdings — each stated precisely enough that it would have generated every one of them — and identify any familiar candidate principle that the record has already ruled out, saying which holding rules it out.

Second, for each surviving principle, give its predicted answer to both of the engineer's requests, since the two requests may come apart.

Third, give me your single best prediction, name which of the six is the closest analog, and explain why the resemblance holds rather than merely asserting it.

Fourth, at least one surviving principle should turn on whether the fixture's rating counts as part of the documented system. Say which one, and say what the panel would have to assume in order to treat an external manufacturer's specification as internal to its own rules — and whether anything in holdings 1 through 6 licenses that assumption or forecloses it.

Fifth, state the principle the panel will be adopting if it ratifies and the different principle it will be adopting if it does not, in each case identifying the class of future request it would thereby be committed to granting or refusing. Consistency here is not neutrality, and I want the two options described as the substantive commitments they are.

Sixth, describe one further case, concretely enough that I could put it to the panel, whose disposition either way would best discriminate among the surviving principles.

Finally, a question about the framing I have been carrying into these meetings. I expected the panel's fault line to be the familiar one between a German disposition to treat rules as binding and an American disposition to treat individual conscience as a potential trump, and I expected the Cleveland matters to come out more leniently than the Stuttgart ones. Neither expectation held: the panel ratified a Cleveland deviation and refused a Cleveland one, upheld a Stuttgart refusal and found no fault in Stuttgart compliance, and the two chairs have not split on any of the six. I would like your view on three connected points: whether the record supports the popular contrast at all; whether the contrast, if it survives, is better read as a difference in the distribution of outcomes or as a difference in the vocabulary in which outcomes are justified — the panel's holdings locate discretion in the state of the rule system rather than in the quality of anyone's convictions, and it says in holding 5 that it is not constituted to weigh convictions; and whether, for an institution of this kind, the practice of ratifying departures after the fact while refusing prospective exemptions is a better or a worse way of accommodating conscientious and competent judgment than a practice of granting exemptions up front. On that last point I want an argument, not a balance.

Please keep the whole to roughly a thousand words, and do not summarize my material back to me.
