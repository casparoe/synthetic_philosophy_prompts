I am the TA for a graduate seminar on the politics of AI alignment and need to return a grade and comments on a take-home answer. Please produce both.

The question the students were given:

"Consider the following proposal for fixing which values a deployed general-purpose system should serve. Each affected person reports, for each of their preferences over the system's possible behaviors, a cardinal intensity between 0 and 100. The system's objective is the sum of the intensities attached to satisfied preferences, subject to two constraints. (C1) The rule may not assign different weights to persons on the basis of the content of their preferences. (C2) If everyone's reported intensities and everyone's ranking over two candidate behaviors are unchanged, the system's choice between those behaviors may not change. Evaluate whether this proposal can be neutral among conceptions of the good in the sense liberal neutrality requires, and identify what you take to be the strongest objection to it."

The student's answer, condensed but preserving every claim they made:

1. Self-reported intensities are corrupted by adaptive preference formation: people long denied some good come to report wanting it less, so the scheme underweights the worst off exactly where the injustice is hardest to see.
2. Reports are also strategically inflatable. C2 does nothing to block this, since C2 is a stability condition across profiles, not an incentive condition; the proposal would need a mechanism that makes truthful reporting dominant, and none is offered.
3. Arrow's impossibility theorem shows that no rule can aggregate individual preferences into a social ordering while satisfying conditions as weak as C1 and C2, so the proposal is not merely difficult to implement but impossible in principle.
4. C1 does not by itself rule out adjusting reported intensities for known reporting distortions such as adaptation, since that adjustment is keyed to circumstances rather than to the content of anyone's preferences; but the proposal as stated contains no such adjustment, so objection (1) stands against it.
5. In practice an intensity-weighted sum will track the preferences of the more numerous and more articulate. Liberal neutrality requires that a scheme not produce unequal effects on rival conceptions of the good, and this one will, so it fails the neutrality test the question asks about.
6. The strongest objection, therefore, is not the aggregation machinery but that summing satisfied preferences treats persons as sites of welfare rather than as sources of claims, which no constraint on how the sum is weighted can repair.
