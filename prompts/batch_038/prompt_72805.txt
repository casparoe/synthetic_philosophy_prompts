I've read the argument that the adoption of agriculture was a catastrophe for human welfare more times than I can count — Diamond's widely circulated essay on it being the worst mistake our species ever made, Sahlins's original affluent society, the paleopathology literature on stature and dental disease at Neolithic sites — and I've become convinced the debate stays muddled because the word affluence is doing at least two distinct jobs that nobody bothers to separate. Let me define the two, then ask you to compare them as answers to the same question.

Conception A, rate-based affluence: welfare measured as calories returned per hour of labor, the share of the day left over as leisure, and the deliberate limitation of wants. This is roughly Sahlins's picture — foragers who met their needs in a few hours and had no reason to want more. On this view a society is affluent if the flow of goods relative to effort and desire is generous.

Conception B, buffer-based affluence: welfare measured as the capacity to hold consumption roughly constant through bad years — grain in a pit, animals on the hoof, a storable, defensible, and if necessary tradable surplus. Here what matters is not the average return but the variance around it, and above all whether the tail is survivable.

A third wrinkle that I think the standard tellings drop entirely: the correlation structure of the risks. Inside a foraging band a drought or a failed season hits every member simultaneously and also hits neighboring bands, so the mutual-insurance channel is thin; farming risk is partly idiosyncratic at the household level (one field floods, the next does not) but catastrophically correlated at the regional scale and concentrated in a narrow set of crop varieties. And a stored surplus is expropriable in a way that a mobile band's food supply simply is not — which is presumably where the inequality story enters.

Please compare A and B as rival answers to whether the agricultural revolution was a mistake. Specifically:

1. What does each conception predict about the evidence we actually have — stature decline, enamel hypoplasia, caries, work-hour estimates, settlement density, the appearance of storage facilities — and does one fit the record noticeably better?
2. Where do they genuinely conflict, as opposed to where the literature just slides between them? Is the transition better described as purchasing variance reduction at the cost of mean leisure and health, or the reverse?
3. Which conception is Diamond's argument actually resting on when it calls the switch a mistake, and is the claim coherent under both, or only under one?
4. The aggregation problem: if per-capita welfare falls while population and total welfare rise, does mistake even have a determinate sense under A versus under B? Which conception, if either, lets you answer that without smuggling in a population ethic?
5. Your verdict: which conception does more explanatory work, which would you keep if you could keep only one, and under what utility function — a mean, or something with a kink at starvation — is the transition rational for the individuals making it? Is there a version in which it is rational for every participant and still a trap for all of them?

Answer as an economic historian with a real command of decision theory, equally at home in the ethnographic and the formal literatures. Think the comparison through carefully before writing, and resist the familiar talking points; flag explicitly any place where you are extrapolating past what the evidence licenses. I don't want a bibliography or a reference list — I want your reasoning, and I'd rather you say where you're unsure than fill space.
