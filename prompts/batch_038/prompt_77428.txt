I'm prepping a talk for a law-and-philosophy reading group on victors' justice and retroactive law, and I've written down where I actually stand so the others can attack it. Before I hand it out I want a cold check on whether the package hangs together. Here it is:

1. Nullum crimen sine lege isn't a formal nicety. Retroactively criminalizing somebody's conduct is a real moral wrong done to that person, not a technical defect you shrug off — and it isn't cured by how monstrous the conduct turned out to be. I don't buy the move where you say some crimes are so grave that legality has to yield.

2. I'm a positivist about international law. I don't think there's a natural-law residue that makes an act criminal when no positive law does; if it wasn't forbidden with a penalty attached, it wasn't a crime.

3. Which is why I think the standard reply to the retroactivity objection at Nuremberg basically works: the Kellogg-Briand pact had already got the great powers to renounce war as an instrument of national policy, so aggressive war was already outlawed, and the men in the dock had fair warning that launching one was criminal. The crimes-against-peace count wasn't new law, it was enforcement.

4. And if you corner me on 3, fine — I'll say a tribunal that got the legality point wrong can still have been worth convening.

5. What I most care about saving is the crimes against humanity count. That's the legacy: the idea that what a regime does to its own people on its own soil can be somebody else's business to prosecute. I think the Charter's provision is the hinge the whole later edifice turns on, and I want my talk to end there.

6. But I also think the drafters mangled that count by pegging it to the war and aggression counts, with the result that the tribunal held it couldn't reach the pre-1939 persecution of Jews inside Germany. That's the single worst thing it declined to do.

7. Tokyo was worse than Nuremberg on legality. I think the Indian judge was right that the aggression counts were ex post facto, but wrong to want to acquit everyone, since the ordinary atrocity charges against most of the Class A defendants didnt depend on any new law. The Dutch judge's dissent about the court never being allowed to look at Allied conduct I take more seriously.

8. I don't accept the tu quoque line, though. What the Allies did at Dresden or Hiroshima or in the Pacific submarine campaign has zero bearing on whether these defendants did what they did.

9. Still, the one-sidedness of the jurisdiction is a genuine defect, and the Dönitz outcome — where the answers put to Admiral Nimitz confirming American unrestricted submarine warfare in the Pacific kept that count from being held against him — seems to me to show the tribunal half-knew it. And a few acquittals dont rehabilitate a court that could only ever look one way.

I honestly can't tell which of these is load-bearing and which is decoration, and I'm not going to tell you which one I'd sacrifice, because I don't know. So: audit it. Rank the tensions you find by severity rather than listing them as equally bad, and for each one say precisely which commitment has to give and what giving it costs me. Then commit to a single revision — the one you'd actually make if this were your position — and defend it against the obvious alternatives, instead of laying out a menu and telling me it depends. And if a pair only looks inconsistent, say so explicitly and explain why it dissolves; I suspect at least one of them does, and I'd rather you discriminate than flag everything to seem thorough.
