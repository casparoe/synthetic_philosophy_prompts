I have no academic training in philosophy — I work in software — but I have spent the last two years reading about the special composition question on my own, and I have run into a puzzle I cannot resolve by myself. The two answers usually presented as the cleanest, on the grounds that neither leaves composition vague or arbitrary, are the view that any things whatsoever compose something and the view that composition never occurs at all. They are normally described as opposite extremes. Yet I have also come across the suggestion that they are the same picture of the world wearing different clothes: on both, the fundamental facts are just simples standing in various arrangement relations, and the only difference is whether one additionally allows oneself to talk about fusions of those simples. If that is right, this supposedly maximal disagreement is no disagreement at all, and both camps have been wasting their time.

I would like you to compare the two views in a way that actually tests this suggestion instead of assuming it. Please address four things:

1. State what each view is committed to at the fundamental level, and identify precisely what the two share.
2. Attempt a translation manual for ordinary cases — a table in a room, a statue and the clay it is made of, the fusion of my left shoe and the Moon — mapping each view's sentences onto the other's, and report whether the manual holds everywhere or breaks down somewhere, and if so where.
3. Tell me what to make of counting. When I ask how many things are on my desk, the universalist and the nihilist appear to give different numbers, and different numbers look like different facts rather than different notations. Does this settle the question, or is the appearance misleading?
4. If the two views do come out equivalent, explain why one feels so much easier to swallow than the other: is that merely an artifact of which paraphrases ordinary English happens to make available, or is there a real asymmetry in what each view costs its adherent?

I want a committed answer, not a survey of the options. Take a side on the central question and defend it, and tell me plainly which of my framing assumptions you think are mistaken, if any are. No bibliography or list of sources — I will track those down myself.

End your reply with exactly one line of the form Verdict: verbal or Verdict: substantive, giving whichever single label best captures the core of the dispute between these two views, even if you believe the full story is mixed.
