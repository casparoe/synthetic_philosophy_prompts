So I'm on my town council — small place, about 9,000 people, I mostly got elected because I was the only candidate willing to touch the sewer budget — and last fall I got put on the seven-person naming committee, which sounded like the easiest job in local government. It has turned out not to be. Here's the situation. A guy named Raymond Vaas died in March at 91. Around here he was famous for decades as the town's philanthropist: he paid for the free clinic downtown, which is basically the only place within an hour you can see a doctor if you don't have insurance, he put up the money for the high school band program, and he funded the little footbridge over the creek in the riverfront park. The council wants to name the whole riverfront park after him, there's a dedication penciled in for September, and his kids are flying in from out of state for it. About two weeks ago I got a phone call from an 84-year-old woman who's lived here her whole life, and I've since confirmed what she told me by pulling old deed records at the library, so it's not a rumor — there's actual documents. In the sixties Vaas made his first real money buying up houses in the Bottoms and reselling them on installment contract to Black families who couldn't get a mortgage anywhere else. The contracts had a clause letting him keep every payment they'd already made and put them out if they missed one month, and he ran the same four or five houses through that process three and four times over. That neighborhood is still here. Several of the people he did it to are still here. The woman who called me is one of them.

The arguments at the table have gotten weird and I can't tell which ones are real. One member says a name on a park isn't a statement about the man at all, it's just an address, the way Madison Avenue doesn't mean New York endorses whatever Madison thought, and anyway in fifty years nobody will remember who Vaas was so we're arguing about nothing. Another says the clinic has probably served thirty thousand people who'd otherwise have gotten nothing, and you can't weigh that against some sharp real estate deals from sixty years ago, and besides everybody in his generation was up to something, if you start down this road you have to rename everything. A third — and this is the one I keep finding strangely persuasive without being able to say why — says split it: name the *clinic* after him because that's the thing he actually gave, and name the park after somebody else. It feels like its doing something other than splitting the difference but I can't articulate what. Fourth option, which nobody has brought up yet: go ahead with the park name, but have the dedication plaque itself say what he did in the sixties, so the monument carries the correction.

Meeting's Thursday and I'm pretty sure I hold the swing vote. I don't want advice about being respectful or scheduling a community listening session, I want to know which of these four actually holds up. Is the Madison Avenue point right, or is it smuggling something in? Does the clinic/park split have a principle behind it or is it just a fudge that lets everyone feel okay? Does the correction-on-the-plaque version change what the naming *is*, or does it just move the problem onto a piece of bronze? And is there something different about doing this while the people he did it to are still alive and could plausibly end up standing in the crowd at the ribbon cutting — versus the usual fights about old statues where everybody involved is long dead?
