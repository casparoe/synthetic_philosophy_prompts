I'm two-thirds of the way through a graduate seminar whose running question this term is whether the past can be told from everywhere at once — whether a genuinely global history is possible, or whether every such narrative turns out to be European history with the other regions fitted in around it. Last week we got into source criticism during our unit on the early modern Indian Ocean, and one of my classmates, arguing that any account built on Portuguese and Dutch company records is European history in disguise no matter how carefully the historian handles them, ended the exchange with "history is written by the victors." The instructor let it stand, two other people nodded, and we moved on. I left convinced the sentence had done a great deal of work it hadn't earned, but unable to say what the work was; I don't want to show up next week with nothing but a vague discomfort.

So I'd like the sentence taken apart. Treat it as a piece of language whose meaning is badly underdetermined and lay out the distinct precise claims it could be making — for instance, a claim about who produces records in the first place; a claim about which records physically survive and in whose repositories; a claim about whose categories, periodizations, and definitions of what counts as an event the surviving records impose on everyone who comes after; a claim about the directional reliability of sources written by or for the party that won; a claim about who controls the telling in present-day universities and publishing; and whatever further readings I haven't thought of. For each, state what exactly it asserts, then classify it: close to a truism, substantive and roughly true, substantive and doubtful, or false. Then say for each whether it is actually needed to carry my classmate's conclusion — that a narrative resting on those archives is European history in disguise, and that global history in the strong sense is unavailable.

Two further things. First, mark the places where readings slide into one another, since I suspect the line gets its force from an unnoticed shift between a descriptive claim about what has survived and a normative claim about what a historian may trust, and I want that shift pinned down. Note also that the slogan presupposes one victor and one vanquished, whereas in our unit the written sources for a single port town might be Portuguese, Dutch, Ottoman, Persian, or local Swahili and Arabic chronicles, and the party that ends up controlling the archive may not be the party that won anything at the time: say what happens to each reading under that complication, including any reading that turns out to be self-undermining for the very method my classmate favors (if only the victors wrote, what is left to read against the grain?). Second, for each reading that fails, give me the longer, less quotable sentence that would have to replace it in order to be defensible, and then tell me which single reading, if any, survives as a real constraint on writing the past from everywhere at once. Please format this as a numbered menu I can bring to seminar, each item headed by the claim, its status, and whether it supports the use it was put to. I'm not after sources, attributions, or a reading list — the sentence is repeated so often that nobody in the room could say where it comes from, and what I want is the analysis of the sentence itself.
