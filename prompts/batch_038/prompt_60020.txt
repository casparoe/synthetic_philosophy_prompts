Got into a long arguement with a friend last night about whether global history actually escapes Eurocentrism or just puts on a nicer coat, and I ended up typing up my side of it as a short essay so I could stop repeating myself in person. I want real comments on it, not encouragement. Three things: (1) is the central claim coherent, or does it dissolve into something trivially true — everybody already knows the archives are uneven? (2) does my friend's objection at the end land, and is there a version of my view that survives it? (3) flag anything that's flat-out wrong or oversimplified about how this kind of history actually gets written. Please answer in American English.

DRAFT

There are two standard moves for decentering European history. The spatial one: tell the story from many places at once, with no single protagonist. The temporal one: take a vertical slice — the world in 1500, the world in 1750 — and describe every region under a single date, which is supposed to kill the one-track narrative in which everyone is marching toward the same destination at different speeds.

My claim is that the temporal move doesn't do the work people think it does. Under a fixed date, each region contributes narrative roughly in proportion to how much datable, surviving, catalogued documentation it happens to have. Places whose records were burned in conquest, or whose record-keeping was oral or on perishable media, contribute silence — and in a synchronic format silence gets read as nothing happening rather than nothing recorded. Call it archive-weighted presence: at any given date, the amount of story a place gets tracks its archival density, not the actual density of events. The slice genre therefore reproduces the hierarchy it was meant to dissolve, and it does so at every date at once, which is part of why it's so hard to notice from inside.

So I want asynchronous global history. Instead of the vertical slice, follow a thread — a crop, a silver flow, a legal form, an epidemic, a weaving technique — and let each region enter the narrative when the thread reaches it, with the lags stated explicitly rather than smoothed away. The unit of narration becomes the connection instead of the calendar year, which also makes documentary unevenness less damaging, since you're asking what moved and who handled it, not what everyone was doing on January 1.

Objection, from my friend, and I think its strong. Any lag notation presupposes a shared track and a shared direction of travel. You can only write that something arrived forty years later here if both places sit on one line, and one line implies one destination — which is exactly the historicist gesture of treating other societies as delayed versions of Europe, except now it looks like a measurement instead of a judgment. And in practice the lags get estimated from European-dated sources anyway: ship logs, trading-company ledgers, missionary correspondence. So the asynchronous narrative is still clocked in Europe.

My half-formed answer: a thread with no destination doesn't imply stages. Silver doesn't arrive at a phase of development, it just moves, and the lag is a property of the route, not of the place. But I don't have a response to the second half, and I suspect that's where the argument actually died.
