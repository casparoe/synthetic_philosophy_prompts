I serve on a seven-member county water-quality board, and I would like a careful adjudication of an argument between two other members that I was present for but could not referee.

Last spring we reviewed a petition to restrict a household cleaning product containing a substance I will call K. The facts as the staff modeling presented them: a single use releases roughly two milligrams of K into the drainage system — below the detection limit of our sampling equipment and, on every dose-response model we were shown, too small to produce any effect in any human body. About 300,000 households in the watershed use the product. The modeling projected that the combined household load, layered on top of industrial discharges we had no authority to regulate, would raise K concentrations at one downstream drinking-water intake enough to cause approximately four additional cases of a severe and permanently disabling illness over fifty years, with a high probability that at least one of the four would be fatal. A restriction was expected to eliminate the household load almost entirely. The board could restrict the product or decline to act. Two members — I will call them Alvarez and Berrigan — argued the question at length across five exchanges.

1. Alvarez: No single user's emission makes any difference to anyone. Add or subtract one household's two milligrams and no one's fate changes at all. So no user does anything wrong; and if no act in the set is wrongful, the set is not wrongful — wrongdoing does not compose. To say that 300,000 individually harmless acts add up to a harm is a category error.
Berrigan: You have merged two questions. Whether each act is impermissible and whether the outcome is bad are not the same question, and the four people are ill either way. Your composition principle may be true of wrongdoing and still beside the point of what this board should do.

2. Alvarez: Not beside it. Grant that the outcome is bad. The board's task is then to weigh, and the cost side of the ledger is not a pile of milligrams but the permanent loss, to 300,000 households, of a product they prefer and would pay to keep. If you are willing to weigh that loss against four cases of illness, you have already conceded that trivial burdens aggregate — and once they aggregate, the aggregate burden of a restriction has to be compared honestly against four cases, and it wins by a wide margin.
Berrigan: The two aggregations are not symmetric. Four illnesses are states of four people. Three hundred thousand small disappointments are not a large state of anyone: there is no person for whom the sum is a burden, so the sum is not a burden at all. And even setting that aside, you are converting one currency into another by an exchange rate you cannot state. Tell me how many disappointed households equal one death. If you cannot, what you are offering is not a weighing but a preference with arithmetic attached.

3. Alvarez: I can state it, and this board states it every time it sets a standard: the statistical-value-of-life figure multiplied by the modeled probabilities, which on the staff's own numbers put the restriction's cost at roughly nine times its benefit. You want a threshold below which numbers cease to count. But any threshold you draw will assign opposite verdicts to cases differing by a milligram, and you will have no non-arbitrary way to say which side of it a given case falls on. My view at least gives the same answer to nearby cases.
Berrigan: It gives the same answer to nearby cases because it has dissolved the question into a single number, and that number is now doing the normative work you claim to distrust in me. And the sorites charge runs the other way: if two milligrams do not matter, neither do four, nor a thousand, nor six hundred million — yet six hundred million of them are what make these four people ill. Yours is not the vagueness-free position; it is the vague one.

4. Alvarez: There is an asymmetry you have skipped. The household load is only about six percent of the K reaching that intake. Even a total restriction leaves more than ninety percent of the harm in place. A measure that prevents six percent of a harm, at the cost of a preference held by 300,000 people, is not equivalent to a measure that prevents a harm, and we should not treat it as though it were.
Berrigan: Six percent of a death is not nothing, and "the harm would mostly happen anyway" is the argument of every contributor to every collective harm there has ever been. More to the point, the six percent figure destroys your first move. The modeling says the intake crosses the effect threshold only with the household load included; the industrial discharges alone do not produce these four cases. The milligrams are therefore not individually inert in the sense you claimed. They are jointly sufficient and jointly necessary: remove all of them and the cases disappear.

5. Alvarez: Then I withdraw my first move, and my conclusion is stronger for it. If the household load is jointly sufficient and no individual contribution is necessary, there is no individual agent to hold responsible, and the only agent in this room is the board. A board that acts is choosing between two outcomes, and it should choose on the numbers. The numbers favor declining to act.
Berrigan: That inference is the one I most want to resist. "No individual causes it" does not yield "therefore the board must weigh the result as a sum." The board may also act on the fact that a practice exists in which each participant's contribution is invisible to the participant. The appropriate response to invisibility is not arithmetic but a rule that makes the contribution visible and shared. Whether that rule costs too much is a further question, and it is the one you keep sliding into.

The restriction failed, 4–3. I abstained, as did one other member, because we thought the modeling assumptions unresolved, and in the months since I have been unable to reconstruct which of the two was arguing better.

Please adjudicate the exchange move by move rather than in the aggregate. For each of the five exchanges, say who has the better of it and why. In the course of doing so, address in particular: whether Alvarez's composition principle could be true and still fail to support his conclusion; whether Berrigan's demand for a stated exchange rate is a genuine objection or a refusal to answer a fair question; whether Berrigan can consistently deny that an exchange rate exists and still cast a vote, which is itself a comparison; whether the sorites charge in exchange 3 lands against Alvarez, against Berrigan, against both, or against neither; and whether Berrigan's assertion in exchange 4 that the milligrams are "jointly necessary" is accurate, given that the necessity seems to attach to the class of emissions rather than to any member of it. Then identify any move that is not merely unpersuasive but straightforwardly mistaken, and any move that sounds decisive and is not. Say what neither of them said that most needed saying. Finally, state whether the 4–3 outcome was defensible on these arguments alone, or whether the arguments leave the question genuinely underdetermined.
