Just spent three hours arguing with my friend Dev, who's two years into a PhD on accuracy-first epistemology, and I came away feeling like I'd been gaslit by a very patient man. His line is that the only thing that can be wrong with your credences is that they're inaccurate, that you measure inaccuracy with a strictly proper scoring rule, and that the math then hands you probabilism and conditionalization for free. My objection — which he kept agreeing with in a tone that suggested he thought the agreement would defeat me — is that the framework can never actually tell him he's *wrong*. Expected accuracy is always computed from inside your own opinion, so every coherent credence function expects itself to be at least as good as any alternative, and the only defect it can ever report is incoherence. He said that's the whole point of propriety, it's a feature, and then he spent twenty minutes defending the log score even though it hands you an unbounded penalty the moment you dare to be certain of something. We also went around on the quadratic score not letting you blame any single belief for the total (it grades the whole credence function in one go, unlike the log score, which at least decomposes), on being perfectly accurate purely by luck and having that count as success, and on how you can only benefit from matching the known chances if you already believe the chances, at which point you didn't need the advice.

So I want to turn the argument into a joke instead of a fourth hour of it. Write me the fold-out troubleshooting card that ships in the box of a consumer gadget for measuring your own epistemic accuracy — call it whatever you like, something like the Proper™ Home Accuracy Monitor, and invent any model names, accessory packs, and warranty terms you need. The voice is the manufacturer's: calm, corporate, faintly defensive, absolutely convinced that every limitation is a design triumph. Deadpan throughout, no winking, and no entry may name a real philosopher, a paper, or the phrase "accuracy-first" — the card should never announce that it's making an objection, it should just answer a customer's complaint in a way that lets the reader feel the objection land. The humor has to come from getting the epistemology right, so exaggerate something genuinely true of the scoring-rule framework rather than inventing a strawman: the self-approving character of propriety, the all-or-nothing nature of a dominance guarantee, the certainty penalty, the impossibility of locating which belief is at fault, the vantage-point relativity of expected accuracy. At the same time a reader who's never heard of a scoring rule should still laugh at a gadget that refuses to tell its owner he's wrong.

Format: give me the card and nothing else — exactly five numbered entries, no introduction, no sign-off, no afterword explaining the jokes. Each entry can be a Problem line and a Solution line of a sentence or two apiece, so the whole thing comes to maybe 350–450 words. Pick the five best complaints from the ones Dev and I circled (or better ones), and make the last entry the one that quietly undercuts the other four.
