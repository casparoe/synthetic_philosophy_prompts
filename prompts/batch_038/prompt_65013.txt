I am three years into a novel set in a valley district whose lower town was drowned in the 1950s to make a reservoir, and whose upper parish — the watershed, the intake works, most of the pumping station's jobs — is now demanding a referendum on leaving the district for the neighbouring region. A political theorist reads my drafts for institutional accuracy. He has advised me to let my councillor make the club argument: a district is like a members' club, the members set the terms of admission and departure, therefore it is the district voting as a whole that decides whether the parish may go, and therefore neither the descendants of the families who were compensated and dispersed when the village went under, nor the city two hundred kilometres downstream that buys the power, has any vote at all. He thinks the figure is intuitive enough to persuade a hostile reader and tight enough to settle the question. My sister, who has also read the draft, prefers another: the district is a marriage, the parish's departure would be a divorce, and since divorces are about dividing assets and providing for children, the argument ought to be about the water rights rather than about the franchise.

I need both figures audited, because the speech has to survive a reader who is not on my councillor's side, and because in a later chapter a different character turns the same images against him. I would rather not write a man I consider honest as someone trading in rhetoric that only sounds like argument.

Specifically:

- Set out each mapping explicitly — what in my valley plays the charter, the membership list, the vote on admission, the exit; and what plays the marriage, the spouses, the children, the settlement.
- Distinguish the disanalogies that actually bear on the conclusion my theorist wants (that the district alone decides, and that affected non-members have no standing) from those that are merely picturesque. I do not care that clubs are small or that families are biological unless that fact is doing work.
- Say what each figure quietly imports from its source that the target lacks. My own suspicion about the club is that it arrives with a membership roll and a constituting charter already in hand, whereas in my valley the roll is precisely what is being fought over: there was a boundary commission in 1974 and the authority that appointed it no longer exists, so I am not sure there is a charter left to point to. I also suspect the divorce image smuggles in a court.
- Tell me whether either conclusion survives the audit, or whether the figure was doing the arguing all along and the argument itself still has to be made by other means.
- If neither will carry it, propose a better figure — one that would let the councillor say something defensible — and tell me what it licenses instead, including whether it hands any vote to the dispersed descendants, the downstream city, or the pumping crews.

One complication to keep in view throughout: the set of affected people moves with the decision itself. If only the parish votes, the downstream city's contracts are quietly devalued; if the district votes as a whole, the parish's watershed becomes an asset it cannot dispose of; and the pumping crews commute from a third region entirely, losing their jobs under either arrangement while having no vote anywhere.
