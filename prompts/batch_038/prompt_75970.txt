Write a short story of about fifteen hundred words that takes the combination problem for constitutive panpsychism as its subject rather than its scenery. Set it in a research institute that has unexpectedly achieved stable first-person reports from individual micro-experiential constituents of a single human brain, elicited one at a time and in isolation from the whole. Your narrator is the institute's chief interviewer, whose task is to assemble those reports into an account of what the composite subject's experience is composed of; she finds that the constituents' descriptions never sum, that each report already has the shape of a report about a whole, and that the composite, interviewed separately, denies having parts at all. Let her also weigh, and provisionally reject, the thought that constitution runs the other way — that the whole is basic and the constituents are merely modifications of it.

Stage the technical difficulties concretely rather than by naming them: the failure of familiar qualities to blend into the unfamiliar ones the composite reports, the absence of any reason why there should be one subject here rather than a crowd, and the suspicion that ought to trouble the narrator most, that the composite's experience is doing no work in the physics and could be struck out without altering a single behaviour. End without resolution.

Then, in a further two or three paragraphs, tell me where the fiction takes liberties: which of the institute's findings is actually licensed by the arguments, which is merely dramatic, and which claim a serious panpsychist would most object to — together with the consideration that would most plausibly make you write it differently.

British English, and continuous prose throughout: no headings, bullet points, bold text or tables.
