A friend and I spent two hours last night arguing about whether expressivism can survive embedded contexts, and we reached an impasse: he holds that the difficulty was patched decades ago, I hold that every patch quietly ships the thing the view was designed to eliminate. I would rather settle this by ridicule than by argument.

Write the export from the issue tracker of a fictional vendor that supplies the moral-language module in home assistants. The module's semantics is expressivist by design: a bare moral sentence is meant to emit an attitude, not to report a fact.

Give me exactly five tickets, each with a one-line title, a status, and a note of one or two sentences — nothing before the list, nothing after it, no footnotes.

Requirements:

- Every defect must be a real one. The tracker should show moral sentences failing inside the antecedent of a conditional, failing under negation, and failing to make an evidently valid moral modus ponens come out valid; the patches should be the ones actually proposed — higher-order attitudes in Blackburn's style, plan-laden semantics in Gibbard's style. Do not invent difficulties expressivists do not have, and do not let any ticket be vague about which inference broke.
- The humour must come from the engineering, not from funny-sounding jargon. Each fix should restore the inference only by introducing something that behaves, to the QA engineer's dawning horror, exactly like a truth condition.
- At least one ticket should arrive as an escalated customer complaint, and at least one should be closed "working as intended" on grounds a quasi-realist would genuinely endorse.
- Deadpan corporate register throughout, no winking, and no ticket may name the problem it is an instance of. Roughly 300 words for the whole export.

The list should reward a reader who knows the literature without depending on one: a lay reader should still be able to see why the module keeps breaking and suspect that the vendor is running out of places to put the fix.
