My dissertation chapter has produced a stalemate I'd like to convert into a document. I argue that the spread across a large ensemble of scientific models — say forty coupled-model runs differing in parameter settings and physical schemes, with no defensible weighting over them — licenses a set-valued rather than a point-valued credence about a target quantity. My two readers disagree with each other more than with me, and their disagreement has stalled the project. Reader A is a precise Bayesian, trained in decision theory, who holds that a rational credal state is a single finitely additive probability function and that anything else is either indecision relabeled as belief or a notational variant of a mixture over priors. Reader B works in the coherence tradition of lower and upper previsions, on which a credal state is a closed convex set of probability functions and choice is governed by one of the standard rules for such sets — maximality, interval dominance, Γ-maximin, E-admissibility. Please draft the joint statement the three of us should have written, in a voice both readers could put their names to.

It should contain, in this order:

1. A joint bottom line of exactly two sentences: what the collaboration settles and what it explicitly leaves open.

2. A numbered list of agreed claims, at least eight, each phrased so that neither reader could retract it without embarrassment. Include among them: the representational duality between closed convex sets of probability functions and their lower and upper envelopes; that passing to the closed convex hull of a family of coherent precise opinions leaves the induced lower and upper probabilities unchanged, so that Reader A's objection on that point is bookkeeping rather than substance; that Ellsberg-type choice patterns violate independence and so admit no expected-utility representation on a single additive prior; that dilation is genuine arithmetic and not a confusion about conditioning; and that both camps are committed to some diachronic norm, however differently they state it.

3. A disagreements table with the columns: Issue | Reader A's formulation | Reader B's formulation | Genuine or verbal? | If genuine, what would settle it. The constraint I care most about is that each side's formulation be one that side would recognize as fair. Where you can produce only a wording one side would call a strawman, mark the row unresolved rather than slanting it.

4. Cruxes. I propose three candidates and want you to judge each: is it a crux — a sub-question whose settlement would actually move at least one reader — or have I misdiagnosed it, in which case substitute a better one. My candidates: (i) requirement versus permission, i.e. whether ambiguous evidence ever obliges a non-singleton credal state or merely fails to single one out, leaving sharpness permissible; (ii) whether an imprecise credal state can be revised by a rule both readers accept as binding, and in particular whether a widening of the interval on learning counts as a defect or as a permissible failure of certainty increase; (iii) whether ambiguity aversion is better captured by imprecision plus a non-additive decision rule or by a single higher-order distribution over priors folded into the functional through a concave transform. For every crux you retain, name the case, dataset, or argument both readers would accept as bearing on it, and record each side's prediction about it in advance, stated so precisely that a third party could later check who was right.

5. One short paragraph isolating the parts of the dispute that are merely verbal, saying which terms are doing the work and what each side should say instead.

Assume full familiarity with the formalisms; spend no words on background, and no references of any kind — I know the literature and want formulations, not a survey. The whole document, table included, must not exceed 750 words.
