I convene a graduate research seminar in epistemology, and after last Thursday's talk two of my colleagues — I will call them Adaeze and Tomas — got into an argument that I have been unable to settle to my own satisfaction. Neither knows that I am seeking a second opinion, and I have deliberately kept my own view out of what follows, so please do not try to guess which side I favor. I want a judgment on the merits, not a survey.

The seed case, which the visiting speaker had used and which both parties accepted exactly as described: a charge nurse on a night shift tells the covering colleague, flat out, that the patient in bed four is allergic to penicillin. Her grounds are a chart entry, a note from an outpatient clinic, and the patient's daughter, all three consistent; she has never seen the patient react to anything, and the hospital's own audit puts the chart's error rate at roughly one entry in fifty. The colleague acts on what she said. The patient is in fact allergic.

1. Tomas said the nurse's assertion was proper and that this is fatal to any view on which the norm of assertion is knowledge: she does not know the patient is allergic, because her evidence, however good, leaves a live possibility she cannot rule out. What assertion requires is rather something like reasonably held belief, which she had.
2. Adaeze said this moves too fast. Even granting the lack of knowledge, that shows only that knowledge is not necessary; it does not show that reasonable belief is sufficient. Her counter-case: a man hurrying down a corridor glances at a partly erased whiteboard and asserts, on that basis alone, that the seminar is in room 204. He is right, and given how little time he had his belief was arguably reasonable — yet, she said, everyone feels he should not have said it, and a reasonable-belief norm cannot explain that feeling.
3. Tomas replied that the whiteboard case marks a difference of degree, not of kind: his evidence is simply much weaker than the nurse's. But, he added, once you admit degrees you are no longer stating the norm in terms of belief at all; you are stating it in terms of evidential strength, which is a less definite thing and not something a speaker can consult in the moment.
4. Adaeze then argued that in both cases the real work is being done by something neither of them had yet named. To assert is to invite reliance: one entitles one's audience to stop checking for themselves and to hold the asserter answerable. On that picture the norm is not truth, not knowledge, and not reasonable belief, but roughly — assert only what you are in a position to stand behind if your audience relies on it. The nurse could stand behind it; the man in the corridor could not.
5. Tomas objected that this collapses in emergencies. He described an interpreter at a checkpoint who is asked whether a mountain road is passable, who has only second-hand reports, who cannot possibly be in a position to stand behind an answer, and who knows that a refusal to answer will leave a group of people outside in freezing weather for hours, perhaps with worse to follow. Her flat assertion, he said, is not merely excusable but required; if Adaeze's account delivers the verdict that it is improper, the account is refuted.
6. Adaeze answered that Tomas had changed the subject: the interpreter is not making an assertion at all but issuing a warning or relaying hearsay. If we keep redrawing the boundary of assertion whenever a case becomes awkward, she said, there is no such thing as the norm of assertion, and the debate was empty from the start.
7. Tomas's final move was that this is precisely his point. The hunt for a single norm is a philosopher's construction; what we actually have is a family of speech acts — telling, reporting, warning, guessing, reassuring — each with its own standard, held together only by a shared pressure not to mislead. Adaeze's insistence on uniqueness is what generates the puzzles, not what solves them.

Please adjudicate move by move rather than in the aggregate. For each of the seven moves, say whether it succeeds, partly succeeds, or fails, and give your reason, flagging in particular any point at which a disputant has slid between two distinct questions — between what makes an assertion correct and what a speaker can sensibly consult while deliberating, or between an assertion's being improper and the asserter's being criticizable for it. State whether move (1)'s premise that the nurse does not know can be resisted rather than conceded, since if it can, the seed case may not discriminate between the candidates at all. Tell me which of the three candidate norms the seed case actually tests, and whether the audit figure of one entry in fifty is doing any work or is merely decorative. Then say what both of them missed throughout, and give me your considered verdict on move (7): whether pluralism about the norms is a substantive position or a refusal to answer the question that was asked. Structure it as a numbered verdict and justification for each of the seven moves, followed by a short section on what both missed, followed by your ruling on move (7). No citations, please — I want the argument, not the bibliography.
