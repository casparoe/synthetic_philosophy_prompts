I am a junior, and this spring I am submitting a paper to my school's humanities symposium on the question of whether a large deliberative body can be relied upon to undo its own worst decisions. My case study is Athens between the Mytilenean affair and the trial of Socrates. Our teacher has asked each of us to write out, before drafting anything, the claims we are actually committed to, so that the seminar can test them against one another rather than against the sources; I have done that, and I would like to know whether the set hangs together, because I have a strong suspicion that it does not and no idea where it fails. Please treat the list below as my own position and not as a report of what scholars say, and do not send me to further reading — I want the reasoning, not the bibliography.

Here is what I hold. (1) A decision reached by a great many ordinary citizens together tends to be better than one reached by a smaller number of better-informed people, because each contributes a different piece of the problem and individual mistakes largely cancel out; this is, for me, the strongest argument for the Assembly over a council of experts, and I think it depends on open argument beforehand rather than on a silent ballot, since it is the exposure of a proposal to objection that does the work. (2) In an institution where every decision is produced by persuasive speech in front of a crowd, the outcome reliably tracks whoever argues most effectively last, and I regard this as a predictable property of the design rather than a corruption introduced by particular dishonest men — Cleon and Alcibiades are symptoms of the arrangement, not causes of it. (3) My central positive case is Mytilene: the Assembly decreed the killing of every adult man on the island and sent a ship to carry it out, reconvened the following day, was persuaded the other way by Diodotus, and dispatched a second ship that overtook the first. I count this as the institution correcting itself — the same body, the same procedure, one day apart, reaching the opposite and better verdict. I first noticed the pattern in my own school's student senate, which carried a resolution on a Tuesday and quietly repealed it on Wednesday, and I have been calling it a cooling-off correction: a body that returns to a decision after an interval tends to reverse whatever it settled on in a hurry. I should add that I know two awkward details about my best case — the show of hands was nearly even, and the second ship arrived only after the general had read out the decree and made ready to execute it — but I have been treating these as irrelevant to the point. (4) My central negative case is Sicily, and I explain it structurally: once the decision had been exported and an expeditionary force was operating weeks of sailing away, the Assembly stopped receiving the feedback that would have prompted a second vote, so nothing resembling the reversal at Mytilene was available. (5) On reliability, I hold that a process counts as reliable only when it gets the right result for reasons that would still have been operating had things gone slightly differently; a success that turned on a fluke is no evidence of a dependable mechanism, however glad we are about it. I took this from the argument about a stopped clock, which reads correctly twice a day and still does not tell you the time. (6) On Socrates: the conviction was lawful under Athenian procedure and Athens was not unusually repressive for its time and place, yet it was a serious injustice, and I think the city's long tolerance of openly mocking philosophy makes the verdict worse rather than better, because the jurors were not driven to it by anything. (7) As a matter of method, I want to judge Athenian institutions by what they were trying to accomplish rather than by our standards, while still closing the paper with recommendations about referendums and citizen assemblies today, which I take to be a different question.

What I want back is an audit, not a survey. Go through the list and tell me which of these genuinely cannot all be held at once, ordered from most to least damaging, and for each real conflict state the smallest revision that would remove it together with what that revision would cost the argument — I would rather give up a claim I like than keep one I cannot support. Separately, tell me which pairs only look contradictory and survive inspection, since I am fairly sure at least one of them does and I do not want to repair what is not broken. And please be explicit about the case in (3): is my verdict there something my own stated principles can actually deliver, or am I citing as evidence of a mechanism a result that principle (5) would instruct me to discount, and if so, which commitment has to go? I am asking you to say which, because if the choice is left to me I will simply keep whichever claim I am fondest of.
