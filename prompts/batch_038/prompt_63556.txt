I chair the capacity and consent review panel at a rehabilitation hospital, and we meet Thursday on a case that has split the panel along lines I suspect are theoretical rather than clinical. Please draft the written opinion I will circulate to the members in advance of the meeting.

The case, as our records stipulate it. The patient is a 43-year-old woman, fourteen months after a severe closed-head injury. Imaging shows a focal lesion centered on the right lateral prefrontal cortex with sparing of posterior and subcortical structures. On a standard four-domain capacity interview — communicating a choice, understanding, appreciation, and reasoning — she performs at or above threshold: she can paraphrase the indication for the proposed procedure in her own words, name its principal risks, compare it against the alternatives, and state a choice that has been stable across four sessions. The choice is refusal of an elective shunt revision. The surgical team's position is that without revision she faces a substantial risk of irreversible decline within six to twelve months.

What complicates matters is a dissociation our neuropsychologist has documented. First, her verbal reports about her own current perceptual and bodily states arrive after a delay of four to ten seconds, during which she says she is working the answer out. Second, when an examiner's question embeds a false presupposition about the stimulus in front of her, her description conforms to the presupposition; yet under a neutral two-alternative forced choice answered by pointing, she is correct roughly 95 percent of the time. Third, under time pressure or a concurrent task, the forced-choice accuracy falls to chance while her verbal reports remain fluent and confident. Fourth, she reports no change in her experience since the injury, and she does not experience herself as having any of the deficits her family and therapists describe. Her stated reason for refusing the operation is that she feels well and does not believe the symptoms attributed to her are hers.

The disagreement on the panel runs as follows. One member, our consulting philosopher, argues that this profile is what we should expect if being conscious of a state depends on the patient's having the right sort of awareness of that state — an awareness that is not itself an inference drawn from external cues, including the shape of the examiner's question — and that on this reading her refusal rests on a self-report that carries very little evidential weight. Another member insists this is a misuse of a contested theory of consciousness: experience is a matter of first-order processing, this woman is plainly conscious, and what is impaired is her ability to bring her own states to bear on a judgment, which is a defect of appraisal rather than of sentience. A third worries that both routes arrive at the same place by a dangerous road, namely discounting a disabled patient's own account of her condition on the strength of a philosophical theory the panel is in no position to adjudicate.

Draft the panel's opinion, roughly 900 to 1,200 words. It should:

- state a concrete recommendation — proceed under a surrogate's authorization, accept the refusal, defer pending a specified reassessment, or some defined middle course — together with a time frame and what must happen in the interim;
- explain which of the two theoretical readings, if either, should change what we actually do, and why the choice between them matters for this decision rather than functioning as an interesting aside (if you conclude it does not matter, show that it does not matter, and say what would make it matter);
- give the panel a decision rule we could apply to a future patient showing a similar dissociation between verbal report and forced-choice performance, stated so that a clinician can use it on a Tuesday afternoon without first settling the philosophy of consciousness;
- put the third member's objection in its strongest form, and then say plainly whether it defeats the recommendation.

The document should read as something this panel could act on Thursday, not as a survey of positions.
