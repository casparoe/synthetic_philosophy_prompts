reading Rousseau's Social Contract book II for a seminar on populism and I'm stuck almost immediately, in the chapter on sovereignty being indivisible. Cole translation:

"Sovereignty, for the same reason that makes it inalienable, is indivisible; for will either is, or is not, general; it is the will either of the body of the people, or only of a part of it. In the first case, the will, when declared, is an act of Sovereignty and constitutes law: in the second, it is merely a particular will, or act of magistracy—at the most a decree."

and a bit later, on the people who carve sovereignty up into legislative/executive/taxation/war:

"they turn the Sovereign into a fantastic being composed of several connected pieces: it is as if they were making man of several bodies, one with eyes, one with arms, another with feet, and each with nothing besides."

three things I can't get straight. (1) is "will either is, or is not, general" a definition or a substantive claim? if it just means "willed by the whole body as against by part of it," then the general will is whatever everybody happens to agree on, and the material a chapter later about partial associations and citizens not communicating with one another is doing completely different work from what this chapter says. so what fixes generality here — the number of willers, or the object willed? (2) why does a non-general will produce "at the most a decree" rather than just a bad law? is the point that it isn't law-shaped, or that it isn't binding? (3) with the juggler/many-bodies image, is the complaint that dividing sovereignty misdescribes what sovereignty is, or that doing it is politically dangerous?

and then the bit I actually need for the seminar: a page earlier he says the power may be transmitted but not the will, so the Sovereign can't be represented except by himself. does that make a populist leader's claim to speak for the people a straight-up category error on his own terms — or does his concession that rulers' commands can pass for general wills when the people, being free to oppose them, don't, leave the door wide open? work from the passage itself, I don't want a survey of what Rousseau scholarship says about it.
