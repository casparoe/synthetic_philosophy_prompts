I co-teach a third-year interdisciplinary seminar on intergenerational justice — 22 students, roughly half of them economics majors who have already sat through a lecture on social discount rates and treat the question as a technical parameter to be estimated, the rest philosophy and environmental science students who mostly arrive convinced that discounting the future is simply wicked. Neither side has ever had to *do* anything with the intuition. On Thursday I have a single 75-minute session in a flat-floor room with movable tables, a photocopier down the hall, index cards, a wall clock, and a jar of 200 poker chips. No laptops or phones during the activity: one student has an access arrangement requiring paper materials in large print, so anything you design needs to work as a handout. I also have something genuinely at stake to offer rather than pretend money — the department's student-engagement fund has given me €50 that will actually be donated, at the end of term, to whichever charity the class's final allocation decision selects, and separately I control the last twenty minutes of the following session, which the class normally spends choosing and watching a film. Tell me which of those two to make the payoff, or whether to combine them, and justify the choice in terms of what it does to the decisions people make.

What I want the session to produce is the felt force of *pure time preference* in both directions at once: the pull of preferring a benefit merely because it is nearer, and the force of the classical objection — going back to Sidgwick and taken up by Ramsey, and which I would like you to paraphrase rather than quote — that a mere difference of position in time cannot itself be a rational ground for preferring one state of affairs to another. Please design a runnable game that delivers this, and build it to satisfy five constraints I care about most. First, the rules must isolate positional preference from its usual companions: the probability that a promised future payoff actually arrives, the expectation that future recipients will be richer, declining marginal utility of consumption, and the option to reinvest in the meantime. Tell me exactly which of those four your mechanics neutralize and how, which ones unavoidably leak in, and what I should say when a student spots the leak. Second, the game must generate dynamic inconsistency inside one session in which no real time passes at all — someone must end up wanting to revise a plan they themselves wrote minutes earlier, and the revision must cost them something. Offer me at least two mechanisms for this (sealed plans and a revision phase, rotation across cohorts, a market in pre-commitments, whatever you prefer), and say plainly what each one sacrifices in realism. Third, there must be a moment, produced by the rules and not by anything I say, where a participant's own allocation schedule gets applied to them from a position they had earlier discounted away. Fourth, nobody may hear the phrases "pure time preference" or "discount rate" until the debrief, and you should tell me how to stop the economics students from announcing the answer in minute six — whether by a speaking rule, by a design feature that leads them to expect the standard instrumental story and then disappoints it, or both. Fifth, all accounting must be completable in under five minutes by people who are not accountants.

Deliver the full kit: a materials list, roles if any, a minute-by-minute timeline for the 75 minutes, the tally sheet, the payoff scheme, and the debrief. The debrief should map each phase of what just happened onto a component of the idea — positional preference as against uncertainty, growth, and marginal utility; the thought that a discounted future self is not quite me; the commitment problem — and should give me my answers to the two objections I am certain will be raised, namely that this was only a game so of course people revised, and that the far future matters less because those people are not real. Then be honest with me about failure: at least four things that will predictably go wrong the first time I run this, with a fix for each, and a frank statement of what the activity cannot reproduce and that I as facilitator will have to supply verbally — I assume this includes the absence of any real waiting, of any real death, and of any actual future person in the room with standing to complain about the schedule being written. Finally, give me a stripped-down 25-minute version for a tutorial group of six, since this session is the first thing the timetable office will cut if the term runs short.
