I am writing a seminar paper on Maimonides' account of the reasons for the commandments, focused on his treatment of the sacrificial cult in Part III, chapter 32 of the *Guide for the Perplexed*. My training is in philosophy rather than Jewish studies: I read neither Hebrew nor Judeo-Arabic, and I am working from Michael Friedländer's English translation. I have already absorbed the one-line gloss that circulates about this chapter — that Maimonides explains the sacrifices as a concession to an idolatrous age — and that gloss is precisely what I want to get past. Below are the excerpts I am relying on, in order, with Friedländer's paragraph divisions preserved; the ellipses are mine and mark omitted material.

> **¶1:** "ON considering the Divine acts, or the processes of Nature, we get an insight into the prudence and wisdom of God as displayed in the creation of animals, with the gradual development of the movements of their limbs and the relative positions of the latter, and we perceive also His wisdom and plan in the successive and gradual development of the whole condition of each individual. … In a similar manner did God provide for each individual animal of the class of mammalia. When such an animal is born it is extremely tender, and cannot be fed with dry food. Therefore breasts were provided which yield milk, and the young can be fed with moist food which corresponds to the condition of the limbs of the animal, until the latter have gradually become dry and hard."
>
> **¶2:** "Many precepts in our Law are the result of a similar course adopted by the same Supreme Being. It is, namely, impossible to go suddenly from one extreme to the other: it is therefore according to the nature of man impossible for him suddenly to discontinue everything to which he has been accustomed. Now God sent Moses to make [the Israelites] a kingdom of priests and a holy nation (Exod. 19:6) by means of the knowledge of God. … But the custom which was in those days general among all men, and the general mode of worship in which the Israelites were brought up, consisted in sacrificing animals in those temples which contained certain images, to bow down to those images, and to burn incense before them; … It was in accordance with the wisdom and plan of God, as displayed in the whole Creation, that He did not command us to give up and to discontinue all these manners of service; for to obey such a commandment it would have been contrary to the nature of man, who generally cleaves to that to which he is used; it would in those days have made the same impression as a prophet would make at present if he called us to the service of God and told us in His name, that we should not pray to Him, not fast, not seek His help in time of trouble; that we should serve Him in thought, and not by any action."
>
> **¶3:** "For this reason God allowed these kinds of service to continue; He transferred to His service that which had formerly served as a worship of created beings, and of things imaginary and unreal, and commanded us to serve Him in the same manner; … By this Divine plan it was effected that the traces of idolatry were blotted out, and the truly great principle of our faith, the Existence and Unity of God, was firmly established; this result was thus obtained without deterring or confusing the minds of the people by the abolition of the service to which they were accustomed and which alone was familiar to them."
>
> **¶5:** "… Although in every one of the signs [related in Scripture] the natural property of some individual being is changed, the nature of man is never changed by God by way of miracle. … I do not say this because I believe that it is difficult for God to change the nature of every individual person; on the contrary, it is possible, and it is in His power, according to the principles taught in Scripture; but it has never been His will to do it, and it never will be. If it were part of His will to change [at His desire] the nature of any person, the mission of prophets and the giving of the Law would have been altogether superfluous."
>
> **¶6:** "I now return to my theme. As the sacrificial service is not the primary object [of the commandments about sacrifice], whilst supplications, prayers, and similar kinds of worship are nearer to the primary object, and indispensable for obtaining it, a great difference was made in the Law between these two kinds of service. … Only one temple has been appointed, 'in the place which the Lord shall choose' (Deut. 12:26); in no other place is it allowed to sacrifice … All these restrictions served to limit this kind of worship, and keep it within those bounds within which God did not think it necessary to abolish sacrificial service altogether. But prayer and supplication can be offered everywhere and by every person. The same is the case with the commandment of ẓiẓit (Num. 15:38); mezuzah (Deut. 6:9; 11:20); tefillin (Exod. 13:9, 16); and similar kinds of divine service."

Four things I would like help with.

1. Is ¶1 doing argumentative work, or is it decorative framing? Milk is not abolished once the mammal matures; it is one stage in a developmental sequence whose terminus is fixed by nature. If that is the operative analogy, what does it imply about whether the sacrificial cult is something to be *outgrown*, as against something merely *explained*? Note also the shift in scope between the two paragraphs: ¶1 is about the development of each individual organism, while ¶2 applies "a similar course" to a whole people across generations. Does that shift survive scrutiny, or does it quietly convert a claim about ontogeny into a claim about historical progress that the analogy does not support?

2. The counterfactual in ¶2 says that a prophet commanding us today to abandon prayer, fasting, and supplication and to serve God "in thought, and not by any action" would produce the same reaction as a command to abandon sacrifice produced then. Taken at face value, this assimilates prayer to sacrifice as an accommodation of the same sort. Does he mean that? If so, what is the first-intention form of worship that both are accommodating, and what is left of the embodied practices he praises elsewhere in the same paragraph? If not, what disanalogy between sacrifice and prayer is he relying on, and is he entitled to it given how the sentence is phrased?

3. ¶6 is where I am most stuck. How much weight can the final clause bear — the restrictions kept sacrifice "within those bounds within which God did not think it necessary to abolish sacrificial service altogether"? That reads to me like minimization short of abolition, i.e. a trajectory. Does it license a graded theory of accommodations, on which some practices are hemmed in because they are on a path toward disappearance while others are left unrestricted because they are already near the primary object? Or is the sacrifice/prayer asymmetry explained purely by differing proximity to the primary object, with no temporal direction at all? Tell me which of these two readings the quoted sentences support and point to the single sentence you take as decisive.

4. ¶5 asserts a permanent constraint: God never changes human nature miraculously, and "it has never been His will to do it, and it never will be." But if human nature is fixed in that way, the very premise that made the concession necessary for the wilderness generation seems to hold forever, which would block any inference from this chapter to the abrogability of sacrifice. How do you square that with whatever direction of travel you found in ¶6? And does ¶5 cut the other way: if the mission of the prophets and the giving of the Law would be superfluous were God willing to change human nature directly, does that make the Law itself the non-miraculous instrument by which human nature is changed slowly?

Two pieces of context for the last question. In his legal code Maimonides rules that the messianic king will rebuild the sanctuary, gather the dispersed of Israel, and restore the sacrificial order as it was; I am not looking for a reflexive appeal to esoteric writing to dissolve that, but for an assessment of whether the paragraphs above, read on their own, are consistent with it, and on which of the readings from questions 3 and 4. I have also come across a rabbinic saying to the effect that in the time to come all offerings cease except the thanksgiving offering; mention it only if you think it genuinely bears on ¶6, and do not build anything on it, since I cannot verify its source.

Since I cannot check Friedländer against the original, please mark explicitly any place where you suspect his wording is doing something the Arabic might not, or where one of your interpretive claims depends on a phrase that could plausibly be rendered differently. Keep these clearly labelled as hypotheses about the translation, and do not supply reconstructed Hebrew or Judeo-Arabic terms or any quotation you have not actually seen.

Finally, on epistemic standing: for each of the four questions, give your confidence in the reading you defend (high, medium, or low is fine) and say briefly what drives that confidence — the wording of a particular sentence, the absence of a distinction you expected, and so on. Then single out the one claim in your overall reconstruction that you are least sure of, and say what feature of the quoted text, rather than what outside reading, would settle it or at least move it. I am after the reasoning about these sentences, not a bibliography, a list of commentators, or a summary of the scholarly debate.
