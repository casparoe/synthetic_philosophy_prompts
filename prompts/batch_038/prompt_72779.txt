I'm a 2L and, against all advice, I'm also auditing a grad epistemology seminar to keep the philosophy alive. Last week we spent two hours on that puzzle where knowledge seems to license you to stop listening: if I genuinely know P, then I also know that any future evidence against P is misleading, so I'm apparently entitled — maybe even rationally required — to refuse to look at it, to pre-commit now against whatever turns up later. Everyone in the room found that picture of rationality repulsive but nobody could say precisely what was wrong with it, and I walked home in a weird mood because it turns out the thing I spend my days reading is a fully built-out implementation of that exact argument, complete with deadlines.

Here's the practice, described as concretely as I can. In federal court, once a state prisoner's direct appeals run out, the judgment is "final," and from that moment there is a one-year window to file a habeas petition. After that, on any claim the state court already decided on the merits, the federal court cannot grant relief unless the state court's decision was contrary to, or an unreasonable application of, clearly established Supreme Court law, or rested on an unreasonable determination of the facts — and the Supreme Court has glossed "unreasonable" as meaning not merely wrong, but wrong past the point where fair-minded jurists could disagree. State-court factual findings are presumed correct and the prisoner has to rebut that presumption by clear and convincing evidence. Second or successive petitions are essentially barred unless there's a brand-new retroactive constitutional rule, or a factual predicate that couldn't have been discovered with due diligence plus a showing that no reasonable factfinder would have convicted. If you missed a state procedural deadline, you're defaulted, and a strong claim of actual innocence operates only as a gateway to excuse the default, not as a free-standing reason to release you. Meanwhile, on the other side of the ledger, an acquittal is final forever: the defendant can walk out of the courthouse, confess on camera the next afternoon, and there is no mechanism whatsoever to reopen it — which is not obviously required by anything, since England and Wales amended their double jeopardy rules in 2003 to permit retrial for serious offences where new and compelling evidence emerges.

Then tell me what the practice *strictly* requires versus what it merely fits comfortably with, and where it's philosophically underdetermined, such that rival theories would license the identical rules — and what observable feature of the doctrine, if any, could discriminate between them.

The part I most want you to dig into is the internal tension. Does the "unreasonable application" standard — which seems to concede that competent judges applying the same law can reasonably land in different places — sit coherently beside a presumption that the trial factfinder's determinations are correct unless rebutted by clear and convincing evidence, which seems to assume those determinations are presumptively *true*? One of those is a humility claim about disagreement and the other is a confidence claim about accuracy, and they're doing work in the same statute. Also: what theory of evidence does the one-year clock presuppose, given that the clock runs from finality rather than from the discovery of anything, and that the discovery-of-new-facts trigger is confined to a narrow lane? And does acquittal-finality presuppose something about truth that conviction-finality denies, or are they the same commitment applied asymmetrically for non-epistemic reasons? Finally, tell me what would concretely have to change — doctrinal machinery, not slogans — if the system adopted a straightforwardly truth-tracking epistemology on which every new claim is just evidence to be weighed, and be honest about whether what we'd lose in that swap is an epistemic loss or something else entirely.
