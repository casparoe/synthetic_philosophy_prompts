I'm two terms into a reading group on contractualism and we've hit a wall on the numbers problem that I don't think is going anywhere. What I want is a diagnosis, not a survey — please don't re-explain what contractualism is or walk me through the rescue case, we all know it, and don't give me a reading list either, that's not the point. Set-up: half the group are pairwise-comparison purists who insist that Scanlon's own balancing-complaints move betrays the whole purpose of the theory, that justification to each has to happen one person at a time or the thing collapses into utilitarianism in a contractarian hat. The other half say the purists are fetishising a distinction without a difference, becuase when the complaints on both sides are equally strong the pairwise person has nothing left to say except "flip a coin", and a theory whose verdict in the five-against-one case is a coin toss isn't respecting the separateness of persons, its ignoring it. Then there's a third who has defected to straightforward aggregation and thinks the project was never going to work, and a fourth who wants to bite the bullet with Taurek-style equal-chance lotteries everywhere and be done with it. Nobody has moved since Michaelmas and we've started going in circles about whether we're even arguing about the same thing.

My hunch — tell me if it's wrong, or only half right — is that we aren't disagreeing about verdicts at all. On every case we've put on the table (the two islands; a hospital that can either save five people from death or spare a million people a lost finger; the same hospital with the numbers and the sizes of harm moving in opposite directions across a chain of cases so that each adjacent pair looks clear but the ends don't match up) everyone either agrees what should be done or agrees it's genuinely unclear. What we disagree about is what the justification is allowed to look like. So maybe "justifiable to each person" is doing two different jobs in the room: a distributive one, where you ask each person separately whether they could reasonably reject a principle, and a collective one, where you ask each person to consider a principle in which everyone else's complaints are already sitting in the balance — and half our argument is those two uses talking past each other. A second hunch: "aggregation" is a term of art for the purists, meaning summing quantities of good and losing track of whose good it is, but an ordinary word for the rest of us, meaning just that numbers count for something, so when the defector says contractualism aggregates after all and the purists say it doesn't, they might both be right and no case will ever settle it. Third hunch, and this is the one I actually care about: maybe there's a formal question underneath the whole thing that nobody in our group has the tools for — whether any principle can simultaneously be justifiable to each person taken singly, never let a huge number of trivial complaints outweigh one death, and deliver transitive verdicts across a chain of cases. If somebody proved that combination impossible, wouldn't that end the debate in a way no amount of case-juggling ever will?

So: diagnose it. Is the disagreement substantive, verbal, two different questions filed under one heading, or is the demand itself ill-posed — is there something confused about insisting a principle be justifiable to each person one at a time, as opposed to justifiable to each person full stop? Tell me which of my three hunches survive contact with what each camp actually says, where the real crux is if there is one, and what would count as progress given your diagnosis: a distinction, a case, a theorem, a change of question, or nothing, with reasons why nothing should be expected. British English please — I'm handing bits of this to a seminar tutor who marks down Americanisms.
