I teach one two-hour seminar a year inside a philosophy-of-education module for trainee secondary teachers — mostly maths, physics, chemistry and modern-languages trainees, all of whom are simultaneously being drilled to produce lesson plans on a fixed skeleton (learning objectives first, then starter, main activity, plenary) and most of whom regard anything said in this module as something to be endured before they get back to their placements. My slot is Whitehead on education: the complaint about inert ideas, and the three-stage rhythm of romance, precision and generalisation. I have taught it three times and never once landed it. The first year it collapsed into a slogan about following the child's interest; the second year a trainee mapped the three stages onto starter–main–plenary in front of me and the whole room nodded; last year it dissolved into an argument about whether Whitehead was for or against drilling. I don't want the material explained back to me — I have read the essays and can summarise them adequately — I want a plan for explaining them to this particular room.

So give me the dependency structure rather than the summary. Which of the two pieces has to be in place before the other can be understood at all, and why: I have never been clear whether the rhythm is the remedy for inert ideas or whether inert ideas are a diagnosis that only makes sense once the rhythm is in hand, and I suspect my ordering is the main source of the failures above. What has to come before either of them, given that the room arrives believing educational theory must be either empirical psychology or personal opinion? Then the carrying example. It has to come from a subject they actually teach, it has to be small enough to trace minute by minute rather than being a whole curriculum, and it has to be a case in which the naive reading of the three stages visibly fails — so tell me what the example must have and what it must lack, and why the obvious candidates (learning to ride a bike, the child obsessed with dinosaurs, a teacher's own favourite lesson) will not do the work.

Then the misconception map. I expect at least these: that romance means entertainment or a motivational hook, so the stages become a lesson-plan template; that the stages are age bands and therefore a claim about when to begin Latin, which makes the theory irrelevant to a single Tuesday period; that inert ideas simply means facts memorised and later forgotten, which turns Whitehead into a point about retention; and that precision means drilling, which lets the resident advocate of explicit instruction recruit Whitehead either as an ally or as an enemy of rigour. For each, give me the exact point in the sequence at which to head it off — before it forms, if that is possible — and the move that does it. There will also be one trainee who says the scheme is unfalsifiable, since any lesson can be redescribed as any stage: give me something honest to say to him that neither concedes the point nor bluffs. Finally, tell me what I should refuse to cover at this level and announce that I am refusing it — I assume the metaphysics and probably the Hegelian ancestry of the triad, but say if cutting either costs me something I need — and give me one question to put to the room at the end that separates someone who can recite the three stages from someone who can tell me whether a lesson plan they wrote last week is manufacturing inert ideas. No reading list, please: the session gets a whiteboard and two short printed extracts, and nothing else.
