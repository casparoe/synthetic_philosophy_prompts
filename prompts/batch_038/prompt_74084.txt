I'm on the scientific review panel of a mid-sized foundation (education programs, about $14M a year) and we've got one awkward renewal decision at Friday's meeting. The applicant group runs an early-literacy intervention — small-group phonics plus an attendance and parent-outreach component — across three districts, and we've funded them for six years. This cycle they've bolted on a twin substudy: roughly 900 twin pairs recruited in those same districts, and they report that at the end of third grade the heritability of their reading composite is 0.22, shared environment 0.61, nonshared environment plus measurement error 0.17. Their pitch, more or less, is that since only about a fifth of the variance is genetic the trait is mostly environmental, so their program should be able to move the mean a good deal — they're projecting a 0.4 SD gain over two years and they've built the whole budget request around hitting it.

One of our panelists, a behavioral geneticist, is having none of it, and he's made two objections that I suspect aren't actually the same objection. First: this group's earlier pilot, run in a much more affluent suburban sample, reported a heritability around 0.68 for basically the same measure, and he says they can't have it both ways — either the trait is heritable or it isn't, and swinging from 0.68 to 0.22 shows they don't know what they're doing. Second: he says twin numbers like these are too noisy to carry any policy claim anyway, because the estimate is just a variance ratio for one particular population living in one particular set of circumstances, and it can't license a prediction about what happens when you introduce something that wasn't in those circumstances. I think he's right about the second and wrong about the first, but I couldn't defend either in a room. I vaguely remember reading somewhere that heritability of cognitive test scores tends to come out higher in higher-income families, which if true would make his two numbers consistent rather than embarrassing — but I have no idea why that would be, or whether it's actually settled, so please don't build the memo on my half-memory of it.

Can you draft the decision memo I'll circulate Thursday night? Two pages max, addressed to the panel, cc the program officer, who is skeptical of the whole thing and will be looking for reasons to say no. It needs an actual bottom line — fund the renewal, reject it, or fund with conditions, and if conditions, conditions someone could check compliance against, with dates. It should say plainly whether the 0.22 does or doesn't support the 0.4 SD projection, and what kind of evidence would. And it has to engage the geneticist's first objection on the merits instead of swatting it, because if he's right we've been funding a credibility problem for six years, and if he's wrong I want to be able to explain that to him across the table without him thinking I'm just protecting a grantee I like. Nobody else on the panel is a statistician, so define terms as you go, but dont write it like a textbook chapter — we all have jobs.
