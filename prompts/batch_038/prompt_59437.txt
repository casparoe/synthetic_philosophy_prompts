A friend and I argued late into last night about local opposition to housing, and we reached an impasse that I would rather stage in front of other people than keep rehearsing between the two of us.

His position: opposition to infill development is asset protection in civic costume. The people who fill hearing rooms to talk about shadow, parking, and neighborhood character are defending the largest, least diversified, most leveraged thing they own, and the public-regarding vocabulary is rationalization applied afterward. Remove the financial stake and the objection evaporates.

My position: the financial motive is real but not load-bearing. What actually produces maximalist blockage is the shape of the procedure. A small project must clear a sequence of gates — design review, a height variance, a waiver of the parking minimum, a discretionary council vote — and at each gate a different person with a different sincere and individually modest objection can raise a flag that costs the project months and money. Almost nobody in that chain wants zero housing; several would gladly accept a revised version of the very proposal they are obstructing. But the conjunction of partial vetoes is far more restrictive than any participant's preference, and the applicant, facing unbounded delay at a known carrying cost, withdraws. Blockage without a blocker.

And my further claim, which he considers evasive: you cannot repair this by enlarging the decision-making public. Seat everyone who bears the consequences — commuters, would-be movers, the region's renters — and you get a different answer, but you have not solved the question of who counts; you have moved it up a level. The enlarged body now excludes the unhoused, minors, non-citizens, and people who have not yet arrived, and it has acquired new neighbors at its own new edge. Every boundary is drawn by someone, and the drawing of it is prior to any vote taken inside it.

I want an activity that lets a room of ordinary people feel both of my claims and, more importantly, test them against his rather than simply absorb them.

The setting. I facilitate a two-hour public workshop for a municipal planning department's outreach program: about 28 people, no prior knowledge of land-use law, ages roughly 19 to 75. Recruitment through the city's mailing list means about a third are neighborhood-association regulars, at least two of whom have actually spoken against projects at real hearings; a third arrive through a tenant union and a pro-housing group; a third are unaffiliated. We will run two tables of fourteen in parallel, each with its own facilitator, and I will circulate.

What they should come away seeing: that the outcome can be far more restrictive than any individual's preference, without anyone's bad faith; and that widening the electorate changes the answer while leaving the arbitrariness of the boundary intact. They should not hear either framing from me until the end, and they should leave able to say whether what happened at their table supports me or supports my friend.

Please design the whole thing, and include:

(a) A dossier for one invented project in one invented city, with invented code sections and an invented site — specific enough to generate objections that are not merely aesthetic: parking spillover, drainage, school capacity, shadow across a community garden, an eighteen-month construction period, and the precedent it sets for a corridor-wide upzoning. Include at least one objection arriving from the left — displacement of the tenants in an adjacent rooming house, plus the fact that the units labeled affordable are affordable at an income threshold well above what the typical local renter earns — and treat it as a position with genuine force rather than as obstruction in progressive clothing.

(b) The roles, with briefs each participant receives, including private lines they are instructed not to read aloud.

(c) The gate structure: how a flag is raised, what it costs and whom it costs, what a revision buys, who rules at each gate, and a token ledger giving each role a scoring function that includes a non-zero-sum public-interest component. No real money anywhere.

(d) The manipulation that makes the disagreement testable. At one table, every incumbent-owner brief carries a concealed financial position: the home is nearly all of their net worth, the mortgage was refinanced at a rate no longer obtainable, and there is deferred maintenance they cannot fund. At the other table, the identical briefs strip all of it out — these participants rent, or own outright with proceeds in diversified holdings, and have no school-age children and no car. Same objections available, same flags, same gate costs. The two tables then compare. Tell me what a difference between them would and would not establish, and tell me what to say if they come out the same, which is the result I expect and my friend does not.

(e) A second round in which jurisdiction moves to an invented regional body that includes the would-be movers and the commuters, arranged so that participants discover through the rules rather than through my narration that the fix regenerates the problem — for instance, the regional vote controls permits but not the school funding formula, which stays local, and one category of seat is held by property owners, excluding precisely the people whose inclusion was the point.

(f) A minute-by-minute timeline for the 120 minutes, marking the moments when the two tables must synchronize.

(g) The debrief, in which these things get their names only after participants have described what happened to them. Map the likely moments onto the composition claim and the boundary claim, and give me the question I should put to the two real hearing regulars, who will know that this is not quite how it works in their city and should be invited to say so at length.

(h) What will predictably go wrong on the first run: the risk that the exercise reads to the association people as a rigged demonstration that they are the villains; the risk that the applicant role gets played as a caricature; the risk that token bargaining swamps the point; the risk that one table's facilitator tips the result and contaminates the comparison. For each, a concrete handling.

(i) An honest statement of what the activity cannot reproduce and what I must therefore supply in words. I suspect that compressing thirty years of lived consequence into forty minutes removes exactly what makes incumbents' objections feel weighty to those who hold them, and that a facilitator-defined scoring function quietly decides whose welfare counts.

Two constraints. Do not cite, recommend, or attribute: no named theorists, no real cities, no real statutes, no literature — the exercise should run entirely on invented local detail so that nothing in it needs checking against a source. And wherever the debrief script asserts something about how this works in the world, separate what is genuinely settled among people who study land-use politics from what remains contested among them. Mark which of my claims and which of my friend's correspond to live disputes, and do not let the facilitator's script present a contested view — mine in particular — as though it were the consensus. Flag also every place where the design of the game itself embeds a contested normative assumption that participants are being steered toward without being told.
