I have been reading, as an interested amateur, the controversy over whether labels such as the Middle Ages, the Renaissance, and the Scientific Revolution pick out anything real or are constructions imposed on the evidence after the fact. My question is not about that controversy itself but about a particular argumentative pattern I keep meeting on both sides of it, and for which I have never managed to find a name.

A period is characterized by a bundle of traits. When a figure or a text outside the period's dates displays those traits, it is classified as a forerunner, an anticipation, or a proto-something, and so is not counted as a counterexample to the dating. When a figure inside the dates conspicuously lacks the traits, it is classified as a survival or a holdover from the preceding age, and so is not counted as a counterexample either. The two moves run in opposite temporal directions and are each individually plausible, but together they guarantee that no case can ever count against the label: the period keeps its dates and its defining features no matter what turns up. Two illustrations from what I have been reading. When a fourteenth-century natural philosopher works out a mathematics of how qualities intensify and remit, he gets folded forward into the Scientific Revolution as an anticipation; when a seventeenth-century natural philosopher turns out to be deeply invested in astrology and in biblical chronology, that gets explained backward as medieval residue, and after both adjustments the Scientific Revolution begins and ends exactly where it did before and is defined by exactly the same traits. Likewise, if I point out that scholastic method was still the normal way of teaching natural philosophy at a good many universities well into the 1600s, the reply is not that the period is misdated but that those universities were behind the times, so the exception confirms the label instead of testing it.

I know of an established complaint about historians who assess the past by how far it led toward the present, and I know that the philosophy of science has a vocabulary for protecting a theory from refutation by adjusting its fringes. Neither seems to be quite the thing I have in mind, because in my case what gets adjusted is not a theory's predictions but the assignment of particular cases to a labeled stretch of time, and the adjustment is made symmetrically in two directions rather than one. So: is there a term of art for this, in historiography, in the philosophy of history, or in the literature on periodization? If there is, tell me what it is and what work it does; if there is not, say so plainly rather than stretching a neighboring notion to cover it.

Please answer in American English, as a numbered list of exactly five items, one or two sentences each, with no introduction and no conclusion. The five items should cover, in order: the two or three established concepts that come closest to what I have described; precisely what in my description each of them fails to capture; your verdict on whether an exact term exists; if it does not, a name you would coin, defined sharply enough that a reasonable person could dispute whether a given case falls under it; and two boundary cases, one in which calling something an anticipation is legitimate and one in which it is not, or whatever pair you think best shows where the concept stops applying. The label matters far less to me than the edges.
