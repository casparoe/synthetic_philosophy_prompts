Question 2. (a) Dennett's Chase and Sanborn case involves two coffee tasters whose verdicts about the same, unchanging coffee drift over the years. Set out the different accounts of what might have changed that Dennett considers — i.e., in each version, which of experience, underlying machinery, and judgment or reactive disposition is supposed to have shifted. And here's the student's answer:

Student answer — Dennett's version of qualia has four marks: they're ineffable, intrinsic, private, and directly apprehensible to consciousness. The Chase and Sanborn story goes after the first of these. Both men are professional tasters, and over the years each comes to say that the coffee he once loved isn't what it used to be, even though the blend hasnt been altered. Dennett's move is to notice that several incompatible stories fit that datum. In one, the experience itself has drifted: the coffee genuinely tastes different to Sanborn now, while the machinery doing the work is the same machinery as always. In another, the experience has stayed fixed from day one and it's the machinery that has changed. In a third, both have changed. And in a fourth, neither experience nor machinery has changed at all — what's changed is only the judgment, since the men have grown jaded and more discriminating, so the very same experience now gets filed under a different verdict.

Chase and Sanborn can rule some of these out on their own. The fourth is impossible, because if neither experience nor machinery changed there'd be nothing for a change of verdict to hang on, and they'd have noticed that nothing about the taste had shifted. What survives is the first story: their experiences changed, and they know that directly. So the case shows that the tasters can't characterize what their experience is like even to themselves — the qualitative character is ineffable. It also shows that comparing my taste experience with yours is hopeless, since neither of us has access to the other's qualia, which is really the inverted spectrum worry transplanted into a coffee cup. Dennett then draws the radical conclusion that there's no such thing as an experience of coffee: when you drink a cup there is nothing it is like for you, and talk about how things taste is just a disposition to issue verdicts.

(b) Yes. If the drug puts Chase's verdicts back where they started without touching anything else, the verdict clearly wasn't tracking the experience, so the judgment-shift account is confirmed and the experience must have been constant the whole time. That settles the question.
