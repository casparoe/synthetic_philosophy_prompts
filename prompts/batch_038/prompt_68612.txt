end of week six in my probability-and-philosophy seminar and we've just done Bertrand's chord problem. the students have already seen all three answers on slides, which is honestly the problem — they're treating it as a cute party trick rather than something that should worry them. I want to close the session with a pantoum that makes the arbitrariness land somewhere other than their notes.

Five or six stanzas, and please close it properly, so the last stanza picks the poem's first and third lines back up.

The conceit: the pantoum's recycling should *be* the three sampling methods. Two points chosen on the rim; a point chosen along a radius; a point chosen anywhere in the interior and taken as a midpoint. At least two lines have to come back in later stanzas with their meaning changed by the new neighbours — a line that meant "pick a point" among the rim-points should mean something else once it's sitting among midpoints. I want the reader to feel that a line, like a variable, is only equipossible relative to the paramterization it happens to be embedded in. A third, a half and a quarter should all turn up, but never as a list: each one should arrive as the obviously correct answer to the stanza that generated it, so the poem is unfair in exactly the way Bertrands setup is unfair.

Ground rules. No names in the verse — no Bertrand, no Jaynes, and please don't use the phrase "principle of indifference" itself. If there's any gesture toward a way out (a distribution that doesn't change when you shrink the circle or slide it across the page, or the flat admission that there is no way out), it belongs in the last couple of stanzas and it has to stay an image, not an argument. And it should be an actual poem: chalk on a floor, a handful of straws dropped from shoulder height, a compass, a triangle nobody can see — whatever you like, but no explaining. Something a student would want to photograph off the projector.

Give it a title that would survive being printed on a handout.

Then after the poem, a section headed "The permutation" — 150-200 words — walking through which line recurs where and which recurrence carries which of the three answers, plus one honest sentence about where the pantoum form actually earns its keep here and where it's just decoration.
