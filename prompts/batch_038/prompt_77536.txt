I'm putting together a session for a moral psych reading group and I want to use Ishiguro's The Remains of the Day as the text, but I don't want the obvious take (repressed butler, wasted life, feel sad at the end). I want to read it against the problem of telling preferences apart from biases — specifically the adaptive prefernce problem, the sour-grapes line of thought: when someone's options are narrow and they reshape their desires to fit, does the reshaped desire still count as theirs, and does satisfying it still count as good for them?

Where I want the reading to live is the epistemics. Stevens doesn't merely have deformed preferences, he has a whole articulated theory of why they're excellent — dignity, professionalism, service to a great gentleman — and he reports them with total reflective endorsement, at length, in his own voice. So the novel forces the question every adaptive-preference theorist gets accused of dodging: if the subject himself calmly says these are his preferences, what entitles an outsider to reclassify them as bias? Walk me through what actually licenses that move in the book. My hunch is that it isn't argument, it's form: we're permitted to see the gap between what Stevens reports and what he lets slip — the night his father is dying upstairs while he keeps the dinner service running, Miss Kenton trying to tell him about her marriage proposal on the evening he's handling Herr Ribbentrop and him steering her back to duties, the way he admits the two Jewish housemaids were dismissed and immediately shrinks that to an insignificant interlude. Is the reader's privileged position doing philosophical work that a theory of adaptive preferences can't do by itself — i.e. is spotting a deformed preference always an interpretive achievement, closer to reading an unreliable narrator than to measuring anything? If so, what does that imply about the confidence with which the theory gets applied to real people who can't be narrated from outside?

Then two counterweights I'd like you to take seriously rather than wave off. First, Mrs Benn: she tells him she's grown to love her husband and is happy enough, with only occasional spells of wondering about the road not taken. Is her contentment an adaptive preference too, and if it is, why does it read as health where Stevens's reads as damage? If the honest answer is that we simply like her more, say that — but its not really an answer, so tell me what else could distinguish them. Second, the closing scene by the sea, where a stranger tells him the evening is the best part of the day and he resolves to spend his remaining years getting better at bantering for Mr Farraday. I want you to commit: first genuine preference revision, or one more adaptive adjustment wearing wisdom as a costume? And does the village pub scene earlier — the locals arguing that dignity is something every Englishman just has by birth, with Stevens privately dissenting — matter to that verdict?

Finally, where does the novel resist the frame? Tell me what the preference-versus-bias vocabulary flattens or straight-up misses, and finish by saying what the book adds to the debate about adaptive preferences that argument alone doesn't — a detection method, a limit on paternalism, something else. No reading list or citations, just argue from the scenes and the concepts, and don't bother quoting Ishiguro verbatim; describing the moments precisely is enough.
