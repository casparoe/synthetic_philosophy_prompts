OK so here's my situation. Our department gives out exactly one small internal grant a year, which amounts to a semester of teaching relief, and I somehow got it. So I have about four months of uninterrupted time and I owe them one paper. When I told my advisor I wanted to spend it on Carroll's tortoise and the justification of deduction, I got a shrug and a "just make sure it isn't exegesis" — which means I can't nibble at five questions, I have to pick the one that's most worth four months of a single person's life and go at it properly.

Here's the candidate list as it currently stands on my whiteboard:

A. Whether the regress is about propositions or about rules. Is the tortoise's trick to demand that each new premise be added to the set, or to demand that the rule itself be written down as one more premise? Does it matter which way you tell the story, or is the difference cosmetic?

B. Whether taking-the-premises-to-support-the-conclusion can be a disposition or a practice rather than a contentful attitude — and if it can, whether that stops the regress or just relocates it into the question of what makes the disposition correct rather than merely habitual.

C. The Boghossian-style move: basic deductive steps transmit justification while the reasoner follows them blindly, and blindness here isn't a defect. Is that an actual answer to the tortoise, a redescription of the problem, or a polite surrender to it?

D. Rule-circularity: any defense of deduction has to use deduction. Is that fatal or benign, and is the tortoise just this same problem in a costume? If it's the same problem, does dissolving one dissolve the other?

E. Whether you can be entitled to trust modus ponens without the entitlement itself resting on an inference, and what the entitlement is supposed to buy you that an inference couldn't.

F. The one my office-mate in computational linguistics is convinced is the whole answer. He says the tortoise problem is dissolved by construction in his system, because the inference rule is compiled into the engine instead of being represented as an axiom the system has to look up and then apply. I want to know whether that's a real response to Carroll or just a place to hide the problem. Does implementing a rule in the architecture rather than representing it in the data get you anything normative, or has he built a tortoise that simply can't talk?

G. Whether the entire project is misconceived — maybe the interesting question isn't whether we're entitled to MP but what work the notion of entitlement was recruited to do in the first place.

What I want back is an ordering, defended, not a tour. For each one: how much of the rest of the list depends on its answer, whether one person with four months could plausibly move it (I'm comfortable with metalogic and hopeless at anything requiring real engineering), and whether it's the sort of question where a negitive result still comes out as a paper rather than as a wasted semester. Flag any that you think are traps — look profound, dissolve on contact — and say what dissolves them. Then commit: which one would you take into the semester, and what's the specific claim you'd set out to establish in it?

I've read the literature and I don't need a summary of the 1895 paper or a reading list, I need the prioritization argued. Finish with a single line in exactly this form: \answer{X} where X is the letter of the question you'd put first.
