I hold a chair in political philosophy and have run the same graduate seminar on the metric of distributive justice for the better part of two decades. Over that time I have become preoccupied by a methodological pattern that I cannot locate anywhere in the literature and for which I have no name. Let me say at the outset what I am not after: I do not want references or a reading list, since I know the substantive debates as well as anyone, and I do not want a label dropped into the text as though the matter were settled. What I want is a carving — what this pattern exactly is, which established concepts come closest to it, why each of those fails to be it, and where its edges lie.

The pattern concerns theories whose normative work is done by a two-sided distinction that allocates a burden. In my field the paradigm case is the distinction between those features of a person's situation for which she is answerable and those that are merely hers, features she did not choose. Such a theory's verdicts are fixed entirely by where the line falls. What I keep observing, in print and in seminar, is that when the theory meets a recalcitrant case the line is relocated, and relocated in whichever direction absorbs the case; that this happens repeatedly and in both directions, sometimes within a single author's work; and that it happens without any appearance of bad faith, each relocation accompanied by a locally compelling piece of reasoning.

Two illustrations. A person's vocation is intrinsically poorly rewarded, or her tastes are costly. Object that compensating her licenses claims that others cannot make, and the reply is that her preference was formed in a household and a milieu she did not select, that she cannot disclaim it, that in the relevant sense it was never an exercise of her agency — so it belongs on the circumstance side and must be compensated. A person develops an expensive condition. Object that the theory now owes compensation for everything and threatens to become extensionless, and the reply is that screening was available to her and she declined it, that she endorsed a way of life carrying that risk, that she might have insured against it — so it belongs on the choice side and is not compensated. In neither case is the direction of movement set by an independent account of where the line lies; it is set by which verdict is under threat.

What makes me suspect this is not a local defect of one metric is that the same relocation occurs on all three sides of the question I teach. The welfarist, pressed, moves between preference satisfaction as it is and preference satisfaction as it would be under better conditions. The resourcist moves between the bundle a person holds and what she is able to do with it. The capability theorist moves between the functioning achieved and the freedom to achieve it. In each case the theory's own internal boundary migrates to meet the objection.

The consequence, so far as I can see, is a theory that cannot be defeated by any case at all, since every case is answered by reclassifying the case rather than by revising the theory — and that therefore never registers a failure, because reclassification is not scored as one. It reports perfect agreement with intuition at every point and says nothing anywhere.

So, five questions. First: does this configuration have an established name, in the methodology of science, in informal logic, or in political philosophy itself? Second: enumerate the nearest established concepts and, for each, state precisely why it falls short — I will note only that I have considered the familiar idea of preferences that adjust downward to constrained circumstances and do not think it is the same thing, since that is a claim about the people being measured rather than about the criterion applied to them. Third: if nothing fits, coin a term and define it so as to exclude each of its near neighbours. Fourth: boundary cases. Is what I have described distinguishable in principle from the mutual adjustment of principles and considered judgements that the standard coherentist method recommends? If it is, what is the discriminating feature — the direction of adjustment, its asymmetry across cases, the absence of any independent fixity in the boundary term — and could a third party apply that test prospectively rather than only in retrospect? Fifth: is the defect in these theories, or in the distinction they are built on; that is, could a non-arbitrary boundary be specified, or does the pattern show that the distinction cannot carry the normative weight placed upon it?

A name alone would leave me exactly where I began. What I am asking for is the thing itself, sharply bounded, together with the cases that sit near the border and a verdict on which side of it they fall.
