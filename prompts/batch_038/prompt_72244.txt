I write novels about families behaving badly, and I've just noticed I've been running a free-will seminar in the margins of my drafts for about fifteen years. Not deliberately. It's the reactions. I keep a private list of my own characters and how much blame I actually feel for them — not what I decide to write about them, what I feel when I'm in the scene with them — and I want to know what principle is underneath, becuase I have a strong suspicion it's not one principle and it's not consistent.

Quick disclaimer so you don't go down the wrong road: I'm not asking about whether fictional people can be blamed. Treat them as real. These are the same gut reactions I have to real people and they're the only data I've got.

Here's the list.

**Marta** was raised in a house where the fastest way to get a parent's attention was to hurt somebody first. At forty she ends a younger colleague's career with a lie and sleeps fine. I blame her, but thinned out, like something you'd water down to make it go further. Reduced, definitely not erased.

**Neil** got everything — adored, safe, well-fed for affection, money, no trauma whatsoever — and he humiliates a waiter because it's funny and because he can. Full-strength blame. More blame than for Marta, and that's the embarrassing part, because the harm is a tenth the size.

**Dee** says the same things to the same waiter with the same pleasure, but in her book she was made that way on purpose: somebody engineered her from birth to enjoy it. Zero blame for Dee. It all slides off her and lands on the engineer, and I can't get any of it to stick to her again no matter how hard I try.

Marta and Dee are the pair I can't live with. Dee had a designer, Marta had a father and a hallway. The causal shape is identical — a set of forces made her the sort of person who'd do it, she did it, she liked it. But one gets thinned blame and one gets none, and the only difference my gut can find is that in Marta's case nobody meant any of it. Which if I say it out loud sounds like I'm blaming her because her damage was an accident. I know that's not good enough and I don't have better.

**Bea** knows she's spiteful, hates it, works on it for years, and still snaps at her sister over dinner because the habit is faster than she is. Less blame than Neil, who never tried anything. But not none, and when I ask myself what the residual is for, I find I want to give her credit for the trying, and then credit for having been able to try, and then credit for whatever made her able to be credited — and it just goes backwards, forever, until there's nothing at the bottom. Which is, as I understand it, exactly the move Galen Strawson's Basic Argument says nobody can make. My paraphrase of it, from memory and at one remove: you do what you do because of the sort of person you are, and to be really responsible for doing it you'd have to be responsible for being that sort of person, and nobody ever is. Correct me if I've mangled it.

**Theo** does Neil's act with a gun at his head. Almost total excuse. **Rosa** does Neil's act out of a compulsion she describes, convincingly, as irresistible. I give her much less of a pass. In both cases she'd have done it anyway and in both cases she'd have preferred a world where she didn't want to. I cannot explain that gap to you.

And the credit side runs backwards from all this. **Iris** is generous because she was raised generously, and I barely credit her for it. **Rae** decided at nineteen to be the opposite of her mother and it cost her everything, and I credit her completely — more than Iris, which is strange, since Iris's goodness is doing more good in the world.

My own half-formed guess, for you to attack: blame tracks how much room the person had to notice reasons and respond to them. Marta had less room, Neil had all of it, Dee had none that counted, Theo had a reason of a completely different species pointed at his head, Bea had some and spent it badly. Test that against every case above, including the two pairs I can't reconcile, and repair it or replace it. I already know it doesn't account for Iris.

What I want back:

One principle, or the two or three genuine candidates, stated tightly enough that I could apply it to a character I haven't invented yet without asking you. Tell me which case forced which clause, so I can see the load-bearing parts.

Then the Strawson question, which is the real one. If my principle grades blame by a person's history, and the argument says no history is ever ultimately yours, am I just grading the thing he says can't exist? Or am I grading something else entirely — some other practice, some other notion — that his argument leaves alone? If it's something else, name it plainly, and tell me what it's for, because my novels need readers to feel blame without turning into a determinist pamphlet, and I'd like to know whether that's a legitimate want or a flinch.

Then: invent a character I didn't give you and tell me what my own commitments say about them. Pick the one that will hurt.

And finally, which of my verdicts has to go. I'd much rather you kill one than build me a machine with nine gears to save all seven. Say which one dies and what it costs me — what I'd have to stop writing.
