I've got a department colloquium in about three weeks and I'm dreading the question period a lot more than the talk. The room is mostly Kantians and people who work on practical reason and normativity, two utilitarians who will want the policy payoff, a visiting historian of nineteenth-century medicine who's sitting in, and one German Idealism guy who knows Schopenhauer far better than he knows Nietzsche. Nobody in there is a Nietzsche specialist, so their Nietzsche is undergrad-lecture Nietzsche.

Here's the thesis. The diet, digestion, climate and walking material in Ecce Homo — mainly the "Why I Am So Clever" chapter — plus the letters where the same obsessions turn up: I want to argue that this isn't confessional padding or symptom, it's a method, and the only coherant version of it. Roughly, Nietzsche treats his own body as the one instrument he can actually calibrate, runs what we'd now call an n-of-1 trial — change one input at a time, coffee, alcohol, how heavy the meal was, how far he walks, which town he winters in, and read off the output not in symptoms but in how quickly and how freely he can think — and then generalizes from that into a claim about evaluation itself: the same posture you take toward your food you take toward your morality, so a critique of morals that leaves out a physiology of the critic is incomplete. Two readings I explicitly refuse: I don't think he's reducing values to chemistry, and I don't think the diet stuff is autobiography wearing a philosophy costume. The middle position is that dietetics is where his perspectivism stops being a thesis and becomes a practice. There's a smaller claim I make in passing that I expect will annoy people: that his insistence on near-total sobriety isn't in tension with Dionysian intoxication, because Rausch for him is something you cultivate in yourself rather than something you swallow, and the regimin is the training ground for it.

Give me the prep sheet for the Q&A. The seven or eight objections this particular audience will actually raise — not the ones a Nietzsche society would raise, I can handle those — ranked by how much damage they'd do to the argument, and each phrased in the words the objector would use. I want the historian's in his register (he'll be talking about dyspepsia and neurasthenia and hydropathy and about how much of what Nietzsche recommends was boilerplate invalid advice in the 1880s, so my whole thing is unremarkable for its time), and the Kantians' in theirs (autonomy, whether you can treat yourself as an experimental subject at all, whether a claim about what's good for me can possibly be normative for anyone else). For each objection, what I actually say back in the two minutes I'll have, and then an honest line about where that reply is softest, because I'd much rather find that out now than in front of them. Tell me which two I should concede up front and get out of the way before they're turned into weapons, and which single reply has to land or the whole talk collapses. End with the question I'm praying nobody asks, why it's so bad, and what my least-bad answer to it would be.

Write it the way you'd write an email — continuous prose only, no headings, no bullets, no bold, no tables, no numbered lists. Each objection gets its own paragraph or two with the reply just following on in the same paragraph, that's all the structure I want. And no bibliography or suggested sources, please; I won't have time to read anything new before the talk anyway.
