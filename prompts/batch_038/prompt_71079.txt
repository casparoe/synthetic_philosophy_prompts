My brother-in-law Marcus coaches a semipro basketball team and him and the assistant coach Dana got into a huge argument at Thanksgiving about a roster move, and half the table took a side, so now I want an actual ruling. Please go through it move by move, not just who won overall.

Situation: they had to pick one guard. Guy A has played in a league they've tracked for years — film, verified box scores, staff who've watched him forty times. They could honestly put odds on him: call him a 12-point-a-night player. Guy B played in a league that folded, no film anywhere, and basically the only numbers anyone has are a handful of box scores his agent emailed over. Marcus picked A without much hesitation.

Dana's opener: you don't actually think A is better. If I told you B was definitely the better player you'd take B, and if I told you B was definitely worse you'd take A. So you'd take A either way, which means A just wins — your "we don't know enough about B" isn't a reason, it's a mood.

Marcus: the numbers on B aren't numbers. Somebody picked which games to send me. With A I know who kept the scorebook and I know they weren't selling me anything. When the person handing you the data profits from it, you weight the bad possibilities heavier, and I'd rather do that than pretend I have information I don't.

Dana: fine, then just say B is somewhere between 4 and 30 a night and plan for the worst of that range. You get one answer, same as with A. Acting like a range makes it undecidable is a dodge.

Marcus: no, the dodge is thinking your endpoints are honest. I don't even know whether there were games I never saw, so the range itself is made up. And besides — if I take B and he's a bust, everybody says "you passed on the guy we could measure." If I take A and B turns out to be a star, that's just luck. Those two screwups aren't the same size and you're treating them like they are.

Dana: so you're not buying insurance against losing, you're buying insurance against getting blamed. Okay, but stop calling it a basketball judgment. Also you took a flier on some nobody from a league nobody'd heard of two years ago and it worked out great, so you dont actually have the policy you're claiming.

Marcus: one time isn't a policy.

Neither budged. So: rate each move — Dana's opener, Marcus's first reply, the range thing, Marcus's blame point, Dana's closer — and say who's right about what, plus whether either one is missing something bigger that would actually settle it. And tell me how confident you are in each of those ratings, and point out the single one you're least sure about.
