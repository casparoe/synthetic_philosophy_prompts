I'm in a political theology seminar where the convenor has asked each of us to post a numbered list of our actual commitments on church and state before the next session, and then everyone else gets to attack the list. Mine is about establishment, and a friend who's a thoroughgoing disestablishmentarian glanced at it over coffee and said it doesn't cohere, but wouldn't say where. Some context: I was brought up Catholic in Bavaria and left on paper when I was nineteen, I've been in England about six years, and I'm not a member of the Church of England either — I just think the arrangement is defensible and I'd rather find the cracks myself than have them found for me in front of twelve people. Here's the list as it currently stands:

1. Establishment is recognition, not oppression. I like that the Church of England is a national church in the literal sense: the General Synod's Measures get the force and effect of an Act of Parliament once the Ecclesiastical Committee reports and both Houses approve (and Parliament can only accept or reject, not amend), there are bishops in the Lords, the monarch opens Synod. The Commons actually used that veto twice on the revised Prayer Book in 1927-28, which I regard as a feature — that's a whole country having an arguement about worship in public. A disestablished church is answerable only to its own most committed members.

2. State neutrality is impossible in principle. Every state has a calendar, public holidays, an official language, monuments, a way of numbering the years. So the only honest question about any arrangement is whether it's open to all and answerable, not whether it's neutral.

3. The German church tax is the model I actually object to. Membership sits in a federal database that employers get excerpts of for withholding purposes, the state's revenue offices do the collecting, and leaving requires a formal declaration to a state authority — so apostasy becomes a paperwork event with a fee attached, and your belief status becomes payroll information. My first employer in Berlin could tell from the data that there was no Kirchensteuer to deduct from me. That's the wrong kind of entanglement.

4. The French approach overreaches when it lands on children. The 2004 ban on conspicuous religious symbols in state schools burdens pupils in the name of a neutrality France doesn't otherwise observe: in Alsace-Moselle the state still pays clergy, the President names the Catholic bishops on the Pope's proposal, and there's religious instruction in public schools with a parental opt-out.

5. The American floor is right for any liberal state — no direct public funding of churches, no religious test for office — although I think the wall of seperation framing overstates what's actually required.

6. There's no general right to exemption from neutral, generally applicable laws. Accommodation regimes let one person's conscience become a veto over other people's interests: employees, patients, students, the registrar who wants to be excused from conducting civil partnerships. I think the courts got this right in the early nineties and the statutes that reversed it were a mistake.

So: does this hang together? I want an audit, not a lecture surveying the options. Which pairs genuinely pull against each other and which only look like they do — please don't flag everything, some of these have to be fine together and I'd like to know which. Rank the real ones by severity rather than treating them as equally bad, and for each say what has to give and what the cheapest repair costs, including anything the repair quietly gives away elsewhere on the list. Then tell me the single revision that would clean up the most, even if I won't like it. The pair I keep circling myself is 3 and 4, since both seem to be about the reach of the state's administrative machinery into belief, but I honestly can't tell whether that's one complaint or two, and I suspect I'm looking in the wrong place. I'm deliberately not saying which commitment I'd sacrifice, because I don't know yet, and I don't want the order of the list to do the arguing for me.
