Q3 (25 marks). State Sen's impossibility result for the Paretian liberal. Give the conditions imposed on the rule that goes from individual preferences to a social ranking, and show, using Sen's own two-person example involving the single copy of Lady Chatterley's Lover, exactly how those conditions are made to conflict. And here is the student's answer, more or less in full (their handwriting is bad, I've transcribed it, including what I think are their own slips):

> Sen's setup is a rule that takes a profile of individual orderings over social states and returns a social ordering. Three conditions. Unrestricted domain: any logically possible profile of individual orderings is admissible. Pareto: if every individual ranks x above y then society must rank x above y. Liberalism: individuals get protected pairs, meaning pairs of social states over which one individual's own ranking is decisive, so that if he prefers x to y then society prefers x to y, whatever anyone else thinks. The liberalism condition is meant to be extremely weak, and this is worth stressing, because it's weaker than the ordinary claim that I get to decide whether I have the salad or the burrito for lunch. That ordinary claim makes me decisive over a great many pairs of complete social states, since it says my lunch choice goes my way no matter what everyone else is doing. Sen only asks that there be one pair, in one configuration of everyone else's choices, where my ranking decides.
>
> The example. There are three states: Prude reads the book, Lewd reads the book, or nobody reads it and it's disposed of. Prude thinks the book is filth and ranks nobody reading it first, then himself reading it, then Lewd reading it, on the principle that if someone has to read it he'd rather be the censor than the censored. Lewd ranks Prude reading it first, then himself reading it, then nobody reading it, because he enjoys the book but enjoys the thought of Prude having to read it even more. Give Prude the protected pair (Prude reads, nobody reads): he prefers nobody reads, so society ranks nobody reads above Prude reads. Give Lewd the protected pair (Lewd reads, nobody reads): he prefers to read it himself, so society ranks Lewd reads above nobody reads. Now Pareto applies to the remaining pair, (Prude reads, Lewd reads), because both of them rank Prude reads above Lewd reads. So society ranks Prude reads above Lewd reads. String it together: Lewd reads above nobody reads, nobody reads above Prude reads, Prude reads above Lewd reads. A cycle, and with only two states you couldn't get one, which is why the example needs three.
>
> One thing that's easy to miss here is how much work the Pareto step is doing. It's only unanimous because Lewd ranks Prude's reading above his own. The unanimity is manufactured out of a preference that is entirely about somebody else's private business, and if you took that preference away the two liberalism rankings would just sit there without closing the loop.
>
> Responses. First, the transitivity point. What actually breaks is the transitivity of the social ordering, and transitivity is only needed if you insist the rule output a ranking. But Sen's aim is a choice, and for a choice you only need the rule to be acyclic, or even just to pick a best element. Neither Pareto nor liberalism says anything about the social ranking being transitive, so if you reformulate the whole thing as a social choice function rather than a social welfare function the Lady Chatterley case stops being paradoxical and the theorem is an artifact of the ordering formulation.
>
> Second, Nozick. Nozick's reply is a domain restriction: the paradox only bites once you let in meddlesome preferences, preferences people have about what other people do in their own protected sphere, so the liberal response is to declare those profiles inadmissible. On this reading Nozick isn't disputing what a right is, he's disputing which preference profiles count, which is why he belongs on the liberal side of the debate rather than as a critic of Sen's whole framework.
>
> There's also the contracting idea — Prude agrees to read the book in exchange for Lewd not reading it, which lands on the state both of them rank above the two that liberalism delivers, and which is pareto optimal. Sen himself offers this as the way out, so strictly speaking the result isn't an impossibility at all but a coordination failure with a known solution.
>
> My verdict: neither response works, because the first is a technicality about how you write the theorem down and the second makes liberalism depend on other people's tastes, which is exactly what liberalism was supposed to insulate you from.
