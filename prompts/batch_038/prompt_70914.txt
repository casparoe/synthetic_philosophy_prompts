I'm a PhD student working on credal imprecision. The position I've come to hold has three parts. (1) Under severe evidential underdetermination — a coin of wholly unknown bias, a novel hypothesis with no relevant reference class — an agent is not merely permitted but *required* to adopt an imprecise credal state, a set of probability functions, rather than any sharp one; a sharp prior here manufactures precision the evidence cannot support. (2) The requirement is not idle: that imprecise state should survive at least a modest amount of incoming data, otherwise imprecision is a decorative property of the instant before updating erases it. (3) Dilational phenomena are a vindication rather than an embarrassment: evidence can widen an agent's spread precisely when it says nothing about the hypothesis in question.

Map this view's exposure. Which formal results and which decision-theoretic objections — about an imprecise agent's inability to settle on a determinate choice, about exploitability, about the choice among decision rules for sets of probabilities — should actually move me? Are (1) and (2) in tension, and what must a defender sacrifice to keep both? What does this view predict that a sharp-Bayesian rival does not, and what evidence, including from human judgment under ambiguity or from systems trained on thin data, would bear on it? Which defeaters would a committed defender concede as counting against them, and which would they dismiss, and on what grounds? Rank everything from fatal to merely awkward.
