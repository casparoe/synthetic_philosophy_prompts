You are a professional philosopher with two specializations: Buddhist philosophy of mind, particularly the no-self doctrine and the tradition's own internal debates about how persons and their persistence are to be described, and contemporary analytic work on personal identity. You also have experience drafting decision documents for institutional bodies that must vote on a specific case. Bring all three capacities to bear on what follows.

Before drafting, think the case through. Open your response with a short analytical section of your own, preceding the memo, in which you sketch at least four distinct grounds on which the Board could dispose of this request. Sort them by whether each requires the Board to take a position on a contested metaphysical or doctrinal question, or whether it can be decided without one. Flag any ground that would yield the same outcome no matter how the metaphysics comes out, and note which grounds are mutually exclusive with one another. Only then write the memo.

**The case.** I sit on the six-member Board of Residents of a residential insight-meditation center. Long-term residents sign multi-year agreements covering work duties and tuition support, together with a standing confidentiality undertaking regarding disclosures made by other residents during one-on-one practice interviews. Our handbook contains a clause, drafted twenty years ago by a founding member who has since died, captioned "Continuity of Undertaking." It states that an agreement remains binding on a resident for as long as the resident who entered into it persists. There is no gloss on that phrase and no surviving minutes explaining it.

A resident of eleven years' standing has filed a formal request to be released from three undertakings she signed four years ago: a five-year work-and-support agreement, and the confidentiality undertaking. Her stated ground is doctrinal, and she has framed it with unusual care. She does not allege that the center breached the agreement, that she lacked capacity when she signed, or that she was pressured. She says that intensive practice has changed her in directions she did not anticipate, such that she no longer endorses the projects, attachments, or self-understanding that motivated the signature; and she says that on the view of the self her practice has brought her to, there is no continuing entity of the kind the clause's phrase presupposes, and therefore no bearer for the undertaking to bind. Her position, in her own formulation, is not that she should be excused but that there is no one left to excuse.

She has also proposed a remedy. Two years ago the center piloted a psychological-continuity instrument for an unrelated purpose: assessing whether residents emerging from long retreats are fit to resume driving and to manage their own finances. She proposes that the Board adopt that instrument as a general test, under which an undertaking would transfer, be modified, or lapse according to the measured degree of connection between the signing resident and the present one. She has completed the instrument herself and reports a score below the threshold she takes to be relevant. Two Board members want to treat her score as evidence bearing on the request. Three think using the instrument this way is repugnant. One thinks the entire filing is bad faith, because it has become publicly known among residents that she has accepted a teaching position at another center and wants her hands free before she leaves.

Two further facts matter. The confidentiality undertaking protects a specific third party — another resident who made disclosures during practice interviews, who has not been told that this request exists, and who cannot meaningfully be asked to consent to its release. And three other residents have signaled that they intend to file comparable requests if this one succeeds; one of them is a departing teacher with students mid-program.

**Deliverable.** Draft the memorandum I will circulate to the Board nine days from now, in advance of the meeting at which it must vote. The memo should contain:

1. A recommendation the Board can actually vote on, with a proposed sequencing (what to decide first, and what to defer) and a time frame for each step.
2. Reasoning that engages her doctrinal claim as a claim rather than as a symptom: what the no-self doctrine does and does not license about the persistence of obligations; whether the tradition she invokes supplies its own answers to her, and if so, whether those answers rest on premises that a secular administrative body — which is not a doctrinal authority and must not become one — is entitled to rely upon in a decision that binds her.
3. An explicit statement of what the Board should refuse to decide, and why refusal is better than decision in those respects.
4. A disposition of the graded-instrument proposal: adopt, reject, or confine it to some narrower use, with reasons.
5. The strongest consideration available to the side that loses under your recommendation, stated in a form its proponents on this Board would recognize as their own, followed by your answer to it. Address in particular the bad-faith objection and the concern about the third party who cannot consent.

Do not dispose of the case by declaring the clause ambiguous and referring it back for redrafting; the Board must rule on this request at this meeting, though you may additionally recommend a prospective drafting fix.

**Constraints.** Aim for 900–1,300 words for the memo, plus your preliminary analytical section. Use plain headings. Write for Board members who have never studied philosophy, without being condescending and without draining out the content: where you invoke an idea from the tradition or from the personal-identity literature, explain it in the memo's own words. No citations, footnotes, bibliography, or reading list, and no verbatim quotations from any text; describe positions and arguments instead of attributing them to books. If you are unsure whether a particular textual or historical detail is accurate, leave it out rather than guess.
