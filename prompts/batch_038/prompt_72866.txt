I am a third-year politics major, currently in a seminar on political representation, and one of nine members of my university's Committee on Student Governance. The Senate has charged us with recommending, before the end of the term, whether to replace the elected Undergraduate Assembly with a chamber selected by lot. The proposal arose from a concrete failure rather than an interest in institutional design: turnout in Assembly elections is now 9 percent of the undergraduate body; last spring 61 percent of the forty-eight seats were filled unopposed; and nineteen of the members now serving are in a third or fourth consecutive term, several of whom hold seats on two other university committees and list Assembly service on their fellowship applications. Two members of the committee want to abolish elections outright. Two want to keep them and treat low turnout as a campaigning problem. I have been asked to circulate a written opinion ahead of our meeting on the 14th, and I would like help drafting it.

Three proposals are formally on the table.

A. Full sortition. Forty-eight members drawn each September by stratified random sample from the undergraduate roll — stratified by year, school, financial-aid status, and commuter versus resident status — serving non-renewable twelve-month terms, with service as the default and a right to decline on any stated ground.

B. A hybrid. Twenty-four seats elected as now, twenty-four filled by the same sortition. Sorted members are permanently ineligible for later elected university office; elected members may serve at most two consecutive terms.

C. Elections retained, with single consecutive-term limits, mandatory renomination of every incumbent by their district, and a "none of these candidates" line that forces a fresh nomination round if it takes a plurality.

All three must survive an institutional constraint. The bylaws require that any body allocating money from the student activities fee be "accountable to the student body." The Office of the General Counsel has told us only that this test is met "where the student body retains a practical means of removing a member," and has expressly declined to say whether a sorted chamber could satisfy it. That question is ours to answer, not theirs.

I also want the memo to address three problems I have raised in committee that nobody has yet answered.

First, the refusal skew. If declining is costless, the students with the least slack — those working twenty hours a week, commuters, students with caregiving obligations — will decline at the highest rates, and the chamber that actually convenes risks being a negatively selected version of the pool we already have. What design feature, other than a payment, counteracts this, and how would we detect it if it failed?

Second, the payment dilemma. We cannot ask low-income students to serve unpaid, yet once service is paid it becomes a job with a wage, which is exactly what attracts the résumé-builders the reform is meant to displace. Recommend a specific arrangement and say what it costs.

Third, discipline without a ballot. A sorted member cannot be voted out and has no constituency to answer to. Given the counsel's formulation, what mechanism would give the student body a practical means of removal that is not an election under another name, and that does not hand a recall power to whatever minority is organized enough to use it?

The strongest objection comes from a fourth-term elected senator, and I think it is partly right, so I do not want it waved off with the observation that elected members also defer to staff. Her case is that institutional memory is what allows a student body to refuse the administration, and that a chamber of novices, redrawn every September and briefed by the same professional staff who brief the administrators, will be captured not by factions but by the bureaucracy's framing of the agenda.

Relatedly, one pro-sortition member keeps invoking the old claim, associated with Montesquieu and with Rousseau, that suffrage by lot belongs to the nature of democracy while election belongs to that of aristocracy, because voting selects the prominent and the well-connected. The memo should say whether that argument does any work on our facts or is decoration — our problem may be less that elections pick an elite than that they have stopped picking anyone.

On format: a memo to the committee, roughly 1,000 to 1,200 words, with the bottom line in the opening paragraph — which of A, B, or C, with what amendments — followed by the reasoning that gets us there, including why the runner-up is worse rather than merely different. Address the three problems with proposals a committee could vote on. Give the senator's objection a paragraph of its own. If you recommend a phased pilot, specify what would be recorded during it and what result at the end of year two should count as confirming or defeating the experiment, stated so that a future committee could apply the standard without relitigating the theory.

No citations, footnotes, or reading list. Where you gesture at real-world citizen assemblies or juries, describe them in prose and only where you are confident of the facts. And do not let the recommendation dissolve into a call for further study.
