Six months ago I sold the small haulage firm I had built up over fifteen years, and for the first time in my adult life a substantial part of my net worth is sitting under my own management with nobody else's hand on it. I have no adviser and no intention of appointing one. Instead I have spent the winter reading about stake sizing and have arrived at a position I should like pressure-tested before I commit much more capital to it. What I want is an audit, not a lecture: tell me which of the commitments below pull against one another, rank those tensions by severity, say for each what would have to give and what the cheapest revision would be, and then tell me which single revision would clean up the most.

Here is the package as I hold it.

1. My decisions concern proportional stakes in a process that compounds. What therefore matters is the long-run growth rate of my wealth, not the expected value of any individual outcome; I treat a bet's contribution to that growth rate as the only quantity I need to examine.

2. The attraction of this criterion is that it requires no taste parameter. The correct fraction is fixed by the odds and by nothing else, and two people facing the same opportunity should stake the same fraction whatever their temperaments. The standard rejoinder — that growth-rate maximisation is merely expected-utility maximisation under a logarithmic utility function, and hence as arbitrary as any other — leaves me unmoved, because on my view the logarithm is not a preference I bring to the problem but a consequence of how the wealth itself evolves.

3. In practice I stake roughly half of what the calculation recommends, and I hold a year's living expenses in cash that I treat as outside the bankroll altogether. My reason is that I could not endure a sixty per cent drawdown, even knowing that the long-run arithmetic favoured it.

4. I insure comprehensively — buildings, contents, income protection, key-person cover on the residual business — at premiums I understand to be actuarially unfavourable, and I regard this as plainly rational rather than as a concession to weakness.

5. I intend to stop at sixty-five, pass most of the pot to my children and live on the remainder. I want the sum standing at that date to be as dependable as possible, and I think of the intervening twenty years as one continuous run rather than as a series of detachable episodes.

6. On probabilities: I accept that the criterion is only as good as the estimates fed into it, and I accept that mine are systematically overconfident. I do not regard this as an objection to the criterion, and I do not shrink my estimates before using them.

7. Two verdicts. A friend offered me a one-off wager — even chances of gaining £20,000 or losing £10,000, against a net worth of roughly £400,000. I took it and think I was right to. The same friend later offered me a stake in a venture with a one-in-five chance of returning twentyfold and a four-in-five chance of losing sixty per cent of what I put in, the expected value being strongly positive. I declined, and think I was right to decline.

8. I apply the same reasoning beyond markets and wagers — to how much capital and working time to commit to a new venture, to whether to extend credit to a customer — and I do not think that is a stretch, since what carries the argument is the multiplicative structure of the situation rather than anything peculiar to gambling.

I would rather have a committed audit than a hedged survey. State plainly where you think the package breaks, and where you think a tension is only apparent, explain why it dissolves rather than listing it alongside the genuine ones. If your diagnosis turns on facts I have not supplied — the size of the pot relative to my spending, the correlation between my residual business interests and my investments, my health — flag the dependence instead of assuming a convenient value. I am not after references or a reading list, only your own reasoning. And if you think the exercise itself is misconceived, for instance that some of these items are not commitments at all but descriptions of behaviour, or that no meaningful audit is possible without those numbers, say so directly rather than producing something courteous and useless.
