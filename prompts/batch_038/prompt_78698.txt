My younger brother has been an antinatalist since graduate school, and in fifteen years of arguing with him I have never caught him out on a factual point — yet as my wife and I now face a decision of our own, I have come to suspect that his consistency does not amount to a single principle I could predict. I would like help reconstructing what he is actually committed to.

His record, as accurately as I can report it:

1. A healthy, well-off couple conceiving a first child with no known genetic risks: wrong, and not merely imprudent. He has said the verdict would not change if the child's happiness were guaranteed, since on his account the pleasures of a life never started are missed by no one, while the suffering of a life started falls on someone.
2. A couple conceiving in the knowledge that their child will probably have a short and painful life: also wrong, and, in his phrase, "not on a continuum" with case 1 — he rejects the suggestion that this is the same act at worse odds.
3. A couple who adopt or foster a child already living: not wrong, and plainly better. But he once criticized adoptive parents who present their choice as a way of sidestepping the moral problem of procreation, saying they had misunderstood why procreation is wrong.
4. Continuing a wanted pregnancy in the third trimester: a different question from deciding to conceive, because once there is a subject with a stake in things, "the arithmetic changes." Asked whether his view implies that people should end their own lives, he said no — that it makes no claim at all about those already here.
5. People who remain childless chiefly for climate reasons: right conclusion, defective reasoning; he insists the conclusion depends on no estimate of emissions.
6. A second or third child: the happiness of the children already in the family counts for nothing against it.
7. When I pressed him once on embryo selection, he said that choosing the less harmful version of an act one should not be performing is not nothing — then added, in the same conversation, that it does not make the act permissible.

Our case is this. I carry a dominantly inherited variant associated with a serious illness that typically begins in middle age; the chance of transmitting it to any given child is about one in two. My wife and I have four options: (a) conceive naturally without testing; (b) conceive through IVF with preimplantation genetic testing, transferring only an embryo that does not carry the variant; (c) adopt or enter a foster-to-adopt arrangement; (d) have no children. He knows all of this and has promised us his honest assessment rather than a diplomatic one.

Please do the following.

- Predict his verdict on each of the four options, and above all on the relation between (a) and (b): will he call selecting against the variant a genuine moral improvement, a distinction without moral significance, or a more presentable version of the same wrong? Say what he will make of (c), and whether he can prefer it to (b) without contradicting verdict 3.
- Reconstruct at least two principles that would generate his seven prior verdicts — for instance a strict asymmetry view, a view about imposing risk on someone who cannot consent, a harm-minimizing view, or something else — and state what each predicts for all four options. Then say which of them best explains the record as a whole, paying particular attention to verdicts 2 and 7, which appear to pull in opposite directions, and to verdict 5, which seems to rule something out.
- Give me your single best guess at what he will actually tell us, with a rough confidence, together with the item in his record that most supports that guess and the item that most threatens it.
- Propose one further case we could put to him — described concretely, in the manner of the seven above — that would discriminate most sharply among the candidate principles, and state what each principle predicts about it.
- Finally, if his commitments turn out not to cohere into one principle, tell us which of the four options he could not condemn without abandoning something he has already said elsewhere, and what he would have to give up to condemn each of the others.
