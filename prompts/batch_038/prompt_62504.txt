I am a junior, and I edit my school's history journal. Three years ago, after a rainy club meeting with nothing on the agenda, I started an alternate-history essay competition as a joke between six of us; it has since grown into a regional contest that drew 118 entries this year from fourteen schools. The prize is a two-hundred-dollar gift card donated by an alumna who owns a bookshop downtown, plus publication in the spring issue, which we print in an edition of two hundred. Entries are judged by twelve students and two teachers: our AP European history teacher, who thinks the whole genre is unserious and votes accordingly, and the debate coach, who rewards any essay with clean structure no matter how wild its premise. Every judge gets the same three criteria — historical plausibility, argumentative rigor, originality — and every judge reads them differently. Last year's first-prize essay was one I loved and three judges considered nonsense, and afterwards the parent of an unplaced entrant wrote to the administration asking on what basis one fourteen-year-old's essay had been ranked below another's. We had no answer except that a majority preferred it. This year I want to hand each judge an actual procedure instead of three adjectives. The constraints are strict: entries run three to six thousand words, each judge reads eight of them in a single weekend, and the procedure has to be runnable in about twenty-five minutes per essay by a sixteen-year-old with no training in philosophy or historiography, fit on one page, and end in a score we can publish alongside a stated reason. Please do not give me a bibliography or a list of works for judges to consult; nobody will read it and I have no way to chase down sources myself. Whatever the procedure rests on has to be stated inside it, in ordinary language.

Here is what the procedure has to handle, described the way I see it in the slush pile rather than in any technical vocabulary. There are essays whose starting event is perfectly reasonable but whose account of the world before that event quietly rearranges the past so the change will fit: a character in an earlier century holding beliefs, or getting hold of information, or using an institution that did not actually exist yet, and the essay never mentions that it has done this. There are essays that argue the opposite of what the form seems to want — the divergence happens, and the whole point is that the outcome was coming anyway, because several separate pressures would each have produced it or something like it on their own — and I genuinely do not know whether that is a failure to deliver consequences or the most rigorous thing an entrant can do; my two teachers disagree about it in exactly this way. There are essays where a trivial accident, a horse going lame or a clerk misfiling a letter or a storm delaying a ship by two days, is claimed to generate a confident, detailed, chapter-by-chapter chain of consequences over fifty years; I can see the case both ways, that the author has found a real causal hinge or that the author is claiming a precision no evidence could ever support, and I suspect the second much more often than the first, but I have no idea how a judge is supposed to tell them apart. There are essays where the divergence and everything downstream are selected so that the result is the world the author obviously wishes for, and I want a way for a judge to notice this that does not simply become a penalty for conclusions the judge disagrees with politically — I am worried that any test for motivated reasoning collapses into that. There are essays whose starting event could not have occurred at all given the technology, geography, or institutions of the period. And there are essays where the divergence is so early and so total that nothing in the actual past constrains what follows, the sort where a famous library does not burn and the entire subsequent history of knowledge is up for grabs, which seems to me impossible to evaluate and yet reliably produces the most beautiful prose.

Please write the procedure itself: the tests a judge runs, in the order they should be run, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure stops and assigns a low score outright, and where it hands the decision to the judge because the question is one of taste or of values rather than of anything a rubric can settle. Tell me which distinctions have to survive the simplification into a one-page sheet and which I can safely drop, and mark the steps that are really judgment calls wearing a procedure's clothes — I would rather label them than discover them during an appeal. Then run it on three entries. First: the library essay, in which the great collection survives and the essay argues that an early modern scientific transformation arrives several centuries ahead of schedule; six of my fourteen judges have already told me it is the best thing submitted this year, it is my personal favorite, and if the procedure scores it poorly I want to know that now rather than find out while defending the results. Second: an essay whose divergence is an avoided political assassination in the summer of 1914 and whose thesis is that a general European war arrives within a few years regardless, though with different participants and a different opening crisis. Third: an essay in which a misfiled telegram in the 1890s prevents a short colonial war and then, across four chapters, redrafts the alliance system of the entire following century. Finally, I want you to answer the question underneath all of this, which is whether any judge is checking anything at all beyond their own priors. If the honest answer is that the plausibility score mostly records whether the judge already thought the outcome likely, then build the procedure so it says so out loud at the relevant step instead of pretending otherwise, and tell me whether it is still worth running. Take a definite position on the contested cases rather than surveying the options — on whether the inevitability essay is being rigorous or ducking the assignment, and on whether confident long-range detail from a trivial divergence should be rewarded or penalized — and give your reasons, because my judges will ask me precisely that and a rubric containing the phrase reasonable judges may differ is worse than no rubric at all.
