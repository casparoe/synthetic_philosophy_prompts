I sit on the registration standards committee of a health regulator — the body that decides what you have to show to be *allowed* to practise, not the one that disciplines people afterwards. Our registration standard has carried the same clause since before anyone on the committee was born: applicants must be of good character, assessed via a referee letter, a self-declaration of past misconduct, and, in flagged cases, an interview where two of us score the applicant from 1 to 5 against anchored descriptions of honesty, empathy and conscientiousness.

Last month we got a petition asking us to strike it. The petitioner was turned down eleven years ago over a conviction for stealing from her then-employer, a pharmacy, during a shift. Her argument isn't mainly "I've changed" (though she says that too). It's that the clause is conceptually empty: she leans on the situationist literature, the stuff where whether a person stopped to help a stranger slumped in a doorway turned on whether they were in a hurry, swamping every personality measure the researchers had collected beforehand. If behaviour is that hostage to the situation, she says, scoring someone's "character" in a conference room is just measuring how well they present themselves in a conference room — which, she adds, correlates with class and education.

One of our members has taken this up and wants outright repeal. I've floated a replacement instead, and this is the part I want pressure-tested: stop certifying the soul and start certifying the situation. Registrants would be admitted on a safeguard-bound basis — they may only practise in settings that meet defined structural conditions (two-person handling for controlled drugs, a hard ceiling on consecutive shifts, a mandatory cooling-off before irreversible decisions, an anonymous escalation route with a response clock), plus an annual self-audit against those conditions. Character stops being a gate and becomes a feature of the environment we require the registrant to work inside.

Draft the memo I'll circulate before Thursday — recommend retain, repeal, or replace, with a concrete bottom line the committee can vote on, and criteria members could actually apply to a specific application. Take the repealer's strongest point seriously rather than strawmanning it, and do the same for the best objection to my own proposal (I already suspect it shifts risk onto employers we don't control and lets us off the hook of judging anyone). Then settle something for me: does "traits aren't robust across situations" actually get you to "so don't select for character at all"? My hunch is that step proves too much, since the same logic would gut our clinical-competence standards — skills are domain-bound too — but tell me if that's just motivated reasoning on my part. Note also that the petitioner says the theft happened on a single-staffed night during a stock shortage and that the same person on a normal shift wouldn't have done it; I want you to say whether that helps her or quietly concedes a disposition.

Don't give me a balanced menu of options — take a position and argue for it. And no reading lists or citations, I can't chase anything down before the meeting.
