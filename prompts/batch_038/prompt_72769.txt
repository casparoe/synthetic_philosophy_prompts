I attend a methods reading group run by a senior colleague who works on causal inference, and he always introduces interventionist accounts of causation through a surgical metaphor. Intervening on a variable in a structural model, he says, is like operating on a patient: the surgeon clamps and severs the vessels feeding the organ, so the organ's state is now owed to the surgeon and to nothing upstream of the clamp, while everything downstream of it goes on exactly as the anatomy dictates. He loads a good deal of weight onto the picture. Because an operation is a concrete, dated, worldly event rather than a claim about whichever possible world is most similar to ours, interventionism supposedly needs no similarity ordering over worlds and no primitive modal machinery, and that, he argues, is what makes causal talk scientifically respectable where Lewis-style counterfactual accounts are metaphysically extravagant. When a member of the group objected that the framework still leans on modality somewhere, he replied that the metaphor shows the modality is operational rather than metaphysical, and let the matter drop there.

Our group's running example makes me doubt that reply. We are working with a turnout model in which one variable is a cohort's year of birth, and my colleague is content to assert that had the cohort been born a decade later, turnout would have come out differently — an operation no one could ever perform, which he describes as a hypothetical cut made on the diagram rather than in the world. He likewise treats soft or partial interventions, and interventions on variables that are locked into deterministic relations with their neighbors, as lesser versions of the same operation; and he assumes without comment that severing the incoming arrows leaves every other equation in the system untouched.

Please audit the metaphor for me, in the following order:

1. A table of correspondences: for the surgeon, the scalpel, the vessels that are clamped and cut, the patient's pre-operative anatomy, the sterile field, the fact that the operation happens on a particular date at particular hands, and the post-operative prognosis, state what each is supposed to stand for in the interventionist framework — and say explicitly when an item has no counterpart at all rather than forcing one.

2. The disanalogies, divided into two groups: those that actually bear on his intended conclusion, and those that are merely decorative. Be explicit about why each item in the first group does damage and each item in the second does not.

3. What the metaphor quietly imports that the framework does not itself supply — in particular, whether there is a fact of the matter about the pre-operative anatomy independent of the modeler's choice of variables and their granularity; whether there is exactly one way to make the cut corresponding to a given change of value; and whether the assumption that the rest of the body keeps functioning normally while the operation is under way is concealing a substantive, contestable assumption about the system rather than a neutral feature of the picture.

4. Whether the picture survives contact with the year-of-birth variable, with hypothetical interventions no one could carry out, and with soft interventions — or whether, in these cases, the counterfactual apparatus the metaphor was meant to eliminate simply reappears, displaced onto assumptions about modularity and variable choice.

5. Given all of that, whether his conclusion holds: that interventionism gets by without modal primitives and so escapes the objection his interlocutor raised.

6. If there is a better analogy available — editing a program's assignment statement and re-running it, the randomized trial, something else you propose — name it, say what it does better, and say what it smuggles in that the surgical picture did not.

Please keep the table where a table belongs and set the final answer as a last line of the form Verdict: followed by exactly one of SURVIVES, SURVIVES-WITH-REPAIRS, or COLLAPSES, then a dash and the single disanalogy that decides the outcome.
