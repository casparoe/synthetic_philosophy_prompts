I have no academic training in philosophy — I work in freight logistics and read David Lewis's *Convention* slowly over one winter, mostly to understand why some of our depot practices change overnight while others never budge. Testing what I took from it, I built the argument below, and I cannot accept its conclusion.

(1) On Lewis's account, a convention is a regularity in what a group repeatedly does such that: everyone conforms to it; everyone believes the others conform to it; that belief gives each person a reason to conform as well; general conformity is generally preferred to slightly-less-than-general conformity; and the regularity is not the only one that could have met those conditions — hence it is arbitrary which one the group ends up with.

(2) Which side of the road a country drives on is arbitrary in exactly that way, and so is whether guests bring a dish or the host supplies everything.

(3) Now take the norms I actually care about: keep your promises, don't lie, don't take what isn't yours. Each holds because nearly everyone conforms, nearly everyone believes nearly everyone else conforms, and that belief gives each of us a reason to conform — if everybody else had stopped keeping promises, my keeping mine would only make me the one person worth deceiving. Each is further held in place by the disapproval that meets anyone who breaks it.

(4) So each satisfies every clause of (1), and each is therefore a convention in Lewis's sense.

(5) So, by the last clause of (1), each has an alternative that would have satisfied the same clauses just as well.

(6) For promise-keeping the alternative is universal treachery: a population in which nobody keeps a promise and nobody expects anyone to. It looks like an equilibrium in the same way, since no one gains by unilaterally starting to keep his word, so it satisfies the clauses too.

(7) Therefore universal treachery is, on this account, an equally good solution to the same problem, and the arbitrariness Lewis identifies is not confined to which side of the road we use but extends to whether we keep our word at all.

(8) Therefore no entrenched norm is better than the alternatives that would have sustained themselves equally well, and there is no footing from which to criticize one: the analysis cannot tell a decent norm from a merely habitual one.

I reject (8) outright. Yet (3) and (4) are the part I find most convincing, and I would hate to lose them, since the thought that norms are held up by mutual expectations is the one real insight I got from the book. So: which step do you reject, why did it look so natural to me, and what do I pay in its place? If (6) is the culprit, I want to know what precisely disqualifies universal treachery as an alternative regularity, because it still looks to me like a state nobody could improve on by moving alone — and whether that disqualification also swallows my own examples in (2).
