I am in the second term of a graduate seminar on formal epistemology, and our current unit is on logical uncertainty. For the final session our instructor is running a structured adversarial collaboration, and I have been assigned to draft the document that the two opposing camps in our cohort should be able to sign jointly. I would like you to write that document. I want the conceptual work done properly rather than a summary of recognizable positions.

The two camps, as the debate has taken shape in our seminar:

Camp A — the coherence-first camp — holds that a reasoner's uncertainty about mathematics is a species of ordinary doxastic uncertainty and should be regimented by the same norms that govern empirical belief, suitably relaxed. Its ideal is something like the logical induction criterion: a sequence of probability assignments over the sentences of a formal language, revised over time, that no polynomial-time computable betting strategy can exploit for unbounded profit, and that comes to respect deductive relations in the limit. On this view a mathematician's report that a conjecture is probably true describes a belief about arithmetic itself, and the fact that the belief cannot be made coherent all at once reflects a computational limitation rather than a difference in kind.

Camp B — the proof-first camp — holds that this inverts the order of explanation. Mathematical truth is not, on Camp B's account, the sort of thing about which an agent can bear genuine uncertainty in the way it bears uncertainty about the weather; what looks like a credence in a conjecture is really a belief about what the agent or its community will be able to establish, about what its own notation means, or about the fruitfulness of a concept. Camp B further presses an objection from feasibility: the criterion is formulated in terms of what efficient traders can do to a market, yet nothing we know how to build could play the market's side of that arrangement, and a norm no bounded agent could approximate is not an idealization but a change of subject.

The document should have four parts.

1. Agreed claims. A numbered list of substantive propositions both camps can sign — about what logical omniscience gets wrong as a model of real reasoners; about what any adequate account must explain (why confidence in unproved conjectures is graded, stable, and responsive to partial results; why deductive constraints seem to bite before the relevant proof is in hand; why an agent should sometimes pay to avoid a bet on a theorem it cannot yet prove); and about which technical premises are genuinely disputed rather than settled. Each item should carry a clause stating what the agreement costs each side. A claim both sides sign only because it is empty is worse than no claim at all.

2. A disagreements table. Columns: the disputed claim; Camp A's preferred formulation of it; Camp B's preferred formulation of it, each written so that the camp in question would recognize it as its own position; whether the disagreement is substantive or verbal; and, where substantive, the difference it makes to what an agent should compute, what prices it should quote, or what it should say about a mathematician who reports a degree of confidence. Treat the verbal/substantive column with the most care, and apply an explicit test: if the two camps issue the same recommendation in every possible decision problem, the residual dispute is not about guidance, and you should say what is left over.

3. Two worked cases, each to be handled by both camps in turn. First: an agent must record, at a fixed deadline, a number between zero and one for each of five thousand open arithmetical conjectures, to be scored later for accuracy against eventual resolution — except that most will never be resolved, and which ones do get resolved is correlated both with their being true and with their being interesting, so the scored subset is a biased sample of the corpus. What do the recorded numbers mean on each account, what norm governs their assignment, and what would constitute the agent having done well? Second: a sentence that makes a claim about the agent's own probability for that very sentence, offered as a share in the market. What should the agent do, and do the two accounts diverge in any behavior here, or only in description?

4. Cruxes. Three or four sub-questions such that settling any one of them would move at least one camp. For each: the case, study, or argument both camps accept as bearing on it; each camp's prediction, registered in advance and phrased so that it could be shown to have been wrong; and what result each camp would count as a defeat rather than as material for reinterpretation. If one of the cruxes is such that no empirical result could settle it, say so and identify what kind of argument would.

Finish with two or three weaknesses each camp concedes in its own position — not rhetorical concessions, but the places where its account is least developed.

Please do not include citations, a bibliography, or a reading list; I have the literature and want none of it reproduced here. Where a technical premise matters — whether some construction is feasible, whether deductive constraints can hold in the limit while the assignment remains computable — state it in prose and mark whether both camps accept it or one is assuming it. And resist ending on a synthesis that dissolves the disagreement. If what survives is one narrow and hard question, name it; if the honest verdict is that the two camps differ about something no study would settle, say that and explain why.
