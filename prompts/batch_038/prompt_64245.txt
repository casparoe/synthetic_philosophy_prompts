You are a formal epistemologist whose work centers on coherence arguments: the family of results that tie an agent's degrees of belief to the bets they would accept, and that treat susceptibility to a guaranteed loss as a symptom of doxastic irrationality. I want to put you to work on something narrower and harder than the usual survey. I have accumulated a set of verdicts about particular agents — reached case by case, by intuition rather than by theory — and I want to know whether they add up to a single principle, or whether some of them have to go. Treat this as collaborative reflective equilibrium: think carefully, and resist the pull toward either a principle with as many clauses as I have cases or a principle so general that it settles nothing.

Here are the cases, with my verdicts. Please refer to them by number.

1. Three horses, mutually exclusive and exhaustive. A bettor will pay 0.40 for a $1 ticket on each of them. She loses 0.20 whichever horse wins. Verdict: irrational, no hesitation.

2. Same race. A different bettor will pay 0.30 for a ticket on each horse, but she will never sell a bet: she would rather forgo a gain than risk a loss, and she says so openly. At her own prices, she comes out 0.10 ahead whichever horse wins. Verdict: not irrational. Over-cautious, unambitious, but not incoherent.

3. A mathematician is certain of the axioms of her system and assigns 0.6 to a theorem that follows from those axioms by a proof she has not yet found. She has never entertained the axioms and the theorem together. Verdict: not irrational.

4. Same numbers, but now she has all three contents in view simultaneously — the axioms, an inference she accepts as trivially valid, and the conclusion — and still reports 0.6. Verdict: irrational.

Note the twist that bothers me: a bookie who is allowed to choose which propositions to offer bets on can exploit case 3 exactly as easily as case 4, simply by putting the conjunction on the table. Yet I do not want to say that the mathematician in case 3 is irrational merely because some sufficiently inventive bookie exists. So exploitability by a proposition-choosing bookie cannot be the criterion — unless you can explain to me why it can be after all.

5. I announce in advance that when the assay result comes back I will set my credence in the hypothesis to 0.5 whichever way it comes. A bookie who knows this sells me one bet before the test and another after it, both at prices I would accept, and takes a profit however the coin of nature lands. Verdict: irrational. I should have planned to conditionalize.

6. The coin-and-awakening experiment: a fair coin is tossed; if heads I am woken once, if tails I am waked twice, with a drug erasing my memory of the earlier awakening. On Sunday I will pay 0.50 for a $1 bet on heads; once awake I will pay only 0.33. A bookie who knows my schedule can trade against both prices, and on at least one way of setting up the book he comes out ahead whichever way the coin fell. Verdict: not irrational. But do not take my word for the arithmetic here. Check whether the book really guarantees him a profit once the double awakening is handled honestly; if it does not, say so, and say what would have to change for the exploitation to go through.

7. My credences about a proposition on which no one could ever bet — the number of grains of sand on a particular beach in 1602, say — are 0.9 that it is even and 0.9 that it is odd. Verdict: irrational all the same, even though the incoherence is out of reach of any book, actual or conceivable.

8. An agent who declines every bet with positive expected value at her own credences, one at a time, each refusal locally unobjectionable. No book can be made against her, since she accepts nothing. Verdict: irrational, or at any rate seriously defective.

Cases 2 and 8 are the pair I cannot reconcile: both involve an agent who forgoes favorable trades, neither can be booked, and yet I have opposite verdicts. If your principle has to buy one of them at the cost of the other, tell me which one you are buying.

9. An agent with merely finitely additive credences over the natural numbers — uniform, in the sense of treating every number symmetrically, which no countably additive probability can manage. Any finite collection of bets she would accept leaves her unexploitable, but a bookie permitted to offer countably many bets, each individually acceptable to her, can guarantee her a loss. Verdict: not irrational. The infinite book is the strange thing here, not the agent.

My tentative principle is this: an agent's doxastic state is irrational exactly when some bookie, permitted to offer any finite collection of bets the agent would accept at their own announced prices and permitted to choose which propositions to wager on, can ensure the agent a net loss in every possible outcome. I suspect it is wrong — it looks to misfire on 3, 6, 7, and 8 — but I would like it repaired if it can be repaired, and told plainly why it cannot be if it cannot.

What I want from you, in order: the principle you would actually defend, stated precisely enough that I could apply it to a new case on my own without coming back to you, including the idealizations it builds in and what it says about agents of bounded computational power; which of my verdicts motivates each of its clauses, so that I can see what work each clause is doing; your check on the arithmetic in case 6, and on case 2 if you disagree with me about how one-sided betting policies behave; your verdict on a case I have not listed, namely an agent whose belief about a proposition is not a number but a range — say 0.3 to 0.7 — and who will accept a bet at any price inside that range, where I want to know whether it matters that she is offered the bets one at a time rather than as a package she can evaluate as a whole; one case of your own construction on which your principle and the most serious rival diverge, with your ruling and the reason for it; an explicit statement of which of my verdicts you would overturn, and, if you were forced to a principle of at most three clauses, which verdicts you would sacrifice and which you would keep; and finally your answer to the question that worries me most, which is whether what survives is still a Dutch book argument at all. If the criterion ends up keyed to coherence whether or not any book can be constructed, has the betting apparatus done any work, or is it decoration on a norm that could have been stated without it?

Two constraints on the reply. First, I want your reconstruction, not the literature: no references, no reading list, no names of papers or authors, and no suggestion of what I should go look up. Second, write it as continuous prose, the way you would write a long letter to a colleague — no headings, no bullet points, no bold or italicized signposting, no tables, no numbered sub-sections, even though my cases are numbered. Refer to the cases by number inside ordinary sentences. Take as much room as the argument needs, but if one of my cases is technically confused rather than merely contentious, say so plainly and move on rather than working around it.
