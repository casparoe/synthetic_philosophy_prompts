Here's my package. I'm writing it up as a position statement for our evolution-and-philosophy reading group and the rest of them are hostile, so I want to know exactly where it gets torn apart. Be adversarial, not diplomatic.

1. I'm a pluralist about the units of selection. Genes, individual organisms, and — in some cases — groups can each be genuine units, and which one is salient is a fact about the case, not about the description you picked. There's no single correct way to carve selection up.

2. I take the Price-style decomposition into a between-group and a within-group component to be exactly true and exactly worthless on its own. It's arithmetic. It's bookkeeping. It can't tell you what caused what, and I reject any argument that infers "group selection is operating" purely from the sign or size of one of those terms. If you want causes you need to go outside the decomposition — contextual analysis, or an interventionist reading of which differences actually made the difference.

3. I draw a hard line between the two flavours of multilevel selection: the one where "group fitness" is just an aggregate of the individual-level characters of the members, and the one where groups are themselves reproducing entities with fitnesses of their own. The first is common; the second is rare. And only the second promotes a group from a mere aggregate to a genuine Darwinian individual.

4. Hamilton's rule is universal. For any trait, in any interaction structure, you can write down a valid rb > c, and when it holds, the evolution of the trait is fully explained by the trait's effects on related bearers. So group selection is never an extra causal force — at best it's a redescription of something already captured by relatedness.

5. But I don't think you can bootstrap a new level of individuality out of relatedness reasoning alone, and the major transitions are the case that proves it. Going from a mere collection of cooperating individuals to a group that is an individual requires that within-group selection be suppressed, and my view is that the suppression is driven by something happening at the group level. This is the commitment I like most and the one I'd fight hardest to keep.

6. Metaphysically I'm a replicator/interactor person in the classic Dawkins-and-Hull sense: only replicators have the whole longevity-plus-fecundity-plus-copying-fidelity package, and groups don't have it. Groups can absolutely be interactors, though, and that's where all the interesting selection happens.
