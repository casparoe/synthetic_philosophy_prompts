I am teaching an upper-level undergraduate seminar on British empiricism — fourteen students, a single 100-minute session, all of whom will have read the *Principles of Human Knowledge* and the first of the *Three Dialogues between Hylas and Philonous* before they walk in. The session is devoted to Berkeley's master argument: the challenge to conceive of some object existing unperceived and unconceived, together with the claim that the challenge cannot be met. When we discussed the reading last week, the class arrived almost unanimously at the objection that Berkeley wins by equivocation — that in claiming to conceive of a tree standing in a wood with nobody about, one conceives of the tree, not of the tree *as unperceived*, and that Berkeley quietly inserts himself into every putative case. One student summarized it as "he defines the word so that he cannot lose," and the room, I included, nodded along. I have since come to think that dismissal is too cheap, but I do not want to announce that from the front of the room. What I want them to discover is that the equivocation objection, even where it is correct, does not get them off the hook: they still owe an account of what exactly they take themselves to be conceiving, and of what could ever confirm that they have succeeded.

Here is the seed I have been sketching on index cards, which you should develop, repair, or discard if you can think of something better. The seminar becomes a game in which I serve as gamemaster describing a house and its contents — students may investigate any room, drawer, cupboard, or garden shed they like, in any order, and I answer only what is actually asked. Unknown to them, some items are governed by sealed envelopes I prepare in advance containing a written description, while others are governed by blank slips instructing me to improvise something consistent with everything already established. So for some items there was a determinate fact about the contents of the drawer before anyone asked, and for others there was not; if I play well, the answers they receive are indistinguishable in kind. Midway through, teams are given a fixed budget of some non-monetary resource and must write down, in advance, a test protocol they believe will settle whether the house was stocked beforehand or rendered on demand. At the end they vote on which envelope governed which request, and we open the envelopes. My expectation is that they will score at chance, and that the moment they realize their own notes contain nothing that could have settled the question is the moment the argument lands: every test they devised turned out to be a way of making something perceived.

What I need from you is the whole thing in runnable form, so please supply: the complete rules for the investigation phase (how questions are posed and answered, how much the gamemaster may volunteer unprompted, whether students act individually or in teams, and whether anyone has a special role); the actual contents of the envelope set, which I would like you to write out as roughly twenty item cards mixing pre-stocked descriptions and blanks, with a note on which items are obvious first targets for a suspicious group, which will likely go untouched, and how to distribute the blanks so that the regime cannot be inferred from the accident of which items happened to be asked about; the token economy, including a scoring scheme for the protocol-writing phase and a payoff for the final vote that rewards honesty about one's own ignorance rather than merely being right; and a minute-by-minute timeline for the 100 minutes, marking exactly where I reveal nothing at all.

The debrief is the part I most need help with, so please be thorough there. I want an explicit mapping from moments in the game onto components of the argument: the challenge itself; the failed attempts to meet it; the equivocation objection and the precise point at which it bites; the further demand that one conceive the object *as* unperceived; and Berkeley's own exits — the appeal to a permanent perceiver, the distinction between ideas and spirits, and his insistence that ordinary objects lose nothing by all this. Fold in a short conceptual audit in which students write a single sentence describing an object existing unperceived and a second group hunts for the smuggled perceiver in it, but design that exercise as a fair test of the objection rather than a humiliation, since at least two of my students are sharp enough to argue that a description can be determinate without being perceiver-involving. Then tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this and how to recover: what happens if I improvise inconsistently and am caught, which yields a false positive and may convince them they have refuted something they have not; if a student flatly refuses to engage with the fiction; if the investigation phase overruns; if one team produces a genuinely clever protocol in five minutes and the rest of the session deflates. Be equally frank about what the game cannot reproduce and what I will have to supply by hand — I already worry that the gamemaster is a human mind whose existence nobody in the room doubts, that the envelopes are physical objects sitting on a table we are all perceiving, and that the exercise is a contained fiction rather than a thesis about everything. Finally, address one risk head on: the activity drives naturally toward the conclusion that talk of unperceived objects is unverifiable and therefore empty, which is a verificationist moral and emphatically not Berkeley's, since he thinks the tree in the quad is perfectly real and goes on existing. Tell me how to steer the debrief so that they land on Berkeley's actual claim rather than the twentieth-century lesson they are likelier to reach for — or, if you judge that the activity cannot be steered that way, say so and redesign it.
