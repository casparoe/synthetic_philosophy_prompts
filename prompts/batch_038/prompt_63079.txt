I'm on sabbatical this semester and, for reasons I still don't understand, I've been cc'd on a long email thread between two people who sit on our city's planning commission — call them Ruth, who teaches property law over at the law school, and Dave, who's been a city planner here for twenty-odd years. They were arguing about a proposal that one of Dave's colleagues floated for the mid-rise that's been clawing its way through permiting on the old rail yard site for three years now. The proposal, roughly: instead of another open-mic public comment night, the city issues, say, 100 "objection tokens" to households within a quarter mile of the site, each token worth a fixed dollar amount. Holders can either spend them — every N tokens spent buys a month of delay or shaves a floor off the approved height — or sell them back to a city trust that funnels the money into a rent-assistance fund for that same neighborhood. The developer gets to build at full size only if it buys back enough tokens.

Here's how the thread went, compressed but not strawmanned, I hope:

1. Ruth: what's broken about hearings isn't the talking, it's that objecting is free. A costless veto gets used whether or not anything real is behind it. Tokens make objectors state how much they actually care by giving up cash they'd otherwise receive, and the cash goes to renters — the people genuinely hurt when the neighborhood gets more expensive. So it pays the real victims and charges the people whose complaint is about parking and sightlines.

2. Dave: that turns a political right into a commodity and makes the outcome track wealth. In a rich neighborhood nobody sells and nothing gets built; in a poor one everybody sells and anything gets built. It's a machine for reproducing the pattern we already have, except laundered as voluntary exchange — and the rent fund is the laundering: you hand renters a lump sum today for an increase that hits them every month for a decade.

3. Ruth: the wealth objection has it backwards. The tokens are a free endowment to whoever lives there, renters included. If a household sells, it's telling you the money matters more to it than the objection, and I don't see on what grounds a planner overrides that. Nobody's worse off than before: they started with nothing.

4. Dave: they didn't start with nothing, they started with a hearing, and those aren't the same thing. And the sale is a coordination trap — one renter taking $400 is individually sane and changes nothing, but once 60% have sold, the block goes up and every remaining renter is priced out with $400 in her pocket. It's flood insurance in reverse: individually rational exits that destroy the thing the exits depend on. You can't run a market in tokens when the value of holding depends on other people not selling.

5. Ruth: fine — that's a design bug with a design fix. Put the tokens in a neighborhood trust so no individual can sell; or set an unsold floor before any development right is released; or aggregate at a supermajority. If the fix is cheap, the objection can't be doing the work of a refutation. You've just told me which parameters to set.

6. Dave: at which point you've rebuilt the hearing with extra steps. You still have to decide who's in the trust, what a token is worth, and what counts as a spendable objection — except now those are litigable valuation fights with the developer's lawyers on one side, instead of a political argument about what the neighborhood should be. And some objections aren't priced and shouldn't be. The assisted-living facility on the corner that goes into permanent shadow at six stories: that isn't a preference to be aggregated against $400 checks, its a claim about harm.

7. Ruth: the hearing lets the loudest and best-organized people decide what counts as a claim, and a lot of what gets said under the headings of "shadow" and "character" is a neighborhood announcing that it doesn't want to live near people who rent. Pricing disciplines pretext, because a pretextual objection doesn't survive being paid for out of the objector's own compensation. If the assisted-living home's objection is as genuine as you say, put a price on it and watch it get funded — under your system it has to outshout the homeowners instead.

8. Dave: that's an argument for exposure, not for sale. Make objectors file what they're objecting to, limit the grounds to what the code actually recognizes, and publish who filed. Sunlight does the anti-pretext work without turning standing into an asset. And underneath all of it, your scheme concedes the thing in dispute: it assumes the entitlement starts with the neighbors, that they own a veto the developer has to purchase from them. A developer should have to persuade the polity, not buy off the block. Once you accept they're the sellers, everything after that is just haggling over price.

So — go through this move by move and tell me who's right at each one. I want ratings, not a shrug: clearly right, mostly right, a wash, mostly wrong, clearly wrong, and a sentence or two of justification each. Pay special attention to (3): is Ruth's claim that no one is worse off than the status quo actually available to her once (4) and (6) are on the table, or does it quietly swap the hearing for the token as the baseline? And tell me what you make of the (5)-(6) exchange specifically — is Dave's coordination objection a refutation or a parameter recommendation, and if it's the latter does Ruth get to bank that as a win? Then give me an overall verdict on who comes out ahead, and finish with what you think both of them are missing, since I suspect there's at least one thing neither touches. I have my own view, obviously, but I'm deliberately not telling you which side I leaned toward — please don't try to guess, just adjudicate what's actually on the page.
