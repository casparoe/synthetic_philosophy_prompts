I'm on a small team building an agent that handles after-sales stuff for an e-commerce company — issuing refunds, disputing chargebacks, updating shipping addresses. It does about 40,000 actions a day in the pilot, and last month we had an incident where a customer's inbound email contained text that got the agent to issue a $2,300 refund it had no business issuing. Were now arguing about what the acceptance criteria for the next version should be, and two of my colleagues, Kenji and Alina, have been at it for three days. I got asked to write up the summary and I genuinely cant tell who's winning, so I'd like you to referee the exchange move by move.

(1) Kenji wants a hard worst-case bound in the spec: no input sequence, however wierd, can get the agent to move more than $50 of value it isn't authorized to move, and that bound has to be checked before we ship. His argument is that our input distribution isn't fixed — customers, competitors, and bored teenagers actively probe the system — so any average taken over last quarter's traffic is measuring the wrong thing, and the tail is exactly where an adversary lives. (2) Alina says that's a guarantee about a mathematical object we don't have: the bound would hold for a model of the agent and the environment, and the thing we actually deploy is a huge learned policy whose behavior in the gap between model and reality is unbounded by construction, so we'd be buying the appearance of rigor. She adds that a genuine worst-case bound over all inputs forces the agent to refuse almost everything, and refusals are harms too — nobody counts the fraud victim whose money sits in limbo for nine days as free. (3) Kenji concedes he can't get worst-case over all possible worlds but says that's not what he's asking for: worst-case relative to a threat model you write down explicitly. His claim is that the difference between them isn't rigor, it's honesty — the average-case number hides its assumptions inside a historical distribution, while his bound states them on the page. (4) Alina says once you grant that, the dispute collapses into bookkeeping, and in practice it's a continuum: robust optimization with some budget on how far the world is allowed to drift, which is average-case with extra knobs. She also says if you minimize the maximum loss without weighting it against the cost of over-refusing, you'll land at the wrong end of that continuum every time, and the wrongness won't show up in any metric we own, because those losses are borne by customers and support staff rather than by us. (5) Kenji's last move is that a catastrophic outcome isn't just a big number inside an average — it's irreversible, and it can destroy the thing we're optimizing, namely the company's permission to run an agent like this at all — so an expected-loss target licenses the system to trade a tiny probability of that away for a pile of ordinary gains, which is precisely the trade we don't want a machine making on its own behalf.

So: go through (1)–(5) and give me a verdict on each — clearly right, mostly right, split, mostly wrong, clearly wrong — with a sentence or two of justification per rating, not just a score. Then tell me where each of them is landing a real point and where they're talking past each other (I suspect part of this is verbal but I can't say which part), whether the disagreement would change if the agent were only advising a human rather than acting, and what the two of them are both missing. Please write in American English, and don't send me off to read papers — I'm on a plane with no wifi and I just want the reasoning laid out.
