I run a methods reading group for grad students and this week we're doing Friedman's positive economics essay — specifically the move everyone calls the F-twist, where "assumptions don't have to be true, only the predictions do" slides over into "and anyway any inaccuracy in the assumptions must be irrelevant, because if it were relevant the predictions would have failed." I want to loosen the room up before the argument starts, so I'd rather open with something funny than with a summary.

Write me a parody: an internal editorial memo, about 600 words, from a fictional journal announcing that starting with the next volume every submission must ship with a certified Assumption Unrealism Statement. Invent the journal and the editor, no real names anywhere.

What I need in it:

- A scoring rubric. Papers get an Unrealism Score; a low score is grounds for desk rejection, and a model whose assumptions turn out to be broadly accurate gets sent back with instructions to weaken them. Include the appeals process and roughly how long it takes.
- A tagging requirement. Every assumption has to be declared as one of three kinds: a claim about the domain the model applies to, a bit of scaffolding used in the derivation and then thrown away, or a claim that some factor left out of the model has a negligible effect. The memo should treat the tagging as though it settles every dispute, permanently.
- At least two worked example assumptions scored on the rubric, drawn from the usual suspects: profit maximization by firms who've never done any such calculation, an expert billiard player who shoots as if he'd solved the equations for the optimal path, leaves distributed around a tree as if each one had picked the sunniest spot, perfect information, zero transaction costs.
- A short FAQ at the end where a nervous author asks the obvious things — but surely the assumption has to be roughly right where it matters? what if the errors don't cancel? can I get credit for an assumption that's true but extremely detailed? is there a minimum number of assumptions? — and the editor's answers are cheerful, procedural, and quietly concede each point while insisting nothing has been conceded.
- A final paragraph applying the whole policy to the memo itself.

The humour has to come from the methodology being gotten right, not from economists being stupid. The editor isn't a villain or a fool: he's sincere, he thinks he's protecting the discipline from untestable metaphysics, and he never notices that his own rubric says both that descriptive accuracy is irrelevant and that accuracy in the relevant range is required. Don't let anyone in the memo say twist, fallacy, question-begging, circular, or smuggle in. Nobody realises anything. A methodologist reading it should wince in recognition, and the last line should make them go oh, it applies to itself too.

Deadpan bureaucratic register throughout. No citations, no reading list, no invented page numbers — everything the piece needs should be inside the piece.
