I am a senior analyst at a public-policy research institute, and I have been asked to prepare an internal briefing on a proposal one of our funders has floated: that part of our country-risk work should be built on what its advocates call quantitative history. My training is in economics; I have read a fair amount about institutional persistence, but I have never worked with the cliodynamics literature and I cannot yet tell how seriously to take it. In preparing, I read a long review essay in a general-interest magazine — written by someone who plainly reads widely and is not a crank — whose summary of the field I found persuasive on a first pass, and then, on a second pass, was unable to locate the weak points in. Here is the passage in full:

"Peter Turchin's cliodynamics is essentially Thomas Malthus with better statistics. Its central building block is the secular cycle: in an agrarian society, population grows until it presses against the food supply, real wages fall, the tax base shrinks, and the state slides into fiscal crisis; the resulting breakdown of order runs its course over two or three centuries before the whole thing starts again, and elite numbers swell along with everyone else's, so that a surfeit of ambitious men with too few offices to go around finishes off what hunger began. Because the same forces are said to operate in modern economies — only now the pressure shows up as wage stagnation rather than famine — Turchin was able to announce in Nature in 2010 that the United States would enter a period of serious political turmoil around 2020, and everything that has happened since has borne him out. The methodological lesson he draws is a hard one: history obeys laws, and given enough data one can forecast not merely the direction of the next crisis but its timing. His more recent books apply the cycle to the present, arguing that the United States has been in a disintegrative phase since the 1970s — stagnating wages, exploding elite numbers, mounting public debt — and warning that unless elite overproduction is brought down, that phase has a long way left to run. Historians, unsurprisingly, are not impressed. They point out that the Malthusian engine driving the model was falsified two centuries ago, when the Industrial Revolution decoupled living standards from population size; that a cycle of two or three centuries yields at most one or two observations per society, so no statistical test applied to it can have any power whatever; and that a theory retrofitted to events that have already happened is not a forecast but a story. If the demographic mechanism does not operate in modern economies, and if the predictions cannot fail, then cliodynamics is astrology with regression coefficients."

What I want from you is an accuracy audit I can act on — not a defense of the field and not a takedown of it. Please take on the role of a historical sociologist who builds formal models of long-run political instability and who knows this literature from the primary sources rather than from press coverage. Think carefully before answering, and begin with an explicit brainstorming step in which you decompose the passage into its discrete factual and interpretive claims — I expect there are at least a dozen — before you evaluate any of them. Then work through that list one claim at a time. For each, tell me: whether a working cliodynamicist would accept it as stated, regard it as a fair popular simplification, or regard it as a misrepresentation of the underlying theory; what the accurate version of the claim is, in your own words; and how much the distortion matters, that is, whether getting it wrong changes what the theory actually predicts. For what it is worth, my own instinct is that the second half of the passage is correct — I am skeptical that two-century cycles can be tested at all — but the phrase "elite overproduction" keeps recurring in the technical material I was sent, and I cannot tell whether it names a genuinely distinct mechanism or is simply Malthus in a new vocabulary. I would like that particular question settled rather than left open.

After the claim-by-claim pass, please treat the passage's two concluding moves as arguments rather than as assertions. First: does the Malthusian objection — that living standards decoupled from population after the Industrial Revolution — actually dispose of the model, or does it strike only one component of it? Second: does the charge that a long-cycle theory retrofitted to events that have already occurred is not a forecast amount to anything, given what the passage itself said earlier about the 2010 prediction? For each, state what a historian or a statistician would have to show for the objection to land, whether the passage shows it, and whether the objection is consistent with the rest of the essay. If the passage is in tension with itself, say precisely where.

Finally, give me a bottom line of about a paragraph: which sentences I could repeat in my briefing without embarrassment, which I should rewrite and roughly how, and which claims about the theory's forecasting ambitions I should avoid making in either direction. Three constraints on your answer. I am not asking for a bibliography, a reading list, or citations — I cannot check them and I do not want invented ones — so reason from the structure of the theory rather than from references. Where your recall of a specific number, date, or title is shaky, say so explicitly instead of papering over it. And do not be deferential to the essayist: if a sentence is unfair to the field, say so plainly, and if it gets something right that the field's critics routinely get wrong, say that too.
