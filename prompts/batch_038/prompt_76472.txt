I have spent fifteen years as a time-series analyst — first forecasting macroeconomic aggregates at a central bank, and for the past eight years building models that project physical-climate variables decades forward for a reinsurer. My working vocabulary is filters and spectra, stationarity and unit roots, structural breaks and regime shifts, impulse responses, signal-to-noise ratios, and the whole apparatus for separating what persists from what merely passes. Three months ago I was made co-lead, alongside an environmental historian, on the project that has to defend our choice of reference period for long-horizon flood and heat projections. She has advanced an argument I cannot yet answer in my own terms, drawn from Braudel's The Mediterranean and the Mediterranean World in the Age of Philip II and its three time-scales: a near-motionless history of human beings and their environment; a slower, cyclical history of economies, populations, and societies; and event-based history at the scale of individuals. Her contention is that our standard practice — estimating a design baseline from a thirty-to-fifty-year record, treating departures from it as deviations to be filtered out, and reporting extremes as tail probabilities — presupposes a world in which the third scale is noise around a stable trend, and that this is precisely the picture Braudel's first two scales deny.

She has also pressed a sharper claim that I would like you to take seriously rather than dismiss: that an archive has a sampling rate, and that a record's resolution determines which time-scales it can represent at all. A port ledger, a sediment core, and a satellite series each pre-filter the past differently, so what counts as an event is a property of the instrument as much as of the world.

Please restate her position as faithfully as you can in my vocabulary — decompositions, low-pass filtering, non-stationarity, unit-root versus trend-stationary models, break detection, proxy resolution, error spectra. Then give me the residue report. Which of her claims translate into technical statements I could write down and test? Which translate only by being distorted, and what exactly is the distortion? Which have no expression whatsoever in the framework? I want you to resist the temptation to render her claims as positions my field already holds and then report a clean mapping; if the honest translation is lossy, tell me where and why.

Finally, tell me what to make of the residue. Is it evidence that my vocabulary is missing something real about how slow structures and events actually relate, or evidence that her claims are less determinate than they sound? And is there any part of her argument that survives as a genuine, checkable objection to how we choose reference periods — as opposed to a temperamental difference between two disciplines?
