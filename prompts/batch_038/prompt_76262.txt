I'm putting together the final for an upper-level political philosophy course on corruption and I need one essay question I can't grade on autopilot. Here's the case I want it built on — it's invented, so don't go hunting for a real one:

A district irrigation office allocates canal turns in the dry season. The formal rule, laid down when the state took the office over, is a lottery on order of application. In practice the water master takes a customary contribution from each household at harvest — a fixed measure of grain, or a season's use of a plough team, or his kids' school fees covered out of a village fund — and contributing households get moved up the queue. Local rule caps the contribution at roughly the value of the household's own water share and it can't be converted to cash, so the water master ends up no better off than his neighbours. The post is part hereditary and is understood locally as a trust over the commons, though the state now pays him a small salary and counts him as a civil servant. The whole thing is public: the schedule of contributions is posted, everyone knows who paid and who moved up, and there's an appeal to the elders. The lottery is used by a handful of households, is known to be fixed by whoever runs the draw, and produces less reliable delivery and lower harvests. But the households too poor to make even the capped contribution, and recent out-migrants outside the customary cycle, lose their turns. A donor-funded reform program classifies the practice as corrupt and makes aid conditional on abolishing it; the village council says that's a foreign norm about the public/private split being imposed on them.

The question has to make students say whether this is corruption, and — this is the crux — what would have to be true of the concept of corruption for their answer to be determinate rather than a stipulation. I want them to hit the fact that the stock definition, abuse of a public office for private gain, gives contradictory verdicts here depending which words you lean on: is a part-hereditary trust a public office, is a capped non-convertible grain payment private gain, and does it matter that the compliant alternative is worse for everyone? And whether the council's objection and the donor's classification can both be right, or whether one of them is confused. It shouldn't be answerable by reciting the gift-versus-bribe literature: nameless nods to Mauss and the anthropology of reciprocity earn nothing, and I don't want students dropping citations.

Deliverables: the question exactly as students would see it, with the case written up tightly and the constraints stated (75 minutes, 30 marks); a model answer of about 750 words; and a marking scheme. The model answer should commit to a verdict on the case rather than sheltering behind it-depends-which-definition-you-adopt, and the marking scheme should be equally definite — spell out which verdicts are creditworthy and which lose marks no matter how well argued, and seperate an A from a B by naming the specific moves each has to make (my hunch is the B applies the definition and catalogues the tensions, while the A says something about what work the concept is doing and whether the dispute with the council is substantive or verbal). Then give me the four or five answers you'd expect to see most often in the pile and what each one costs.

Start straight with the exam question as the students would see it — no preface, no restating the brief, and no closing note about how you'd tweak it.
