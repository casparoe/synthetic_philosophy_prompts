A retired anesthesiologist, Dr. Vance, sits on our hospital's ethics committee and is candid that she has no moral views of her own: on each contested case she adopts the conclusion of a philosopher whose work she reads but cannot defend. Two other members argued about her at length after last month's meeting.

(1) Alan said Dr. Vance's committee votes are not moral judgments at all. When she votes that continuing dialysis is not in the patient's interest, she has at most a true belief: testimony of this kind cannot transmit knowledge or understanding, and someone who cannot say why a conclusion holds does not know why it holds.

(2) Bea said this is special pleading. We treat a GP who follows a nephrologist as knowing that dialysis is indicated, and if Alan's argument worked, her belief would fail too. She added that whatever is supposed to make moral testimony peculiar gets no grip here: the philosopher is, by stipulation, reliably right, and if moral facts obtain independently of anyone's grasp of them, there is nothing further that Dr. Vance could be missing.

(3) Alan said the disanalogy is that the GP defers about a conclusion while grasping the reasons bearing on it — she knows what dialysis does and why it helps this patient — whereas Dr. Vance grasps nothing of the moral reasons, only the verdict. He added that even when she votes correctly she is not praiseworthy, since she acts because an authority says so rather than because of the features of the case that make it right, and praiseworthy action requires the latter.

(4) Bea said that standard would strip praise from most ordinary moral life, since nearly all of us act on beliefs about, say, the wrongness of factory farming that we could not defend under questioning; and she said Dr. Vance may deserve more credit than her critics, because she has deliberately subordinated her own judgment to someone better placed to judge, and would do so even when the conclusion offends her instincts — which looks like epistemic humility rather than a vice.

(5) Alan's closing move was that this is precisely the vice: a standing policy of deferring regardless of content is not humility but abdication of the responsibility her vote carries, and if she would defer even to a conclusion she found monstrous, her being right is accidental to her, so her vote should not count in a deliberative body at all.

Neither budged. Please adjudicate the exchange move by move: for each of the five, say whether it is right, partly right, or mistaken, and justify the rating; then say who is more right overall and identify anything important that both of them missed.
