Suppose a homicide case in which the only evidence against the defendant is a DNA match produced by trawling a large database of stored profiles. There is no prior connection to the victim, no motive, no witness; his profile simply surfaced when the database was searched. In most courts the jury hears a single statistic: the random match probability, the chance that an unrelated person drawn at random from the population would coincidentally share the profile — perhaps one in a million. The number of profiles that were searched is usually not mentioned at all.

Forensic statisticians disagree about whether that is right, and they disagree in both directions. One camp holds that the figure should be multiplied by the size of the database, on the ground that when a million profiles are compared, a coincidental match is to be expected even if the true source of the crime-scene sample is nobody in the database. The other camp holds that this is a confusion: a search that returns exactly one match has eliminated a million innocent people, so a defendant identified by trawl is in a worse position than a defendant already suspected on independent grounds who then happens to match, and his match should carry at least the weight it would have carried had he walked into a police station and volunteered a sample.

I want this treated as a question in the epistemology of self-locating evidence rather than as a technical exercise. Recall the anthropic distinction: the self-sampling assumption says to reason as though you were a random sample from the observers in your reference class, while the self-indication assumption says to update additionally on the bare fact that an observer like you exists, which systematically favors hypotheses containing more observers.

1. Reconstruct the implicit rule the unadjusted practice follows. What does it presuppose about which reference class governs the statistic, and about which features of the situation are admissible as things to conditionalize on? Does it treat the fact that this man was located by a search procedure as evidence, or as epistemically irrelevant — and which rival accounts of evidence does that choice quietly rule out?

2. Does either camp amount to a self-indication-style adjustment, in which the size of the candidate pool is allowed to do evidential work independently of the particular match? And is the "multiply by N" recommendation, pushed to its limit, the same error the presumptuous philosopher commits — reading a conclusion about how the world must be populated off the mere fact that the search found somebody — or is the disanalogy decisive?

3. Which camp gets the reasoning right? Be explicit about what each side is conditioning on, and say whether the disagreement is really about probability or about the question the jury is being asked.

4. Is the practice coherent with the rest of the same legal system's epistemology: its warnings against the prosecutor's fallacy, its refusal to convict on naked statistical evidence (the gatecrasher-style hypotheticals where the statistics alone make it more likely than not that the person in the dock did the thing, yet everyone agrees a verdict on that basis would be improper), and its insistence that a defendant be tried for what he did rather than for what people resembling him tend to do? Does the cold hit survive those commitments, or is it an exception that undermines them?

5. Separate what the practice strictly presupposes from what it is merely consistent with, and mark where it is philosophically underdetermined — where incompatible theories of evidence would license the very same instruction to the jury.

6. Finally: if the courts accepted that the search procedure is evidentially relevant, what would concretely have to change — what gets disclosed, how the statistic is framed, what further showing is required before a cold hit can support a conviction — and would the resulting practice still be one a jury could follow?
