At a methods workshop last week a colleague (organizational theory, very sharp, essentially no philosophy training) spent ten minutes defending a research programme that hasn't produced a genuinely novel prediction since the early 2000s, and the load-bearing move was this: "facts don't kill theories — only better theories do." Since nobody in the room could offer a better theory, he concluded he was perfectly entitled to keep going, and probably to keep going indefinitely. Half the room nodded. I muttered something about protective belts and sat down, which was not my finest hour.

I want to do this properly at the debrief, and I want it as a menu rather than an essay. Can you lay out the distinct precise claims that one sentence could be making? My sense is there's a whole spectrum hiding in it: something about auxiliary hypotheses and ceteris paribus clauses that's basically a platitude about how prediction works at all; something about underdetermination; something about whether there can be "crucial" experiments; a strong conventionalist claim that any theory can be saved by enough surgery; a claim that conflates logical refutation with rational abandonment; and maybe a claim that isn't about logic at all but about the sociology of a field. For each reading, tell me (i) what it says precisely, including what "kill" and "better" have to mean for it to come out true, (ii) whether it's a truism, defensible-but-contestable, or just false, and (iii) whether it would actually license what he did.

Two follow-ups after the list. First: is there a reading where the absence of a rival is doing all the work, and if so — is "there is no better theory available" even the sort of thing that could function as a reason for a particular person? It smells like it slides between a claim about the logical structure of theory choice and a contingent, time-indexed claim about the current state of a literature, and I want that slide pinned down. Second, and this is the part I really care about: Lakatos usually gets blamed for the slogan, but my recollection is that his own version is more modest — falsification only happens when a successor shows up with excess empirical content, and his progressive/degenerating appraisals were meant as appraisals of programmes rather than decision rules for individuals. Correct me if I've got that wrong. Then tell me whether that machinery helps my colleague or hurts him: if a programme can only ever be appraised as degenerating and never terminated, in what sense is "degenerating" a criticism at all, and can a programme be killed by the sheer absence of novel predictions rather than by any fact?

One more wrinkle. He was talking about a whole framework — a hard core with a flexible belt of auxiliaries — so I'm not convinced "the theory" in his sentence even has a fixed referent. Run the list again with that ambiguity made explicit and seperate out which readings survive if "the theory" means the core, versus the core-plus-belt, versus the whole programme including its heuristics.

Numbered list I can hold in my hand, please, with the verdicts attached to each reading. No bibliography, no reading list — I'll chase the sources myself once I know what I'm looking for.
