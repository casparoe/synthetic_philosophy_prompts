I am setting the final exercise for a graduate seminar on distributive justice, and before I commit to the case I would like to see how it actually plays out across the rival metrics.

Ada and Bea are two women in a small town who are, in every ordinary respect, identically situated: the same income and wealth, the same liberties and opportunities, the same standing in their community. Bea has been blind since birth. Ada is fully sighted but carries a diagnosis: on the best clinical evidence available she will have lost her sight within five years, though nothing in her present functioning yet shows it. The town has just received a one-off endowment, sufficient for either of two programmes but not both. The first is an intervention available only to Ada and only now, which on the same evidence would very probably prevent the loss of her sight. The second is a scheme of accommodation — reorientation of public space, accessible formats, supported employment — which would substantially improve Bea's prospects and do nothing at all for Ada. Ada is at present no less happy and no less satisfied with her life than Bea, who has never known sight and reports herself entirely content.

Rank the five candidate currencies of egalitarian justice — welfare or preference satisfaction, Rawlsian primary goods, Dworkinian resources including the hypothetical insurance device, the capability approach, and the access-to-advantage proposal of Cohen and Arneson — by how well each determines what the town ought to do. Give each a score out of ten, with a sentence or two naming the consideration that decides where it lands. Where a metric could be revised to handle the case better, say what the revision costs it, and say in each instance whether it is the currency itself that fails or merely its temporal specification.

Finally, tell me which of these rankings is least secure, and what argument or feature of the case would most plausibly move it.
