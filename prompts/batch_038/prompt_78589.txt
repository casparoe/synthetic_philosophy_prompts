I am a high-school senior, and for our school's philosophy society I wrote an argument meant to show that fair algorithmic risk scoring is impossible in principle — not merely difficult, but mathematically ruled out. My faculty sponsor thinks the conclusion is far stronger than the premises support, and I cannot locate the break myself. The argument runs as follows.

(1) A risk score is fair in one sense if it is calibrated: among the people it assigns any given score, the same fraction go on to reoffend, whether they belong to group A or to group B.
(2) A risk score is fair in a second sense if its errors fall evenly: groups A and B have the same false-positive rate and the same false-negative rate.
(3) There is a proved impossibility result to the effect that, unless the two groups have the same base rate of offending or the score is essentially perfect, no score can satisfy (1) and (2) together.
(4) In any real population the base rates differ across groups, and no real score is essentially perfect.
(5) Therefore no real risk score is fair.
(6) The alternative to using a score is unstructured human judgment, which is also uneven across groups, so that option is unfair as well.
(7) Consequently every option open to an agency is unfair. But if every option is unfair, then no decision an agency makes can be faulted as unfair, and the whole public dispute about these tools is empty.

I most want to keep (3) and (4), since they carry the mathematical content. Which step should I give up, why did it look acceptable when I wrote it down, and what do I lose by giving it up? If some weaker conclusion survives, state it precisely and say what it leaves an agency that accepts (3) with to decide.
