I work in the elections division of a mid-size state, and my job is certifying citizen initiatives for the ballot. We get around forty petitions a cycle and have fifteen business days to rule on each. Three of us sit on the panel and two of them aren't lawyers, and whatever we write gets appealed to our state supreme court, so the reasons have to hold up.

Right now we apply basically one test — does the measure embrace more than one subject — and our courts have read it so loosely (anything "reasonably germane" to some broadly stated common purpose passes) that it knocks out maybe one petition a decade. My deputy director just got back from an observation trip in Europe and wants to bolt on the Swiss idea of unity of subject matter: a proposition should be one thing, its parts should have a genuine material connection aimed at a shared goal rather than an artificial or purely political one, and no voter should be pushed into a dilema where a yes on the whole is a no on something they actually want. I'm not sure that's importable into our law, but I want to see what a workable version would look like.

So: write the procedure my panel could actually run on a petition. The questions in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where it terminates in a no, and where it stops and hands the thing back to the three of us because what's left is political judgment rather than anything a staffer can look up. It has to keep us out of the merits — we can't kill an initiative because we think it's a bad idea — and it should be honest about which steps are judgment calls wearing a procedural costume.

Then run it on three petitions. (1) One that pairs a large police hiring and overtime package with a right to counsel for tenants facing eviction, packaged as a single safe-and-stable-communities act. (2) One identical to a rival petition we already certified except for a clause sunsetting the whole thing after four years, filed so that whichever of the two collects more yes votes prevails under our tiebreak statute. (3) One that declares a right to clean air and directs the legislature to appropriate whatever money is needed to guarantee it, with no implementing machinery of its own. One of these is going to come out a way I don't want, and I'd rather find out now which.

End by telling me how confident you are in the parts you're actually asserting rather than just arranging, and flag the single step you're least sure survives appellate review.
