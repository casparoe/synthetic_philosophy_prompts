Got roped into defending the Reflection principle at a reading group and, to prepare, I built an argument against it — which backfired, because now it seems to prove too much and I can't find where it breaks. P is any proposition, t is some future time of mine, credence means subjective probability.

1. Reflection: if you're confident now that at some later time t you'll have credence r in P, then your credence in P now should be r.
2. At t I'll have some credence or other in P — I won't be in a state of having no opinion at all.
3. So I'm confident now that at t I'll have credence r in P, for some r.
4. So by (1) my credence in P now should be r.
5. (4) doesn't care what P is or how far off t is, so my whole present credence state gets fixed by my future ones. Run the same move from t onward and my credence in P now equals my credence in P at the last moment of my epistemic life, for every P.
6. So nothing I actually find out between now and then can matter to what I'm rationally required to believe: the requirement was discharged before the evidence showed up.
7. And it bootstraps. I'm planning to spend August reading a guy who I'm confident will talk me into the idea that crystals cure cancer; come September my credence in that will be .99. Reflection then makes it .99 today, before I've read a word. Every credence I can arrange for my future self is one I'm required to hold right now.
8. It also locks in: once a credence is 1, ordinary conditionalization can never lower it, so the agent who obeys Reflection becomes unable to respond to contrary evidence. Dogmatism isn't a side effect here, its mandated.
9. Therefore Reflection is false — or it survives only where it does no work, since when I don't know what I'll later believe it doesn't apply, and when I do know it hands me certainty for free.

I don't want to just accept (9). The thought that my own future self, better informed than I am now, is an expert about my beliefs feels right to me, and the fallback position — that there are no diachronic requirements on credences at all — looks to me like it makes evidence-gathering and planning unintelligible, which is a big thing to give up. So: which step do you reject, and why did it look innocent to me when I wrote it down? Then price the alternatives. If the principle needs restricting, what does the restriction say about my crystal-reading August, about the unremarkable case where I know I'll trust my eyes and my memory tomorrow morning, and about the sure-loss argument that's supposed to motivate Reflection in the first place — does that argument still go through on the restricted version? And if you think the reasoning is actually sound and I should swallow (7), say so, and tell me precisely what the swallowing consists in.
