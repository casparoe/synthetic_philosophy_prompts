I chair a nine-member Transmission Review Panel at an international foundation that funds and hosts projects deliberately sending radio or optical signals of sufficient power to be detectable by other civilizations. We receive roughly six proposals a year plus renewals, and every determination is irreversible in a strong sense: once a signal has left, nothing we later decide can retract it. A critic has written to our board alleging that our record is arbitrary — in our first year we cleared a low-power omnidirectional beacon without dissent, and three years later we blocked a targeted optical transmission, and we never articulated a principle that distinguishes the two. The board has asked me to produce a written screening procedure before the next quarterly cycle.

Please write the procedure the panel can actually run, on the explicit assumption that the dark forest reasoning is the governing framework for the existential-risk dimension of our mandate: the argument that because civilizations cannot know one another's intentions and cannot rely on shared enforcement, the safest posture is silence, that a detected technological civilization may be destroyed preemptively at low cost to the destroyer, and that mutual suspicion together with uneven and accelerating technological growth makes even a well-meaning sender a threat worth eliminating. Do not quote any novel, article, or spokesperson at me; I want the argument's structure converted into steps, and I want to be told which structural assumption each step depends on, so that when an assumption fails we know which step fails with it.

The procedure should specify:

1. Ordered steps, each with the question to ask, what would count as a sufficient answer from a proposer, and what the step outputs: proceed, halt, or refer.
2. Where it halts with a no — conditions on which the panel should decline without further deliberation.
3. Where it refers the question upward or outward, because the issue is one of values, consent, or political legitimacy rather than fact, and this panel has no warrant to settle it.
4. How to handle the burden of proof. Our charter currently reads a proposal as authorized unless the panel finds positive reason to block it. I suspect that polarity is wrong under a catastrophic-risk framework, but I want the procedure to tell me whether the default should shift and what would justify shifting it, rather than assume my suspicion.
5. What to do at any step where no probability can be estimated even approximately. Say explicitly which steps require a number, which can proceed on an ordinal comparison, and which must be treated as unknowable rather than merely unknown, and what the panel does in the third category.
6. A robustness check: for each step, whether its recommendation would invert if the dark forest premise were false, and what the procedure should say about steps that make sense only if the premise is true.
7. A one-page version for our two non-technical members and for the board, which has no scientific expertise and can only accept, reject, or return a determination.
8. An honest marking of which steps are judgment calls wearing the clothes of procedure.

Then run the procedure on four cases and report the determination each produces, with reasons and with the steps at which the panel would have to exercise discretion:

(a) A single high-power optical transmission, aimed once at a planet twelve light-years away whose atmosphere has yielded disputed but not dismissible biosignature candidates, carrying a curated archive.

(b) A continuous low-power omnidirectional beacon in a protected band, repeating a mathematical message for fifty years, with no target selection and no reply channel.

(c) A reply to a narrowband signal we have actually received, from a source forty light-years away. Our staff assume that detection settles the concealment question in our favor, since the sender already knows roughly where we are; I want the procedure to say whether that inference holds and whether receipt of a signal changes what the framework recommends.

(d) Our own past record: the beacon we cleared and the transmission we blocked, retro-audited under the procedure, with a statement of whether they were consistent with each other and, if not, which one the procedure would now reverse.

At least one of these should come out in a direction I would rather it did not. I have spent my career defending deliberate transmission, and I would prefer not to be handed a procedure that quietly authorizes everything — but I also do not want one whose blocking conditions are so loose that no proposal could ever satisfy them, since that is indistinguishable from a veto. Finally, state plainly what the procedure cannot do, including, if it is the case, that it cannot supply the standing of nine people to decide for populations that were never consulted and for generations not yet born, and that this gap cannot be closed by better technique.
