Background so you don't have to guess what I'm after: I run a small Thursday reading group — six of us, two psych students who've never read Dennett, one retired engineer who keeps smuggling a homunculus back in through the back door — and this week we're on the color phi material. The setup: two differently colored lights flashed a few degrees apart with a sub-second gap, and people report seeing a single light slide across the gap and change color partway, which raises the obvious problem of how you can see a change before the second light has even landed. Dennett and Kinsbourne's two ways out are the Orwellian one (the subject really does have the discrimination, and then the record of it gets quietly rewritten once the second light shows up) and the Stalinesque one (the two events get reconciled before anything counts as conscious, so the discriminate-then-correct sequence never happens at all). Their claim is that the two stories agree about the brain and agree about how it feels to be the subject, so nothing observable can tell them apart, and anybody who thinks otherwise is still running a little theatre in their head.

I want to hand the group a case that pushes back. So: build me one thought experiment — a single normal human subject with normal vision, no lesions, no drugs, no exotic hardware; one color-phi-style stimulus sequence; one report protocol — in which the Orwellian and the Stalinesque account genuinely disagree about an observable outcome. Constraints, all of them, please: (1) the only data allowed are the subject's own outputs — button presses, forced-choice answers, spoken reports, and their latencies — no EEG, no single-unit work, nothing invasive; (2) the disagreement has to be about a correlation between two measures taken on the same trial (or across a block for the same subject), not about the level of any single measure — both accounts must predict exactly the same marginal distribution for each measure on its own, so a lazy one-report experiment can't settle it and the split only shows up in how the two measures covary; (3) the inter-flash gap stays in the ordinary apparent-motion range, roughly 100–400 ms, and your timing resolution can't be better than about 40 ms — no magical millisecond-precision introspection; (4) the difference has to fall out of what each account independently says about when a discrimination becomes available to guide behavior, not out of a stipulation like assume the Stalinesque brain simply takes longer here. You're welcome to include a cue that forces a guess partway through the sequence, and it has to be describable out loud to the psych students in a couple of sentences, so give me a plain-English walk-through of the protocol before anything technical.

What I want back: the protocol in a paragraph or two, then a small table with measures as rows and the two accounts as columns, then an explicit demonstration that constraint (2) really holds — show me that the marginals come out identical and only the correlation differs, since that's the part most likely to be faked. Also say plainly what the case is supposed to establish. My reading is that it should show the Orwellian/Stalinesque choice has behavioral consequences after all, which would undercut the no-fact-of-the-matter move; if your case can't do that, tell me so and say what weaker conclusion it does support. And be honest about the load-bearing feature: name the one extra assumption you had to add to wedge the two accounts apart, say what happens to the case if I delete it, give the best reply a multiple-drafts defender would make — something along the lines of you've just relabeled a processing stage as an experience — and tell me whether that reply closes the gap or only relocates it.
