For a seminar on legal positivism and natural law, I want a thought experiment built to specification rather than a retelling of one already in the literature. Construct a legal order that satisfies every one of Fuller's desiderata of legality — its rules are general, published, prospective, intelligible, mutually consistent, possible to comply with, stable through time, and applied by officials exactly as written — while being substantively wicked in a way that its very legality makes worse rather than merely tolerable. The setting must be an ordinary modern institution (a licensing regime, a municipal ordinance, an eligibility scheme for public benefits), the injustice must be generated by what the rules say rather than asserted in the narration, and the harm must fall on people who never violate any rule. You may not use Nazi Germany, the postwar grudge informers, or apartheid South Africa, and nothing in the case may depend on secret rules, retroactive decrees, contradictory provisions, or impossible demands, since I want the easy desiderata kept firmly satisfied. The point of the exercise is to hand the positivist their strongest possible exhibit, so a case that quietly fails one of the eight conditions, or that simply labels its rules unjust, does not count.

After the scenario — one paragraph, no more — show your work. State where in the case each of the eight desiderata is met. Then give Hart's verdict on whether this is valid law and what he thinks we should say about it morally, and Fuller's verdict, including what survives of his claim that the inner morality of law carries moral weight once a system meets all eight conditions and is monstrous anyway. Finish by identifying the single feature of the case that generates the pressure on Fuller, saying what happens to the case if that feature is removed, and giving Fuller's best reply — one that does not concede that his eight conditions collapse into mere principles of efficient rule-drafting.
