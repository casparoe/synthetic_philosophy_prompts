Ok so I'm the volunteer treasurer for a climbing-gym co-op — about 4,000 members, a board of nine, nobody gets paid — and I'm in a mess I'd like a second pair of eyes on before a hearing next Thursday. In March one of our staff quietly started also doing paid contract work for the gym through his own little company, and nobody on the board knew. A member dug it up, posted it in the forum, and it blew up. As treasurer I put out a statement on behalf of the board to calm things down, and three sentences from it are now being used to try to get me removed. They are: "No board member was involved in setting up this arrangement" (true — it was the general manager, who's staff, not board); "the co-op has not paid any outside company for work that staff should be doing" (true, if you read "outside company" the normal way — it's his own company, and he's inside); and "I was not aware of any conflict when I signed the budget line" (true — the line said "facility consulting" and I didn't know what was behind it). Every single sentence is literally accurate. I've gone through them word by word, several times, at 2am, which I realize is not a great sign.

Here's the wrinkle. Our bylaws have an honesty clause that got added maybe six years ago after a worse scandal, and the guy who drafted it was a retired logic teacher who wanted it airtight: a statement counts as false if the sentence, read literally and with the ordinary meanings of its words, is not true, and the clause explicitly says it doesn't cover "impressions, implications, or what a member may reasonably have taken away." He argued at the time that anything looser would be impossible to apply evenly, and honestly at the time I thought he was just being careful. So I want to say I'm clear. But three board members have already told me they'll vote to remove me anyway, and one of them — a philosophy grad student, of course — said at dinner last week that "true" isn't the kind of word I think it is, and that my defense is confused at a level deeper than the bylaws, which was annoying and also kind of stuck with me.

What I actually want is not vote-counting advice. Tell me whether the defense is any good. Is the clause's picture of truth — sentence by sentence, ordinary word meanings, nothing at all about what I conveyed — a coherent thing to build an accountability rule on, or is it broken in a way that only shows up in a case like mine, where each piece is fine and the whole is engineered? And then where does that leave me at the hearing: if I'm technically in the clear but everybody who read it experienced it as a lie, what do I actually say? I keep going back and fourth between "I stand by every word" and "I'm sorry for the impression I created," and I dont know whether there's a third thing that isn't just cowardice in nicer clothes. Bonus question, if you have room: a couple of members want to rewrite the clause after this, and I'd rather not be the person who argues for keeping it exactly as it is — is there a version that covers what a statement conveys without turning every hearing into a fight about vibes?
