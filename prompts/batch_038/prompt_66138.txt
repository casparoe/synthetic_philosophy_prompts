You are a social choice theorist who has taught graduate voting theory for twenty years and who sets examinations that separate candidates who can state a theorem from candidates who can say which of its hypotheses a given institution actually violates. I need your help designing one item.

I teach an upper-level seminar on social choice theory and democratic legitimacy: advanced undergraduates and first-year doctoral students, one term of formal methods behind them, no measure theory. They have been given the Gibbard–Satterthwaite theorem in full — proved by Gibbard in 1973 and Satterthwaite in 1975, having been conjectured earlier by Dummett and Farquharson — together with its hypotheses, and they have read the usual popular gloss on it. For the final examination I want one essay question, answerable in seventy-five minutes and worth twenty marks, that cannot be answered by reciting the theorem or by repeating the slogan that every voting system is manipulable.

Here is the material the question is to be built on. Please treat it as a fixed scenario:

A constitutional convention is drafting rules for a polity of nine million in which, once a year, the residents choose among four budget packages. The draft charter provides:

1. Every resident submits a strict ranking of all four packages.
2. If some package defeats every other package in a head-to-head majority comparison, that package is adopted.
3. If no package does so, the adopted package is drawn by lot, with equal probability, from among the members of the smallest set of packages each of which defeats every package outside the set in a head-to-head majority comparison.
4. The convention defends the draft as follows: pairwise majority comparison is the purest expression of the popular will; the lottery in provision three is outside any individual's control; therefore no resident can gain by misrepresenting a ranking, sincerity is a dominant strategy, and the charter delivers a rule that is both non-dictatorial and strategy-proof. Since the textbooks say that is impossible, the convention concludes, the textbooks must have overstated their result.

The crux I want the question to force is this: the convention has denied the wrong hypothesis. Its rule is not deterministic — provision three introduces a lottery — so the theorem does not apply to it in its literal form; and yet the rule is manipulable all the same, because the pairwise comparisons that determine whether provision two or provision three is reached can be moved by burial and truncation, and the lottery immunizes only the tie-breaking stage. The convention's complaint that the literature has overstated its result is therefore correct for a reason that gets it nothing it wants. Beyond that, a strong answer should ask what the convention could change instead, and what each change costs: restricting the domain (and the problem of enforcing a restriction without eliciting the very preferences the restriction is about), randomizing more thoroughly (and how thin the set of genuinely strategy-proof lottery-valued rules is), moving to cardinal reports with transfers (and the assumptions this charter conspicuously lacks), or weakening strategy-proofness itself to a graded robustness criterion.

Deliver the following.

First, the examination question. You may tighten my prose, but you must preserve all four provisions and the convention's stated justification in substance, since the traps are load-bearing. State explicitly that the theorem's hypotheses are reproduced on the formula sheet, so that nothing turns on recall of the literature.

Second, a model answer of roughly eight hundred words.

Third, a marking scheme out of twenty marks, built around five required moves, the marks each carries, and a single line on the characteristic way candidates botch it. Then a short paragraph saying what separates a first-class answer from a competent one, and a list of seductive errors that should cost marks — for instance, inferring from the theorem that all non-dictatorial rules are equally manipulable, conflating non-dictatorship with strategy-proofness, assuming the lottery in provision three purifies the whole rule, assuming without argument that the theorem covers rules with random tie-breaking at all, and treating manipulability as a fatal defect of legitimacy rather than a matter of degree. If you want to reward the point about lottery-valued mechanisms, mark it as an excellence move and phrase it so that a candidate need not name any further theorem.

Work the design out before you write — which hypothesis does each provision engage, and in what respect is the convention actually right? — but keep that thinking out of the response.

No reading lists, no author-year citations, no suggestions for further reading anywhere in the question, the model answer, or the scheme.

Begin your response with the examination question itself, with no preface, no restatement of these instructions, and no commentary on the assignment. End with a clearly labeled design note of no more than one hundred and fifty words. There, tell me where your framing rests on a contested reading of the theorem — in particular, whether reading strategy-proofness narrowly, or holding that random tie-breaking puts a rule outside the theorem's scope altogether, is the right call; which of your grading decisions you are least confident of; what consideration would move a point from required to excellence, or shift the first-class boundary; and where the model answer's reasoning is most likely to break down if a well-prepared candidate pushes back on it.
