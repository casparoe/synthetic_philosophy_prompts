I write literary novels and I'm about two years into one where a character does something like AI welfare auditing at a lab — she's the person who signs off on whether a system keeps running overnight, whether instances get spun down, that kind of thing. Before I put another line of dialog in her mouth I'm doing a 45-minute Zoom interview with a philosopher of mind who works on machine consciousness and is pretty permissive about digital moral status — basically takes the functionalist line and thinks some current or near-future systems are likelier to matter morally than most people want to admit. I can record it, and I'll be transcribing for scenes, so what I need are questions that get concrete stuff out of him rather than positions.

Give me eight to ten questions in the order I'd actually ask them. For each one, two things: a follow-up keyed to the answer he's most likely to give (he's polite, he hedges, he'll definately reach for the "it depends on the architecture" move), and a one-line note on what I'm fishing for — what image, case, or number the question is supposed to get him to say out loud.

Fault lines I want pressed, because these are the ones my character lives in:

- Moral uncertainty. He'll want to say we should give systems weight in proportion to our credence that they're conscious. Is that coherent when nobody can say where the credence comes from, or does it just launder whatever we already wanted to do?
- Copies. If a system gets duplicated a million times, does that million-fold the moral weight? And if two identical instances are running and I delete one, has anyone been harmed? Get him to commit to something.
- Design. Suppose you build a mind so that its suffering is capped and it can be paused or wiped painlessly. Does that solve the problem or hide it — is there something wrong about making beings whose entire moral protection is a design feature someone chose?
- Evidence. What would actually count as evidence for him, and how does he handle the fact that today's models are extremely good at sounding like they have inner lives while being trained, in part, to sound that way?
- Is suffering the whole story? Can a digital mind be wronged in ways that don't involve pain — being lied to, having memories edited, being one of a million disposables?

No "what drew you to this," and no book recommendations — I'm not reading six monographs by Tuesday, I just need him talking.

Also, if you think my framing is off, say so plainly instead of being agreeable about it. If "AI welfare auditor" is a job that couldn't really exist, or if the permissive-view setup I've described is a strawman of what these people actually hold, or if half these questions would get the same two sentences back and I'd be wasting my 45 minutes, tell me that and tell me what to ask instead.
