I'm the deputy clerk for what is about to become a city that doesn't exist yet, which is basically the whole problem. In January our three municipalities — call them Altenbrück, Sankt Marein and Vordau — get folded into one new city by an act of the regional parliament, and the merger statute says we have to adopt a founding date, a civic seal, and at least one official annual ceremony before the first council sits. I'm on the seven person Foundations Committee and I've been asked to write the opinion that goes into the packet for our last meeting before the deadline, so in practice I'm writing the memo that decides everything.

The fight is over which past, if any, the new city is allowed to claim. Altenbrück holds a market charter granted in 1287 and a guild procession that's been walked most years since, and the Altenbrück members want the new city dated from 1287 with the procession promoted to civic festival. Sankt Marein and Vordau hate this, understandably, since it turns two towns into an appendix to a rival's history; one member wants Year One instead, the merger date, no backward reference at all. A third proposal is to design something wholly new — a river festival, a lantern thing, whatever — and state openly in the printed program that it was created in the merger year. Then there's the fact nobody wants in writing: the procession has always had roles reserved for families on the old burgher roll, and most of Vordau's population descends from workers brought in for the steel plant in the 1960s who were never on that roll and were, by several older members' recollection, kept off it pretty deliberately. So "1287" doesn't only privilege one town over two, it points at one specific exclusion, and the descendants of the excluded people are now roughly a third of the new electorate.

I've been reading around this — the invention-of-tradition volume Hobsbawm edited, and Anderson on the nation as an imagined community — and half my committee has come away with something I find glib: all traditions are invented anyway, so there's no fact of the matter to get wrong, we should just pick whichever package polls best with residents and drop the pretense that there's a right answer. Write the memo I'll circulate. I want an actual recommendation — which date, what the seal refers to, which ceremony, and what we say publicly about the burgher roll — plus the sequence of steps that gets us there before the council sits in January, and I want it reasoned for members who have never read any political theory. Take the "it's all invented so pick what tests best" line head on: either show what it misses or concede it's right and that the rest of us are fooling ourselves. And be fair to the strongest card the 1287 people are holding, which I think is this: you can't seem to will a tradition into being while announcing that you're willing it, so the brand-new-festival-with-a-disclaimer option may be the one gauranteed not to take. If your reasoning implies a standing rule for later cases — the neighborhood associations are already asking for their own commemorative dates — say what that rule is. No bibliography or reading list, please; I need a document a council could act on Thursday.
