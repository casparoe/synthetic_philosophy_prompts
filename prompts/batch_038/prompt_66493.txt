I've been on our hospital's research ethics board for about seven months now — I'm the first member who isn't faculty, which means I spend a lot of time wondering whether I'm supposed to have opinions at all. In the last couple of months the disagreements have started piling up and its getting to me, so I'm trying to write down what I actually believe about when you should give way to someone who disagrees with you and when you should dig in. The standard advice I can find is either conciliate toward your peer or stay put, and honestly neither fits what I do. Here's what I actually think, case by case.

One. Another member and I read the same file, same regs, same meeting, and split on whether a waiver of assent for a minimal-risk registry study was defensible. Nothing distinguished us that I could see. I moved most of the way to him and I think that was right.

Two. Same setup, different protocol, but this one has the sponsor also funding the PI's lab. The other member is a company person — soft on industry money every single time, everyone knows it. Only the specific point he made cuts against the sponsor: he wanted the data-sharing clause tightened. I moved toward him more than I would have if he'd been neutral, not less. Arguing against your own pattern buys you something.

Three. A junior member and I disagreed about whether a consent form was readable by an eighth grader. She has read maybe two thousand of these. I have read ten. I didn't split anything, I just took her view. Full deference, and I think that's right too.

Four. Two members who have each read thousands of forms disagreed with each other about a form, and it fell to me as acting chair to break it. I picked one and went all the way over. Going to the midpoint felt like a cop-out and I still think so. On the face of it this is three with the roles flipped — non-expert plus expert, no split, total move — and it comes out opposite. I have some feeling that the difference is that in four the experts had already used up their disagreement on each other and there was nothing left for me to learn from it, only a decision to make, but I can't turn that into a rule.

Five. A member said my judgment on one protocol was compromised because my wife works at the sponsoring company. I do not think I could split with him on the question of whether I'm compromised. I either think the charge lands or I don't, and using the size of our disagreement to settle it feels like using the thing in question to settle the thing in question.

Six. The regs have an ambiguous sentence about paying people for research-related injury. Our lawyer read it one way, I read it another. On what the sentence means I think I should just fold. On what the board should do about it, given that meaning, I don't think I should fold at all. Two questions, and deference only travels on one of them.

Seven. Three solid members all disagreed with me about whether a study's risks were reasonable in relation to benefit. One person wouldn't move me much. Three starts to feel like real evidence I'm wrong. But not three times as much, because those three sat in the same corridor talking to each other all week.

Eight, the one that keeps me up. When a colleague argued against a protocol and I found out she'd written the clause it departs from, I moved toward her. She knew exactly where it cut corners and she had every reason to say nothing. Fine. But when I catch myself reasoning to a verdict that happens to be convenient for me, I distrust the verdict even when the argument looks clean. Same shape, mirror images: a stake making someone more believable, a stake making me less believable to myself. I've tried telling myself the rule is just discount whoever the stake favors, but I don't believe that's what I'm doing in the second case, and I can't say what else it is.

Nine. On one protocol I moved all the way to a senior member's view and it turned out badly — the study got suspended and his reading was the generous one. I don't know yet whether that means I was wrong to defer or just unlucky. Right now I think I'd do it again, and I'm not sure that's a verdict I should be allowed to keep.

So: is there one principle behind all of this — when to defer, when to hold, how much the number of people matters, what a known stake does? I want it stated precisely enough that I could check whether it really delivers each verdict, with each clause tied to the case that forces it. Then tell me what it says about cases I haven't hit, like: someone much better at this than me disagrees and we haven't discussed it yet, so I don't know their reasoning; or I discover the disagreement was between me and me, my own judgment from last month versus today's. And be straight about which of my verdicts the principle can't save. If saving nine of them costs you a principle with nine clauses, say so, and tell me which one you'd overturn for the simpler version instead.

Please commit to the best account rather than laying out the options and shrugging, and then tell me what would change your mind — which argument or which of my cases, read differently, would push you to a different principle, and where in your reasoning you think it's most likely to break. I'm not looking for references and I don't want a reading list, I'll chase that down myself if I need it.
