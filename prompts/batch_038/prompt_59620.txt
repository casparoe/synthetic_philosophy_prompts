I'm a self-taught amateur — I left school at sixteen and worked in a print shop for most of my life, and only came to history late, through the books people happened to leave on the counter. For the past two years I've been reading my way through the French Revolution, and one question has slowly taken over: not why the Revolution happened, but why it so reliably turned on the people who had made it. My friends and I argue about this at length and get nowhere, because we keep talking past each other. One of them thinks the whole thing was latent in the ideas of 1789 — that once you declare the will of the people to be one and indivisible, you have already written the warrant for everyone who later disagrees with you. Another thinks that's cheap hindsight, and that the real culprit was invasion, defeat, and the sheer administrative panic of 1793. A third thinks both of them are reaching for grand causes when the honest answer is fear, rivalry, and a run of bad luck. I suspect all three are partly right and that the disagreement is mostly about how much weight each cause can bear, but I have no way of settling the weights on my own, and I don't trust myself to know which of my instincts are informed and which are just preferences wearing a costume.

So I've done something a little embarrassing and written out my candidate explanations in my own homemade shorthand, and I'd like you to sort them into four tiers — A doing the most explanatory work down to D doing very little — and also give each a score out of ten, with a sentence or two saying why it lands where it does. Here they are. First, the purity filter: the conviction that the nation speaks with a single voice, so that organised opposition is not an opinion but a betrayal, and each successive round narrows the set of people who still count as patriots. Second, the ratchet: emergency powers granted for one crisis are never handed back, and the threshold for what counts as an emergency keeps dropping. Third, the war: invasion, military defeat, and the need to feed and equip armies, which produced both the terror and the machinery to administer it. Fourth, the empty chair: the old authorities were demolished before any new source of legitimacy could be built, so power went to whoever could claim to speak for the people and there was no one left to arbitrate between rival claimants. Fifth, the bread: shortage and price rises made the capital ungovernable and handed leverage to whoever could feed it. Sixth, the missing playbook: nobody had done this before, so the actors improvised, and improvisation under stress tends to reach for the crudest tool to hand. Seventh, the men themselves: the temperaments, vanities, and mutual suspicions of particular leaders, and the fact that losing a factional fight meant losing your head.

Two things I'd like you to do beyond the ranking. First, tell me which of these items are really the same cause counted twice, and which are downstream of another item on the list — I have a nagging feeling that the ratchet and the empty chair are not independent, and that the bread is a trigger rather than an explanation. Second, tell me which two entries would swap places if I changed the question slightly, from what most explains the self-destruction as such to what most explains why it took this particular form in France in those particular years rather than somewhere else. A last request about how you present this. Please be explicit about the difference between what is genuinely settled among people who know this material properly and what is still contested, and don't let a reading that is really one school's position wear the clothes of a consensus. I have no access to a library and no way of checking references, so please don't give me citations, bibliographies, or a list of names to look up — just your reasoning laid bare. And if any of my homemade labels smuggle in an assumption that a historian would object to, say so directly; I would rather be corrected than flattered.
