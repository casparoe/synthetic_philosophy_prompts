Compose a poem of six quatrains on the temptation to write the past as a march toward the present — the whig interpretation Butterfield attacked. Let the form carry the diagnosis rather than merely naming it: the final word of every stanza must rhyme with "now" (or be that word), so each episode, however remote and incommensurable, gets hauled back to end on us. Have the stanzas stage the distinct charges in turn — a past actor flattered by being made modern, a moment valued only as an anticipation of something later, a party written off as an obstacle or a foil, a dead end quietly abridged out of the story, the historian's itch to hand down a verdict on who was in the right. Then exactly one stanza must refuse the terminal rhyme, and the poem should let that refusal stand without glossing it.

Afterward, add a short note on where the poem cheats: where the constraint buys you a whiggish consolation you did not intend, and whether the one broken stanza escapes presentism or flatters the present all over again by congratulating us for noticing. If you think the assignment is misconceived — if a poem built this way can only parody whig history rather than diagnose it, or if the fixed rhyme guarantees that every episode is recruited after all — say so plainly instead of delivering something that merely looks compliant.
