Last week I was making dinner while two friends of mine argued across my kitchen counter for about an hour and I still don't know who won. Neither of them is a philosopher but they got way deeper into it than I expected, so I'd rather have someone actually referee it move by move than be told it's complicated.

Background: Rae runs a small mailing list, about 400 subscribers, where every month she picks a "film of the month" and writes a paragraph on why people should watch it. Here's the thing — she hasn't seen a single one of the films she's recommended in two years. She has six friends whose taste she knows extremely well, they watch everything, they send her ratings and long voice notes, and she writes the blurb out of that. Dev found this out and was delighted rather than appaled, which is what started it.

Their moves, roughly in order:

1. Dev: there's nothing sketchy here at all. Almost everything Rae knows, she knows on someone else's say-so — where Canberra is, what's in the news, whether a bridge is safe to drive over. Knowing a film is good because six careful viewers say so is the same kind of thing.
2. Rae: it isn't, because with those examples you can check afterwards, and with a film the only thing to check it against is more opinions. Also she said she couldnt answer a single follow-up question in a reply thread — if someone asks whether the ending lands she has to go back to the group chat, and a recommendation you can't defend isn't a recommendation, it's a forward.
3. Dev: fair, but that's a problem with her not having watched it, not a problem with testimony. If she had watched it and someone asked about the lighting in scene four she'd be just as stuck. First-hand experience doesn't make you answerable either, it just makes you confident.
4. Rae: she discloses in every blurb that she hasn't seen the film, so nobody is being deceived — Dev agreed that part is honest — but she says honesty isn't the issue, because people still read it as her endorsement, and you can't endorse something you haven't experienced just because you bolted a disclaimer onto it.
5. Dev: then nobody could ever recommend anything to anybody. Rae's own single viewing would be a sample of one, and six calibrated reports are strictly better evidence about whether people will enjoy a film than her own eyeballs would be. He said he'd rather trust six people who know what they're doing than one person's mood on a Tuesday.
6. Rae: "will enjoy it" and "is good" are different claims and he keeps swapping them around. She said she'll happily be told that a documentary is accurate, but nobody can tell her a joke is funny — you can be informed that people laugh at it, which isn't the same as finding it funny.
7. Dev: exactly, and that proves his point, because it means what needs first-hand experience is a response, not a value. If being good just is being the sort of thing that gets a favorable response out of the right viewers, then reports from the right viewers are the best evidence going. He said something here about Hume and ideal critics that I couldn't follow and neither of them bothered to explain.
8. Rae's last word: if that's all "is good" means, then one of her six has been rating everything five stars for four months because his girlfriend started film school, and her whole system quietly rotted with no way for her to notice — whereas if she'd actually watched the films she'd have caught it in one evening. She says that's the difference between borrowing taste and having it.

So: go through the eight moves and tell me who's right at each one, including where somebody lands a real point and where they're just restating their position in nicer words. Then say who comes out ahead overall, and what neither of them thought to say — I have a feeling there was a distinction they kept circling without grabbing hold of it.
