I'm a doctoral student in a formal epistemology program, and next week I'm presenting a section of my first chapter at a departmental work-in-progress workshop. The chapter argues that the standard Bayesian apparatus is the wrong place to hang an account of confirmation in mathematics, and that the repair has to be structural rather than a matter of tacking on extra evidence. My supervisor has read an earlier version and thinks the middle section, where I try to motivate the structural proposal, is doing too much work with too little argument. I have rewritten it twice and I can no longer see it clearly. I would be grateful for a careful, adversarial read of the draft below: I care much more about whether the reasoning holds together than about whether it is original.

Here is the draft as it currently stands.

> **2.1 The standard picture.** A Bayesian agent assigns to each sentence φ of a fixed language L a credence Cr(φ) in the unit interval. The assignment is coherent if it is non-negative, assigns 1 to the logical truths of L, and is finitely additive over mutually exclusive sentences. The classic motivation is pragmatic: an incoherent agent is vulnerable to a Dutch book — a finite package of bets, each of which the agent accepts at her own prices, and whose payoffs sum to a sure loss regardless of how the world turns out. Coherence, in turn, is sufficient to rule such books out.
>
> **2.2 The cost of coherence.** If Cr assigns 1 to every logical truth of L and is monotone with respect to entailment, then a coherent agent is an ideal logician: she cannot be uncertain about anything that follows from what she already knows, and her credence in a conjunction can never exceed her credence in either conjunct, however remote the inference connecting them. This is a strong claim, and it is usually acknowledged and then set aside on the grounds that idealizations are harmless when the idealized agent's judgments are good approximations to a real agent's. I want to argue that in this case the idealization is not harmless, because it distorts precisely the phenomena confirmation theory exists to explain.
>
> **2.3 Two symptoms.** First, mathematical learning. Let ψ_k be the sentence saying that a specific k-step computation halts in an accepting state. Suppose I have run the corresponding computation for a thousand smaller inputs, all of which halted, and that the construction is uniform across inputs. My credence in ψ_k ought to be high but short of 1, and it ought to move as I accumulate cases. Coherence does not by itself forbid an intermediate credence here, but it does something worse: it makes the permissibility of my credence depend on a fact — whether ψ_k is a theorem of my language — to which I have no epistemic access at any cost I can pay. The norm is therefore not merely unmet by real agents but unusable by them. I cannot determine whether my present credence is rationally admissible without performing the very computation I am uncertain about.
>
> Second, old evidence. A theory T is proposed, and it entails a fact E that has been known for centuries. Everyone agrees that T's derivation of E is a real epistemic achievement and a reason to prefer T to a rival that leaves E unexplained. Yet if I already assigned E credence 1, then conditionalizing on T leaves my credence in E exactly where it was, and the familiar measures of confirmation report that T has gained nothing whatsoever from E. On this account, a theory that unifies the known data is no better supported than a theory that is silent about it.
>
> **2.4 The proposal.** I suggest we abandon the assumption that a single coherent function is the proper object of assessment. Let the agent be represented instead by a sequence Cr_1, Cr_2, Cr_3, … of credence functions indexed by the computational resources the agent brings to bear, with later members refining earlier ones. Impose three conditions. (i) *Local coherence*: each Cr_i is coherent with respect to those inferences that can be carried out within the resource bound attached to stage i, so that Cr_i must respect the entailments it can actually see and is permitted to ignore the rest. (ii) *Convergence*: for every sentence φ, the sequence of values Cr_i(φ) has a limit. (iii) *Inexploitability*: there is no procedure, itself operating within a modest resource bound, that reads the agent's prices off the sequence and constructs, in the limit, an unbounded profit against her by betting on logical relations.
>
> My hope is that (iii) does the substantive work, in the same way the Dutch book condition does in the classical case. An agent whose credences drift too slowly to track provable relations, or that drift erratically, will be fleeced by a bookie who can detect the pattern; an agent who is never fleeced is, in the limit, doing well at logic without ever having to be a logician at any particular moment.
>
> **2.5 A worry I have not resolved.** Condition (iii) quantifies over procedures that the agent cannot herself enumerate, and it refers to a limit the agent never reaches. It may therefore be that (iii) is a norm with which no finite agent can check her own compliance — which is the same complaint I lodged against coherence in 2.3. My instinct is that this is an important disanalogy rather than a repetition of the problem: coherence is infeasible at a stage, whereas inexploitability concerns a pattern across stages, and an agent can be governed by a pattern-norm without being able to verify it. I am not confident, however, that this amounts to more than a rhetorical move.

Could you comment on the following, in order? First, is my characterization of coherence in 2.1 and 2.2 accurate? Specifically, do the usual axioms really force monotonicity under entailment and unit credence in logical truths, or have I overstated what coherence commits one to, and is my gloss on the Dutch book argument correct as far as it goes? Third, and most importantly, does the analogy between condition (iii) and the classical Dutch book argument hold up?
