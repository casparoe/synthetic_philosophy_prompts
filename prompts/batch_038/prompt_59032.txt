I'm co-teaching a seminar next term, sort of an interdisciplinary thing on reason and the bomb — half the room are philosophy grad students who know their decision theory cold, the other half are IR/poli-sci kids who can set up a game of chicken on the board but have never really thought about what it takes to make a threat *credible* as opposed to merely severe. Week three we're screening Dr. Strangelove, and I've drawn the short straw of writing the 4-page reading that gets handed out beforehand. I want it to be a genuine piece of film criticism through the philosophy of deterrence, not the usual "Kubrick shows the absurdity of the arms race" thing that I can get from any study guide. Can you write it?

Here's the line I want you to develop and then interrogate, because it's my hunch and I'm not sure it survives contact with the film. Both sides in that movie independently build the same broken machine, and it's broken in the same way. Ripper's Plan R is delegation as commitment: hand launch authority down to a level that will still be alive after a decapitating first strike, and make the recall path run through a code only one man holds, so the threat is genuinely irrevocable. The Soviet doomsday machine is the same idea taken to its limit — cobalt bombs wired to go off automatically, with the deactivation switch itself a trigger. But neither machine can be *addressed*. Ripper's radio discriminator cuts off every channel that isn't his own three-letter code, so once he's committed there's nobody left to negotiate with, and the thing that was supposed to guarantee retaliation guarantees instead that the retaliation can't be called off by anyone who still has a reason to call it off. And the doomsday machine is kept secret until a week after it matters, so it deters nobody — its whole deterrent force was supposed to live in the adversary's mind, and there was nothing in the adversary's mind. So: irreversibility without communicability, on both sides. Is that reading actually supported by the film, or am I forcing a Schelling-shaped template onto a satire that doesn't care about the template? I want you to say which, and to be blunt about it.

Then work through the stuff that a handout has to handle. The paradox of deterrence itself — a threat whose execution would be catastrophic for the threatener too, and therefore, on the usual account, not something a rational person would ever carry out; so what makes it believed, and does the film think anything makes it believed? Turgidson's arithmetic in the War Room, where he weighs a few tens of millions of American dead against keeping the loss ratio favorable and concludes the trade is worth taking, and then proposes letting the wing finish its mission — I want students to see whether the film is attacking his inference or his premises, because I think most of them will assume it's attacking the inference and I suspect it's the premises. Strangelove's gloss on what deterrence actually is, that it's an art of producing fear in the enemy's mind rather than a matter of physical balances — is that a fair statement of the theory, and does the movie's own plot vindicate or embarrass it? And the final act, the mineshaft-gap business and the plan about ratios and survivors underground: is that the collapse of deterrence logic or its continuation past the end of the world, i.e. a claim about the structure of the security dilemma rather than about anyone's psychology? That last one is the bit I most want argued rather than asserted, since everybody remembers the scene and nobody knows what to do with it.

Two constraints. Don't quote the dialogue — nobody will have the script in front of them and I don't want a handout that pretends to remember exact wording; paraphrase the scenes instead and trust that we all know them. And skip the recommended-reading section entirely, I've already built one. Finally, end by telling me where the film resists this whole apparatus: what a satire built around one general's private psychosis about water fluoridation can't be an argument about, whether the comedy depends on the strategic material being taken seriously or on it being mocked, and what the film gives the deterrence debate that the debate doesn't already have in argument form.
