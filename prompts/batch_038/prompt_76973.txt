I work in localization for a small academic press in the humanities — mostly French and German monographs, with a modest back list of translated poetry — and last month our managing director asked me to draft the internal case for moving our translation workflow onto a large-language-model pipeline with one human post-editor per volume. I have been circling that memo for three weeks, and the version I can defend to the board is not the version I actually believe. Partly to sort out my own thinking, I reread Douglas Hofstadter's *Le Ton beau de Marot*, which one of our translators pressed on me years ago: the long stretch of the book built around Clément Marot's little poem to a sick young lady, where he collects dozens of English renderings of the same handful of very short lines and uses them to argue that the systems of his day were doing something categorically shallower than translation. The book was published two decades before the architectures that now drive the tools I am being asked to adopt.

Here is what I want. Make the best possible argument for the position that the fluency of contemporary neural translation does not refute Hofstadter's charge but on the contrary vindicates it. The version of the charge I care about is not the old Turing-test-flavored claim that the machine lacks an inner life; it is narrower and, I think, harder to dismiss. A translator, on his way of putting it, has to construct a model of the situation a text describes and then choose words that re-present that situation in another language — which is why the same person can answer questions about the passage, say what is at stake in a pun, flag an ambiguity the author left open deliberately, and tell you which of two candidate renderings is closer to the writer's intent. Build the case that today's systems still do none of this, and that their characteristic failure modes — flattening register, resolving ambiguities the original kept live, harmonizing an idiosyncratic voice into house style, and confabulating confident content where the source underdetermines it — are precisely the signatures of a process that matches textual form without consulting any situation. I would also like you to develop a point I have not seen anyone make cleanly: because these failures are largely invisible to a reader who does not know the source language, a market that grades output on fluency alone systematically selects for errors that no one on the buying side is positioned to catch. Fluency has come apart from fidelity, and fidelity was what Hofstadter's complaint was about all along.

Two constraints. First, do not strawman the other side — confront the strongest replies head-on, in particular that these are engineering defects of the current generation rather than a conceptual limit, of the same kind people confidently predicted about phrase-based statistical systems before neural methods embarrassed them; and that modern systems now actually satisfy Hofstadter's ancillary demands, since they can summarize a text, answer questions about it, explain a joke, and justify a translation choice, which arguably means his test has been met and the charge of shallowness has lost its content. Say explicitly why those replies do or do not defeat the argument, and state what observation would count for you as evidence that a system genuinely possesses the situation-model you are claiming it lacks. Second, keep it usable: I want something I could read aloud to a skeptical board rather than a survey of the debate. State your thesis in a single opening sentence, then develop the argument in numbered steps, with your treatment of the two objections and your evidential standard in their own short sections at the end. The whole thing must not exceed 750 words.
