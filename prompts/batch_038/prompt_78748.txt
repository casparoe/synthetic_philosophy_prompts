I'm a PhD student in biology and I work on microbial cooperation — strains that leak metabolites their neighbours can use, that kind of thing. So I have read far too much theory about when helping pays and when it doesn't, and yet I cannot figure out what to do about one actual person, which is humbling.

Here's the situation. In my department there's an informal arrangement where grad students help each other with analysis and code. Nobody keeps score, there's no system, you just ask in the group chat and whoever knows the trick spends an hour with you, and eventually you're the one who knows the trick for somebody else. It works, and it saves everyone real money, becuase the department's actual support is one shared statistician with a three-week waitlist.

There's a guy I'll call D. D is by a wide margin the best person to go to — he'll diagnose your model in twenty minutes. But when someone asks him for help he's almost always slammed: deadline, reviewer 2, family thing, whatever. It's never a flat no, it's always "next week, I promise," and then next week he's slammed again. And here's the part that sticks in my throat: D helps constantly when it's visible. Whole afternoons with an undergrad whose name is going on a poster. He'll sit in on a first-year's committee prep when the PI is in the room. So it's not that he doesn't help people. He helps precisely where it gets him something.

Two more wrinkles. D is finishing — maybe 18 months, job lined up, his own network is built, he doesn't need anything from the rest of us anymore. And: there are people around here who take way more than they give and nobody minds them at all, because they're useless anyway and nobody ever wanted their help in the first place. Nobody loses sleep over them. It's specifically D, the one everyone wants an hour from, that people are quietly furious about.

I've kept helping D anyway, on the theory that if I hold up my end he'll eventually notice. Six months of that now. Three options as I see them:

1. Quietly stop helping him. Match what he gives, no announcement, no drama.
2. Stop helping him and say why in the group chat, so everyone else can stop wasting their time too.
3. Keep helping and say nothing, because the whole thing runs on trust and one public fight might make everybody stop asking anybody for anything.

My gut says 2 — it feels like this isn't just about me, the information is worth something to the whole group. But my advisor, who is not a biologist and has more sense than me, says I'd be eating the entire cost of a benefit that lands on everyone else, and that I should ask whether I'd still do it if no one ever found out it was me.

And the part I genuinely can't reason my way through: every time I try to picture doing 1, I get this loop. He drops the ball once, I go cold, he catches on and stops answering my messages, and now we're two years into a feud over an hour of R code that neither of us remembers starting. Half the time I can't tell whether he's lying about being busy. Some of it is probably true. I've watched him blow deadlines that mattered to him personally.

So what do I actually do? I don't want the "just have an honest conversation with him" answer — I know that's the real answer and it's not available here, he'd be mortified and it would poison the well for everyone who's never done anything wrong. Tell me which of the three is right and why, whether my advisor's objection to 2 actually holds, and if there's a fourth move I'm not seeing.
