I am writing the methodology chapter of my dissertation on probabilistic risk assessment for a low-probability, high-consequence engineering hazard — a tailings-dam failure, say — where the expert judgements I have elicited are naturally reported as intervals rather than point values, and where I intend to model the assessor's belief state as a closed convex set of probability measures. Before committing to that representational choice, I would like a careful account of the most important implications of replacing a single measure with a credal set, addressed to three specific questions.

First, updating: conditioning on a long run of uneventful monitoring years can leave my interval for the annual failure probability strictly wider than it was beforehand, even when those observations seem evidentially irrelevant to the hypothesis; what does that imply for how learning should be represented in a safety case that is revisited annually?

Second, recommendation: the credal set does not by itself determine an action, since maximin, α-maximin, and maximizing expected utility relative to some single member selected from the set can rank my remediation options differently. How much of this divergence is genuine normative underdetermination, and how much is an artifact of the decision rule I happen to adopt?

Third, rationality: how should I answer the objection that an agent with imprecise credences will decline each of two bets separately that, taken together, guarantee a net gain, and is therefore paralysed precisely in the stepwise decisions that risk management involves?

Throughout, please indicate which of these difficulties are merely presentational and which are genuinely blocking.
