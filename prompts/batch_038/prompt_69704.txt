I am moderating the Q&A after a departmental colloquium in philosophy of biology, and I need a sequence of questions for our speaker. Do not name a real philosopher or attribute this position to an actual published author: I have constructed it for the exercise, and the speaker is simply its articulate defender. Please treat everything below as the material you may work from, together with the standard, widely known features of the debate; do not ask the speaker to cite, list, or name sources, since I want questions that turn on argument rather than scholarship.

The speaker's thesis, in brief. They accept Dennett's framing of the choice as between illegitimate mind-first lifts and legitimate accelerators of design, but they deny that the second category is stable. Their claim is that every crane, on inspection, borrows an already-structured higher level whose availability has not been explained: to say that some device speeds up the traversal of a possibility space is to presuppose that space as a fixed, landmarked terrain, and the landmarks — the recurring "good tricks," the reusable modules, the fidelity conditions on transmission — are doing the explanatory work while their own provenance goes unaudited. On their telling, the crane is not a third option between greedy reductionism and a skyhook but a promissory note written against a skyhook, and the load-bearing case is design space itself: the machinery of cranes only accelerates anything if the space being accelerated through already has the shape it is credited with. Hence their slogan: the honest disjunction is between a reductionism that admits it is skipping steps and a mind-first cosmology that admits it is lifting, with no coherent position in between. They are a naturalist and think the greedy horn is the less bad one, but they insist this is a concession forced by the collapse of the middle, not an enthusiasm.

Please draft nine to twelve questions, each with a follow-up keyed to the answer the speaker is most likely to give. The follow-ups matter more than the openers: I want the second question in each pair to be the one that makes the first reply unavailable. The sequence should cover, in an order you judge best for a live audience:

- the charge of vacuity, and specifically whether it mistakes a burden-of-proof device for a criterion — if a crane is anything a naturalist applauds and a skyhook anything a naturalist dreads, is that a discovery about explanation or a description of the speaker's own side in an old quarrel?
- whether "greedy" can be identified at all without an independent standard of how fast a reduction may legitimately proceed, and what the speaker offers in place of one;
- a concrete biological test case, such as the merger of lineages that produced eukaryotic cells, or the repeated independent arrival at some familiar engineering solution: force the speaker to classify it as crane, skyhook, or outside their framework, and press on whichever classification costs them least;
- culture and its vehicles, which are Dennett's flagship cranes: the speaker's audit seems to bite hardest exactly where transmission already presupposes imitation, language, and an audience, so ask whether they are committed to calling the flagship case the clearest skyhook in the book, and if so what is left of the distinction;
- the idle-vocabulary objection: no working biologist reasons in these terms, so ask what is lost if the pair is dropped, and whether the speaker's collapse argument would still have a target;
- self-application: if every accelerator that lifts understanding faster than the base level allows is unaudited, ask whether the speaker's own argument escapes its own criterion, and refuse to accept a reply that merely distinguishes explaining from reasoning without saying why the distinction is available to them;
- the trap I most want to spring: if the middle collapses, ask whether the speaker has thereby conceded the main premise of a teleological, order-first cosmology of the kind Nagel defends in Mind and Cosmos, and if they resist that, ask them to name the resource they resist it with, given that they have just dismantled it;
- whether the speaker is in fact a greedy reductionist, and how they answer the standard rejoinder that greedy reductionism has a poor track record in the actual sciences;
- a closing question about what finding or what kind of account would count against their own thesis — what a genuine crane would have to look like for them to admit one exists.

Keep every question self-contained enough that a listener who knows the material can follow it without notes, and keep them short enough to be asked aloud: no question longer than about three sentences.

Finally, after the questions, add a note of roughly two hundred words for my own use, separating what is settled from what is genuinely contested. I want to know which of my descriptions of cranes, skyhooks, and greedy reductionism are matters of agreed record about the view they come from; which readings of design space and the space of possible genomes are live disputes among people who know that literature, rather than settled interpretations; and which elements of the speaker's position as I have described it are minority commitments that I must not introduce to the room as though they were common ground. Where a point is contested, say so plainly and indicate roughly how the disagreement divides, rather than presenting one side as the consensus.
