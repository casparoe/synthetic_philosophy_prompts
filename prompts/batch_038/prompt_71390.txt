I lead a small team that builds a production agent system, and two of my senior engineers have now spent the better part of a year arguing about something I do not have the vocabulary for. I would like you to draft the document their argument should have produced: a joint statement from an adversarial collaboration, written so that both of them could put their names to it.

First, the system, because I think the dispute is only intelligible against it. Our agent is a large transformer-based policy plus a separate declarative store we call the ledger: a set of plain-text propositions about the task domain ("this account is suspended", "a refund has already been issued") that is serialized into the context window every turn and that the agent itself can write to. Two experimental results are on the table. Both parties accept both results; they disagree completely about what the results show.

- Ledger edit. Flipping a single stored proposition reliably changes downstream tool calls, and the effect survives rewording, prompt reshuffling, and transfer to unrelated contexts, with no retraining.
- Activation patch. A linear probe on the policy's hidden states reads out that same proposition, and patching the probed direction reproduces the behavioral change with the ledger left untouched.

One of the two, whom I will call R, holds that the first result shows the proposition is a genuine cause: the intervention is clean, the counterfactuals hold, and the generalization is about the content rather than about any particular string or vector — so the second result merely identifies how that content happens to be realized. The other, M, holds that all the causation in the system happens in weights and activations, that a ledger edit is an input perturbation described in a compressed vocabulary, and that the activation patch shows the ledger is one of several routes to the same causal work and therefore not privileged. M also presses a point I only half follow: that if the low-level computational story of a run is already complete, there is nothing left over for the proposition to do, so treating it as a cause either double-counts or merely redescribes.

I read Hofstadter years ago and remember the image he used for exactly this: a sealed vessel full of small objects careening into one another, where the objects are meant to be simultaneously physical marbles and symbols, and where the question is who is really shoving whom around inside. My recollection of his terms is unreliable, so please do not lean on the book — use the question, not the text. The question I want the document organized around is this: when a ledger fact changes what the agent does, do symbols push the substrate around, or does the substrate push itself around in a pattern we find convenient to describe with symbols?

The deliverable should have four sections:

1. Agreed claims. A numbered list, ideally ten to fourteen items, that both R and M can sign as written. Some should be about the system itself (what the ledger is for, what an intervention is, what the two experiments license, how to test a claim of this kind in our codebase), and at least one should be a claim a旁观 reader would have assumed the two camps share nothing about. If a claim is only signable because it is ambiguous, keep it and flag where the ambiguity bites.
2. Disagreements. A table with four columns: the disagreement; R's own formulation of R's position, in wording R would accept; M's own formulation of M's position, in wording M would accept; and whether that row is a substantive disagreement or a dispute about words, with one sentence of justification.
3. Cruxes. The sub-questions such that settling one would actually move at least one of them. For each, give a concrete test — something we could run on this system, or a clean case both would reason about — that both agree bears on it, and record each side's prediction in advance, including what outcome would oblige each of them to revise.
4. Falsifiers. For each camp, what would count as evidence or argument against its own view.

Two structural requests. If R's position as I have described it is really two positions — for instance, that symbol-level generalizations are irreducible to the substrate level, and that symbol-level facts are causally efficacious — split it, and say which of the two M is objecting to. And if you conclude that some part of this dispute is verbal, mark that part explicitly rather than papering over it.

Constraints: do not adjudicate and do not tell us who is right, since the point is that both of them have to be able to sign this; no strawmen — each camp's self-description must be one its proponent would endorse; no citations, no named papers, and no reading list, because I want the arguments themselves and you would only be guessing at the references anyway. Where you need a term of art (downward causation, causal closure, overdetermination, multiple realizability, or anything similar), use it but gloss it in a clause — I have no formal philosophy training and neither does most of my team. Plain engineering vocabulary elsewhere. Target 1,200 to 1,800 words.
