You are an expert in axiomatic bargaining theory who has also sat for many years as a commercial mediator. Please work through this carefully and show your arithmetic explicitly; do not simply recite the axioms of the standard solutions, but apply them to the figures below.

I have taken over the caseload of the independent adjudicator whom our growers' co-operative appoints to settle the break-up of joint ventures between members. She has retired, and I want to be consistent with her. In each of her cases there was a single sum to divide — the value of the venture sold as a going concern — and each member had a stated fallback: what they would be left with if the venture were wound up and they went their own way. Her written reasons refer to nothing else: no mention of fault or need, and no reference to the parties' opening demands (in the first ruling below the two members asked for £140,000 and £85,000, and she ignored both).

Her four rulings:

(i) two members of a packing venture — fallbacks £60,000 each, sum £200,000, awarded £100,000 each;
(ii) a nursery and a landscaping firm — fallbacks £25,000 each, sum £90,000, awarded £45,000 each;
(iii) two orchardists — fallbacks £10,000 each, sum £180,000, awarded £90,000 each;
(iv) two orchard tenants — fallbacks £150,000 and £50,000, sum £200,000, awarded £150,000 and £50,000.

Now before me: two members dissolving a marketing venture. The larger one's fallback is £240,000, since they can go straight to the supermarkets; the smaller one's is £60,000, since they would be reduced to the local market. The venture as a going concern is worth £900,000.

Please answer four things. First, which rules for dividing the sum are consistent with all four of her awards — flagging any place where rules that sound like different principles turn out to be the same rule in disguise. Second, what each surviving rule implies for my case; which of her rulings is the closest analogue to it; and why that analogue fails to settle the question. Third, a colleague of mine is confident that this case will finally reveal whether she reasoned as Nash would have or as Kalai and Smorodinsky would have. Is he right? If not, what sort of dispute would tell those two apart? Fourth, commit to a single prediction of how she would have divided the £900,000, with your reasons — including any theoretical ground for preferring one surviving rule over another — and tell me what principle the co-operative would be adopting whichever way I rule.
