I am presenting a working paper at a two-day interdisciplinary workshop on the measurement of political violence, hosted by a quantitative social-science institute, and I would like a question-period preparation sheet tailored to this room rather than to a generic philosophy audience.

The audience will be about twenty people: a plurality of computational social scientists who build or maintain conflict event datasets and whose professional credibility depends on producing defensible casualty and incident counts; two historians, one working on the Rwandan genocide largely through oral testimony and one on famines under colonial administration; a human-rights litigator who has contributed to briefs on atrocity classification; a political theorist who works on transitional justice and public memory; and several graduate students split between the data side and the history side. There will be no analytic moral philosophers present and, more importantly, almost nobody who has spent time on aggregation and incommensurability as such. The house norms favor operationalization, coding decisions, and sensitivity checks, and are suspicious of anything that sounds like conceptual evasion.

My thesis, briefly. First, comparative judgment about mass atrocities is both possible and sometimes obligatory, and the refusal to compare is not a neutral abstention but a conservative act that ratifies whatever distribution of attention, funding, and legal category happens to be in force. Second, such judgments cannot take the form of a single ordinal ranking or a scalar severity score, because any scalar is either a concealed weighting scheme or an aggregation across dimensions that do not share a unit. Third, the correct form is a structured profile across a fixed and publicly declared set of dimensions — I use scale of killing relative to the population placed at risk, degree of deliberateness and administrative coordination, intent to destroy a group as such, duration and reversibility, the dependence and vulnerability of victims, the alternatives realistically available to the perpetrator, and the diffusion or concentration of agency across accomplices and bystanders — together with stated priority rules. Fourth, some of those dimensions are lexically prior rather than tradeable: an intent to destroy a group as such cannot be outweighed by a larger death toll elsewhere, and victim dependence constrains how much work the language of collateral damage can do. Fifth, and this is the part I expect will be pressed hardest, the profile is answer-relative: the ordering it supports changes with the question being asked, so that legal classification, the allocation of preventive capacity, and the priorities of a memorial institution may each be served by a different reading of the same profile. My deliverable is therefore not a list but a protocol: state the question, state the dimensions, state the priority rules, and state what the resulting comparison licenses and what it does not.

Please draft the preparation sheet I need. I want roughly eight to ten objections this particular audience will actually raise — not the objections I would field in a philosophy department — ordered by how much damage each would do in this room, each phrased in the vocabulary of the person likeliest to raise it, and each accompanied by a reply I can deliver in two to three minutes. For every objection, tell me where my reply is softest and what I should give away in the opening five minutes so that the concession costs me nothing later. Flag any objection that is really my own thesis restated in a different register, since I suspect several of them are, and note where an objection's force comes from the audience's professional commitments rather than from its argumentative weight. Then identify the single defensive move without which the entire protocol collapses, the one I have to make convincingly for the paper to survive the session. Finally, tell me how to handle the question I am dreading: someone asking me to apply the protocol live to an ongoing conflict in the news that week and to say, in front of the room, which of two current situations is worse.

I am not looking for references or further reading. I want the arguments themselves.
