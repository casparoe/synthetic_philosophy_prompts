I'm meant to circulate a page or two on my own view ahead of a reading group on reasons and motivation — we've done Williams's internal reasons paper and roughly four decades of people objecting to it, and when I sat down to write what I actually think I ended up with more claims than I expected. Before I send it round I'd like a proper consistency check rather than a summary of the literature; assume everyone in the room, me included, knows the text. Here's the list:

(a) internalism in Williams's sense: A has a reason to φ only if A could reach the decision to φ by a sound deliberative route from A's actual subjective motivational set;
(b) I read that deliberative route generously — correcting false beliefs, vividly imagining what one has overlooked, weighing what one had been refusing to weigh — and I explicitly reject the reading on which deliberation can only surface motivations that were already latently in the set. On my view thinking well can produce a new end that wasn't there beforehand in any form;
(c) internalism is supposed to be a substantive thesis. It should rule cases out, and it should tell us what has gone wrong in the external reasons theorist's story — I dont want a formulation an externalist could sign up to after relabelling their view;
(d) I'm not a Humean about reasons. Desires aren't reasons, and the motivational set is a motley — projects, loyalties, patterns of emotional response — so the reason an agent has is never simply identical to one of his desires;
(e) there are agents for whom no route, generous or not, gets from their actual set to the relevant consideration — the man who is simply blind to his wife's unhappiness, say, or someone raised into casual brutality — and I want to say of them that they have the reason all the same, that they are straightforwardly wrong when they deny it, and that this isn't just a way of blaming them;
(f) but I also want to keep the explanatory motivation, which for me is the whole point: a reason has to be the kind of thing that could figure in a rational explanation of the agent's action, so reason-talk can't come wholly apart from the possibility of moving the agent;
(g) and none of this is relativism. Internalism is a claim about what makes something a reason, not about the agent's own verdicts on the matter; there's a fact of the matter about what's in the set and about what sound deliberation from it would yield, and agents are frequently wrong about both.

So: which of these actually pull against each other, and which pairs only look like they do? I've a suspicion that at least one of my worries about (d) and (g) is a nothing-burger, but I'd like that said rather than assumed. Rank the genuine tensions by severity instead of listing them as equally bad, and for each one say what would have to give, what it costs me to give it up, and whether the same move that fixes one tension creates another elsewhere in the list — I have a feeling that's true somewhere but I can't see where. And please don't just tell me it all depends on how I gloss the sound deliberative route and stop there: tell me which gloss the rest of the list has already committed me to, and then whether that's a gloss I can live with. Finish with the single revision that would buy me the most consistency overall, even if it isn't the one I'd want to make.
