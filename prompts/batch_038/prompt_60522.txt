I help organize a joint speaker series run by our philosophy department and the machine learning group next door. This term's theme is the question of whether it is literally true that neural networks have representations, and next week's guest is an interpretability researcher who answers yes with unusual confidence. For my own convenience I have been calling her position joint-carving realism, though that is my coinage rather than hers. As I understand it, she holds that when interpretability work identifies a direction in a model's activation space as a feature — say, one associated with a certain landmark, a certain syntactic construction, or the notion that a passage contains a factual error — this is often the discovery of a genuinely structured kind rather than the imposition of a useful fiction, and that the analogy to scientific realism about unobservables is not merely rhetorical but exact. What makes her view specific, and worth interviewing rather than simply summarizing, is the three-clause criterion she proposes for when a candidate direction earns the title. Clause (i) is causal leverage: steering along the direction must produce behavioral changes predictable from the hypothesized content, not merely correlated with it. Clause (ii) is cross-procedural recoverability: the same direction, up to sign and scaling, must turn up under methods whose failure modes do not overlap — for instance a sparse autoencoder trained on activations, a supervised probe trained on human-labeled examples, and an ablation-driven search conducted for unrelated reasons. Clause (iii) is content determinacy: there must be a single statable content that explains the direction's behavior across contexts better than any disjunctive rival does. She also has two recognizable habits in debate. When pressed on whether she is projecting her own taxonomy onto the model, she tends to reply that the objection covertly assumes a behaviorist notion of representation, which is precisely what is in question. And she is very good at conceding a peripheral point — some particular feature claim was overstated, some published result would not replicate — in a way that leaves the general thesis untouched.

Please draft nine to eleven interview questions for a forty-five-minute conversation in front of a mixed audience of philosophers and ML graduate students, so the questions may freely use vocabulary like superposition, polysemanticity, residual stream, steering vector, probing, and sparse autoencoder, but should not require that anyone in the room has read any particular paper. For each question, supply a follow-up keyed to the answer she is most likely to give, so that I have something to say other than thank you when the reply arrives on schedule; where her stock deflections are predictable, the follow-up should be the one that makes the deflection cost her something. Arrange the sequence as an escalating arc rather than a grab bag: open with questions about her own criterion, move through the harder structural objections, and close on falsifiability and stakes. Along the way, please make sure the set includes a question about how a realist individuates features in the presence of superposition and polysemanticity, given that a single direction may encode several apparently unrelated properties and a single property may be smeared across many directions; a question about whether clause (iii) can actually do the work assigned to it, since the difficulty of choosing between a natural content and a disjunctive rival reading looks like a familiar problem about the indeterminacy of content, and she should say why her case is different; a question about the human-labeled data feeding clause (ii), and whether the recurrence of a direction across independent procedures is evidence of mind-independent structure or evidence that all the procedures inherit our own categories and our own training corpus; a question about whether recurrence of the same feature across architectures and scales supports realism or instead reflects the fact that every model was trained on roughly the same text; a question pressing the scientific-realism analogy directly, including whether instrumentalism about features is any more troubling than instrumentalism about other posits nobody has ever observed; a question about misrepresentation, that is, whether anything deserves to be called a representation if there is no room for the model's getting it wrong in a way that is wrong relative to its own standards rather than ours; a question about whether the analogy to representation talk in psychology and cognitive science helps her or threatens to make her claims trivial; and one question designed for the non-specialists in the room that forces her to state what would change, practically, if she turned out to be right and her critics turned out to be right.

Two further constraints. The questions should be fair rather than a trap sequence: they ought to be worth asking even if she is correct, and a specialist in her area should recognize that whoever wrote them understands what is genuinely disputed rather than what merely sounds paradoxical. Skip anything whose answer she has already given many times in public — no questions about what drew her to the field, no questions about how she feels about the pace of AI development, and nothing that can be answered with a single slogan she has already used. Write everything in American English, keep each question to one to three sentences and each follow-up to one or two, and label them so I can read from the page. Do not invent quotations, do not attribute claims to her that I have not described above, and do not include a bibliography, a reading list, or citations of any kind.
