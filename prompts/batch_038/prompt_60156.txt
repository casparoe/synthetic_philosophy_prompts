His view was that it is routinely inflated into a claim about the limits of human knowledge, and that the inflation does no argumentative work; mine was that something survives the deflation, though I could not say what. We did not settle it. I am marking the final examination for an upper-level philosophy course on the epistemology of computation. Question 4 was worth 20 points and read as follows:

"It is commonly said that P ≠ NP captures the intuition that some problems are easier to check than to solve, and that this asymmetry underwrites modern cryptography. State precisely what the conjecture asserts; assess whether the phrase 'easier to check than to solve' is an adequate gloss on it; and explain what does and does not follow from it about (i) the existence of cryptographic primitives and (ii) the feasibility of automated reasoning. Wherever you rely on a conditional claim, indicate the direction of the implication."

Here is one candidate's answer, reproduced in full:

---

P is the class of decision problems solvable by a deterministic Turing machine in time polynomial in the length of the input. NP is the class of decision problems for which a proposed solution can be verified in polynomial time by such a machine, given a certificate whose length is itself bounded by a polynomial. Every problem in P is trivially in NP, since one can simply recompute the answer; the open question is whether the inclusion runs in the other direction. The conjecture P ≠ NP says that it does not.

The gloss "easier to check than to solve" is accurate for NP-complete problems, which are the hardest problems in NP in the sense that a polynomial-time algorithm for any one of them would yield a polynomial-time algorithm for all of them — Cook–Levin establishes this for satisfiability, and the property propagates by polynomial-time many-one reductions. The asymmetry between finding a solution and certifying one is therefore exactly what the conjecture is about, and the slogan is not a popular distortion but a fair statement of the technical content.

Because the asymmetry is genuine, two consequences follow. First, if P ≠ NP then one-way functions exist: there are mappings that are feasible to compute but infeasible to invert, and these are the primitives on which secure cryptography rests. The conjecture would thus secure the foundations of the discipline, rather than merely describing a curiosity about combinatorics. Second, if P ≠ NP then automated theorem proving faces a hard ceiling. Searching for a proof is at least as difficult as solving an NP-complete problem, so no feasible general procedure for generating proofs can exist, however much our search heuristics improve. What does not follow is that such problems are unsolvable in any practical sense: NP-completeness is a worst-case property, and worst cases need not resemble the instances that arise in applications, which is why contemporary satisfiability solvers dispatch industrial instances with millions of variables.

Finally, care is needed in separating the hard from the impossible. The halting problem is undecidable, and that is a strictly stronger limitation than intractability; NP-hard problems are decidable and merely resist efficient solution. Integer factorization, for instance, lies in NP and also in coNP, and is not known to be NP-complete, which is why it is used in cryptography rather than some NP-complete problem. And P ≠ NP remains a conjecture, not a theorem.

---
