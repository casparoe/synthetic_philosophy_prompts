I sit on the curatorial advisory panel of a municipal archaeology museum, and in fourteen months we open a temporary exhibition on the fifth and sixth centuries in our own region. The panel must settle the title and the interpretive spine at next month's meeting, because the design contract and the signage tender are released immediately afterwards and cannot be reopened cheaply. I would like a decision memo I can circulate to the other six members in advance: a recommendation they could vote on, not a survey of the scholarship.

The local evidence is what makes this hard. Our Roman provincial town was given up over the course of the fifth century: the street grid was never reoccupied, the aqueduct was not repaired after its last datable intervention, and the medieval settlement that eventually grew in the valley lies four kilometres away under a name with no Roman root. Yet the bishopric persisted on paper for another two centuries, Latin continued in inscriptions and liturgy, and a fourth-century civic basilica was quarried for stone to build a church on a different plan. Any visitor will see rupture and continuity in the same gallery, and the objects will quietly overrule whatever we put on the title wall.

Three titles are on the table. First, The Fall of Rome, pressed by our head of visitor experience: it is recognisable, it matches what school groups arrive already saying, it is honest about the material record here, and he argues that an unfamiliar title costs us the whole schools programme. Second, After Rome, proposed by our senior academic adviser, a Late Antiquity specialist: she holds that "fall" names a single event and smuggles in a verdict, that nobody in the period experienced the deposition of the last western emperor in 476 as an ending, that the successor kingdoms were accommodation rather than replacement, and that the decline-and-fall plot is an inheritance from Gibbon that we should not be handing to children unexamined; she adds that an invasion-centred framing has a political afterlife she will not feed. Third, Did Rome Fall?, from a member who wants the exhibition to be about the disagreement itself — to which marketing objects that a question in the title reads as the museum having no view, and our archaeologist objects that it invites visitors to conclude the collapse never happened.

Our archaeologist is the fourth voice and I want the memo to engage her rather than route around her. She works on the material record and says the transformation vocabulary is defensible for bishops, texts and law but indefensible for what she excavates: settlement contraction, the disappearance of wheel-thrown pottery and fired tile, smaller cattle, and the tree-ring and ice-core evidence for a sharp cold interval in the middle of the sixth century. A private lender has offered us a hoard of coins buried and never recovered, which is her case in a single vitrine: people hid their savings and did not come back for them. She wants a title that does not oblige her to soften that.

Two external constraints. A national heritage foundation is contributing forty per cent of the budget, and its brief requires the exhibition to address how societies end, with a small adjacent gallery drawing lines to contemporary climate and migration. And a re-enactment society that has volunteered at the site for thirty years has told us it will feel written out of its own story if invasion disappears from the interpretation altogether.

Please write the memo so that it does the following.

Recommend a title and a one-sentence interpretive spine to go with it, plus your second choice and the specific condition under which we should prefer that instead.

Give a concrete decision about 476: does the exhibition timeline include it, and if so how is it marked? Label copy is short — supply wording of twenty-five words or fewer that we could actually print, and say what a visitor who knows nothing would take it to mean.

Separate three things explicitly, and keep them separate throughout: what our panel may state flatly as settled because specialists do not dispute it; what is genuinely contested among people who know the period and must be signposted as contested wherever it appears; and what is a contested position that our recommended framing would be silently resolving in one direction. Do not let the recommendation present a contested view as the consensus — including in the direction of the transformation vocabulary, which is itself a position with a history and with critics, not a neutral default that everyone else has to argue against.

State the strongest consideration on the losing side in a form the panel member who holds it would recognise, and say what we should change in the exhibition — not merely in the title — to answer it. If your answer is that we cannot answer it and should instead build it into the display, say so and say how.

Give your judgment on whether the disagreement belongs inside the exhibition at all. A short historiography section risks losing visitors in the third room; suppressing it risks the museum asserting as fact what specialists argue about. Pick a side and give the reason.

Address it to lay panel members who have not read the scholarship and should not be assumed to know the technical names of positions. Aim for around 1,200 words. Be candid about which parts of your recommendation rest on a judgment call rather than on the evidence, and flag anywhere you think I have mis-stated a stakeholder's position, or where your recommendation turns on a fact about our site or about the scholarship that you would want checked before we print anything.
