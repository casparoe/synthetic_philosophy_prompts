I have been reading about personal identity in the rationalist-blog idiom — Parfit arrived at by way of the sequences, plus a good deal of Hofstadter — for the best part of twenty years, and I have at last written my own position down. Before circulating it to the small reading group I belong to, I would like it audited rather than defended. That group is skilled at finding the weak joint in a list of this kind and uninterested in being polite about it, so I would prefer to find out from you first.

Here is the position, in eight claims:

1. **Reductionism.** Personal identity consists in nothing over and above physical and psychological continuity; there is no further fact about who I am. What matters prudentially is not identity but the relation of connectedness and continuity, which comes in degrees and can branch.

2. **Patternism.** The self is a high-level, self-modelling pattern — Hofstadter's strange loop — and patterns are real in virtue of what they explain and predict, not in virtue of being made of any particular stuff. Substrate therefore does no work: if my neurons were replaced one at a time by functionally equivalent silicon, I would persist.

3. **Distribution.** On the account Hofstadter gives in *I Am a Strange Loop* (2007), and which I accept, each of us carries simplified but genuinely real models of the people we know well; to the extent such a model captures the pattern, part of that person is instantiated in another head. The self is already distributed across brains, before anyone uploads anything. My own gloss, which I take to be in the same spirit: grief is partly what it feels like to tend a model that will no longer be refreshed.

4. **Copying.** A whole-brain emulation of me, built from a destructive scan, would be me in every sense that matters; and if two were run at once, my prudential concern would extend to both, undivided. Causal continuity from the original cannot be what does the work here, since I accept that verdict while holding that a destructive scan severs every causal link worth having.

5. **Death.** My death is bad for me solely as deprivation: the ending of my experience and the frustration of my projects. That my pattern persists in the heads of people who loved me does not mitigate the badness of my death in the slightest. I regard that thought as a consolation, not as a fact about my welfare.

6. **The second death.** When the last person who knew someone well dies, something real goes out of the world — and in some cases the loss is greater than the first death, because a pattern distributed across several intimates can be better integrated into ongoing life than the pattern in its original host was by the end.

7. **Simulations.** A conversational model of a dead friend, trained on her letters and recordings, would not be her. Let me be exact about the stipulation: suppose the model is behaviourally indistinguishable from a whole-brain emulation built from the same source material. The emulation would be my friend; the model would not be. I do not think the difference is one of fidelity, and I do not think it is one of causal history.

8. **No substance.** I am not a dualist of any variety. Talk of souls is at best talk about levels of description.

What I want from you is an audit, and specifically the following.

- Which of these claims, singly or in combination, are genuinely in tension, as against merely sounding uncomfortable next to one another? I am confident that some apparent problems will dissolve on inspection; I cannot tell you which, and I have no prior about where the weak joint lies, so please do not take my phrasing as a hint about which claim I would sacrifice.
- Order the real tensions by severity, worst first, and state the criterion you are using. My own guess is that a tension is worse the more of the remainder of the list depends on the claim that has to give, but I would rather you told me if that is the wrong way to rank them.
- For each real tension, give the cheapest revision that removes it and say plainly what that revision costs me elsewhere. If the cheapest revision is to draw a distinction I have failed to draw, name the distinction and identify which claim it forces me to weaken.
- Finally, tell me which single revision would clean up the most. If the answer is that one of these claims is doing covert work on behalf of several of the others, I want to know which one, and what work.

Please do not reassure me. A verdict that the set is coherent would be a far less useful gift than a verdict that claim 5, or claim 7, or claim 3 cannot survive contact with the rest.
