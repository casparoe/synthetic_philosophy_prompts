Acting as an archaeologist who actually knows the collapse literature — Tainter, Diamond, the resilience crowd, the whole did-they-fail-or-just-change debate — think carefully, and brainstorm candidate terms before you settle on one.

There's a pattern I keep hitting and I don't have a word for it. Every marker we use to date a collapse sits at the top of the system: palace archives stop, dated royal monuments stop, the scribal script vanishes, prestige-goods trade dries up, big building halts. Meanwhile the markers of ordinary life go the other way — skeletons a couple generations later show fewer enamel defects, more varied diets, settlements spreading out into the countryside instead of crowding one center, grave goods less lopsided between rich and poor.

Two cases that made me notice: Roman Britain, where the coin supply just stops in the early 400s and the next century is near-blank archaeologically; and the southern Maya lowlands going quiet on dated monuments while the north keeps building. Also that the Linear B tablets only survive because the palaces burned down, which feels telling.

So it seems like two things are tangled up here: (a) the ruling apparatus goes away while most people actually get better off, and (b) the society stops producing the artifacts that made it legible, so the aftermath reads as a hole in the record no matter how people were living.

Does either of those have an established name? Give me the nearest concepts, how each differs from what I described, which fits best, and whether (a) and (b) are really separable or the field deliberately keeps them fused (and why). If (b) has no name, coin one and give me the boundary cases — does it still apply when the writing survives but the state doesnt? A bare label wont cut it, I want the edges.
