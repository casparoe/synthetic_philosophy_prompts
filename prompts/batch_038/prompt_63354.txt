**Working title: Four variables, or why "the society collapsed" is a badly formed sentence**

Collapse talk has one verb and at least four things it could mean. When we say a society collapsed we're usually fusing four separable variables: (1) political centralisation — is there a state, a palace, a king, a tax and corvée system; (2) population — how many people are alive and fed; (3) investment in public and monumental works — are statues being carved, temples built, roads kept up; (4) material-culture elaboration — the density and refinement of the stuff archaeologists actually find: fine pottery, seals, writing, bronze.

The claim I want to press: these four routinely come apart, and every famous collapse is a distinctive *pattern of decoupling* rather than a case of all-four-falling. The pattern is the interesting datum. The collapse is a headline.

Bronze Age eastern Mediterranean, c. 1200 BCE. (1) crashes — palatial administration, Linear B record-keeping, the redistributive palace economy, gone within a generation or two. (3) crashes — no more big building. (4) crashes — writing vanishes from the Aegean for centuries, seal-cutting, the fancy ceramic styles, the bronzeworking networks. (2)? Much less clear. Settlement shifts; some regions depopulate badly (Messenia especially); elsewhere there's real continuity. Part of the 'dark age' is an artefact of the fact that unwritten, non-palace-centred people leave fewer finds. So: an elite administrative collapse, with a demographic shift and a legibility collapse stacked on top of it. Whether an average farmer was worse off in 1000 than in 1200 may not be answerable, and the answer might be no — palace tribute and labour drafts are not a picnic.

Classic Maya, ninth century. The striking feature is the stagger. Monument dates stop over roughly a century, polity by polity, in the southern lowlands, while the northern Yucatán keeps going and in places flourishes. Divine kingship loses its legitimacy; the people don't vanish — they move, some regions empty out (drought, endemic warfare, and the fact that a legitimation crisis in a k'uhul ajaw ideology removes the coordination mechanism the food system depended on). Here (1) and (3) fall, (2) falls regionally and rises elsewhere, (4) persists. 'The Maya collapsed' is close to a category error; 'Maya divine kingship collapsed, unevenly' is a sentence you can argue about.

Rapa Nui. The worst of the three as a test case, for a reason the popular telling never admits: the timeline. The moai carving-and-transport tradition peters out before Europeans arrive in 1722, whereas the demographic catastrophe — smallpox, the Peruvian slave raids of the 1860s — is entirely post-contact. So the ecocide story is trying to explain (3) and (1) with a variable whose own main cause is contested: there's a serious case that the Polynesian rat, arriving in the first canoes, ate the palm nuts and seedlings and did most of the deforesting, with people as accelerants rather than authors. Add that island agriculture was *intensifying* — lithic mulching, rock gardens — right up to the point where statue-making stopped, which is not what a society in freefall does. Rapa Nui looks less like a parable and more like a case where the prestige economy changed its target.

So: Tainter is right that collapse is diminishing marginal returns on investment in complexity, but he treats complexity as a single dial. It isn't. And the Diamond-style ecocide-suicide genre fails for a subtler reason than its critics usually give — it assumes the four variables are welded together, so that when the environmental one moves, all of them move. Legibility bias is what makes that feel natural. We see pots, palaces and statues, and their disappearance is loud. A hundred thousand adequately fed farmers in 1100 BCE make no noise at all.
