You are to take on the role of a historian of the Holocaust who also works seriously in moral psychology — someone who has read both Christopher Browning's *Ordinary Men* and Daniel Goldhagen's *Hitler's Willing Executioners* closely, and who can also navigate the person–situation debate in psychology without relying on popular summaries. Please think carefully before answering: the exchange below is long, and almost all of the interesting questions are about inference rather than about which side is more sympathetic.

**Context.** Last summer a regional teacher-training consortium rewrote its secondary-school unit on the Holocaust. I was one of the writers, assigned to the postwar-trials week, so I sat in on the perpetrator-psychology sessions but was not a participant in them. Two of the other writers — Ruth, a social studies teacher who has run the unit for nine years and reads Browning's way, and Elias, a philosophy instructor brought in to write the new moral-psychology sidebar, who is more sympathetic to Goldhagen than anyone else in the room — spent most of the second working day arguing about how the reading should be framed. They asked the group to arbitrate at the end of the day, we deadlocked, and the framing question is still open. I took notes and have tried to render the argument as it actually went, without sharpening either side. Neither of them was a strawman, and I am not telling you which way I leaned, because I changed my mind about three times during the hour.

(1) **Elias:** The unit as drafted treats Browning as having refuted Goldhagen with the Józefów opt-out offer, and that is backwards on Browning's own evidence. Trapp stood the battalion up, was visibly distraught as he gave the order, and announced that anyone who did not feel up to the task could step out of the ranks. By Browning's count a dozen or so of roughly five hundred did. Goldhagen accepts that fact and reads it the other way: no German was ever seriously punished for refusing to kill Jews, so the exit was real and cheap, and the men who stayed bought what they were doing. A situational account owes us an explanation of why compliance held when the exit cost nothing, and "peer pressure" does not by itself explain why the pressure had traction.

(2) **Ruth:** It had traction because the offer was public. The cost of stepping out was not legal, it was social — you separated yourself from your comrades, in formation, in front of them, on the morning of an operation. That is precisely the mechanism Browning describes, and it is not vague: the pressure is strongest exactly where the official penalty is zero. Nor are you entitled to treat Trapp's offer as transparent. He was breaking down as he made it. If the battalion were full of men who wanted this, the offer is unintelligible; if it were full of men who merely went along with it, the offer is what a commander trying to keep a unit functioning would do.

(3) **Elias:** Now you are arguing from one officer's affect, and that is the correspondence bias running in reverse — inferring the absence of a disposition from a display of feeling, then reading the situational pressure off that inferred absence. Trapp wept and then organized the operation, assigned the details, and saw it through on schedule. Distress plus full compliance is not evidence against the dispositional account; it is evidence that the account was never about how anyone felt.

(4) **Ruth:** Then the archive you cite is full of men who did not feel like it either, and unevenness is what has to be explained. At Józefów men wept, men drank, men were visibly relieved to be posted to the cordon rather than the firing line; two fathers asked for other work on the grounds that they could not shoot children; one man demanded release, obtained it, and according to Browning was promoted after he got back to Germany. A monocultural thesis about what these men wanted should predict more uniformity than that.

(5) **Elias:** Unevenness is what cue-sensitive dispositions predict, once you say which cues were present — that is a claim about activation, not a patch. And your presentation of the book is the review-page version: the claim is not that every man enjoyed it, but that the perpetrators held an eliminationist conception of the Jews and acted willingly rather than under compulsion, with the cognitive endorsement of the killing kept separate from the affective experience of performing it. So Józefów cannot be the deciding case at all, since both theories predict near-total compliance there. What decides it is the counterfactual record: how many men tried to get out over the eighteen months, and what happened to the ones who did.

(6) **Ruth:** If squeamishness about the mechanics counts as compatible, and unevenness across operations counts as compatible, and a costless exit counts as proof of willingness, then there is no observation the record could supply that would count against the theory. A hypothesis is not strengthened by having absorbed everything thrown at it.

(7) **Elias:** Apply the same test to your side, which has been borrowing its instrument from a literature in bad shape. The classic studies this unit leans on are not what they were: the effect sizes in that tradition have long been around .2, and the prison study has been picked apart out of the archives, with recordings showing that the guards were steered toward the behavior the researchers wanted. If the situational framework is imported from that literature and the literature wobbles, the framework goes with it.

(8) **Ruth:** Two non sequiturs in one move. Browning did not derive his explanation from effect sizes; he derived it from the battalion's own interrogation records and used the obedience experiments as an analogy rather than a measurement — and he has kept revising, since the twenty-fifth-anniversary edition carries an afterword in which he takes up the photographs and the newer work on the other Order Police battalions. Second, the correlation point misuses the statistic: a predictor that is weak for a single low-stakes act by a stranger in an unfamiliar laboratory can be decisive when the same person faces the same kind of operation, with the same peers, for eighteen months. Aggregation is the standard answer to small single-occasion effect sizes, and Battalion 101 is nothing but aggregation.

(9) **Elias:** There is a deeper problem with the framing, and it affects my sidebar more than your week. Neither book is about character in the sense the sidebar will use the word. Browning's "ordinary" is a demographic description — Hamburg reservists, middle-aged, working-class, drafted into the Order Police and judged unfit for front-line service, not SS volunteers. Goldhagen's claim is about a political culture, not about traits of individuals. Both are making claims about the distribution of beliefs and commitments across a population. A unit that stages this as situation versus character teaches students a moral psychology that neither author held, and my sidebar will end up contradicting your history week.

(10) **Ruth:** Not quite. Goldhagen does make a claim about individuals: that the killing was wanted rather than merely tolerated, that the executioners were willing. Willingness is a predicate of persons, not of cultures, and Browning denies it. So there is a live dispute about persons here even if it is not about Aristotelian traits, and the sidebar can be built around that instead of around the laboratory studies.

**What I want from you.** Work through this in stages rather than answering in the order the questions are asked.

1. *Mapping first.* Before any adjudication, lay out the structure of the dispute: which of the ten moves are claims about what the books actually say, which are methodological claims about what would count as evidence for or against a causal thesis, and which are claims imported from psychology. Note any move whose force depends on a concession the other side has already made.

2. *Move-by-move verdicts.* For each of the ten moves, in order, give a verdict — sound, partly sound, or unsound — with two to four sentences of justification. Where a move fails, say which of three things has gone wrong: a false or overstated premise about the historical record, an invalid or question-begging inference, or a concession that gives away more than the mover intends. Where a move succeeds but does less work than its author thinks, say that too.

3. *Accuracy check on my notes.* Flag every place where you believe I have garbled what one of the books says, or where a claim I have reported needs checking rather than accepting. Do not supply quotations from either book, and do not invent page numbers or passages; if you want to gesture at something in the text, describe it.

4. *What they both missed.* Name two or three of the strongest arguments available in this dispute that neither Ruth nor Elias made. At least one of them must cut against whichever side you end up favoring.

5. *Bottom line.* Say which of the two accounts the working group should build the unit around — or whether the honest answer is that the framing itself should be abandoned, and if so what should replace it. Do not hedge toward a tie if the balance genuinely tips; if it tips, say at which moves it turned and why.
