I am a high school senior writing an extended essay for an independent research seminar, and I need to defend a thesis about the absent-minded driver problem that I find persuasive but have not yet been able to argue properly.

Fix the payoffs as follows. There are two exits that look identical from the road; taking the first pays 1, taking the second pays 4, and driving past both pays 0. The driver settles on his plan before setting out, but at an exit he cannot tell whether he has already passed one, so whatever he does at an exit he does at both. The difficulty usually reported is that at an exit the local calculation seems to favour taking it, even though a driver who reasoned that way all along ends up worse off than one who follows the best plan available to him, which involves randomizing.

My thesis is that none of this is an embarrassment for causal decision theory. The tension appears only because the best plan available to him is a lottery while the choice he faces at an exit is a single action, and the two are simply not comparable. As evidence: reverse the payoffs so that the first exit pays 4 and the second pays 1, and the best plan is just to take the first exit one reaches, at which point the tension disappears entirely.

Please give the strongest argument you can for this thesis, and then state and answer what you take to be the most serious objection to it.
