ok so this blew up at our department's grad reading group last week and I genuinely still don't know who's right. I'm a third year in political theory and we were on luck egalitarianism, and someone floated a case they'd half-invented for a term paper. Two people went at it for like twenty minutes. I'll try to reconstruct it faithfully, including the bits that sound contrived, because they were contrived and everyone knew it.

The case: Maya and Tess leave school with identical prospects. Tess takes a stable municipal job. Maya moves across the country to try and start a company, on the strength of a city-run incubator that had published survival and placement figures which later turned out to be badly inflated — nobody's alleging fraud, just careless boosterism. Maya's company folds after three years and she ends up back home earning considerably less than Tess. Stipulated detail: during those three years Maya developed what she now concedes are expensive tastes — she genuinely can't enjoy cheap food or cheap housing anymore, and the case builds in that these preferences are stable rather than strategic. Separately, Tess has developed a chronic illness whose treatment is ruinously expensive, entirely through no fault of her own. So Tess is better off on income and worse off on basically everything else.

The argument went roughly like this:

(1) Marcus said the verdict is obvious: Maya gambled and lost, that's option luck, and the whole point of the distinction is that she can't now make Tess underwrite a risk Tess declined.

(2) Priya said that assumes the gamble was informed, but Maya chose against numbers the city itself put out and never checked, so this is brute luck in an option-luck costume — and if the state supplied the misdescription, the state eats the cost.

(3) Marcus said that proves far too much: basically every risky choice rides on somebody's forecast, so if second-hand misinformation converts option luck back into brute luck, the distinction collapses into "you're only responsible when you were better informed than everyone else around you."

(4) Priya said fine, then make it about the source rather than the accuracy — a bad forecast from a private pundit leaves the choice yours, a bad forecast from the body that's supposed to be steering is a failure of the steering, not of the chooser.

(5) Marcus said he could live with that, but the expensive tastes are a seperate problem: those preferences were formed inside the failure Maya chose, so responsibility should propagate down the chain, otherwise every bad outcome launders itself into a fresh claim.

(6) Priya said that's bootstrapping — one choice can't be responsible for an unlimited amount of downstream psychic life — and Tess's illness is the reductio, since Tess's preferences adapted to her condition too and nobody wants to say Tess is on the hook for that.

The moderator moved on and we never came back to it. Can you go through (1) to (6) and tell me who's actually right at each step, not just who wins on aggregate? I'd want a rating per move — clearly right, defensible, questionable, clearly wrong — with a line or two of justification each, and then a verdict on which of them has the better of the exchange overall and on what the score would look like if you changed one stipulation (say, if the incubator's numbers had been deliberately inflated rather than merely sloppy).

Then the part I actually care about: tell me what both of them are failing to engage with. My hunch is that the real trouble in this case is something neither one names — I'm thinking it's about what the metric is supposed to be and over what stretch of a life you're permitted to measure whether someone's been unlucky — but I could easily be wrong and I'd rather you push back on me than just validate the hunch.
