I'm the treasurer of a small volunteer-run charity — we do a weekday lunch club and some homework stuff in a church hall, been going eleven years, one paid coordinator and about twenty volunteers. A volunteer who died in the spring left us £25,000, which for us is an enormous sum, and the trustees are split down the middle on what to do with it and we vote Thursday. The safe option is boring: it covers our coordinator's salary for about eighteen months, which keeps everything running exactly as it is now. The risky one is to spend the lot converting the hall kitchen into a proper commercial one and buying a second-hand refrigerated van so we can deliver meals to homebound people, which is what we've wanted to do for years. My own estimate, after going round and round with the diocese and two caterers, is maybe a 30% chance it takes off — in which case we'd reach roughly five times the people and become the kind of thing bigger funders will actually back — and a 70% chance the building regs and the delivery contracts eat us alive and we've spent everything, though we could sell the van back for maybe £4k. And if it fails there's no salary, so there's no coordinator, so realistically we close within the year.

One of the other trustees, Dave, does pricing analytics for an insurer and has taken it on himself to tell me that my preference for the safe option isn't just wrong but incoherent. His argument is that last month I happily signed off £600 of the float on a festival stall — a genuine coin flip between raising about £2,000 and raising nothing — so I obviously don't have diminishing marginal utility of money in any consistent sense, and he says he's read somewhere that refusing small bets is precisely what forces you to refuse absurdly good big ones, so since I don't do that I'm not really risk-averse at all, I'm just squeamish, and squeamishness isn't a reason. Take the higher expected value, end of story. Another trustee, more gently, made a different point: it isn't my money and it isn't my charity, we're deciding for people who aren't in the room, so my personal attitude to risk has no business entering the calculation and we should just maximize the expected number of people fed. I half agree with her and half don't and I can't tell which half.

Here's the bit I cant articulate and I'm hoping you can. It isn't that I care less about each extra thousand pounds. If failing meant we had a grim year and then carried on, I'd take the gamble in a heartbeat, genuinely. What I can't get past is the closing — the eleven years and the coordinator's job and the sixty-odd people who come on Tuesdays just stop, and it isn't a thing you can undo later when we're feeling braver. Dave says that's exactly what he means by squeamishness. The other thing I keep turning over: the losses land on people I can name and see every week, and the gains land on several hundred homebound people who aren't a group yet and will never know we existed, so whose risk is this even supposed to be — mine, theirs, the organization's? Tell me whether Dave has actually caught me in an inconsistency, or whether there's something coherent in my position that his argument slides right past — and then tell me what I should vote for. Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then give me the reasoning; no preamble and no restating my setup back to me.
