I've just been handed the animal ethics block of our third-year applied ethics module, which is an uncomfortable fit: for four years I worked at a wildlife rehabilitation centre, releasing animals back into populations where I expected most to die of starvation or predation within a season, and my new conservation colleagues insist that was the wrong instinct. Please write one essay question on wild animal suffering that a student can attempt in fifty minutes and that cannot be answered by recounting the standard debate over whether we should intervene in nature. The question should require students to state a principle governing when intervention in wild populations is called for, and then to run it over three cases: oral vaccination of wild animals against a newly emerged pathogen; contraceptive control of an overabundant herbivore population; and removal of a predator species from an island. A strong answer must say which of the three the principle licenses and which it forbids; must explain why the objection that we cannot foresee ecological consequences is not automatically a reason for inaction in all three; must say whether the fact that a harm arises naturally rather than from human activity can carry moral weight without begging the question; and must confront the possibility that preventing animals from existing at all looks like an improvement if their lives are net negative.

Give the question, a model answer of about 600 words, and a marking scheme out of 25 that names the specific moves an A answer must contain and the particular errors, with the marks each costs, that a competent but merely well-read B answer commits.
