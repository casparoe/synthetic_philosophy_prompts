I cover science and ideas for a magazine — mostly history-of-science and economics-adjacent stuff — and I've spent the last three weeks down a rabbit hole on that old chestnut, why did the Industrial Revolution happen in Britain and not in China, the Needham question / Great Divergence business. The piece I'm writing (~2,500 words, plus a graphic our designer has to be able to actually draw) isn't going to be another pass through coal and wages and institutions, because everyone has done that and my readers have too. What I've realised after a bunch of interviews and one extremely fractious workshop is that the interesting fight isn't between competing answers, it's about whether the question is even well posed — and the people having that fight are talking past each other in a way that's almost impossible to see from the inside. A philosopher told me the question was indeterminate, an economic historian told me it was loaded, somebody else told me it was perfectly fine but unanswerable in principle, and all three of them were convinced they were disagreeing with the other two. That confusion is the actual story, I think, and I want a diagram that makes it visible.

So: please build me a decision tree / taxonomy of the *positions on the question*, not of the people. Adopt the role of a philosopher of science who has also sat through a decade of economic-history seminars — someone fluent both in how contrastive "why P rather than Q?" explanations work (foils, difference-making versus background conditions, what fixes the unit of analysis) and in the divergence debate itself (the conjunctural coal-and-colonies account, the expensive-labour-plus-cheap-energy account, the institutions account, the culture account, the "there was no real divergence until very late" account, the "China was on a different but equally sophisticated path" account). Think carefully and don't skip the first stage: start with brainstorming. Give me a dozen or so candidate branch questions, say which ones you cut and why, then build the tree out of the survivors.

Constraints on the tree itself:
- six to eight branch nodes, twelve to fifteen leaves;
- every node phrased as a question a smart non-specialist could answer on the merits, without knowing which leaf it leads to — no node should be answerable just by naming a school or a scholar;
- every leaf gets a name (use the common shorthand if one exists, coin one if it doesn't) plus one sentence on its most awkward commitment and one sentence on what a newspaper story about the divergence would look like if it were written *from* that leaf;
- mark the combinations your space permits but nobody actually occupies as "unoccupied," and diagnose each: incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored;
- flag every pair of leaves your tree can't really separate, and say what extra question would seperate them;
- at least one branch has to turn on whether the foil is doing any work — i.e. whether "not China" is load-bearing or just decoration — and one leaf has to be the view that the question is secretly "why didn't China?" wearing a costume.

Here's the raw material I collected, and every one of these has to land somewhere in your tree; if two of them land on the same leaf, tell me they're the same position in disguise rather than papering over it. (1) "The question is fine, you're just asking it at the wrong scale — ask it about the Yangzi delta versus Lancashire and half the standard answers evaporate." (2) "It smuggles in an assumption about when the divergence started; date it to 1400 and it's a different question than dating it to 1850, so the answer you give tells you which date you'd already picked." (3) "A why-this-not-that answer has to cite a difference, but most of what gets cited — coal, secure property, a culture of useful knowledge — other Eurasian societies had in some measure too, and you can't tell which differences matter without running a counterfactual you can't run." (4) "China isn't doing any work in that sentence; what people want to know is why the breakthrough happened at all, anywhere, when it did. Britain is a placeholder for the fact and China is a placeholder for everyone else." (5) "Half of those answers only work by describing something China lacked, which means they've quietly changed the subject." (6) "The explanandum isn't an event, it's a trajectory, and you can't contrast trajectories until you say what makes two stretches of history the same trajectory — there's no principled way, so the question has no determinate subject." (7) "'Well-posed' isn't one thing — indeterminate, loaded, mis-scaled, and unanswerable are four different complaints and your sources are using them interchangeably."

Two house rules. No bibliography, no reading list, no titles, no page numbers, no "for more see" — I can't check any of it and my editor cuts that stuff anyway; shorthand labels are fine, attribution isn't. And don't invent sources or smooth over disagreements you aren't sure about: if you think one of my seven quotes above misstates something about the actual debate, say so plainly instead of working around it. Take your time on this. Finish with about 150 words aimed at a general reader explaining what changes, practically, about how you read any "why did some places get rich" story depending on which leaf you end up standing in.
