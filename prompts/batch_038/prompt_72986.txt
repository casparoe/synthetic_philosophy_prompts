I cover mathematics and the philosophy of science for a general-interest magazine, and I am drafting a piece on what independence results do and do not show about truth in set theory. A philosopher I interviewed handed me the argument below, which he offers as a reductio of the view that the continuum hypothesis is simply true or false. I cannot find the exit from it, and the piece stalls at step 9.

1. A mathematical claim is true when it holds in the intended structure; for set theory, that structure is the cumulative hierarchy V.
2. If a sentence S is independent of ZFC, then some models of ZFC satisfy S and others satisfy its negation.
3. By forcing, from a model of ZFC + CH one can build a model of ZFC + ¬CH, and conversely. The two constructions are on a par: nothing about V privileges one over the other.
4. Where two candidate structures cannot be told apart by any means available to us, there is no fact of the matter which one is intended.
5. Therefore CH has no truth value.
6. The same reasoning applies to Con(ZFC), the claim that ZFC is consistent, which is independent of ZFC by Gödel's second incompleteness theorem.
7. But there plainly is a fact about whether ZFC is consistent: either some particular finite string of symbols is a ZFC-proof of a contradiction, or it is not. That is a question about the natural numbers, and forcing leaves the natural numbers untouched — every transitive model has the same ω.
8. So (4) is false, and independence as such does not threaten determinacy.
9. Yet (4) was the only route to (5). If we keep determinacy, then CH is true or false in a way that lies in principle beyond any evidence we could ever have, and a truth like that does no explanatory work: the intended V is a decoration rather than a ground.
10. So either CH lacks a truth value, which (7) and (8) rule out, or it has one and the claim amounts to nothing.

For what it is worth, I would most like to keep (1) and (7): I do not want to end the piece telling readers that mathematics has no subject matter, or that we cannot say whether ZFC is consistent. Tell me which step fails, why it looked sound until you pressed on it, and what rejecting it costs — including what a single-universe realist and a multiversist each pay at that step.

Please answer with a table, one row per step from (1) through (10), with these columns: step; the claim in a single sentence; verdict (hold, reject, or equivocal); why it looks plausible; what it costs to reject. Below the table, give me a paragraph naming the one load-bearing error and how the argument manages to conceal it, plus a note on what I may safely assert in print without overclaiming.
