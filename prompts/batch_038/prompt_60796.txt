I'm teaching a 90-minute session in a second-year "minds, machines and metaphysics" module, 22 of them, most of whom have picked up the simulation hypothesis off reddit and think it's either obviously true or obviously stupid. I want an in-class game they actually play, that makes them run into the ancestor-simulation argument themselves and either feel it land or feel exactly where it doesn't. No lecture first, and they shouldn't hear Bostrom's three-way disjunction stated until the debrief. What I want them walking out seeing is which premise is doing the work, and that the move from "almost everyone is simulated" to "so I am" is a separate move.

Here's the conceit I have, tell me if its broken or make it better. Each small group is the governing council of a civilization, and every round they pick between not developing simulation tech, developing it under a cap, or developing it and running as many ancestor sims as they can afford. Simulated civilizations get their own turns and face the same choice, so the tree branches and by the end the class has physically built a census of persons on the tables, the overwhelming majority of them simulated. Then each student has to place a bet on whether they personally are one of the simulated ones.

Two twists I really want:

1. A damping rule. Someone always says "a civilisation that works out it's a sim will stop running sims," and I want that to be a dial they can actually turn, so they can find the setting at which the whole argument stops going through and see how much of the conclusion was riding on the ratio exploding.
2. The final bet has to be self-locating rather than about the world. Half the room will slide from a claim about the population to a claim about themselves without noticing, and I want the game's structure to expose that rather than the debrief just asserting it.

Give me the full rules and round structure, a timeline that leaves a real 20 minutes for debrief, cheap materials (index cards, dice, sharpies — no laptops, the room's wifi is bad and the projector doesn't work), and a payoff scheme for the bets that uses no real money but where they still care about winning. Then the debrief: map particular moments of play onto each premise, and name which premise the game quietly assumes instead of testing. Tell me what predictably goes wrong the first time I run it — the counting getting out of hand, a group that just refuses to develop the tech so nothing branches, someone refusing to bet, a fast group finishing three rounds early — and how to handle each. And be straight about what the activity can't reproduce: I already know it can't touch whether a simulation would be conscious at all, so tell me what else gets smuggled in by the fact that it's a game with points and a facilitator who built the tree.

Last thing: say how confident you are that this runs as described and teaches the right lesson, and single out the one piece of your design you're least sure about — the part most likely to fall flat or to hammer home the wrong point. I'd much rather you flag it than sand it over.
