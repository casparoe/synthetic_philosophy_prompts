my mum is in the later stages of dementia in a care home, and every month they hand me a quality-of-life form to complete as her family contact. One of the items is basically "does she still enjoy things," scored 0 to 4, and there's a couple of others about her mood and about whether she takes pleasure in activities versus in company. I have frozen up over it three months running. She never says she's enjoying anything and can't hold a minute in memory, and yet she reaches for her tea, she settles and taps in time when someone puts music on, and she turns away from the chaplain. I did a philosophy degree years ago and its become uncomfortably obvious to me that whatever I write on that form is me plumping for a theory of pleasure I've never actually articulated. So before I fill in the next one, give me the spec: what does any account of what pleasure is have to deliver to be adequate?

I want the whole requirements list, and for each one the case or consideration that makes it a requirement rather than somebody's hobby horse. Include the pressures coming from outside philosophy as well — the wanting/liking stuff, where the neural machinery for pursuing something and for enjoying it come apart, so you can have a rat (or a person) that works hard for a reward it doesn't like, and possibly likes things it doesn't seek; the fact that feeling good isn't the same as feeling activated, so an account that can't tell contentment from agitation is broken; and the fact that you can be completely absorbed in something and enjoying it without noticing that you are, which seems to wreck any view where pleasure has to be registered to exist. And the distinctions an account must not collapse: relishing a taste versus enjoying an activity versus being glad that something is the case; pleasure versus the relief of pain ceasing; a mood that colours a whole afternoon versus a single pleasant moment; taking pleasure in something versus approving of it.

Then the part I actually want most: tell me which of these requirements are non-negotiable and which are just the house style of one camp or another, and whether some subset of them is jointly unsatisfiable — because I have a hunch that "there is one felt quality common to all the things we call pleasure" and "the account has to cover being glad that your daughter came" and "you can be pleased without noticing it" cannot all survive together, and I'd rather be told which one gives way than be reassured. Also the aggregation problem, which is the one my form actually creates: it wants a single number, so if a theory insists pleasures are irreducibly heterogeneous and not comparable in intensity across kinds, does that make it useless for anything like my form — and is that a defect in the theory or in the form, and what would a theory have to add to license summing at all?

Then briefly, one paragraph and no more, how the main shapes of answer do against the list: pleasure as a distinctive feeling tone present in all of them; pleasure not as a sensation at all but as a manner or quality of what you're doing; pleasure as a propositional attitude of being pleased that p; pleasure as getting what you want, or as a disposition to keep something going. Flag which single requirement does the most work knocking candidates out.
