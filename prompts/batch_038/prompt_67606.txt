I write a monthly column on science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and I am currently reporting a long feature on how cheap it has become to read and write DNA. One formulation turns up in nearly every interview I do, in institutional press releases, and in the trade coverage of the field: DNA is a code — not a metaphor for one but a literal cipher, a table pairing three-letter words with amino acids — and because it is a code, the genome is a text; because it is a text, it can be read; because it can be read, it can be rewritten; and because it can be rewritten, an organism can in principle be specified in advance, the way software is specified before it is compiled. A synthetic-biology professor put the conclusion to me bluntly by phone: we now have both the alphabet and the grammar, so what remains are engineering problems rather than conceptual ones, and the people still arguing about what a gene *means* are fighting over vocabulary while the rest of the field ships results. A developmental biologist I spoke with the following morning accepts only the first clause; everything from "text" onward, she said, is doing work nobody has paid for, and the reason her side keeps insisting that genes have no privileged causal role in development is precisely that this sentence smuggles the privilege in. My editor has asked me to adjudicate the exchange in print, and I would rather not, because my working suspicion is that the two of them are disagreeing about a word — but I am aware that suspicion can be a way of avoiding the substance, so I want it tested rather than flattered.

Please audit the analogy as it is actually deployed. Give me the correspondence table for the strongest available reading: what in the cell plays the alphabet, the codewords, the key, the message, the sender, the medium, the reader, and the decoded output. Then tell me where the mapping fails, separating the disanalogies that are merely picturesque — the ones a critic recites to sound rigorous — from the ones that genuinely threaten the professor's inference. My specific hunch, which I want you to attack as hard as you can, is that the argument equivocates between two distinct senses of "code": a substitution cipher, meaning a fixed table mapping symbols of one alphabet onto symbols of another, and a program, meaning a set of instructions whose execution produces a specified result. If the hunch holds, say exactly what would have to exist in the cell for the program reading to be literally correct — who or what is the interpreter, where the instruction set resides, what fixes the reading frame, and what to make of the fact that the machinery doing the reading is itself built in accordance with the sequence it reads, so that the reader and the read cannot be prised apart the way hardware and software can be. If the hunch fails, tell me where the two senses turn out not to be separable, and what I should say instead. Either way, I want to know whether some weaker claim does survive the audit — for instance that the sequence is a highly reliable difference-maker, such that changing it predictably changes the outcome — and whether the analogy is best understood as an overreach from that weaker claim or as something else entirely.

Two further things, both because this is going into print. First, the details: I gather that even the cipher sense frays at the edges — that the same triplet is read differently inside mitochondria than in the nucleus, that a stop signal can be overridden to insert an exotic amino acid when a particular RNA element lies downstream of it, that ribosomes sometimes shift frame mid-message — and I want to know how much damage that does, and specifically whether it damages the cipher reading, the program reading, or both. A third interlocutor, a bioinformatician I met at a conference in Leipzig, offered what he thought was a strictly better computing analogy: the genome is not source code at all but a compiled artifact, the unreadable output of a very long optimization run, thick with duplicated libraries, dead code, and workarounds that made sense only in ancestral environments. Assess that variant on the same terms — whether it earns more of the conclusion or less, and what it costs, since a compiled artifact implies a source and a compiler that nobody has ever located. Second, two housekeeping requirements. Be explicit throughout about what is settled among people who work on this and what is genuinely contested; I have been told that the question of whether genes hold any special causal status in development is a live dispute rather than a closed one, and I do not want to report a contested position as though it were the consensus of biology, nor to present the professor's confidence as marginal when it may not be. Flag anything you are extrapolating beyond what is established. And begin your reply with your bottom line in a single sentence — whether the inference from "DNA is a code" to "the organism can be specified from the sequence" survives the audit — and only then give me the reasoning. No preamble, and no restating of what I have asked.
