I TA a philosophy-of-history seminar and every single year the same fight breaks out about what "inevitable" is even supposed to mean when a historian writes something like "after Sedan, the collapse of the Second Empire was inevitable." I've decided to build a whole session around it and I want you to compare two readings of inevitability and then tell me which one is doing the actual work.

Reading A — call it the robustness reading. To say X was inevitable is to say that from a wide range of nearby starting points, and across a wide range of different choices the actors might have made, you still end up at X. It's a claim about the shape of the space of possibilities: lots of paths converge on the same destination.

Reading B — call it the credence reading. To say X was inevitable is to say that an ideally well-informed observer at some earlier time t, reasoning correctly from the evidence actually available then, would have assigned X something very close to certainty. It's a claim about what the evidence supported, not about how the possibilities were arranged.

Here's what I need:

1. Show me that these come apart in both directions. Something robustly inevitable that no competent observer could have foreseen, and something an ideal observer would have bet the farm on that turns out not to be robust at all — one small change upstream and it doesn't happen. Worked case for each, real or invented is fine, and spell out exactly why each one satisfies A but not B or vice versa.

2. Then bring in path dependence, the increasing-returns / lock-in story people tell about keyboard layouts, technical standards, where a city ends up, how an institution gets stuck. It gets called inevitable constantly, but notice the shape of it: contingent at the beginning (some trivial early event tips the balance) and then locked in afterwards. Where does that live? Is lock-in a species of inevitability under either reading, or a third thing both readings mishandle? My hunch is its really a case where A and B pull apart in time — high robustness later, none of it earlier — and I want you to say whether that's right or whether its just an artifact of how I've framed the two readings.

3. Pick the reading you'd defend as the one historians ought to mean, and then run it against the two standard attacks. The Carr-flavored dismissal: history is about what happened, not what might have, and counterfactuals are unfalsifiable parlour games, so a notion of inevitability that trades on how other paths would have gone isn't a notion a historian can use. And the Cleopatra's-nose complaint: since anything is contingent on a pile of trivial antecedents, nothing is ever robust in the strict sense, so A collapses into "nothing was inevitable." Does your preferred reading survive both, and what does survival cost it?

4. Last one, and this is the part my students actually care about. Is there a normative residue here? People say "it was inevitable" when what they mean is "no one could have done otherwise," and I want to know whether either reading licenses that inference at all or whether it's a flat-out equivocation — and if it is an equivocation, which of the two readings gets abused that way more often and why.

Be concrete throughout, and if you think one of the readings isn't just different from the other but actually confused, say so and say where the confusion is.
