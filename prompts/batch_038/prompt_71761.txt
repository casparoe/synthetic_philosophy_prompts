I am drafting the term paper for my social epistemology seminar, in which I defend a modest version of the claim that democratic decision-making has an epistemic advantage over rule by a well-informed minority. The argument below is the backbone of my first section; it starts from Condorcet's jury theorem. A classmate who read the draft says the conclusion is far too strong to be believed, and that the argument therefore dismantles my own project. I cannot locate the offending step, and I would like a diagnosis rather than reassurance.

(1) Jury theorem: for a question with exactly two candidate answers, one of which is correct, if each member of a group independently has probability p > 1/2 of selecting the correct one, then the probability that a majority selects correctly exceeds p and approaches 1 as the size of the group increases.

(2) Hence, on any such question, the majority verdict of a sufficiently large electorate is as close to certain as makes no practical difference.

(3) A procedure whose verdicts are that close to certain is a better guide to the truth than any individual judge, however skilled, since every individual's probability of being right is strictly below 1.

(4) Hence, on any question that has a determinate correct answer, deferring to the majority is more reliable than deferring to an expert.

(5) Because (2) improves monotonically with the size of the electorate, reliability is maximized by enfranchising as many people as possible: those whose own p sits at 1/2 contribute only noise, which the millions of better-informed voters dilute, and even a voter below 1/2 makes negligible difference at that scale.

(6) Hence the franchise should be extended without restriction — to children, to the wholly uninformed, to anyone whatsoever — and expert agencies should be replaced by referenda wherever a question has a right answer.

(7) (6) is absurd, and I do not accept it.

(8) Therefore the jury theorem will not bear the weight I placed on it in (2) through (4), and the epistemic argument for democracy that depends on it collapses.

Tell me which step you reject and why it looked unobjectionable to me when I wrote it down. There are two I want very much to keep: (1), which is a theorem and whose combinatorics I have checked by hand for small groups, and (4), which is my thesis. If both can survive, specify what has to be added to (1) before it will support (4), and price each addition — in particular, whether a defender of democracy may assert those additions without begging the question against someone who prefers rule by experts. If they cannot both survive, say which one goes and where my attachment to it came from. Finally, classify the failure you identify: is it a formal error, an unstated assumption about actual electorates, or a shift in the sense of some expression between steps? And does (8) follow even once the failure is granted, or is it possible for the theorem to survive the collapse of my paper?
