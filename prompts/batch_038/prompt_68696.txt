I am a philosopher in residence at a neuroengineering institute, and next month I give the opening session of its internal seminar series. The room will hold about fifteen people: interventional neurologists, two neurosurgeons, and doctoral students building implantable memory and motor prostheses. They have no philosophy training, they are unusually sharp about mechanisms, and several already regard the substitution of a synthetic element that reproduces a neural circuit's input–output profile as an engineering milestone rather than a thought experiment. I have seventy-five minutes and a whiteboard. Nothing will be assigned in advance and nothing should be handed out; I would also rather the session not depend on any author's name or any book, since nobody in that room will look anything up afterward. The distinction I want them to leave with should be one they can state in their own vocabulary.

What I want them to understand is not whether uploading is feasible. It is that two questions their working assumptions keep fused have come apart: whether the person who wakes up after a replacement procedure is numerically the same person who went under, and whether that person has what they actually care about when they say they want to go on existing. Nearly everything else in the session is in service of prying those two apart.

The case I intend to carry the whole thing is one I will call shadow mode. Every neuron in a target circuit is replaced by a synthetic unit wired in parallel with it. For a long interval both are present and both compute, but only the biological unit drives downstream activity; the synthetic unit's output is suppressed and merely compared against the biological one, with any disagreement logged. At the end of the interval a single switch transfers the drive, after which the biological units are removed. By construction, functional organization is uninterrupted, no behavior changes, and the only moment at which anything about who is doing the driving changes is the switch itself. Two dials I can turn: the length of the shadow interval, from a millisecond to a decade; and whether the transfer is one switch or a slow ramp over which the weighting shifts gradually from biological to synthetic drive.

What I need from you is the plan for the session, not the session. Specifically:

1. The order in which the pieces must go in, with the dependencies made explicit — what has to be in place before the shadow-mode case is even intelligible rather than merely picturesque, and whether some piece that feels foundational must in fact be held back until after the case has done its work.

2. An assessment of shadow mode as the carrier. What must it have in order to work, what must it conspicuously lack, what will it inevitably suggest to this audience that I do not want suggested, and whether the dials are worth turning in front of them or are a distraction. If a simpler case does the job better, tell me so and give me the replacement.

3. A misconception map, ordered by the point in the sequence at which each one forms, with the move that heads it off. My guesses, which I expect you will revise and extend: that the question is empirical and a sufficiently good measurement would settle it; that because every step is small, no step could be the one at which anything is lost; that identical behavior makes further questions empty; that the whole issue is a dispute about words; and, from one or two of them, that I am asking about a soul.

4. The first ten minutes. At least two people in that room will classify the topic as a word game within a minute of my opening my mouth. How the opening either converts them or loses them.

5. Where the session will look as though it is cheating. There is a moment at which a reasonable listener will say that I have simply described a case in which nothing happens and then claimed a verdict about what matters. Locate that moment in the sequence and tell me what I do at it.

6. One question I can put near the end whose answers genuinely discriminate between having grasped the distinction and having nodded along — something this audience can be asked in front of their colleagues without humiliation, and where a particular kind of wrong answer is diagnostic rather than merely mistaken.

7. What I should cut, and what I should announce as cut. I have in mind dropping duplication cases, the teletransporter, and everything about the metaphysics of persistence over time, but tell me what else has to go and where in the room I should say aloud that I am leaving it out, so the omission reads as a decision rather than an oversight.
