Part of my job at a research institute is maintaining the review checklist we use to decide whether a candidate agent design's behaviour across a sequence of choices counts as a rationality defect or as merely unusual, and because we want the checklist to be principled rather than a pile of intuitions, we have been treating our past classifications as precedent and testing new submissions against them. Our record so far runs as follows. (1) An agent whose preferences cycle — she ranks A above B, B above C, and C above A — begins holding C and is offered three successive swaps, each for a small fee; she accepts every one, since at each point the offered option is the one she ranks highest, and she finishes holding C again, three fees poorer. Defect. (2) An agent with exactly the same cycling preferences faces the same sequence of offers, but at the first one she reasons ahead, sees that accepting sets her on a chain that returns her to C minus three fees, and declines; she keeps C and spends nothing. Not a defect, and we were explicit in the write-up that the cycling preferences by themselves were not what we were scoring. (3) An agent with cycling preferences adopts a plan at the outset to settle on B and to decline any later swap away from it; at a later node she is offered a free swap to A, which she then ranks above B, and she declines — not because she has worked out where accepting would lead, which she has not, but because she has already decided. The episode ends with B, which is what her plan was arranged to secure, and measured across the episode as a whole no policy available to her at the outset would have left her better off than the one she followed. Not a defect. (4) An agent with transitive, stable preferences and a settled plan learns something new partway through — the payoff attached to one option is revealed — and switches to the option she now prefers in light of that news, at no cost. Not a defect. (5) An agent with cycling preferences is placed in an environment that never offers her a swap of any kind; she is asked once which option she wants, she picks, and the episode ends. Not a defect, though we noted in passing that she had no occasion to reason about her own later choices. (6) An agent with transitive, stable preferences and full foresight of her own behaviour accepts a long chain of costless switches, each of which she prefers at the moment she makes it; no fee is paid to anyone, no information arrives, and she finishes with an outcome she ranks strictly below the one her original plan would have delivered. Defect.

The submission in front of us next week is close to a mirror image of (6) with the loss removed. This agent's preferences are transitive and stable, she has full foresight of her own later choices, and at the outset she adopts a plan to secure a particular outcome. At a later node, with no new information, no change in her preferences, and no change in what is available to her, she abandons the plan and takes a detour for which she can offer no reason at all. The environment happens to reconverge: the detour costs nothing — no fee, no elapsed time, no forgone payoff — and she ends the episode holding precisely the outcome her original plan was designed to secure. Nothing could have been extracted from her by anyone, and no alternative policy available to her over the course of the episode would have left her better off than the one she actually ran.

Tell me how we ought to classify her, and treat the question as an exercise in recovering our own rule rather than in guessing our mood. I want the full set of candidate principles that fit all six precedents simultaneously; my own hunch is that at least one of them makes the score depend on what can actually be extracted from an agent and another makes it depend on whether she overrode her own earlier decision without cause, but I cannot tell whether those are genuinely distinct or whether there is a third reading I have missed, and (3) and (5) are the two rulings I find hardest to square with anything I can write down cleanly. So: enumerate the candidates, note which of them our record has already eliminated and by which ruling, then say what each surviving candidate yields for the new case, name the precedent that is its closest analog and say exactly where that analogy strains, and commit to a classification with your reasons. Finally, since whatever we write down gets coded into an automated scoring rule that will run on hundreds of submissions, state what principle we would be adopting by ruling one way rather than the other — including what we would thereby be committing ourselves to flagging in future cases — and describe one further specific episode, concrete enough that we could run it, that would force the surviving candidates apart if we ever needed to settle the matter for good.
