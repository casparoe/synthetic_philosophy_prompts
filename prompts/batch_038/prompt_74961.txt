I have spent the better part of two decades in rationalist-adjacent comment threads, where the argument about what words are "about" comes up roughly once a month and almost never gets anywhere, and I have come to think the standard presentation of Twin Earth quietly hides the more interesting variable. As the case is usually run, you get a duplicate who is internally indistinguishable from the original but whose causal commerce is with a different stuff; meaning is not in the head; everyone goes home. The inverted case seems to me far richer and far less discussed: a speaker whose internal states come packaged with what look like causal connections to things she has never met, because those connections were installed in her from outside by a third party. Ridley Scott's *Blade Runner* is the best-worked-out dramatization of that inversion I know, and Rachael is not a Twin Earthling at all. She is someone whose entire causal history with the world has been supplied to her second-hand — the family photograph she uses to prove her humanity, the piano she plays in Deckard's apartment and the lessons she remembers taking, all of it borrowed from Tyrell's niece and fitted over an artifact that has never had a childhood. So I would like a reading of the film through semantic externalism: Putnam's Twin Earth argument and his division of linguistic labour, the causal-historical picture of how names and natural kind terms get anchored, and the familiar trouble about what happens when such a chain is acquired in some non-standard way.

There are several specific questions I want pressed rather than waved at. First: what, if anything, does 'mother' refer to in Rachael's mouth, and what does the photograph depict — the niece's mother, nobody, or something stranger? Lay out the options and say which you would defend. Second: is the Tyrell Corporation's advertising slogan, promising products that will exceed the humanity of their makers, best read as a claim about a natural kind with a hidden essence — in which case the Voight-Kampff test is a scientific instrument for detecting kind membership — or as a claim fixed by a cluster of capacities, in which case the test is a stipulation and the slogan is not obviously false? Does the film commit, or does it get much of its mood precisely from refusing to? Third: I take it her implants are a deviant causal chain of the sort that already embarrasses causal theories, but there is an asymmetry I have not seen handled anywhere — the chain is deviant from the borrower's standpoint and perfectly orderly from the lender's. Does that asymmetry buy the externalist anything, or is there a coherent third reading on which the implantation constitutes a genuine link, making Rachael a Twin rather than a counterfeit, and if so does that reading vindicate externalism or humiliate it? Fourth, separately: Batty's closing monologue turns on the loss of memories of events nobody else alive witnessed. On a causal-historical account, what happens to reference to those events when the last speaker hooked to them dies — does the film assume witnesses matter in a way the theory has no room for? And if you think the unresolved question about Deckard's own nature bears on any of this, take a stance on it rather than hiding behind it.

What I want is criticism, not summary: I know the film well and do not need the plot retold, and I would much rather be told where the externalist lens fails to fit than have it stretched over the whole picture. My own suspicion, which you should test rather than defer to, is that the replicant question in the film is fundamentally about personhood and moral status, and that a philosophy-of-language reading is a partial hijacking that leaves only a handful of scenes to work with. Tell me plainly how much of the film survives the reading and what the rest is doing instead. Finally, here is the analogy I keep reaching for and half-distrust. A language model's causal contact with words like 'mother' and 'piano' is entirely textual and second-hand, in a way that looks structurally like Rachael's rather than like yours or mine, which is exactly why people in those threads keep invoking her. I want you to say whether the film licenses that analogy or whether it quietly depends on something the analogy lacks — an embodied history, a body that can be tested, a maker who is present to be confronted. If the analogy is bad, say so and say why; I would far rather be corrected than flattered. No bibliography and no quoted dialogue, please: describe the scenes and I will supply the lines myself.
