I lead a six-week internal curriculum for engineers rotating onto our alignment team, and I'm writing the graded exercise for the week on agent architectures. I have no formal philosophy training, so I want the exercise framed around a concrete system rather than in the vocabulary of the literature.

Please write one problem-set question on approval-directed versus goal-directed agents, pitched at engineers who are fluent in reinforcement learning but have never read the primary sources, answerable in roughly 60 minutes and worth 20 marks. It should not be answerable by restating the definitions. The crux I want tested is that approval-directedness relocates the specification problem rather than dissolving it. To force that out, the question should first make students separate two readings of the target behavior: the action a human actually scores highly right now, versus the action a human would score highly if fully informed about the agent's internals and reasoning. Then have them show that the first reading gives the agent an instrumental reason to reshape the human's future scoring, since an easier-to-please human is a higher-reward human; and that the second reading forces the agent to fix how much information and how much reflection counts as ideal, a choice the approval signal itself cannot adjudicate. Close by asking them to assign each failure mode to one of the four categories of Goodharting — regressional, extremal, causal, adversarial — and defend the assignment.

Please deliver three things: the exercise exactly as students would see it, including a short system description concrete enough to reason about; a model answer of around 700 words that stands on its own argument rather than leaning on references; and a marking scheme that splits the 20 marks across five must-hit points, stating for each what a merely adequate answer says instead, and naming the errors that should cost marks outright — in particular treating approval-directed as a synonym for RLHF, and concluding that the informed-counterfactual reading solves the problem rather than trading one underdetermination for another.
