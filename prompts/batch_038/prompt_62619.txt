I'm a junior and I sit on my school's student senate, and next Thursday I get ten minutes at the open session to pitch a change to how we decide stuff. Right now every policy question — whether to move money out of the club budget into the spring trip, whether to ask the district for a later start time, that kind of thing — goes to a vote of the whole student body through a google form that's open for two days. My proposal: kill that and replace it with a "student jury." 24 kids picked at random off the roster each month, they recieve a briefing packet a week ahead, they deliberate two evenings, then they vote by simple majority and whatever wins is binding on the senate.

My whole argument leans on the Condorcet jury theorem, which I learned about from a book my brother left at our house plus alot of videos. As I understand it: if each person is more likely than not to get a binary question right, then the bigger the group the more likely the majority is to be right, and it approaches certainty as the group gets huge. I'm planning to say four things — (a) random selection beats elections because kids who run for senate just campaign and tell everyone what they want to hear, (b) a poll we ran last semester showed 63% of students got a factual question about the budget right, so our competence is above a half and we're in the regime where the theorem applies, (c) 24 jurors who actually read the packet will make better calls than 900 kids voting in forty seconds on their phones, and (d) the deliberation is what pushes each individual juror's accuracy up, so it's a bonus stacked on top of the theorem.

I don't want a defense of my idea, I want prep for the question period. The room is specific: our faculty advisor Mr. D, who teaches AP stats and will make me show the math on the spot; Ms. Okonjo from social studies, who is way into sortition and citizens' assemblies and will mostly be on my side but might push me on things I haven't thought about; the current student body president, whose job I'm basically proposing to make pointless, who cares about legitimacy and whether anyone will accept what the jury decides; and two kids who write for the school paper and love finding holes. Give me the objections those people will actually raise, in roughly the order I expect them, phrased the way they'd phrase them — not the ones I'd get in a philosophy class. For each one: how much damage it does, a reply I can say out loud in under a minute, and where that reply is honestly weakest. Then tell me which of my four points (a through d) I should drop or soften before somebody else does it for me, and finish with the single question I'm praying nobody asks. Keep the whole thing under 650 words, I need to be able to memorize it on the bus.
