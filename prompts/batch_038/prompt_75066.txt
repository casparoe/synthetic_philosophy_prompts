I'm on a working group for a city charter commission that's about to bolt a binding citizen-initiated measure process onto a local government of about 400,000 people — basically the California initiative transplanted somewhere it has no business being — and before we wade into the literature on whether referendums are a good idea at all, I want the spec. Write out the desiderata: what any adequate account of *when a referendum produces a legitimate, action-authorizing collective decision* has to deliver. Not arguments for or against direct democracy. The requirements list.

For each item, give me the case that motivates it, one or two lines is plenty — I want to be able to point at something real in a meeting and say "any account that can't handle this is out." Cases I'd expect to see, though you should add ones I've missed: the UK's 2016 vote (52-48, one binary question, nothing on the ballot about what leaving actually meant, then four years of arguing about what had been authorized); California's Prop 13 and the way an initiative can install a fiscal straightjacket the legislature can't undo without going back to the voters; the Swiss setup — double majority of people and cantons, initiatives that can only amend the constitution, the parliamentary counter-proposal with its tie-breaker question, and the 2009 minaret vote which raised whether there's content a popular initiative is simply not allowed to have; the Irish habit of running a question through a citizens' assembly or convention first, as with 2015 and 2018; Australia 1999, where plenty of people who wanted a republic voted no because of how the model on the ballot got chosen, so the wording problem and the mandate problem turned out to be the same problem; the Italian turnout quorum, which turns staying home into a campaign strategy; the Scottish 2014 franchise fight over 16-year-olds and who was left off the roll.

Some of these we allready have shorthand for, feel free to use it: **mandate specificity** (how much detail does a Yes vote license the winners to go implement), **reversal asymmetry** (whether undoing a measure is harder than passing one, and whether that's a bug or a feature), and what I've been calling **the twenty-six percent problem** — a measure that carries with 52% of a 50% turnout is the choice of 26% of the electorate, and I want to know whether that's a genuine defect in the account or just a cost we accept everywhere else in democracy too.

Also tell me which distinctions a theory is not allowed to collapse: legitimacy of the process versus wisdom of the outcome, procedural versus epistemic legitimacy, authorization versus instruction, advisory versus binding, "the people" versus "those who showed up," a one-off decision versus a standing rule. If a view slides between two of these mid-argument, that's a failure of the desiderata, not a rhetorical quirk.

Then the part I actually want: which of these are non-negotiable for anything that deserves the name, and which are the settled preferences of one camp dressed up as requirements. My suspicion is that a lot of the standard complaints — too emotional, question too vague to authorize anything, voters too ignorant of the details — are doing work for people who allready think legislatures should be sovereign, and I'd like that flagged rather than smuggled in as a requirement. And say plainly whether some subset is jointly unsatisfiable. I have a feeling that popular control, a deliberated and informed outcome, decisiveness/finality, protection of the losing minority, and revisability can't all be had at once, and if that's right then the list is itself the result and our commission should stop pretending we can write rules that satisfy everybody.

Finally: which single desideratum does the most work knocking out existing positions — the one that, granted, collapses most of the field. And then briefly, five or six lines total, how three camps score: the plebiscitary view (a majority vote is self-validating, full stop), the epistemic view (legitimate insofar as it tracks the right answer, jury-theorem flavoured), and the constitutionalist view (referendums are at best advisory or a veto, never a way to make new law). Keep the scorecard short, the criteria are the deliverable.

No reading list or citations please, we've got more of those than we can use. Numbered list or table, whatever makes the requirements easy to argue about.
