I'm a trustee at a small family foundation (about $12M a year, $90M in assets) and I sit on the grants committee. We meet Thursday to allocate $2.4M and the rule has, uh, broken. Proposal A is boring and good — a well-evaluated maternal health program, $2.4M, staff estimate 40,000 life-years, 90% interval roughly 30k to 52k, no catastrophic downside anybody can identify. Proposal B is a five-year program to get an early-warning and containment capability for engineered pathogens in place ahead of the curve. Our staff economist modeled it as a race that gets decided at some stage n: probability 1/2^n that stage n is the decisive one; if n is odd we end up ahead of the curve and the payoff is roughly 2^n/n hundred-thousand life-years; if n is even we've accelerated the thing we were trying to slow and the payoff is the same magnitude with a minus sign on it. (Her rationale is that the exposed population roughly doubles each stage with the technology's reach, while our leverage falls off like 1/n.) So each scenario contributes ±1/n units to expected value and the total is 1 − 1/2 + 1/3 − 1/4 + ... = ln 2 ≈ 0.693 units ≈ 69,300 life-years, which beats A, so B wins under our rule.

Except she then told us the series only converges conditionally — she called it "the Pasadena thing" and wouldn't elaborate in front of the board — and a consultant we brought in confirmed the trouble. Group the scenarios two favorable for every unfavorable and you get about 1.04 units; go one-for-two and you get about 0.35 units, i.e. 34,700 life-years, which is *below* A. There's also no "nothing happens" state in her model at all, and by stage 20 she has us either saving or destroying about five billion life-years, which seems obviously silly but that's what the exponential does.
