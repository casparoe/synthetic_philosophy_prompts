I'm TAing a senior seminar on well-being and I've got a take-home problem set to mark tonight. Here's the question I set:

**Question (25 marks).** Hedonism about well-being says that what makes a life go well is the balance of pleasure over pain in it. That looks like it presupposes pleasure is one thing, or one kind of thing, which turns up across very different experiences. But the pleasure of cold water when you're parched, the pleasure of suddenly seeing how a proof goes, the pleasure of a long hot bath, and the pleasure of watching someone you dislike fail seem to have nothing in common. State this heterogeneity problem as precisely as you can, then assess two responses to it. For each response, say whether it saves hedonism or changes the subject.

Mark scheme I gave them: 8 for stating the problem, 12 for the quality of the assessment of the responses, 5 for actually delivering the "saves it or changes the subject" verdict.

And here's the answer:

---

The heterogeneity problem is the worry that hedonism rests on a false picture of pleasure. The classic picture, which you find in Bentham and Sidgwick, is that pleasure is a single kind of feeling, a distinctive hedonic tone that gets attached to different experiences the way a colour might get attached to different shapes. If that were right hedonism would be a substantive thesis: there'd be one good-making property, it would come in degrees, and you could in principle weigh lives against each other. The trouble is that the picture looks false. Ryle argued that pleasure isn't the name of one feeling at all — enjoying a joke, enjoying a hot bath and enjoying the smell of coffee are related only the way members of a family are, by overlapping similarities with no single feature running through all of them. If Ryle is right, hedonism never gets off the ground, because it isn't a claim about any one property.

One response is Feldman's. He separates sensory pleasure from attitudinal pleasure: what unifies the good life isn't a feeling but a stance, being pleased that such-and-such is the case. Pleasure is then one thing after all, because taking pleasure is one kind of attitude however different the objects are. This does answer Ryle, but I think it changes the subject. What the hedonist wanted was an account of why the hot bath and the good joke are both good, and being pleased that a proposition is true doesn't obviously capture that. It also seems to leave out animals and infants, who clearly enjoy things without having many attitudes toward propositions.

The better response comes from affective neuroscience. Berridge's work separates liking from wanting and locates liking in tiny hedonic hotspots in the nucleus accumbens and ventral pallidum, which can be switched on independently of wanting. So the heterogeneity is only apparent at the surface: underneath, all these different pleasures involve the same core affective reaction, and that is the common factor the hedonist needs. It also explains what bothers us about Nozick's experience machine — what matters is that the liking be caused by the world actually being a certain way rather than by an electrode, and the machine fails that condition.

One consequence worth noting: if there were no common factor, pleasures couldn't be measured on a single scale, so a hedonist couldn't say whether a life of many mild pleasures beats a life containing one intense one. Since Ryle's view denies the common factor, it can't make that comparison at all.
