I'm in a reading group on the philosophy of time — six of us, mostly philosophers, plus one member who is a postdoc analyzing gravitational-wave data — and he has a stock way of explaining why the Rietdijk–Putnam argument ought to settle things. Spacetime, he says, is a box set. Every episode is on the disc whether or not anyone has watched it; the disc was pressed long before you bought it, and nothing you do in the living room alters its contents; the playhead is simply where the viewing happens to be, which is a fact about the watching and not a fact about the disc. On his telling, the Andromeda case — Penrose's version, in which for one of two people passing each other in the street "an Andromedean space fleet has already set off on its journey," while for the other the decision whether to send it has not yet been made — shows only that different observers have their playheads at slightly different places on the same disc. He draws two conclusions. First, that the future exists in just the way the past does, so nothing in it is genuinely open, and our sense of weighing real alternatives is an artifact of where the playhead happens to sit. Second, that anyone still disputing whether the present is metaphysically special is arguing about the furniture of the room rather than about the disc.

I would like an audit of the box-set analogy before I concede either conclusion. Please set out the correspondences explicitly — disc, episodes, playhead, viewer, the pressing of the disc in advance, the ability to scrub forwards and backwards — saying for each what in Minkowski spacetime it is meant to be and how good the fit is. Then sort the disanalogies into those that actually bear on his conclusions and those that are cosmetic, and take up three in particular: whether a disc's canonical track order has any counterpart at all when simultaneity is frame-relative and there is no privileged foliation for a playhead to travel along; whether "already" in the analogy is being made to do indexical work from a standpoint outside the disc's own time that the target cannot supply, especially given the objection one of us pressed, that the present in relativity is a local notion which resists being stretched into a global hyperplane; and whether the pressed-in-advance feature of the disc smuggles in determinism, which is not what an argument from relativity is supposed to deliver.

Then tell me which conclusions survive once the disanalogies are on the table — in particular whether eternalism on its own supports anything like the fatalist gloss, or whether that was riding entirely on the authored and already-recorded features of the source, and whether his treatment of the presentism dispute is a neutral description of the dialectic or the eternalist thesis restated in friendlier clothes. If the box set is the wrong image, propose better ones and cost them out: what does a landscape you travel through, in which distant cities exist unvisited, capture that the disc misses, what does it break in turn, and would a four-dimensional worm or a light-cone structure be an improvement. Finally, please keep the status of each claim visible throughout: separate what is actually settled — what special relativity does and does not establish about distant simultaneity — from what remains live dispute among people who work on this, including whether the Rietdijk–Putnam argument succeeds and whether presentism can be made compatible with relativity. If my friend's confident gloss is one side of an open argument rather than an established result, say so plainly instead of presenting it as the consensus view.
