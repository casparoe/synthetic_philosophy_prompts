I'm a distributed systems engineer, eleven years in, event-sourced backends mostly. Never taken a philosophy class. I picked up Dennett and Kinsbourne's "Time and the Observer" (1992) and half of it reads like my day job, which is either a great sign or a sign im missing something.

The setup, as I understand it. There's an apparent-motion experiment (color phi): a dot of one color flashes at one spot on a screen, then a fraction of a second later a dot of a different color flashes a short distance away. At some spacings and delays, people report seeing a single dot travel between the two spots and change color partway along the path. The problem: the second dot hasn't flashed yet at the moment the traveling dot is supposedly already changing color, so where does the midpoint color change come from? Two answers get floated. The Orwellian one: what actually got presented was "dot here, then dot there," and the record was edited afterward, so the subject sincerely describes a trajectory they never had. The Stalinesque one: nothing got presented at all until after the second flash, at which point the whole motion-plus-color-change was staged in one go, so the report is honest, just late. Then the paper argues you can't tell these apart, and there's no further fact about which one happened, because there's no single place and time where the show gets screened.

My mapping — check it, don't just accept it. We have an append-only log and consumers that build projections off it. When something arrives out of order or gets superseded we have exactly two remediation paths in the codebase, `amend()` and `hold()`. `amend()` rewrites history: compact out the superseded event so no downstream reader ever sees it, and the projection ends up as though the bad event never happened. `hold()` buffers: don't commit until we have enough information to write the correct event once, and publish a watermark so nobody reads past it. Both produce an identical final state for every consumer. We also have an old, tired argument about whether, when two writes are concurrent and nothing downstream can distinguish the two orderings, that's one history or two. I say one history; my staff engineer says thats just an observability failure wearing a metaphysical costume.
