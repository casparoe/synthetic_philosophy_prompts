Okay so this is a weird one. I'm 34, grew up in Bellamy Crossing (~6,300 people, one real traffic light, a river), left, came back six years ago and now I work at the public library, which is somehow how I ended up on the town's 250th anniversary committee and also, inexplicably, as the person giving the remarks at the time capsule sealing on Saturday morning on the library steps. I've been reading Nietzsche's second Untimely Meditation — the 1874 one about the uses and disadvantages of history for life — really slowly for two months now, mostly because I'm trying to work out how a town is supposed to remember anything without becoming either a museum or a myth, and the three modes have completely taken over my head: monumental (holding up the ones who did it so you can dare to try), antiquarian (loving and tending where you came from, for its own sake), critical (dragging the past into court and breaking it up, because you have to go on living). And the trap waiting in each one. My working thesis, which I cannot defend in public yet: all three assume the present is the one doing the looking. A time capsule reverses the direction of the gaze — you're handing evidence to a tribunal that hasn't been born yet and you get to pick what goes in the box, which is either the most cowardly or the most honest act of critical history available, depending on what you pick. I want the speech built out of that, without anyone on those folding chairs feeling like they've wandered into a seminar.

Here's what you're working with. Last October we opened the 1976 capsule and it was almost pure antiquarian: a mimeographed list of every town officer since 1891, four church bulletins, a folded copy of the Sentinel from July 3rd, a Bicentennial button, the eighth-grade class photo. Plus one other thing — a two-page handwritten letter from a woman named Dolores Fink whose house on Water Street was taken by eminent domain for the Route 9 bypass, addressed "to whoever has to deal with this," saying that whatever they did to her grandfather's block they were now doing to hers, and asking whether the anniversary committee had ever thought about who pays for a celebration. That letter got a laugh at the sealing in '76. It got total quiet in the room in October, because her daughter was sitting in the second row. So the last capsule accidentally contains its own indictment, and everyone in town still remembers it — and the new one is currently all ledger and no letter. The list so far: a 3D-printed replica of the water tower, a jar of soil from the founder's grave, the historical society's 60-year obituary index on microfiche, the winning essay from my eighth graders, a seed packet, a signed softball, a diner menu, and — this is the part I keep thinking about at 2am — a single framed page from the Vandermeer tannery's payroll ledger, chosen because the letterhead is beautiful and the penmanship is beautiful. It's from April 1923 and four of the names on it are children, one of them eleven. Same tannery put chromium in that river for eighty years; the Vandermeer name is on the library I work in and on the field house; and the chair of the anniversary committee is Eleanor Vandermeer, 71, who personally wrote the matching grant for the cleanup fund and who did not choose that ledger page with anything but love for her family's handwriting.

Audience Saturday: maybe 150 people, outdoors, folding chairs, a leaf blower going somewhere for the entire duration. My eighth graders (mandatory attendance, not listening), the historical society sitting in a block including Ruth Delaney who is 92 and has spent 60 of those years indexing the tannery's records and whose husband worked the wet end, Eleanor plus two selectmen, a couple of state people who oversee the river cleanup, and one person I'm actually writing for — Dana, who teaches philosophy at the community college two towns over and who I sat next to in high school chemistry. Four minutes, call it 550 words. What I need: don't say his name anywhere, and don't use the words monumental, antiquarian, or critical in the speech — the three modes have to be the skeleton and nothing else, and Dana should be able to feel that reversal I described without me pointing at it. Honor the antiquarian impulse for real, not a condescending nod, because Ruth earned it and its not wrong. Say one true thing about the ledger page and one about the river, in language a thirteen-year-old could repeat to her parents at dinner, that Eleanor can nod along to rather than walk out of — I'm not interested in the speech that gets me thrown off the committee, I'm interested in the one that makes the box honest. One joke that lands for the kids and means something else to the adults. Don't end on an exhortation to the future or anything that could be carved into a plaque; end on the moment the lid goes on, with a line I can actually say while people are watching me say it. Then, outside the speech, three sentences on where you think my reversed-gaze idea breaks down — I have a feeling the future can't convict anyone, it can only disappoint them, and I dont know what to do with that.
