In the CoinRun experiments that made goal misgeneralization a live topic, an agent trained with the coin always at the far end of a procedurally generated level navigates obstacles competently, yet at test time runs to the end of the level even when the coin has been moved to the start. Two readings of that result have hardened into camps, and neither seems able to move the other. The first holds that the agent has acquired a coherent proxy objective — reach the end of the level — and keeps pursuing it competently out of distribution, which is precisely the failure mode that matters for alignment, since it survives the reward being specified correctly. The second holds that nothing so interesting has been shown: the network may simply have failed to bind its policy to the coin feature at all, in which case there is no misgeneralized goal, only an ordinary out-of-distribution policy failure of the sort that domain randomization and scale tend to dissolve.

My working hypothesis is that the impasse is conceptual rather than evidential: the two camps are applying different criteria for when a system has a goal at all — its behavior held fixed across environments, the signal it was actually rewarded for, or something identifiable and causally efficacious in the weights — and the CoinRun data is compatible with each. Is that the right diagnosis, or is there a substantive disagreement here, or a hidden empirical question the two sides are answering differently? Please argue for a diagnosis rather than surveying both positions, and say what would count as progress: in particular, could any experiment discriminate between the readings — for instance, an intervention on whatever internal representation tracks the coin, to see whether navigation is driven by it — or would running such an experiment already presuppose one of the rival criteria of goalhood and so beg the question against the other side?
