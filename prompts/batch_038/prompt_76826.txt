I report on science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and I am currently working on a piece about mitochondrial replacement therapy — the form of in vitro fertilisation, lawful in Britain since Parliament approved it in 2015 and more recently in Australia, in which some or all of a future child's mitochondrial DNA comes from a third party. In the version my reporting has focused on, maternal spindle transfer, the spindle–chromosome complex is lifted out of the intended mother's egg and inserted into a donor egg whose own nucleus has been removed; that reconstructed egg is then fertilised. A small number of children have now been born this way in Britain.

Trying to get the metaphysics straight for a general audience, I put the case to a bioethicist, who responded not with an answer but with a twelve-step argument she said was widely accepted and that she herself endorsed. I have reconstructed it below as faithfully as I can. My difficulty is that every step sounded defensible when she said it, and the conclusion is something I cannot print as true.

(1) Origin essentialism: for any person P and any biological origin O, if P originates from O, then in every world in which P exists, P originates from O. Origins are essential, not accidental.
(2) An origin is a particular pair of gametes, and a gamete's identity is fixed by its own history: which body it matured in, which precursor cell it descended from, and which chromosomes that cell sorted into it at meiosis.
(3) Origin essentialism applies to gametes as well as to persons, so a given egg could not have carried a different chromosomal complement than the one it actually carried.
(4) Hence the egg and sperm that produced any given person could not have existed at all unless the meiotic events that made them unfolded exactly as they did.
(5) Those events unfold as they do only because of the state of the body they occur in, and that state is the cumulative upshot of everything that has happened to the body — its nutrition, its illnesses, its sleep, the hour of the night in question.
(6) Therefore no person could have come into being under circumstances materially different from the actual ones.
(7) Consider now any act that would have altered those circumstances: a war, the closure of a clinic, a change in tax or leave policy that pushes childbearing back by three years.
(8) In any world in which such an act is performed, by (6) none of the people who actually exist exist at all, and by (1) whoever does exist there is not one of us differently situated but someone else entirely.
(9) So no act of that kind can harm or benefit any actual person; the only individuals it bears on are ones who, had the act been performed, would never have existed.
(10) For anyone whose existence turns on such an act, the comparison between existing and not existing lacks a subject on one side, and so cannot be a comparison of better and worse for that person.
(11) It follows that no one is ever made better or worse off by existing.
(12) Therefore the comparative judgements my desk asks me to canvass whenever a government announces a fertility or population policy — that one possible future population would be worse or better than another — are not false but unintelligible.

Three things I should tell you about my own stance, since I have been asked to declare it. I want very much to keep (1) and (2): they are what makes the spindle-transfer material philosophically interesting rather than merely technical, and abandoning them would leave me with no way to describe what the technique does to a child's origins. I cannot accept (12), and I have come to distrust (11) as well. Step (6) makes me uneasy in a way I cannot articulate.

Separately, and I raise this only because it is the reporting I actually have: spindle transfer seems to leave it indeterminate which egg is the origin. The nuclear material came from one woman's egg; the cytoplasm and mitochondria came from another's, from which a nucleus had been removed; the resulting egg matured and was fertilised outside either body. If (1) presupposes that there is exactly one determinate O for each person, MRT children look like counterexamples. If instead the origin is said to be the nuclear material, then (2)'s account of gamete identity in terms of maturation in a particular body looks wrong, since the material in question left one body's egg and completed its development in another's.

What I would like from you:

- Identify which step or steps the argument actually fails at, and say for each whether the failure is one of validity or of a premise that is false or unwarranted. Do not simply list everything that could be doubted; rank what you find, and say which single failure is load-bearing, in the sense that repairing it would let the rest stand.
- For each step you reject, explain why it looked acceptable in the first place. I need this more than I need the refutations, because my readers will find (4), (6) and (10) intuitively compelling and I have to be able to say what makes them seductive rather than merely wrong.
- Price the alternatives. Suppose one insists on keeping (1) through (6) intact: which of (7)–(12) must go, and what does giving it up cost in terms of what we can then say about policies that change who is born? Suppose instead one gives up (2) or (3): what happens to origin essentialism for persons, and does (1) survive without them?
- Tell me whether the spindle-transfer material does any real work against the argument, or whether there is a reply available to the bioethicist that I have not thought of. If there is such a reply, state it in its strongest form and then say whether it survives.
- Finally, and this matters for how I write: please be explicit about how confident you are in your diagnosis, and single out the point in your own answer that you are least sure of — the place where a competent philosopher on the other side of the question would most likely say you have got it wrong.
