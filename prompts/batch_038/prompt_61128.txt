I'm a third-year history student, and this term's seminar has been on nationalism and the construction of historical memory — Hobsbawm and Ranger on invented traditions, Anderson on the nation as an imagined community. Somewhat to my own amusement I have ended up on the other side of the reading: last spring I proposed a new matriculation ceremony to my college, the fellows approved it, and on Friday I give the opening remarks at its first-ever performance. The room will hold about four hundred people — the incoming first years, their parents, faculty, and my seminar tutor, who has watched me write about exactly this problem for three months.

Please write those remarks: four to five minutes, to be spoken aloud by a student rather than read like a paper. Two moves are closed to me. I cannot gesture at antiquity, or allow anyone to suppose the ceremony is older than it is; several people in the room helped draft it. Nor can I simply observe that every tradition had to begin somewhere, which is the line I would reach for if I were lazy — that gets me off the hook of the interesting problem instead of solving it.

What the speech must do is show, without lecturing and without naming any theorist, why four hundred people who will mostly have left this college within three years should take themselves to be bound to cohorts of students they will never meet and who do not yet exist; and why performing the same words and gestures again next year, and the year after, is what makes that binding real rather than leaving it as one pleasant evening. My tutor will be listening for whether I think candour about the invention disables the thing, or whether candour is precisely what allows it to work — take a position on that and commit to it. It has to land for the parents, who have never heard of any of this, and satisfy him.
