For nine years I was an auditor, and I still cannot think about questions of value except in terms of ledgers: entries that are large, entries that are small, and — which is a different category altogether — entries that are out of scope. I am now auditing a graduate seminar in population ethics, and next week's session concerns views that refuse a single scale of value: negative utilitarianism in its lexical form, and more broadly whether pleasure and pain are commensurable at all. The instructor has asked each of us to open the hour with something that is not an argument, on the ground that these views are usually attacked with trade-off intuitions before anyone has felt what it is like to hold one. I am bringing a poem, and I would like your help writing it. If the auditor's sensibility can be made into the poem's engine rather than its decoration, so much the better.

Here is the constraint I have in mind, and I would like it kept exactly. The poem should run to twenty-four lines. The first twenty must be strict ten-syllable lines, so that the syllable count is the poem's unit of account and the whole passage reads as an accumulating arithmetic: line after line of modest, entirely ordinary well-being being entered into a running total, with the count itself felt — the speaker may tally aloud, may keep a figure in the margin of the sense, or may do both. At line twenty-one the poem admits a single instance of intense suffering, and from there the syllabic contract is void: the remaining four lines may run to any length whatever. The reason I want this form rather than another is that the break has to be legible as cancellation and not as outweighing. On an ordinary utilitarian scale the agony wins because it is heavier on the same scale; the counting continues and the sign merely flips. On the lexical view the two hundred syllables already banked have no purchase on it at all — they are not outbid, they are out of scope — and the reader should feel that the measuring has stopped rather than that the measuring has come out the other way. That difference is the whole content of the view, and it is the thing I most want the form to carry.

As to tone and content, please do not let the poem become advocacy, and do not let the suffering arrive as a rhetorical trump delivered with relish; I want the cost of the position present as well. Somewhere in the four broken lines the count should try to resume and fail — the intuition that there is, after all, a common measure, and that a bad hour is worse than a good one without being infinitely so, deserves its say, and it should go unanswered rather than refuted. I would also like the pressure on the cutoff to be visible: what happens one notch below the line, since a sharp boundary looks arbitrary and a blurred one seems to let the trading back in at the margins. Keep the technical vocabulary out of the poem entirely — no "lexical," "threshold," "utility," "hedonic," "commensurable," and no philosophers' names anywhere in the verse. If you wish, you may close on a single syllable, but only if that reads as the ledger being shut and not as a punchline. After the poem, add no more than four sentences of commentary telling me which line the break falls on, what you take the cancellation to consist in formally, and where in the last four lines the opposing intuition is voiced.
