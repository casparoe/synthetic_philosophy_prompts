I'm a third-year philosophy major currently taking a seminar in the philosophy of language, and this week we finished the unit on Russell's theory of descriptions — the analysis itself, plus the standard round of complaints about it. Outside the seminar I work two shifts a week on the copy desk of a small regional daily, which is why an idea occurred to me for the creative option on our final project (my instructor permits non-standard formats in place of the essay, provided the piece does real philosophical work). I would like you to write the centerpiece of that submission: a satirical in-house style guide, roughly 900 words, issued by the paper's newly appointed copy chief, a former logic lecturer who has decided that the newsroom's endemic use of definite descriptions is both a philosophical error and a legal liability. The voice should be deadpan, bureaucratic, and utterly unselfconscious — a real style manual written by someone who believes every problem in journalism is a problem of logical form — and the satire should never wink or explain itself. Please format it as numbered rules under short headings, with before-and-after examples drawn from the paper's own sections, and include one sidebar reproducing several actual copy-desk queries sent back to reporters that week. There should also be a plausible institutional motive running through it: the legal department has embraced the policy because claims about there being exactly one such-and-such are checkable, and a libel suit over a superlative is expensive.

The comedy has to come from getting the philosophy right rather than from random absurdity, so please build in the following beats. First, the general prohibition and the mandated paraphrase, along with the practical consequence that a reporter may not write a description whose uniqueness she cannot verify — which makes ordinary work on the metro desk, the obituaries, and the sports pages nearly impossible. Second, a rule about where negation belongs, i.e., the difference between the two readings a negative sentence involving a failed description can have, illustrated with a headline about a monarch nobody has. Third, a short policy section covering the paper's most exposed franchises: the horoscope column, the restaurant reviewer who insists on naming the best meal in the county, and the local history feature series. Fourth, a letter to the editor objecting that a story containing a non-referring description was not so much false as a failure to say anything at all, together with the copy chief's curt published reply. Fifth, a rule for the case where a reporter is looking directly at the person a description is supposed to pick out, in a courtroom story, and it is not settled that the description is true of him. Sixth, a caution about reports of what officials believe, and why the desk may not swap one co-referring description for another inside them. Finally, the piece should end by undercutting itself: the guide should violate its own central rule, or concede in a closing note that the desk cannot state the rule at all without using the banned construction.

A few constraints. The target of the satire is the doctrine that logical form is the real content of ordinary sentences, and the practice of regimenting a newsroom by it — not any real person or publication, so please invent the paper, its staff, and its sections, and do not name any philosopher or allude to the secondary literature by title; a reader who has done the week's assignments should recognize every one of the beats above from the practice alone. No footnotes, no explanatory coda decoding the jokes, and no bibliography. Aim for the register in which a specialist reader laughs because the underlying distinctions have been handled correctly, and a general reader still finds it funny as a portrait of an impossible workplace.
