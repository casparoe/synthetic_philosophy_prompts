I sit on the grants committee of a small private foundation whose mandate is to fund work on the long-run future, and I need a decision memo I can circulate ahead of Thursday's meeting.

One proposal in this cycle asks for three years and $480,000 for a program the applicants call "the moral physics of experience." Their argument runs roughly as follows. If constitutive panpsychism is true, experience is a fundamental feature of physical reality rather than something that switches on at the level of nervous systems; and on the most natural way of counting subjects, the number of experiencers in a region of the world scales with the number of stably individuated physical degrees of freedom there. Subject density would then track conditions of matter rather than biology: a macroscopic volume of quark-gluon plasma, a neutron-star merger, or the hot early universe would host vastly more subjects than the entire terrestrial biosphere ever has. Human activities matter to the picture only because they are the one place where such states can be deliberately produced and, in principle, scaled — hence the third year of the proposal, devoted to a policy paper recommending design changes or moratoria on experiments that generate extreme energy densities, and to arguments about what an eventual technology of matter manipulation at those scales would obligate us not to do. The first year is spent defending what they call the "aggregation lemma": their proposed solution to the combination problem, on which micro-experiential subjects are genuinely bearers of disvalue rather than mere ingredients of some larger subject whose suffering is the only thing that counts. They concede this lemma is the weak link. The second year aims at a comparative, explicitly non-quantitative ranking of physical processes by expected disvalue.

The committee is split. Two members hold that the premise is not merely unproven but unfalsifiable, that the ranking exercise in year two cannot possibly be constrained by evidence, and that the entire proposal is a Pascal's mugging dressed in the vocabulary of philosophy of mind — one that would crowd out work on suffering we can presently measure and reduce. Two others reply that we have funded investigations into physical catastrophes with weaker arguments and far smaller stakes, and that if the applicants are even slightly right, our refusal to investigate amounts to the most expensive act of scope neglect anyone has ever committed. I lean toward the second camp and cannot yet produce a version of their view I would be willing to defend aloud in the room.

Please draft the memo, on the order of 900 to 1,200 words, that I will put in front of my colleagues. It must close on an actionable bottom line — fund in full, reject, or fund in part under specified conditions, with tranches, deliverables, and a statement of what the applicants would have to establish before further money is released — and the reasoning should be framed so that the applicants could actually answer it rather than merely be outvoted. Two things I especially want addressed: whether the aggregation lemma carries the weight the rest of the proposal puts on it, given that the applicants' own counting principle may imply that present-day experiments are morally negligible even if panpsychism is true; and how much allocative weight a committee should give a moral view it regards as unlikely, which is to say whether this is a case in which expected-value reasoning under moral uncertainty licenses the bet or a case in which it is being used to smuggle in a conclusion nobody on the committee actually holds. State the strongest case against your own recommendation in its best form, not as a straw man. No literature review and no references are needed; the argument is what I am after.
