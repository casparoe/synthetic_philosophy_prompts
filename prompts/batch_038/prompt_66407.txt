I teach a 100-minute upper-level seminar on the philosophy of infinity and paradox — eighteen students, mostly third-years, in a room where they can get out of their chairs. I want an activity they physically perform rather than a discussion. Their prior exposure is Zeno at the level of a sentence or two; I have deliberately not used the words "supertask" or "Thomson's lamp" with them, and I do not want either term spoken by anyone until the debrief.

Design the session so that the students run into the following three things themselves, in this order, before anything is named.

First, that there is a difference between *we could not carry out this procedure* and *carrying it out would have left nothing determined*. The class should attempt a schedule of actions at one-half minute before noon, one-quarter minute before, one-eighth, and so on, using a beeping timer and a two-sided paddle as the state. Build in, deliberately and visibly, the step at which human reaction time gives out: I want that breakdown to be a datum the debrief works with, not an embarrassment to paper over.

Second, that two procedures which look alike from the outside can be governed by different rules with different upshots. Run three groups in parallel on an urn procedure: at each step ten numbered slips go in and one comes out, but the group that removes the lowest-numbered slip, the group that removes the highest-numbered slip, and the group that removes the tenth slip each time will not agree about what is in the jar at noon. Each group should log slip numbers, not merely counts, and each should seal a prediction about noon before the procedure ends. At least one group should be pushed to say out loud how the jar can be empty when the number of slips in it increased at every single step.

Third, that an infinite sequence of acts is not automatically completable in finite time. Include a short companion run in which the intervals are one minute, then a further half-minute, then a further third of a minute, and let the students notice unaided that this one never arrives.

Please supply: exact rules and materials, assuming index cards, a jar, a phone timer, and no budget for anything else; a minute-by-minute timeline; a scoring scheme that motivates the sealed predictions without using money; a variant in which no student performs two consecutive acts, together with a note on what that variant does to the question of who is performing the task; a debrief that maps identifiable moments in the room onto the distinction between physical impossibility, underdetermination, and mere surprise, and that has an answer ready for the student who insists nothing happens at noon because there is no final step; the two or three things most likely to go wrong the first time this is run and how to recover from each; and a frank statement of which feature of these cases a live classroom cannot reproduce, and what I must therefore supply in words. No pre-reading for the students, and no bibliography or reading list in your answer.
