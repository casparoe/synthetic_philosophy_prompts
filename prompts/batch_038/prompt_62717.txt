I belong to an informal reading group devoted to formal epistemology and decision theory, and the phrase "bet your beliefs" circulates among us constantly — usually, I have come to suspect, as a way of closing arguments rather than opening them. Last week it was turned on me in a form I could not rebut at the time.

I had remarked that I put the probability of a certain proposed regulation being adopted within five years at roughly 60 percent. Another member replied that, on pain of simple inconsistency, I ought therefore to be prepared to wager 60 percent of my savings on it at even odds; that this is not a matter of intellectual hygiene but a consequence of the Kelly criterion, which he described as showing that a rational agent stakes a fraction of her wealth equal to the probability she assigns; and that Kelly is the uniquely defensible standard because, unlike expected-value reasoning, it is not exploitable by a Dutch book applied repeatedly across time. He closed by observing that my evident reluctance to take the wager demonstrated that I did not in fact hold the credence I had just avowed, whatever I said.

I left with the impression that a four-word slogan had been made to carry several quite different loads at once, and that at least one of the technical claims recruited in its support was false. Could you pull it apart for me? I would like a numbered menu of the distinct precise theses that "bet your beliefs" can be used to assert — recognizing that a phrase like this may be doing work anywhere from a claim about what it is to have a credence at all, through constraints linking credences to the odds at which one should accept wagers, to a norm governing when one may assert something, to plain practical advice about staking money. For each thesis, say what it commits one to, what assumptions it requires, and whether it is trivially true, substantive and defensible, or false. Then state explicitly which reading, if any, would license the move from my avowed 60 percent to a recommended stake, and locate the error in his appeal to the Kelly criterion: what quantity the criterion actually tells one to bet at even odds and why that quantity is not the credence, what kind of result it is and what it is not a result about, and why optimality in long-run growth rate across a series of reinvested bets is not the same property as immunity from a Dutch book. Finally, tell me whether there is any reading on which refusing his wager genuinely would show that I lack the belief I professed, and whether the fact that this was a single, non-repeatable bet involving my life savings rather than one round in a long sequence changes anything about the analysis.

I would rather be told where my friend is right than be reassured, and I would like the readings sharp enough to bring back to the group.
