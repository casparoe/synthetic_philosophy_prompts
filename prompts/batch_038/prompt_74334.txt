I run the philosophy society at my sixth form and for our next meeting I want a sorting quiz on virtual worlds — basically a decision tree that takes someone from a few intuitions about games to a named position in the debate about whether virtual things are real, without them knowing in advance which label each answer leads to.

Can you build it out of the logical space first rather than out of a list of -isms? The splits I think actually matter: (a) whether "real" is all-or-nothing or comes in degrees; (b) whether a virtual sword is a pattern of bits, a genuine object of a digital kind, a fictional object, or nothing at all; (c) where a virtual harm lands — on the avatar, on the player's feelings, on their real-life interests, or genuinely nowhere; and (d) whether it makes any difference that the server can be switched off or the save deleted.

Then turn those into six to eight questions, each a concrete scenario with two or three options someone could pick without having read anything. Please use a spread like: a friend griefing your build in Minecraft; a sword you paid real money for being stolen; an NPC that begs you not to delete it; a couple who got married inside a game; your late brother's half-finished save file; an assault between players in VR.

The leaves have to include the view that virtual objects are just as real as anything else (Chalmers-ish virtual realism, as far as I know), a fictionalist/pretence view, a "it's only pixels and code" eliminativism, and a deflationist who says the objects don't matter but the harms definately do. Any combination of answers that no one actually holds, mark it as unoccupied and say whether that's because it's incoherent, unattractive, or just nobody's got round to it. And if two of your leaves can't really be told apart by the questions, say so instead of pretending they can.

Last line should just be "Verdict:" followed by the single position you'd back yourself, one label, nothing else.
