Okay so — I TA'd a political philosophy seminar section last week and afterwards two of the grad students got into it in the hallway for like forty minutes and I just stood there unable to referee, which is embarassing because it was my section and I'm supposed to be the one with the answers. I'm reconstructing the exchange from memory as best I can. You're a political philosopher who works on migration ethics and has refereed a lot of arguments like this, so please think carefully and don't just split the difference down the middle — I want to know who's right at which step.

The setup they were arguing about: a hypothetical-but-barely low-lying Pacific island state, call it Atea, whose freshwater lens is salinating already and whose territory is mostly uninhabitable within fifty years or so. Atea's government wants a standing entitlement to relocate its population into other states gradually, and it's grounding that claim on the fact that the high-emitting states did this to it. In the background there's a second group: subsistence farmers in a drought-stricken Sahel-adjacent region who are moving right now because they've lost three harvests running. Nobody persecuted them; they're just being made destitute by the same emissions.

The two students were Mira and Daniel.

(1) Mira opened by saying the refugee/persecution frame is the wrong frame for both groups, and that climate displacement generates a *reparative* duty rather than a humanitarian one: emitting states broke something and now owe the particular people they broke a particular remedy. She said the difference isn't cosmetic, because reparative duties are agent-specific — they attach to the one who did the harm and run to the one who suffered it — so they can't be discharged by paying into a fund, and they can't be shuffled off onto whichever state happens to have spare capacity.

(2) Daniel said that's backwards, and that the reparative framing is precisely what traps these people. His argument: a liability duty has to be traceable from a specific wrongdoer's conduct to a specific victim's loss, and emissions are the textbook case of diffuse, cumulative, non-traceable harm. Most historical emitters are dead; present-day poor people in high-emitting states contributed almost nothing; Atea's own per-capita emissions have been climbing. So if you insist on liability you get twenty years of litigation about causal shares while nobody has to admit anybody. He said the humanitarian frame is deliberately *worse at assigning blame* and better at getting people through a door, and that Atea's government knows this, which is why the claim is being dressed up in the language of repair.

(3) Mira's reply was that Daniel had smuggled in an assumption: that a duty is only any good if it's cheap to specify. She said the point of agent-specificity isn't blame, it's that the assignment is enforceable. Under a humanitarian duty every capable state can point at every other capable state and nobody moves — she called this the substitution problem, that a duty anyone can discharge is a duty no one will. Under a liability duty there's a named addressee, and named addressees can be pressed. She added that his traceability objection proves too much: on that logic no one ever owes anything for cumulative harm, which would let off everybody who ever overfished a shared stock or acidified anything.

(4) Daniel conceded the assignment point but denied it gets her anywhere. He said you can have a named addressee without going through liability at all — allocate the humanitarian duty by formula (capacity, with some weighting for contribution) and you land on exactly the same list of countries, minus the causal-attribution swamp. Then he made what I think was his best move: on Mira's view, Ateans are entitled to entry *because they were wronged*, but the Sahelian farmers — worse off right now, wronged by the same emissions — come in on the humanitarian track with a much weaker claim. So the reparative frame builds a two-tier system among people in morally identical positions, and it ranks them by who was harmed *how* rather than by who is in more danger. He asked her straight out whether she was prepared to say an Atean family outranks a family that has already lost three harvests.

(5) Mira said she was prepared to say it, and that his claim of moral identity was false: losing your country isn't the same bad thing as losing your income, because the first destroys the political community that was securing your standing as someone whose claims count at all, and the second doesn't. She was emphatic that this isn't a sentimental point about attachment to land. It's that when Atea goes under, its citizens stop being anyone's citizens, and the system of states is the only machinery that makes rights claimable in the first place — so states collectively have an interest in never manufacturing rightless people, and that interest is what the duty is really tracking.

(6) Daniel's closer was that (5) is a bait-and-switch. He said she'd quietly abandoned the reparative frame from (1) and swapped in a systemic one: the duty now falls on all states because the state system as a whole is damaged, not on emitters because they emitted. Fine, he said, but that's his frame wearing her coat — and it has a consequence she hasn't noticed. If the duty exists to prevent the production of rightless people, the cheapest way to discharge it isn't admission at all: it's continued recognition of Atea, a seat, extraterritorial citizenship, a trust fund, keeping the polity alive on paper while everyone stays put. He said she owes an argument for why entry is required rather than recognition, and that her own new premises don't deliver one.

Mira said something about dignity and about the fund being administered by the same people who caused the problem, and then we ran out of hallway.
