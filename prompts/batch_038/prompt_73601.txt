I'm not a philosopher and never took a class in it, I do IT for a living, but I've been reading about laws of nature on my own for about three years now -- the online encyclopedia entries, a couple of actual books, some lectures -- and I've landed somewhere I'm pretty happy with. A friend of mine who did a degree in the subject read my write-up and just said it was a mess, wouldn't say more than "you can't have all of that at once," which is infuriating becuase I can't see which part he thinks breaks. Please write it as continuous prose, like an email or a letter -- no headings, no bullet points, no bold, no tables. I want to read one running argument, not a checklist.

Here's what I hold.

1. There's nothing in the world except particular goings-on. This particle here, that field value there, each event just being itself, sitting alongside the others with no glue between them. I don't believe in necessary connections anywhere, in anything.

2. Laws are the best summaries of the whole pattern of goings-on. Simplest, strongest, best trade-off between the two. There's nothing behind them. If you had the complete history of every particular fact you'd have everything, and the laws would just be the best way of compressing it.

3. Even so, I think laws explain. Ask me why the water in that pot boiled when it did and if I trace it back to a law I think I've given you a real explanation, not just pointed back at the pattern. And I do think there's a sense in which the universe has the history it has because of the laws -- not that they push things around like little hands, but they're what's responsible for how it all went.

4. Two worlds can't have exactly the same distribution of particular facts and different laws. Different laws means a different pattern, end of story.

5. Chances in physics are out there in the world, not a measure of anybody's ignorance, and they get in the same summarizing way: a half-life counts as a law because putting it in beats listing every single decay.

6. Something I've never seen dealt with: if the laws are the best summary of the whole pattern, future included, then whether a given claim is a law depends on decays that haven't happened yet. If it turns out that way out in the future nearly every unstable atom goes off almost immediately, doesn't that change what the best summary of the total history is, and so change what the laws were in 1920? I don't know whether to swallow that or treat it as a reductio of the whole approach, and I'd like your view.

7. Induction has no rational foundation. Any argument from the past to the future begs the question somewhere and I've made peace with that. But you're also irrational if you don't induct, and I mean that as a real criticism, not a shrug.

8. My own addition, which I haven't seen anyone else say. The best summary depends on the language you write it in, and I don't think there's a God's-eye language, so which propositions come out as laws is partly down to our descriptive choices. But at the same time, when two people working in different vocabularies disagree about what the laws are I think they're genuinely disagreeing about the world and not talking past each other, and one of them can just be wrong.

9. The laws were already there at the very first moment, before there was much of a pattern to summarize -- arguably when there was barely anything going on at all -- and they're what carried things forward from there.
