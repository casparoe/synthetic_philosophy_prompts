I'm taking an introductory decision theory course this term, and I'd like an assessment of an argument a classmate made against me rather than a defense of my own position.

I'm choosing among three summer options: a paid analyst rotation at a bank, an unpaid research position in a lab here, and a field program with an environmental NGO abroad. When I compare them two at a time, my preferences go around in a circle: bank over lab, lab over NGO, NGO over bank. Each comparison seems defensible to me, but each leans on a different consideration — the first on the money, the second on the skills and the reference letter, the third on whether the work is worth doing at all. I have not been able to produce a single ordering that respects all three.

My classmate says this makes me not merely confused but exploitable. He proposed trading me out of whichever option I currently hold into the next one in my circle, three times in a row, charging me $20 for each swap. He's right that I would accept each swap if it were offered to me on its own, which would leave me holding exactly what I started with and $60 poorer. His conclusion is that preferences permitting such a sequence are defective, and that the only repair is to attach a number to each option and take the largest.

Two things trouble me. First, when he described all three swaps to me at once as a package, I knew immediately that I would refuse it; the loss only arises if the swaps arrive separately and I evaluate each in isolation. Does that distinction carry any weight, or am I simply irrational with extra steps? Second, he insists that immunity to that kind of exploitation is not a symptom of rationality but the whole of it, so that I must either rank the three options on one scale or concede that I don't genuinely prefer any of them to another. Is that claim as strong as he thinks? And given all this, what should I actually do about the three offers before the deadlines close?
