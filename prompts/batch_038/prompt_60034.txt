I am a junior in high school, and for the past two months my school's philosophy club has been reading and arguing about the special composition question: under what conditions, if any, do some things compose a further thing? The discussions have gone nowhere, and I think I finally understand why. One member insists that any restricted answer is intolerably arbitrary, since nothing about a scattered pair of objects changes gradually as you slide them together, so composition must always occur. Another insists universalism is absurd, because it commits us to an object composed of my left shoe, the Eiffel Tower, and a distant quasar. A third says only living things genuinely compose. When our adviser sat in last week, she pointed out that we are not actually disagreeing about an answer at all; we are disagreeing about what would make an answer good, and none of us could say what that means. Since I have to submit a thesis proposal in the spring and I am seriously considering this topic, I would like that prior question settled first. So: write out the full specification of what any adequate answer to the special composition question has to deliver.

To give you something to push against, here are the candidate requirements my own list so far contains, none of which I am confident about. An answer should be statable without smuggling in the very notion it is supposed to define, so that it is not vacuous or trivially satisfied by every collection whatsoever or by none. It should not leave composition vague, because it is hard to see how a borderline case could be borderline rather than simply indeterminate, and indeterminacy in the world is a heavy thing to accept. It should be conservative enough that our ordinary beliefs about tables, cats, and rocks are not massively and systematically false. It should be applicable by finite creatures like us, meaning that we could in principle tell whether composition has occurred without having to survey the whole universe or do metaphysics first. It should be compatible with what our best mereological pictures allow, including endless divisibility and talk about many things at once without forcing them into a single thing. And it should tell us why composition occurs where it does, rather than accepting that it just does, as an unexplained brute fact. Some of these I suspect are genuine constraints on any answer, some I suspect are really the preferences of one camp dressed up as requirements, and I would guess at least two of them cannot be satisfied together.

What I want from you, then, is the reasoned list rather than a summary of positions. For each requirement you end up endorsing, give the case or consideration that motivates it, ideally a concrete scenario that makes vivid what goes wrong if the requirement is violated. Say explicitly which requirements you take to be non-negotiable and which are contestable commitments, and name the camp whose prejudice each contestable one belongs to. Identify every conflict between requirements, and if some subset is jointly unsatisfiable, demonstrate it, because that result would matter more to my thesis than anything else on the list. Tell me which single requirement does the most work in eliminating candidates, and, for each requirement you reject or demote from my list above, what argument would justify dropping it. Finally, in one short paragraph and no longer, note how universalism, nihilism, and the view that only living things compose fare against the finished list. A table is fine for the requirements themselves, but the reasoning behind each has to be in prose.
