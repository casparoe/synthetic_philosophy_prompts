I've spent a while with the recent work on standing to blame, and every account I run into makes standing a function of the blamer's own record: she's guilty of the same thing, she's complicit, she's a meddler with no business in it, or she applies the norm selectively. I want a case that breaks the pattern, built to spec, because I'm trying to figure out whether "standing" names a single normative status or is just a bundle tag for several unrelated objections.

Design a thought experiment in which someone who is entirely blameless with respect to the wrong in question, not complicit in it even indirectly, not a meddler, and not guilty of selective enforcement — she is a well-informed, legitimately positioned witness with every ordinary basis for raising it — nevertheless plainly lacks standing to blame, and lacks it in a way that reads as a standing problem rather than as a complaint about rudeness or imprudence. Constraints: one paragraph; a realistic setting (an office, a family, a volunteer organization, a small town), with no courts and no life-or-death stakes; the feature that defeats her standing must be structural — a fact about her relationship to the wrongdoer or to the wrong itself — not a fact about her tone, her motives, or how much she says; and the case has to generate the verdict rather than smuggle it in by describing her blame as obviously impermissible.

Then the demonstration:

1. Run the four accounts above against the case and show explicitly why each returns "standing intact."
2. Name the feature actually doing the work, then give the minimal edit that removes it while holding everything else fixed, and report whether the intuition flips and how sharply.
3. Steelman the objection that this isn't a standing problem at all — that it's tactlessness, or epistemic overreach, or a norm about forgiveness that I'm mislabeling — and tell me honestly whether that objection wins.

If you conclude that the case can't be built and that standing really does collapse into those four conditions, say so and argue it; that's a useful result for my purposes, but only if you show where the attempt fails.
