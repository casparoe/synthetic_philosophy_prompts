For thirty-four years I worked as an analytical chemist in a refinery laboratory, where a great deal of my time went into a question that has followed me into retirement: when a junior analyst hands you a result, what are you actually entitled to check? I now facilitate a Saturday-morning discussion group at our town library for fourteen people — mostly retired engineers, a couple of former schoolteachers, one statistician — on how knowledge gets produced and warranted. We meet for one hundred minutes, we have two long tables, a whiteboard, a box of index cards, a printer, and a jar of dried beans that serves as our universal currency. None of us has any formal training in machine learning, and I should like the activity you design for us to require no arithmetic beyond what one does at a supermarket checkout.

Our next topic concerns two different ways of judging a system that produces answers: checking only the final product, versus checking the intermediate work that led to it. I have a half-formed idea that I would like you to develop into something properly runnable, and I would like the difference to be discovered by my group rather than explained to them. My sketch is this. The participants split into "apprentices" and "inspectors." Apprentices are dealt a series of small puzzles — logic or arithmetic, nothing that needs a computer — each of which admits several routes to a solution. Some routes are genuinely sound and will carry over to new instances; some routes are shortcuts that happen to yield the right answer on the particular cards dealt and would collapse on any other; and some apprentices will do the work correctly and then spoil the final line through a slip. Inspectors are assigned, by lot, either to see the finished answer alone or to see the answer together with the written reasoning — and the second assignment must cost them something real, which I think means timed rounds, so that an inspector who reads every line of work has fewer rounds in which to inspect anything at all. At the end, without warning, a fresh set of instances is dealt to the apprentices, and it becomes visible which inspections paid off. I would also like the rules to permit an apprentice to write up handsome-looking work that does not correspond to the method actually used, because I suspect that possibility is central and not a footnote.

Here is the wrinkle I want you to handle carefully, because it is the reason I am not simply asking you for a morality play in which thoroughness wins. In one round, the step-by-step rubric handed to the reasoning-inspectors should itself be quietly defective — drafted, say, by an earlier cohort who misunderstood the task — so that the careful inspectors reward a method that is wrong and the answer-only inspectors do better. I want the group to end up holding two conclusions at once: that inspecting only the product is blind, and that inspecting the work is only as good as the picture of good work that the inspector was given. Please tell me whether this can be built into the rules without anyone feeling cheated or confused about the point, and how and when the defective rubric should be revealed. If you judge that my wrinkle will backfire with a group of this size and composition, say so plainly and substitute something better.

What I need from you, concretely: a materials list and the exact content of the cards, or a generation rule simple enough that I can produce the whole deck in an afternoon; role assignments and how to divide fourteen people so that no one is idle; a bean-based payoff scheme that yields a winner but in which winning is emphatically not the lesson; a minute-by-minute timeline for the hundred minutes, including where the hidden second set of instances enters; a debrief that maps particular moments of play onto particular features of the distinction, and withholds the practitioners' vocabulary until the participants have lived it; a candid list of what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this with people of mixed numeracy and considerable stubbornness, and how to handle each; and an honest statement of which feature of the real situation the game cannot reproduce and which I, as facilitator, will have to supply by hand. I would also welcome one optional extension of about fifteen minutes in which the reasoning-inspectors must deliberate as a committee and reach agreement, if you think pooling their judgments would repair a bad rubric rather than merely average it — but drop this if it bloats the session. Please do not give us references or a reading list; we will not be looking anything up, and a design that depends on our having read something is no use to me. End your reply with exactly one line of the form \answer{pivot = Round N; lesson = "<one sentence>"}, where Round N is the round in which my group, obeying only the rules you have written, first discovers that checking the product and checking the work have come apart.
