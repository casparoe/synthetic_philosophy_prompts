I'm the clinical ethicist on call at a 480-bed regional hospital, and two or three times a month I get paged for the same shape of problem: there's a signed advance directive, the patient is now in or approaching the state it describes, and somebody on the floor — usually a bedside nurse, sometimes an adult kid — tells me the patient doesn't seem to want what the paper says. The canonical version is the person who wrote years ago that they'd never want to be kept alive "like that," and is now sitting up in a chair, humming, clearly enjoying pudding, and pulling her arm away when a phlebotomist approaches. Then the family splits down the middle: one side says the document is her voice and ignoring it is a betrayal, the other side says she's content right now and the document was written by someone who isn't in the room.

Our policy is useless for this. It says a valid directive is binding unless the patient has decision-making capacity at the time of the decision, in which case a present refusal wins. That handles maybe a fifth of the real consults, because nearly everything interesting sits in the capacity-gray zone, and because "valid" and "actually applies to this situation" are precisely what's being fought over. I need something better than that sentence.

So: write me a decision procedure I can run on these consults. Constraints on it, because they're real constraints and not decorations —

- It gets used by me and by two medical social workers who have no philosophy background at all, so no term survives unless you define it in a line where you use it.
- It has to be runnable in about 45 minutes from a chart, a bedside visit, and one conversation with whoever's at the bedside. I usually cannot get a formal capacity or neuropsych eval inside 24 hours, so the procedure cannot have "obtain an expert determination" as a load-bearing step.
- It has to terminate in one of four routings, and it should say plainly which routing: (a) follow the directive as written; (b) follow the directive with named modifications, which have to be written down; (c) treat what the patient expresses now as controlling and set the directive aside; (d) not decidable on this record — escalate, and say to whom: full ethics committee, the legal surrogate, or the courts. If you think I'm missing a fifth routing, tell me and add it.
- For each question in the sequence, say what counts as a sufficient answer and what doesn't. A procedure where every step can be satisfied by "the family feels strongly" is not a procedure.
- Tell me where it stops and hands the thing to a human because the question is one of values rather than facts. I want those points flagged in the text, not buried.
- Tell me which of the steps are judgment calls wearing a checklist's clothes. I'd much rather read "step 4 is a gut call, here's how to make it a *disciplined* gut call" than a fake decision tree. And say where the procedure will systematically misfire — which kind of patient or which kind of family it gets wrong, and in which direction.

Ground it in the arguments that actually bear on the routing, not in a tour of the debate. I know roughly what's in the literature: the idea that a life is governed by the standing commitments and values a person endorsed rather than by the sum of their pleasant moments, and that a directive can express the former kind of interest; the worry running the other way that welfare might just be experience, so that a person who no longer remembers writing the document and is not distressed cannot be harmed by continuing to feed or treat them; and the identity-flavored worry that the earlier self and the later one are enough of different subjects that the earlier one's authority is at least contestable. What I need is for you to tell me which of those distinctions actually changes what we do Monday morning, which are decorative in a clinical setting, and what the cheapest bedside test is for each of the ones that matter.

And here's the piece I most want you to work on, because it's my own hunch and I can't tell if it's any good. I think directives come in two kinds that need different handling. Call them condition-directives and treatment-directives. A condition-directive says something like "if I can no longer recognize my children, do not treat my pneumonia" — it can be *mistaken about the facts*, because the patient in front of me may not be in the state the writer imagined, and most of the argument in those cases is descriptive: what does "recognize" mean, what state is she actually in, is this the thing she pictured or something adjacent she never pictured? A treatment-directive says something like "no artificial nutrition, ever, regardless of how I seem" — it can't misfire on the facts that way, because there's no condition to check; the only live question is whether the earlier self gets to bind the later one at all, which is the hard philosophical question and not a factual one. My suspicion is that a lot of the consults that feel irresolvable are treatment-directives being argued as though they were condition-directives — people spend forty minutes litigating whether the patient "would have wanted this" when the paper doesn't make wanting relevant. Does that taxonomy hold up? Where does it break? Is there a third kind — directives about *who decides*, or about dignity, or about a threshold of burden rather than a state of mind — that needs its own branch? Build whatever survives into the procedure as an early sorting step.

One more evidentiary problem the procedure has to solve explicitly: what counts as the patient not wanting something now, when the patient can't tell us. Turning the head away from a spoon? Opening the mouth for it? Smiling at a stranger? A videotape from fifteen years ago saying she'd rather die? Repeated attempts to pull a line? Each of those gets read in opposite directions by the two camps in the family, so I need the procedure to say what weight each carries and against what — and to say when the honest answer is that we're watching a body and inferring a mind, and how to record that inference so it's visible later.

Then run it on three cases, showing the questions in order and the routing it lands on.

1. Woman, 81, moderate-to-advanced dementia. Directive from age 68 says no antibiotics if she reaches a state where she no longer knows her family. She still knows her husband of fifty years and lights up when he walks in; she does not know her two adult children, one of whom is her healthcare proxy and wants the directive followed. She has aspiration pneumonia, is febrile but not distressed, and has been eating pureed food with prompting. Oral antibiotics are available and would very likely work.

2. Man, 46, severe traumatic brain injury eleven months ago, now minimally verbal, follows one-step commands inconsistently, has a documented lifelong and repeatedly stated horror of being dependent on other people, and a directive that says no artificial nutrition or hydration under any circumstances. He has a PEG. He is not in pain on exam, he laughs at the television, and he reaches for the nursing staff. His sister, the proxy, wants the tube feeding stopped and says the directive is unambiguous and the hospital is torturing him by keeping him alive. He cannot swallow safely without it.

3. Give me one of your own construction where the procedure produces a routing I will hate. I lean autonomy-strong — I default to the paper being the person's voice — so build it so the procedure, applied honestly, ends up overriding a directive that looks clear, specific, recent, and freely written, and then tell me exactly which step did the damage and whether that step could have been written to avoid it.

Finish with what the procedure cannot do: the classes of case it will get wrong no matter how carefully it's run, and what I should tell a family who asks whether it's fair.
