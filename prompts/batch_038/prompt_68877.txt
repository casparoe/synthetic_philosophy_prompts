I am writing the framing chapter for a volume that grew out of a standing seminar our philosophy of science group shares with the archaeology department, and I have run into a wall I cannot argue my way past. For roughly two decades the study of societal collapse has been split into two camps that read the same sites and reach opposite conclusions. On one side stands a tradition running from Joseph Tainter's treatment of collapse as a sudden, pronounced loss of sociopolitical complexity, explained by diminishing marginal returns on investment in complexity, through Jared Diamond's much more popular checklist of environmental damage, climate change, hostile neighbours, lost trade partners, and above all the choices societies make. On the other side stands a continuity-and-resilience camp — signalled in its title by the McAnany and Yoffee volume Questioning Collapse (2009) and developed in Guy Middleton's Understanding Collapse (2017) — which argues that what ends in these cases is usually a particular elite regime, an administrative apparatus, or a style of monumental building, while the people go on living, farming, speaking and worshipping, so that the honest description is transformation rather than failure. Their paradigm cases are the Maya, where southern lowland polities disintegrated over generations while northern centres rose and Maya peoples and languages are still here, and Rapa Nui, where they hold that the ecocide narrative displaces the documented nineteenth-century catastrophe of slave raiding, introduced disease and missionisation onto a pre-contact environmental story.

What puzzles me is that the two sides appear to agree on nearly every first-order fact about any given case. Both will accept that around 1200 BCE the Aegean palaces were destroyed or abandoned, that Linear B stopped being used, that settlement hierarchies flattened and long-distance exchange contracted, and that the region later re-complexified; both will accept the Maya chronology; both will accept the Rapa Nui palm loss. They disagree about whether to say that something collapsed. My working hypothesis — and I want it tested, not adopted — is that this is a dispute in social ontology rather than in archaeology: a disagreement about the identity conditions of the social individual that is supposedly persisting or perishing, with one side taking the persisting object to be a population, a language, and a repertoire of practice, and the other taking it to be a political structure with offices, records, and a division of labour that cannot survive its own dissolution. I suspect a second feature keeps the impasse going: both sides may have concepts that are very hard to lose, since almost any society that persists in altered form can be redescribed as resilient, and almost any pronounced loss of complexity can be redescribed as collapse. And I suspect a third layer, which neither side likes to name as the real issue, about what the word is for: a technical description of a recurrent process, or a cautionary narrative aimed at present-day audiences, which the continuity camp regards as a distortion imported from Western ideas of decline.

Diagnose this rather than survey the cases. Which of those layers is actually load-bearing, and is there a substantive disagreement buried underneath the verbal one — for instance about the rate of change, the magnitude of demographic loss, or whether a re-complexified region counts as the same society resuming or a new one beginning — that better chronologies, ancient DNA, or paleoclimate proxies could in principle settle? Would a stipulative operationalization of collapse in terms of measurable complexity indicators, with fixed thresholds and time windows, count as progress, or as a surrender that simply builds one camp's ontology into the metric? Is there a formal or modelling result worth wanting here, such as a demonstration that under any reasonable weighting of demographic, institutional, and material strata the same cases come out both collapsed and continuous, which would show the dispute to be a weighting question rather than a factual one? Tell me what you think each side would themselves concede as counting against them, whether the diagnosis is case-relative — verbal for the Maya, substantive for the Late Bronze Age Aegean, and perhaps something else again for Rapa Nui — and whether the persistence of this argument is better explained by conceptual structure or by the incentives of disciplinary identity and a public market for warning narratives. I want an honest answer to whether anything could move it, or whether my chapter should say plainly that it cannot and explain why.
