I run a once-a-month philosophy night in the back room of a bar, about fifteen people, half of whom have never taken a philosophy class, and next month's theme is Moore's paradox — the whole it's-raining-but-I-don't-believe-it business. I want to kick things off with a sorting quiz called something like "which way out of Moore's paradox are you already committed to," so that people argue about the sentences instead of about labels. Can you write me nine or ten items plus a scoring key?

Rules I care about. Every item has to be a sentence someone is asked to judge, with answer options roughly like: flat-out absurd / weird but I could say it sincerely / totally fine / depends on the setup. Nothing may name a theory or use phrases like assertion norm or transparency of belief, because otherwise the quiz just measures who's read the encyclopedia entry. The key should map answer patterns onto positions rather than onto people: the view that the trouble is in the believing (you can't coherently believe p and that you don't), the view that the trouble is in the asserting and the belief itself is perfectly possible, the view that it all runs through some kind of self-intimation, the view that belief and assertion share a truth norm and that's what does the work, plus a quietist option along the lines of there being no puzzle at all, just a misuse of the word but — and whatever else you think genuinely belongs in the space. If a leaf really is best known by somebody's name, use it only if you're confident the association is right; otherwise coin a descriptive label and say it's yours.

Three of the items are ones I made up at the bar last week and I'm not sure they work, so tell me which to keep and why. First: It's raining, but I wouldn't bet on it — seems fine to me, which is embarassing for any account that ties the absurdity to what assertion commits you to. Second: If it's raining, then I don't believe it — burying the thing inside a conditional seems to drain the absurdity, and I want an item that forces people to notice that. Third: It's raining, but I don't believe that I believe it's raining — the second-order version, which I suspect will split the room down the middle. Fill out the rest with whatever variants you think are actually diagnostic: the third-person version with somebody else's name in it, the past-tense one, the reversed it's-raining-and-I-believe-it-isn't one, something involving sincere self-deception, and one where the speaker is a genuinely self-blind amnesia patient who has no access to their own beliefs. Then build me a small grid of the logically possible combinations of answers — is the belief impossible? is the assertion the thing that goes wrong? is there a truth norm doing work in the background? — and tell me which cells come out empty and why: incoherent, unattractive, or just nobody's gotten there yet. Finally, flag any pair of leaves your items can't really pull apart; I'd much rather know that going in than have the quiz pretend to a seperation it doesn't have.

Oh, and American English please.
