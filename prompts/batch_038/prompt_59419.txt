I do failure analysis for a living — about fifteen years of FMEAs, fault trees, root-cause writeups, the whole grind, mostly aerospace-adjacent hardware. Lately I've been reading about the societal-collapse debate purely for fun and it's messing with my head, because half of it sounds exactly like my day job and the other half sounds like nothing I've ever actually done. I want you to help me figure out which half is which.

Take the three main positions in that debate and restate each one, as faithfully as you can manage, in the vocabulary I use at work: top events, failure modes, initiating events, contributing causes versus root cause, latent versus active failures, single points of failure, common-cause failure, redundancy and the ways redundancy quietly doesn't help, load versus capacity, design margin, tight coupling, graceful degradation, load shedding, and mean time between failures. The three positions, roughly as I've got them:

1. The Jared Diamond-style one: a society damages its own resource base, its institutions are too rigid to register the damage, and it gets driven over a threshold.

2. Tainter's: complexity is an investment with diminishing marginal returns, so a society keeps paying more to hold itself together until the marginal return goes negative, and at that point shedding complexity is the economically sane move rather than a catastrophe.

3. The archaeologists behind Questioning Collapse (McAnany and Yoffee and their contributors), who think "collapse" is the wrong word for most of what actually happened: dynasties and state apparatuses end, but the people carry on, often fine or even better off, and what looks like failure in the material record is frequently just a change of strategy. The Rapa Nui line of argument is the version I keep running into — the palm deforestation is real but the islanders adapted their agriculture, and the catastrophic population loss comes much later from slaving raids and introduced disease, not from cutting the trees down.

For each of the three, give me the translation first, then a separate residue report: which claims map cleanly onto something I'd actually put in a fault tree, which ones you had to bend or fudge to fit, and which ones have no expression at all in my vocabulary. Then tell me where you think the residue is pointing — is my framework missing something real, or is the original claim less clear than it sounded when the archaeologist wrote it?

Two things I specifically want you to chew on, because they're what's keeping me up:

- In my world the top event is fixed by a spec. Somebody wrote down what the machine is supposed to do, so "failure" is failure-of-that. Nobody in this debate seems able to say who wrote the spec for a society. Position 3 basically amounts to saying the top event everyone picked — monumental construction stops, elite administration dissolves, writing falls out of use — is the failure of a subsystem most of the humans involved didn't especially care about. Can my vocabulary express "you chose the wrong top event," or is that a hole in it?

- Position 2 wants to say the collapse was in some sense selected for, that it made things better for the people living through it. I've got graceful degradation and deliberate load shedding, which get partway there. But in a real load-shedding event the components don't decide anything, and I suspect the difference matters a lot. Tell me how much of Tainter survives once you stop letting the metaphor do quiet work.

Please don't smuggle my framework's assumptions in and hand them back to me as translation — if a claim only looks translatable because you silently assumed societies have functions the way machines do, flag that instead of papering over it. And be honest about places where you think the honest answer is that the two vocabularies just aren't commensurable.

Oh, and write the whole thing in American English, please.
