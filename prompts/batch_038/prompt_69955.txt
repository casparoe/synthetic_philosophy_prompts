My seminar partner built an arguement that starts as the usual raven paradox and ends somewhere I can't accept, and I cant tell whether it's a cheap trick or a real result. Everything turns on one observation report, S = "this shoe is brown," and one hypothesis, R = "all ravens are black."

(1) Nicod's criterion: a report that some object is both F and G confirms the generalization that all F are G.
(2) Equivalence condition: logically equivalent hypotheses are confirmed by exactly the same reports.
(3) R is logically equivalent to "all nonblack things are nonravens."
(4) So by (1)–(3), S confirms R. The brown shoe is a tiny bit of evidence for the ravens. This is just the standard paradox, and honestly I'm willing to live with it.
(5) Converse consequence: if E confirms H, then E confirms every H\* that entails H. He insists this is nothing exotic — its just the hypothetico-deductive method written as a condition: a hypothesis that entails something you observed gets credit for it.
(6) Special consequence: if E confirms H, then E confirms everything H entails.
(7) Now take any sentence H whatsoever — the composition of the moon, my uncle's alibi, whatever — and form the conjunction R∧H. That entails R, so by (4) and (5), S confirms R∧H.
(8) R∧H entails H, so by (6), S confirms H. H was arbitrary, so S confirms every sentence in the language.
(9) Two instances he likes to say out loud: S confirms "some ravens are not black" (because R∧that is a contradiction, and a contradiction entails R); and S confirms "this shoe is not brown." So the report confirms its own negation, and confirms a generalization and its denial.

Which step goes, and what does it cost? A few things I specifically want answered:

- My instinct is to drop (5). But if (5) really is H-D in disguise, dropping it means a successful prediction stops supporting the theory that predicted it, which feels worse than the shoe confirming everything. Is that the actual price, or is there a version of hypothetico-deductivism that survives without (5)?
- He says going Bayesian dissolves the whole thing. Does it? I can see P(H|S) falling for most absurd H, yet S still seems to raise the probability of the conjunction R∧(the moon is cheese) while leaving the probability of each conjunct on its own untouched. Bug, or the fix working as intended? And if Good is right that background knowledge about how many ravens and nonblack things there are can make a nonblack nonraven *lower* P(R), then the Bayesian loses (4) — the one step I wanted to keep. Which side is paying more?
- Is there any principled restriction available: relativize confirmation to a background corpus, impose a total-evidence requirement, ban conjuncts that mention the shoe, ban unentrenched predicates? Or is every one of those a patch selected to block (8) and nothing else?
- Hempel's own line, as I understand it, is that the shoe genuinely does confirm R and the shock is merely psychological, since the amount is negligible. Does that survive triviality, or does "negligible" stop discriminating once the shoe negligibly confirms absolutely everything?

I don't want "confirmation comes in degrees" as the entire response unless you show me the degrees actually block step (8). Tell me whether the argument is valid, and if it is, which of these plausible premises is cheapest to give up and what stops working once you do.
