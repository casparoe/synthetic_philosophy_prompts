I am a high school junior, and for two terms I have been part of a six-person independent study in philosophy of mind that meets Thursdays before first period with my teacher, Ms. Ferreira. This term the seminar is on higher-order accounts of consciousness: the family of views that make a first-order state conscious in virtue of its being represented by some further state, including the thought version, the inner-perception version, the dispositional version that requires only that the state be available for such representation, and the self-representational version on which there is no separate further state at all. Ms. Ferreira runs a standing ritual: on Friday she posts a case, we each write a one-paragraph verdict with a reason, and on Thursday she announces hers before we argue. She has now announced her verdict on eight cases, and I have realized that I have been predicting them badly, because I keep guessing which named theory she sounds like rather than working out what constraint her verdicts jointly impose. In April I am entering a regional philosophy essay competition, and I want to get better at exactly that skill, so I would like to use next week's case as practice.

Her verdicts so far, in her own order: a word flashed for thirty milliseconds that reliably shifts a subject's later judgment, with the subject reporting that he saw nothing at all — not conscious; the gorilla walking through the scene while the subject counts basketball passes — not conscious of the gorilla; a blindsight patient guessing above chance about the affected quadrant while insisting he sees nothing there — not conscious; a newborn's response to a heel-prick, with no conceptual repertoire to speak of — conscious; a rat trained on a two-alternative discrimination that can press a third lever to opt out of hard trials for a smaller reward — its uncertainty is a conscious state; a colleague who arrives at "I am furious" only by noticing her own tone of voice and her clenched jaw — the anger is not conscious at that moment, and Ms. Ferreira insists the case does not turn on availability, because if the colleague had paused and turned her attention inward she would have found the anger already there; someone absorbed in a novel, conscious of the story but not of her own reading — and, when asked about the noticing itself, she said that when you notice you are annoyed, the noticing is not something you are separately aware of, and that this is no embarrassment because it starts no regress. The eighth is a hypothetical: asked whether a higher-order representation produced in a subject by an experimenter's electrode, rather than by the subject's own monitoring of the target state, would make that state conscious, she said no, and said she does not consider that exclusion an ad hoc patch on her view. One of us, Daniel, argues every week that all of this is just a global workspace story in disguise; she has never agreed with him, but she has also never told him which of her verdicts he cannot reproduce.

Next Thursday's case is already posted. A patient, "M.," has a focal lesion affecting visual processing in one quadrant. Spontaneously and repeatedly, in the absence of any stimulus to that quadrant, she has what she describes as a vivid, simply-given awareness of a determinate teal patch in her upper left field: she locates it, describes its hue, is distressed by it, and says she is not inferring or theorizing — it is just there. The lab's imaging and electrophysiology indicate no first-order visual state of the relevant kind in that quadrant, activity there sitting at baseline. And when they probe her with a forced-choice discrimination task in that quadrant, her ability to tell teal from neighbouring shades is at chance: she cannot pick out the colour she says she sees. Predict Ms. Ferreira's verdict — is M. conscious of a teal patch, conscious of nothing at all, or is the case so described impossible, and if so which clause of it she would reject. More importantly, I want the reverse-engineering: state the rule you think she is actually running, give at least two further rules that reproduce all eight of her earlier verdicts just as well, and say what each of those predicts about M. and why. Tell me which of her eight verdicts is doing the real narrowing work and which ones are consistent with nearly everything, since I suspect I have been over-reading the blindsight and masked-priming cases. Flag any candidate rule that survives only if it is reinterpreted, and say what the reinterpretation costs. Then deal with the wrinkle I cannot handle: she reports a determinate hue that she cannot discriminate, so it is unclear how determinate the content of her higher-order state really is, and whether that undercuts the case's own description. Finally, name the one additional case that would best discriminate between the rules you have left standing, and explain why it is more diagnostic than the obvious alternatives. I am not asking for references, a reading list, or papers to chase down — Ms. Ferreira marks name-dropping down, and I could not look anything up during the school week anyway.
