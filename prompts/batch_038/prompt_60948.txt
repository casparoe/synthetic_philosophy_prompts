I cover science and ideas for a national magazine, and I am one of three outside members of the archive review panel that hears requests from people named in our old reporting. The case before us Thursday runs like this. In 2003 we published a news brief naming a then-22-year-old among several people arrested in a raid on an indoor cannabis grow operation; he pleaded to a reduced charge, completed a diversion program, and later had the record set aside under a state law passed years after the fact. Cannabis is now legal where we publish. His name still surfaces first in searches, he reports losing a pharmacy job offer over it, and he is asking not for deletion but for de-indexing plus a dated editor's note explaining the change. Two complications. The request was filed on his behalf by a reputation-management firm that charges for exactly this service, and one panel member argues that granting it makes oblivion a purchasable good; the same member notes that we received a near-identical filing last month from a former county administrator named in our corruption series. Our digital team, meanwhile, reports that legacy crime stories account for a noticeable share of search revenue, and says quietly that de-indexing will cost us.

Draft the panel's decision memo — grant, deny, or grant in modified form — with a remedy that has a date attached, a written standard the panel can actually apply to the next filing including the county administrator's, and a serious engagement with the strongest case against us, which I take to be that a newsroom owes a duty of memory and that de-indexing converts an archive into a curated record without anyone deciding to do so. Please distinguish sharply among deleting, de-indexing, downranking, and annotating, and say which of those four our obligation actually runs to, and why the two readings my colleagues keep circling — that we owe memory, versus that an archive no one can escape is not memory but an open-ended sentence — cannot both govern the same request.
