For thirty-five years I worked in sensory neurophysiology, most of it on proprioceptive afferent signalling, and since retiring I have turned to philosophy with the seriousness of a man who has finally been given time to be wrong slowly. I am currently co-teaching an advanced seminar — final-year undergraduates and first-year postgraduates, none of whom read Arabic and only some of whom have met any medieval philosophy — on the question of what a self could be known to be when the body is abstracted away. I have been asked to set the final assessment, and I want your help drafting one essay question on Ibn Sīnā's Flying Man.

The scenario will be given to the students in an excerpt on the paper, from the psychology section of al-Shifāʾ (The Book of Healing); I understand he returns to it in the shorter treatments he later produced, and that commentators dispute how it sits with his broadly Aristotelian description of the soul as a perfection of the body. Do not make the question depend on any of that, and do not require students to adjudicate it — but do make it possible for a student who notices the tension to be rewarded rather than penalised.

Here is what I want the question to turn on. In my old discipline an ablation study is only as good as the logic that licenses its conclusions, and that logic is narrow. That a function is absent after a lesion does not tell you where it was implemented; that a capacity survives the removal of a tissue does not tell you the capacity is independent of that tissue; and, most importantly, a subject's failure to *identify* something is not evidence of the *absence* of that thing. A patient who has lost large-fibre sensory input from the neck down must watch his limbs to move them and will describe them as not his own — yet nobody concludes that his arms have ceased to be parts of him. The Flying Man, it seems to me, is precisely an ablation experiment: strip away sight, hearing, touch and the limbs' contact with one another, and ask what the subject still affirms. Avicenna's answer is that he affirms his own self and nothing bodily, and from this he moves to a conclusion about what the self is. I want students to interrogate that move with the discipline a reviewer would apply to a lesion paper: what further premise is required to get from unavailability-to-awareness to non-bodily existence; whether awareness that is non-inferential and non-observational, and hence immune to error about which thing it is of, can carry a substance conclusion at all; and whether the empirical record of disturbed bodily selfhood in deafferented patients refutes the argument or is simply irrelevant to it, since the Flying Man is stipulated rather than observed.

Please deliver the following:

1. The question exactly as it should appear on the paper, with a stem of no more than two hundred words that states the scenario and the clinical material in plain language. The stem must be self-contained; nothing in the question may require knowledge of Islamic philosophy beyond it. State the scenario in your own words — do not quote or paraphrase any translation, and do not build the question on textual exegesis.
2. A model answer of eight hundred to one thousand words, written to distinction standard. It should argue a position, not survey the field, and it should not lean on a comparison with Descartes beyond what is strictly load-bearing.
3. A marking scheme of eight criteria totalling thirty marks, each with its mark value and a one-line note naming the characteristic way candidates fail it — for instance, treating the negative result as though it were a positive one, or sliding from incorrigibility of self-awareness to independence of the self.
4. A paragraph of a hundred to a hundred and fifty words describing the ceiling: the best answer that is still only a solid upper-second, and the single omission that keeps it there.
5. Flag anywhere the model answer does more than the question requires, so that I can trim it.

The question must not be answerable by narrating the thought experiment, nor by reciting that Avicenna anticipates the cogito. Sixty minutes is allowed, so the crux has to be reachable. If you judge that the ablation analogy misdescribes what Avicenna is doing, say so in a short note before the question and build the question on the crux you prefer — but the analogy is what I most want tested, so I should need to be persuaded.
