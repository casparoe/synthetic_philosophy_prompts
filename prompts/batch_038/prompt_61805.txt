I direct the audience-ethics review function at a nonprofit that runs two kinds of campaign out of the same communications team: behaviour-change work aimed at the people whose welfare is at stake (vaccination uptake, smoking cessation, road safety), and fundraising appeals aimed at donors who are not the beneficiaries of the gift. Roughly fifteen campaign elements reach us each week — messages, images, landing pages, defaults in our patient portal, targeting parameters, and occasional proposals to work through a community partner — and two officers with communications backgrounds but no philosophical training have to classify each one and write a rationale that goes into a public register. We have forty-five minutes per item. We have no capacity to run experiments, so we cannot test a disclosure variant against a non-disclosure variant; we work from the materials in front of us and whatever the team already knows. The director signs off only on escalations.

Our current instrument is a single question we call the 'disclosure test': would this technique still work as well if the audience knew we were using it, and why? Answers go into three bins. Bin A, works just as well — clear argument, accurate statistics made vivid, plain description of a benefit. Bin B, works somewhat less well — loss framing, a suggested giving amount, a deadline that is real but emphasised. Bin C, stops working if disclosed — invented scarcity, an authority we cannot substantiate, guilt aimed at a named individual, a message whose force depends on the audience not knowing who paid for it. Bin C is refused, Bin A is cleared, and everything else is meant to be easy. In practice almost all of the consequential items land in Bin B, where the test tells us nothing: a technique that loses a third of its effect on disclosure is neither transparent nor deceptive, and we currently settle those by seniority and mood.

Please build the procedure we should be using instead. Specifically:

1. State, in plain language and without jargon, what the disclosure intuition is actually tracking, and then what it is not tracking — why the trichotomy collapses exactly where our hard cases sit. Keep whatever in it is worth keeping.

2. Give an ordered set of questions, no more than seven, each with an account of what counts as a sufficient answer and what counts as a non-answer, such that an officer who did not create the material can run the set in forty-five minutes.

3. Preserve at least these distinctions, and tell me explicitly which of them must survive the simplification and which can be dropped without corrupting the instrument: influence that supplies reasons versus influence that changes how reasons are processed; techniques that exploit a known condition or vulnerability of the particular audience versus techniques that merely make use of a general feature of human cognition; and cases where the person being influenced is the person whose welfare is at stake versus cases where they are not. Draw on whatever the philosophical work on manipulation, on the ethics of choice architecture, and on the difference between manipulating and persuading has that is genuinely usable at a desk. Do not give me a reading list; give me the parts that change an answer.

4. Handle the structural cases the disclosure test cannot see at all: a default or pre-set option where disclosure does not reduce efficacy but the choice is still steered; an intervention the audience asked for, such as a commitment device or a self-exclusion request; and influence routed through a third party the audience trusts.

5. Specify the output. A classification code, a register entry of one or two sentences that a member of the public could understand and that a campaign manager could contest, and two explicit lists: conditions under which the officers must stop and refer to the director, and conditions under which the question is one of values that no procedure can settle and must be handed to a person.

6. Mark every step that is a judgment call dressed as a measurement. Where the procedure asks an officer to estimate a magnitude — how much efficacy is lost on disclosure, how severe a harm, how specific a vulnerability — say what to do when no estimate is available, and whose estimate counts when two officers disagree.

Then run the finished procedure on three items and report which way each comes out, including where the result is one I will not like:

First, a winter fundraising appeal that encloses a sheet of personalised address labels as an unsolicited gift, states that the recipient may keep the labels whether or not they give, and pre-fills a giving amount at the second-highest tier of our historical distribution. It is our best-performing appeal of the year and the finance director has asked that it be repeated four times.

Second, a cessation campaign aimed at fourteen- and fifteen-year-olds using short-form video of a young person describing what treatment for mouth cancer involves, accurate in every particular, delivered through the platforms' own interest and lookalike audiences.

Third, a portal default that books eligible patients into a screening appointment they can decline in one click, with the default and its rationale explained on the same screen, which moved uptake from forty-one to sixty-eight per cent.

Finally, state what the procedure cannot do: the kinds of item it will systematically misclassify, the direction of the error, and what would have to be true for a determined team to game it while remaining formally compliant on every question it asks.
