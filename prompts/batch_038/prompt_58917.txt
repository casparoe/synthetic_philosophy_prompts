I work on training infrastructure for large neural networks — precision formats, memory hierarchies, redundancy schemes, and the statistical behaviour of optimizers. I am fluent in the modern vocabulary and do not need any of it explained to me. What I need is help reading a historical text in that vocabulary without flattering either side.

I have been working through von Neumann's short posthumous book on the brain and the computer — the Silliman lectures, published by Yale in 1958, deliberately unfinished. My reading notes reduce to roughly six theses, which I paraphrase here rather than quote, since I want the argument and not the prose:

1. The nervous system is a computing machine whose individual components are considerably less precise than the electronic components they are being compared with, and whose aggregate performance nonetheless exceeds them.
2. Reliability can be bought out of unreliable components by redundancy, but redundancy costs logical depth — extra sequential stages. He took the number of successive steps in any human act of thinking to be very small compared with the number of elementary operations a machine performs in the same interval, and he treats that scarcity of depth as a binding constraint on how much error correction the brain can afford.
3. The system is a hybrid: at the level of the single nerve cell the signalling looks digital and all-or-none, while other levels involve continuously graded, chemical and aggregate phenomena, so the machine is neither purely digital nor purely analog.
4. On memory: he derives an upper bound on total capacity by supposing that each nerve cell can register something like one bit per second across a lifetime — a figure I recall as being of the order of ten to the twentieth bits, though the exact number is not what interests me. More interesting is his suggestion that this memory is not organized by address the way a machine store is, that recall works by content instead, and that one must separate an inherited, chromosomal memory from an acquired, synaptic one.
5. His declared unfinished business: the nervous system plainly uses some code, but it is not arithmetic and not formal-logical in the way an instruction repertoire is; its primitives are structural and statistical rather than arithmetical; and describing it will require a genuinely new formal discipline sitting between logic and thermodynamics rather than either one.
6. The physical economy differs in kind as well as degree — energy per operation, miniaturization — and the clean architectural separation of arithmetic unit from memory that defines the stored-program machine may have no counterpart in nervous tissue. Equally important is what is absent: there is no account anywhere of how the machine acquires its program.

Please translate each of the six into the vocabulary I actually use — weights and gradients, forward and backward passes, quantization and low-precision arithmetic, dropout and ensembling, overparameterization and double descent, capacity versus sample complexity, noisy stochastic optimization as implicit regularization, inductive bias, superposed and distributed representations, content-based retrieval via attention, memorization versus generalization, calibration and scaling laws — as literally as the material permits.

For each thesis, give me: the closest counterpart in my vocabulary; a verdict of clean mapping, distorted mapping, or no counterpart at all; and where the mapping is distorted, a precise statement of what had to be bent to make it fit. Two of his notions worry me in particular. His redundancy argument is a theory of reliability against component failure at run time, whereas my redundancy devices are theories of variance and generalization at train time — tell me whether these are the same idea wearing different clothes or genuinely different problems, and if different, which one his argument needs. And his digital/analog hybrid classification seems to me to have no live meaning in a framework where everything is floating point and the only real axis is discrete tokens versus continuous embeddings; if that is right, say plainly that the distinction has died rather than reconstructing it for him.

Then the residue report. List the claims that have no counterpart in my vocabulary, and for each say whether the failure is a gap in my framework, a claim that was vaguer than it looked, or a question that has dissolved rather than been answered. Pay particular attention to the asymmetry: a large part of the residue may be his framework's blind spot rather than mine, since learning and credit assignment — essentially my whole subject — do not exist in his account at all. I want that separated from the residue that runs the other way, where I have nothing corresponding to his notion of a code, or to logical depth treated as a scarce cognitive resource rather than an architectural property.

Finally, flag every place where my vocabulary makes his claim come out true by definitional fiat. The associative-memory point is the obvious candidate: content-based retrieval is now so standard that mapping his remark onto it risks turning a speculative hunch into a prediction after the fact. Tell me where that kind of anachronistic flattery is happening and where the anticipation is real.

I do not want the celebratory reading in which he foresaw deep learning, and I do not want the dismissive one in which a mathematician guessed at neuroscience. No bibliography, no reading list, no recommendations — just the translation and the accounting of what it could not carry.
