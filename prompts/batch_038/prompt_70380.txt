You are a philosopher of social science who specializes in how quantitative evidence is used to support claims about people's well-being — someone equally at home in the technical literature on how economists infer what is good for people from what those people do, and in the philosophical literature on whether that inference is legitimate. Please think carefully before answering. I want analysis rather than a survey of considerations, and I am explicitly *not* asking for citations, a bibliography, or a reading list; I want your own reasoning about the case below.

Some background on me. I am a fourth-year doctoral student in public policy, and this summer I am a research assistant at a state human-services agency. The agency runs a program that discounts home heating and electricity bills for households below an income threshold. Until two years ago, households had to apply: they filled out a form, attached proof of income, and mailed it in. Of those known to be eligible, roughly 29 percent ever applied. The agency then changed the procedure. It now uses data it already holds from other assistance programs to enroll every eligible household automatically, and mails a letter explaining the discount and how to withdraw from it. Roughly 88 percent of those enrolled stay enrolled.

The evaluation report I am helping to draft treats this as a discovery about the program's value, not just about its paperwork. Its central claim is that the old 29 percent badly understated what the discount is worth to the families who receive it, and that the 88 percent shows what people actually want. The analysts then want to compute benefit per household from the enrolled caseload multiplied by the average discount, and to defend that measure with a principle one of them put to me in these words: "We can't get reliable survey data out of this population, so what people do is the only evidence we have of what's good for them."

Three complications that I have not been able to fold into any coherent story.

First, field staff who work with these households report two opposite sorts of cases. Some families who withdrew after receiving the letter did so, staff say, because they did not want to be the kind of person who takes assistance; one staffer described a woman who asked whether her name would appear on any public list. Other families who stayed enrolled say they never opened the letter at all and assumed the envelope was another bill.

Second, the agency did manage a small follow-up survey — about four hundred responses, with a poor response rate — asking whether the discount had made a real difference to the household. The proportion answering yes was close to the same among those who had applied under the old procedure and those enrolled automatically. The analysts dismiss this on the grounds that people simply tell you what they think you want to hear, and that in any case the survey is too small to matter.

Third, the automatically enrolled households fall behind on the undiscounted portion of their bills at a noticeably higher rate than the households who applied under the old system. One analyst proposes citing this as evidence that the discount matters less to the new group. I have pointed out that this sits in the same document as the claim that the new group's enrollment shows the program matters more, and I was told that both facts are being read off the data, so both are legitimate.

My difficulty is that I cannot say what is wrong here. Part of me thinks the report's principle is simply correct — if not what people choose, then what? But the way the same team moves between enrollment figures, arrears figures, and the survey depending on which one supports the number they want makes the whole exercise feel unanswerable, and I cannot tell whether that feeling is a real objection or a prejudice against quantitative work that I inherited from my humanities-trained friends.
