I'm teaching a graduate seminar on the philosophy of style, and this term I'm pairing two things that almost never meet: Borges's Pierre Menard, Author of the Quixote, and Hofstadter's Letter Spirit project — the FARG work on machine letterform design collected in Fluid Concepts and Creative Analogies. The pairing is mine, and I want it stress-tested before I put it in front of students, one of whom is a working type designer and will spot a decorative analogy immediately.

Letter Spirit asks what makes every 'a' an 'a'. Its answer isn't a definition but a family of realizations: glyphs assembled on a grid from a fixed repertoire of stroke elements, where any single letter matters less than a whole alphabet designed in one coherent hand, and where style is what shows up in the artifact when a designer makes consistent, non-arbitrary choices among near-equivalent possibilities under constraint. Menard inverts the setup. He gives up on becoming Cervantes — the easy route — and instead produces pages verbally identical to Cervantes's while remaining a twentieth-century French symbolist, and his narrator insists those pages are nevertheless a different and richer work, because of who made them and how. Same body, different spirit; Letter Spirit's whole business is different bodies, same spirit.

Here's what I want from you:

1. Run the mapping seriously rather than metaphorically. What plays the role of the grid, of the stroke element, of the completed typeface family, when the medium is a novel? Does the mapping predict anything about the story, or only redescribe it?

2. Is Menard a counterexample to Letter Spirit's implicit assumption that style is legible in the form? Consider whether the question dissolves once you ask where style lives: at the level of the individual mark, or of the whole family. Menard's visible output is a couple of chapters and fragments plus a mountain of symbolist ephemera; Cervantes's is a corpus. Also: Letter Spirit treats variation-within-consistency as the mark of a real style, so a font that merely repeats itself is styleless — does that make Menard's ideal of exact repetition anti-stylistic on Hofstadter's own terms?

3. Where does Goodman's autographic/allographic distinction land here? If literature is notational, Menard's pages just are Cervantes's pages — one work, not a copy — and the narrator's claim of extra richness stops making sense. Do letterforms sit cleanly on the other side of that line, or does Letter Spirit straddle it, given that a font is the paradigm of the repeatable type and calligraphy the paradigm of the singular trace?

4. Tell me where the pairing breaks. I want the specific points at which forcing Borges into Hofstadter's vocabulary distorts one or the other, and where the honest critic has to concede the analogy is clever and empty.

5. End by saying what the story contributes to the debate about style that argument alone wouldn't deliver — in particular, whether it establishes that style attaches to a process of making rather than to a product, and whether Letter Spirit could absorb that without abandoning its aim of producing style a viewer can read off the glyph alone.

Work from the gist of both texts; I'm not asking for quotations and would much rather you flag a detail you're unsure of than invent one. No reading list, no bibliography. Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then give the reasoning — no preamble and no restating my question. American English throughout.
