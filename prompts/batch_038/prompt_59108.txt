I'm on a small team that has to decide whether to believe an interpretability finding before we let it drive a safety decision — things like "the model represents whether the user is a minor" or "there's a direction for sycophancy that the RL stage amplifies." Half of us think claims like that are as real as claims about tables; the other half think they're useful bookkeeping fictions. The problem is the argument never actually gets joined, because nobody on either side can say what would make one of them right.

So before I wade through more accounts of mental representation, I want the spec. What does a theory of representaion in a neural net have to deliver to count as adequate? Fix the purpose for me like this: it has to make "the model represents X" come out *true* for the findings we'd stake a decision on and *false* for the ones we wouldn't, and false for a better reason than "we couldn't find it."

Give me the requirements list, each one with the case or consideration behind it. I want the awkward cases covered, at minimum:

- a direction that decodes some property with high accuracy but where ablation does nothing to behaviour, and it sits in a layer that gets overwritten downstream;
- a polysemantic unit that fires for cat faces and car fronts — does the model represent cat face, car front, both, neither, or the disjunction?
- a model that reliably says Rome is in France: for it to be wrong about Rome, it has to represent something falsely, so tell me what has to be true for that;
- two models with identical behaviour and totally different internals — is one of them misdescribed, or does the theory have to say both are right?
- a feature that only exists under a particular prompt format or tokenizer choice;
- something the model demonstrably uses but that nobody can find as a direction, a unit, or a circuit.

Also the distinctions it's not allowed to collapse (decodable vs. used, representing-that vs. representing-as, whatever else you think matters) and the facts about how these systems are actually built that it can't contradict — superposition, features living in directions rather than neurons, ablations routinely underdelivering, and so on.

Then the parts I care about most. Which of these requirements are genuinely non-negotiable, and which are one camp's priors wearing a lab coat? I suspect "representations must be causally efficacious" is doing a ton of unearned work — push back if I'm wrong. Which single requirement knocks out the most candidate theories? And if some subset is jointly unsatisfiable — I have a feeling that something like mind-independence plus abundance plus causal efficacy can't all be had at once — show me the tension explicitly rather than hedging, because if the list itself is a no-go result that's the finding.

End with a short scorecard, one paragraph each, of the obvious contenders: a resemblance/isomorphism account, a causal-role account, a learning-history or teleological one, and a deflationary one that says representation-talk is just a stance we take. Keep that brief, the criteria are where I want the weight.
