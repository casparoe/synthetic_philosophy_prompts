I'm in a phil-sci seminar this term and we just did Lakatos. Our prof handed out a case study about a metallurgy group and two people in the section, Sam and Anouk, argued about it for basically the whole second hour, and I honestly couldn't tell who was winning. Here's the case as we were given it, then their exchange.

The case: a group has argued for about twelve years that a family of alloys hardens because dislocations get pinned at solute clusters. Three separate times the measured hardening rates came out wrong, and each time they patched the belt around the core rather than touching the core — first grain-boundary segregation soaking up solute, then residual stress shielding, then impurity pinning at low temperature. The stress-shielding patch implied hardening should reverse above some temperature, which nobody had ever measured; a different lab measured it two years later and the reversal showed up roughly where they said. Meanwhile a rival group explains the same three misses from a precipitate-coarsening mechanism with one free parameter and no patching, but the rivals never made the reversal call.

The argument went:

(1) Sam: you can't kill a programme with anomalies, that's the whole point — the core is protected by the negative heuristic, so patching is normal and not evidence against them at all.
(2) Anouk: sure, they're allowed to patch, the question is whether the patches are progressive. All three auxiliaries were invented after the failure they fix and none had independent support at the time, so that's a degenerating problemshift by definition.
(3) Sam: not all three — the shielding one predicted something nobody had measured and it came out right, and the group says they weren't working from any reversal data when they wrote it down.
(4) Anouk: that's not novelty in the sense that counts. If an auxiliary gets fitted until the numbers behave, a later match is luck, and on your version any fudge that survives becomes retroactively novel. Novelty has to be temporal — predicting something not yet known — otherwise the criterion just rewards good guessing.
(5) Sam: temporal novelty makes progressivity depend on who happens to publish first and on what's sitting unanalysed in someone's drawer. The real test, per Zahar, is whether the fact went into constructing the auxiliary. Also if you demand temporal novelty you lose a bunch of the historic successes everyone agrees were progressive, including the Mercury one.
(6) Anouk: even granting you that, one hit against three patches in twelve years isn't a progressive sequence. And appraisal is comparative in Lakatos anyway, so with a rival covering the same misses on one parameter, the group should have switched long ago.
(7) Sam: he has no meta-criterion for switching, and you can't be instantly rational. Degenerating-looking programmes have come back before. All she's shown is that the group is stubborn, not that it's irrational.
(8) Anouk: then the framework never actually tells anybody to do anything, so it isn't a methodology, it's a comforting story about the past.

Can you go through this move by move — rate each of the eight from clearly right to clearly wrong, with a sentence or two of justification per move, and say where the facts of the case (not the theory) are doing the work? Then tell me who's more right overall, and what both of them are missing that a better reading of Lakatos would have brought up.

Start with your bottom line in the very first sentence, then the reasoning — no preamble, no restating my question.
