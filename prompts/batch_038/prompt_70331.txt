I convene a graduate seminar this term on Marx's critique of technology, co-taught with a colleague from our institute's machine-learning group, and a dispute has arisen that I would like adjudicated rather than merely hosted. In preparation for our third session he circulated a short position paper whose central move is an identification: a large language model, he argues, is precisely what Marx meant by the general intellect objectified in fixed capital. The model is the accumulated knowledge of the species — its sciences, its administrative routines, its linguistic habits — congealed into an artefact that now confronts the individual producer as an alien power; the human beings arrayed around it do not so much work with it as supervise it, which he takes to match Marx's sketch, in the notebook section on fixed capital, of the worker stepping to one side of the production process as its watchman and regulator. From that identification he draws two conclusions he considers licensed: that Marx's value theory predicts its own dissolution under automation, since the creation of wealth has come to depend less and less on directly expended labour time, and that the passage therefore obliges us to read Marx as a theorist of post-capitalist abundance rather than of exploitation at the point of production.

To keep the exercise textual rather than impressionistic, here are two sentences from that section of the notebooks, written in 1857–58 and first published only in the twentieth century, which he quotes at the head of his paper: "Thus, the specific mode of working here appears directly as becoming transferred from the worker to capital in the form of the machine, and his own labour capacity devalued thereby"; and "But to the degree that large industry develops, the creation of real wealth comes to depend less on labour time and on the amount of labour employed than on the power of the agencies set in motion during labour time …" My own hesitation is that the analogy is being made to do double duty — it must carry a claim about what Marx's concepts mean and a claim about what contemporary artefacts are — and neither duty seems to be discharged by resemblance at the level of imagery. I should add that his paper leans heavily on the draft status of the passage, treating it as the esoteric core of the mature theory rather than as a working note, and that this is one of the things I want examined rather than assumed.

Could you audit the comparison for me? I would like an explicit table of correspondences setting out what, on his reading, plays the role of fixed capital, of living labour, of the piecemeal transfer of the means of production's value into the product, of socially necessary labour time, of the general state of science, and of the worker as watchman and regulator. Then please separate the disanalogies that actually threaten his inference from the merely decorative ones: I suspect, for instance, that a trained model's relation to the labour embodied in its production data, and to the depreciation of its own value over a service life measured in months, is not the relation Marx has in mind when machinery passes its value on to the commodities it helps make — but I want to know whether that asymmetry does any real argumentative work or marks only a difference of degree. Say too what the analogy quietly imports from its source: what must be true of knowledge, of the artefact, and of the labour theory of value for the identification to go through, and whether Marx's point in the passage is a claim about productivity or a claim about the value-form and its internal contradiction. Finally, tell me whether either of his two conclusions survives once the disanalogies are on the table; if the passage will not bear them, indicate what in Marx's treatment of machinery elsewhere would be the better anchor, and whether a better analogy is available for the relation between a model and the labour that produced it.

Close with a single line of the form Verdict: <score> — <label>, where <score> is a number between 0 and 1 giving the fraction of his intended conclusion that the analogy can carry, and <label> is exactly one of: carries both conclusions; carries the value-theoretic conclusion only; carries the abundance conclusion only; carries a weaker conclusion; carries nothing.
