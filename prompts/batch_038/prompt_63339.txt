I sit on a small working group drafting the criteria by which an uncrewed ocean-world mission would be permitted to report that it has found life. Its instrument suite can image at sub-micron resolution and measure bulk composition, molecular chirality, and isotope ratios; it cannot culture anything, cannot obtain long sequences, and will not be around to answer follow-up questions. In trying to pin down what such criteria ought to track, I have accumulated a set of verdicts about terrestrial cases that I cannot reconcile, and I want to know what definition, if any, they add up to.

The verdicts, in the order I recorded them:

1. A human cheek cell on a slide — metabolizing, transcriptionally active, incapable of division, certain to die within a day: alive.
2. A bacterial spore, a century old, metabolism below any threshold I could measure, which germinates when plated: alive throughout the century.
3. The same spore heated until it will never germinate: dead. But I cannot say at what point during the heating it ceased to be alive, and the property that distinguishes 2 from 3 was not, at the start of the heating, detectable by me.
4. Purified tobacco mosaic virus particles in a buffer: not alive.
5. That same virus replicating and evolving inside an infected leaf: still not alive. I hold this even though nearly every functional description that is true of it in the leaf is false of it in the vial.
6. A giant virus whose genome is larger than that of some obligate bacterial endosymbionts and which carries genes for several aminoacyl-tRNA synthetases and translation factors, but has no ribosomes and no energy metabolism of its own: not alive.
7. An obligate intracellular bacterial endosymbiont of an insect — genome far smaller than the giant virus's, no longer culturable outside its host, its metabolism so interlocked with the host's that most of what it needs is imported: alive.
8. A mitochondrion: not alive. A beating heart in a perfusion box: alive, but not an organism. A brain-dead patient on a ventilator whose cells are largely viable: a dead organism containing living parts.
9. A mule: alive, and an organism, though it will never reproduce.
10. A self-organized assembly built from the dissociated skin and heart-muscle cells of an amphibian embryo, which swims, closes its own wounds, and — in a dish containing loose cells — corrals them into piles that develop into similar assemblies: alive, but not an organism. I am uneasy about both halves of that.
11. A synthetic minimal cell whose genome was written on a machine from a human-designed file and placed into a recipient cytoplasm: the DNA in the freezer was not alive; the dividing cell is.
12. A self-replicating program in an artificial-life simulation, with mutation, competition for resources, and measurable adaptation over thousands of generations: not alive.
13. A flame: not alive, though it grows, holds itself far from equilibrium, consumes fuel, and propagates. A growing crystal: not alive, though it transmits its own structure, defects included. A prion: not alive.
14. A protocell system in my own lab — vesicles that grow and divide, containing a catalytic RNA network that amplifies itself far too sloppily to transmit its variations: not alive, and not dead. I have no verdict here, and I suspect the language does not supply one.

The best I have managed myself is this: something is alive if and only if (i) it is a discrete, spatially bounded individual; (ii) it maintains itself in a persistent non-equilibrium state by harnessing free energy from its surroundings; and (iii) it is capable of participating in open-ended evolution, in that it belongs to a lineage where heritable variation meets differential persistence. Call it the boundary–maintenance–evolution account. It handles 13 and 4, and seems to handle them for the right reasons. But clause (ii) contradicts 1, 2, and 3; clause (iii) contradicts 7, 9, and 10; and all three clauses are satisfied by a case I would refuse to call alive — a fleet of solar-powered machines that mine ore, repair themselves, and assemble copies of themselves from a stored design file, where copying errors are themselves inherited and selected against. Meanwhile I am committed to calling an organism of wholly non-terran chemistry found beneath the ice alive. So any clause that excludes the machines because of what they are made of looks like exactly the substrate chauvinism our criteria must not contain.

Cases 6 and 7 are the pair I cannot live with. What separates them is not genome size, not gene count, not independence from a host, not possession of translation-related genes, and not evolutionary antiquity. If the answer is that 7 maintains its own boundary and synthesizes its own proteins while 6 does neither, that is beginning to look like a list rather than a principle, and I want to know whether it can be made into one.

Please state the principle, or the small number of candidate principles, that these verdicts add up to, as precisely as you can, clause by clause, saying for each clause which numbered case motivates it and what it costs. Then give its deliverance on two cases I have deliberately left undecided: a membrane-free extract of broken cells, supplied with fuel, that transcribes, translates, and replicates a plasmid for a day and a half; and the machine fleet. State plainly which of my verdicts your account cannot reproduce, and where you think I should revise rather than complicate — I would sooner be told that 5, or 7, or 14 is simply wrong than be handed a definition with nine clauses and three indexicals. Finally: does the account you settle on define one property or two, given that my usage runs on both *alive* and *is an organism*; does it treat life as all-or-nothing or as admitting of degrees, and if the latter, what fixes the threshold and can that threshold be drawn only by someone who knows what would have happened had conditions been different; and can any such account be operationalized by an instrument that measures only what ours measures, or is the honest conclusion that our criteria must be tracking something other than life?
