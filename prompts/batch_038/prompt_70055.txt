I freelance on the ideas desk and I keep running into the same move in stories about doomscrolling, radicalisation, falling birth rates, that sort of thing. Somebody says a belief is basically a parasite: it spreads because it's good at spreading, not because it's true or because it makes its carriers better off, which is why smart people can hold ideas that wreck their lives and why you cant just argue them out of it. The framing goes back to Dawkins coining the meme and to the idea-as-virus line in memetics, and now it's everywhere.

I want to audit it before I repeat it in print. Give me the correspondence table (what's the parasite, what's the host, is there anything doing the work of an immune system or a germ line), then the places the mapping snaps, then what it quietly smuggles in — I suspect the harm claim is circular, since "bad for the host" gets measured by the same spread-logic the analogy is supposed to explain, and I suspect treating believers as hosts undercuts the arguer's own position.

So: exactly five numbered points, no intro and no conclusion. The first three should be the mapping and the disanalogies that actually matter, the fourth the smuggled premises, and the fifth a plain-English verdict on whether the conclusion survives or whether the analogy was carrying it the whole time. A sentence or two per point. No bibliography needed, just make it something a biologist and a philosopher could both read without wincing.
