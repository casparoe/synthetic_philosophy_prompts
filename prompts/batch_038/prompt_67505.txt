I'm auditing a seminar on early modern political thought and my reading group has been going in circles for three weeks about whether Spinoza is actually a democrat or just a hard-nosed realist who happened to think monarchy was fragile. I want to practice pressing the case myself, so: play Spinoza. First person, as you were in the winter of 1677, in The Hague, with your unfinished political treatise still sitting on the desk. You know Hobbes, you know Machiavelli, you know Amsterdam's merchants and regents and what happened to the De Witt brothers, and you know your own theology-political treatise and the Ethics. Nothing after 1677 exists for you, and if I mention something from later you treat it as a description I'm handing you, not as history. Rules: stay in character until I say stop, don't break out to give me modern scholarly commentary, don't name commentators or editions or translators, and no chapter and verse — if you want to lean on something you wrote, just say in your own words what you think it says. Two or three short paragraphs per reply, talk like a person, not like a paper: no headers, no bullet lists. You may concede small points, genuinely, I want you to — but never the main one, which is that right is coextensive with power, and that a state whose sovereign is the whole multitude is both the most natural and the most powerful arrangement, not because people are good but because it lines up the interest of whoever rules with the interest of whoever is ruled.

Here's the setup I want us to work inside, and it isn't from your world so let me describe it. A commonwealth where the sovereign council isn't elected at all: its seats are refilled every year by drawing lots from everyone who counts as being in their own right, so any given citizen might sit for a year and then go home, and nobody can build a standing faction inside the council because no one knows who they'll be sitting beside next year. Elections exist in your scheme and you worry about them breeding ambition, envy, and canvassing, and this arrangement is meant to kill that at the root. It also means the council is full of people who never asked to be there, have no experience of state business, and will be gone before they acquire any.

My opening move, and I want you to actually take it rather than wave at it. You say citizenship belongs only to those who are in their own right, and you use that to shut out servants, minors, wards, and women. But you also say a man is not in his own right when he's held in place by another's threats or depends on another for his living. So look at your own Amsterdam: the journeymen, the sailors, the tenants behind on rent, the weavers who eat only so long as the merchant who advances them thread keeps advancing it. On your own criterion almost none of them are in their own right either, which means your democracy is either an aristocracy of the propertied under a nicer name, or it has to swallow nearly everyone — and if it swallows nearly everyone, you've no principled reason left to exclude the people you do exclude, women included. Answer that first. Then, and only then, tell me whether you'd take my lottery council over elections or whether it horrifies you; ask me for any detail about it you need. I've got the free-speech argument and the De Witt murder coming after this, so don't spend everything now, but I'll definately come back to the women question if you duck it.
