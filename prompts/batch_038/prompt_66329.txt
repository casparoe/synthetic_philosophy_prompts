For thirty-six years I earned my living by measuring things — neutron scattering first, then two decades calibrating instrumentation at a national laboratory — and since retiring I have taken up philosophy with the seriousness I once reserved for error budgets. This autumn I am leading a twelve-week evening seminar at a community institute: nineteen adults, mostly retired schoolteachers, a nurse, a structural engineer, and two graduate students in the humanities. None has mathematics beyond secondary-school algebra, and their cosmology is largely documentary-derived. They are bright, sceptical, and entirely willing to be told that they are wrong.

In week seven I want to spend a single hundred-minute session on the fine-tuning argument, and I want to do it without lecturing, because I have found that when I explain the argument my students either accept it instantly and badly or dismiss it instantly and badly. What I want them to acquire is narrower and harder: a felt grasp of why whatever weight the argument has sits in an asymmetry between two conditional probabilities rather than in anybody's surprise; why the retort *of course we would have found the universe life-permitting, since we are here* feels decisive and yet, on inspection, does not obviously perform the work its users expect of it; and why the final verdict depends on choices the argument itself declines to make — the choice of a sample space of possible universes, and of a way of weighting the members of that space.

Design that session as a game or staged exercise the participants physically play, not a thought experiment I read aloud to them. I require the following.

1. The apparatus: cheap and procurable at a stationer — cards, dice, an urn, a hat, a whiteboard. Tell me what I must prepare beforehand and roughly how long preparation takes.
2. Rules and a scoring scheme that needs no money, only tokens or points, and that makes the likelihood asymmetry bear upon something the players actually want to win.
3. A sequence of rounds in which the retort quoted above is generated by the players themselves, at least once, and in which the game then confronts them with a companion situation where precisely the same retort would license an inference they will flatly refuse to make. I do not want to be the person who points out the discrepancy; I want them to notice it, quarrel over it, and fail to dissolve it.
4. At least one round in which a player proposes a very large or unbounded number of trials as the solution, and is then required to specify what proportion of those trials are life-permitting, and discovers that this cannot be settled by counting anything available in the room.
5. A minute-by-minute timeline, including where I am permitted to speak and what I am permitted to say. I expect the temptation to intervene to be strong and I would like it fenced in advance.
6. A debrief that maps particular moments of play — the ones you expect actually to have happened — onto the structure of the argument, and that releases the technical vocabulary only at the end, when the participants are already holding the thing.
7. A candid list of the ways this will misfire the first time I run it: the participant who does the arithmetic aloud and spoils a discovery, the one who declines to play on principle, the round that ends in a tie and therefore teaches nothing, the pair who collude. Tell me how to salvage each, and where salvage is impossible and the round should simply be cut.
8. A closing statement of what the game cannot reproduce and I must therefore supply verbally — for instance, that real fine-tuning concerns continuously varying quantities rather than a shuffled deck, that no prior for a designer hypothesis is furnished by anything in the room, and that observer selection in cosmology is not the same operation as being one of several players who happened to draw a favourable card. I would rather know the limits than be sold a toy.

Two constraints, and I ask you to respect them strictly. First, no quotations and no citations: I do not want a reading list, my students will not check anything, and a plausible-sounding attribution is worse than none at all. Second, if you need a number — a probability, a ratio, the width of a range — invent it, round it, and mark it clearly as invented. I spent my career defending measured values and I have no intention of ending it by circulating counterfeit ones.
