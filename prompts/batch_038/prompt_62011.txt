You are a philosopher of medicine with deep expertise in the debate over whether "disease" picks out a value-free biological kind or an irreducibly normative one. Begin by briefly sketching the space of candidate analyses you think could fit the pattern below — including ones you expect to fail — and only then commit to a recommendation.

Here are my verdicts, arrived at case by case, which I am fairly confident of:

1. Untreated high blood pressure, no symptoms, nothing feels worse: a disease.
2. Chronic widespread pain with no findable lesion: a disease.
3. Situs inversus totalis, everything functions, nobody would ever notice: not a disease.
4. Short stature caused by growth-hormone deficiency: a disease. Familial short stature reaching exactly the same adult height, with exactly the same social and occupational consequences: not a disease.
5. Sickle-cell trait, one copy, in a malarial region: not a disease. Two copies: a disease. Same variant, opposite verdicts.
6. An audiogram showing severe hearing loss: a disease at twenty-five, mere ageing at eighty.
7. Infertility in a thirty-year-old who wants children: a disease. The identical physiological state in a seventy-year-old who does not: not a disease. And I insist this is not about the desire — if the thirty-year-old were happily child-free by choice, I would still call it a disease.
8. Pseudocholinesterase deficiency — unable to break down succinylcholine — is a disease now, though it has never harmed anyone outside an operating theatre that has existed for about seventy years.
9. A variant making someone markedly more susceptible to urban particulate matter — disabling in a city, unnoticeable in the countryside — is not a disease, even though it has exactly the same shape as (8): harmless for most of human history, harmful only in an environment we recently built.

My tentative principle is that a condition is a disease just when some part of the organism performs its species-typical function below the level typical of healthy members of its age-and-sex class, assessed against the environments humans occupied for most of their history. It seems to deliver (1)–(7), and it visibly breaks on the pair (8)/(9), which I cannot tell apart.

Deliver: a small set of candidate principles, each stated precisely enough to be applied mechanically, with the clause of each that is motivated by a specific case above; your own verdicts on two cases I have not raised — a variant that shortens life by a decade after age seventy while producing no symptoms whatsoever, and left-handedness in a world built for right-handed people; and a candid statement of which of my verdicts the best principle forces me to abandon, together with whether that principle is ultimately naturalist, normativist, or hybrid. Where a simpler principle overturns a verdict and a more complex one saves it, say explicitly which trade you recommend and why.
