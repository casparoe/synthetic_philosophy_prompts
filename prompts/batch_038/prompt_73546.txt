I am a novelist. My current book covers the weeks between the assassination at Sarajevo and the first general mobilizations, told from inside six chancelleries and two general staffs. I have lived with the Fischer controversy and the reactions to it for years; what I need now is not more history but a principle, because how I distribute blame determines how much interiority each character gets.

My verdicts on my own people, as they stand:

The chancellor who gives his ally an unconditional assurance in the first week of July and then leaves for the coast, sincerely convinced the crisis can be localized: responsible more than anyone in the book.

The imperial count who has wanted this war for twenty years and says so at every council: less responsible than the chancellor — which I know is perverse and cannot yet explain.

The chief of staff who insists that once the railway tables begin nothing can be halted: all but blameless, I suspect only because his options were foreclosed before my first chapter.

The foreign secretary whose studied ambiguity about whether his country will intervene quietly emboldens the gamble, and who otherwise does nothing: heavily responsible for a pure omission.

The staff officer who learned of the plot and said nothing: responsible, and yet I find I cannot write him as a villain.

The finance minister who warned that war would bankrupt the state and was outvoted: exempt, on grounds I am unable to state.

My working distinction, invented at the desk and possibly nonsense, sorts wrongdoing three ways: the moment of an open option (they could have said otherwise and did not); the irreversible commitment (a signature, a train movement, after which nothing can be undone); and cultivated ignorance (an arrangement not to know). The chancellor is my paradigm of the second and third together, the general of the second alone, the foreign secretary of the first alone.

So: is there one principle that delivers all six verdicts, or the nearest thing to one — grounded in foresight, in office, in the difference between doing and allowing, or in the justice of blaming a man whose choices were foreclosed by others? If no such principle exists, which verdict must I surrender, and what does the novel lose with it? And what should I make of the case I have not resolved: if the timetable excuses the general merely by relocating guilt to those who drew it up decades earlier, does my ending cease to be a reckoning and become a diagram?
