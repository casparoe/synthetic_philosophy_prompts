I drafted the core section of a blog post I've been sitting on; it's below. He thinks the first objection is airtight and the second one is cheating, so we mostly just yelled at each other.

DRAFT:

Risk-weighted expected utility theory (Buchak) is supposed to rescue a genuinely global form of risk aversion from the charge that it's irrational. The setup: instead of taking a probability-weighted average of utilities, you apply a risk function R to the probability that a gamble turns out at least this good, and then integrate across utility levels. R has to be increasing with R(0)=0 and R(1)=1, and if R is convex the agent is risk averse. The selling point is that this leaves the ordinary probability calculus alone — your credences still have to be coherent, on pain of the usual Dutch book — while letting you violate the sure-thing principle. That's how it's meant to handle Allais: you can prefer the guaranteed million over the slightly-better-but-uncertain prospect and also prefer the riskier of the two non-guaranteed options, without anything incoherent in your beliefs.

Two worries. First, the convexity of R is doing exactly the work that concavity of u does in expected utility theory; the nonlinearity has just been moved from the utility axis to the probability axis. Any preference pattern a convex R generates, some concave u could generate too, since both are monotone reweightings of the same distribution. So REU theory doesn't license any new preferences — it's a notational variant, and if the Allais pattern is irrational on the standard account it's still irrational here. Changing the notation doesn't launder it.

Second, and worse, REU isn't closed under splitting. Take one real option: donating a chunk of your income to a longshot program that averts a catastrophe with probability one in a billion and has an enormous payoff otherwise. Described as a single act, a risk-averse R discounts that tail so hard the donation looks impermissible. Described instead as a sequence of smaller decisions the agent evaluates one at a time, she takes a chain of steps that leaves her worse off by her own lights — the classic money pump. The reply, as I understand it, is that acts must be individuated finely and that attitudes are directed at the whole act rather than its parts. But which description is correct is a metaphysical question about act-individuation, not a decision-theoretic one, so REU hands out different verdicts on the same practical problem depending on how you slice it and gives you no way to decide how to slice it.

Conclusion: REU can't be the foundation for a normative account of how to give under deep uncertainty, which is the main place anyone wants to put it to work.
