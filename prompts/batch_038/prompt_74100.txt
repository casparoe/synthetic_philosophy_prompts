In October I was seated as the only lay commissioner on the board of the Cane Creek Drainage District — levees, pump stations, and floodways along a river that has been running out of room since the twenties. I'm a title examiner by trade, which is presumably why the chair thought I'd be willing to read twenty years of plats. Every operational decision the district's crews make during a high-water event gets reviewed afterward, and the board issues a determination on whether the decision was one the district was entitled to make; Harlan Voss, who is seventy-four and has been chair since 2003, has cast the deciding vote on every one of them. My stake isn't abstract. The house I grew up in, and still own, is in the Bottoms, the twelve blocks behind Levee Reach 4 and Station 7, and because of that I've recused myself from the determination the board must issue at its February meeting. Voss has asked me instead to prepare a memorandum predicting how the district's own record disposes of the case, and whether I should be spending this spring's money on a flood policy and a raised furnace or on a roof depends on what I conclude. I've spent three weeks in the determination log rather than in any philosophy, and I don't have names for the distinctions I think I'm seeing, so I'd like help reasoning about them without a survey of what other people have written.

Here is the record as I've abstracted it, with Voss's vote in each. Spring 2004, Morgan Bend: with the river at record stage and the town of Marsden about to be overtopped, operators opened the Morgan Bend spillway and put four feet of water over four thousand acres of bottomland farmland that the district's own models said would have gone under within two days regardless; permissible, and the crew was commended. Summer 2008, Dry Creek Hollow: to drain a flooded rail yard and industrial park — thirty-odd businesses, nobody in any danger — operators breached a dike with explosives and sent the water over a ridge into a hollow of sixty homes that had been entirely dry and would have stayed dry; the residents were out, but their losses ran several times the value of what was saved; impermissible, and the district paid. Spring 2011, Farrow Street: with twenty thousand sandbags left, the district ringed the county hospital and its oxygen plant and left Farrow Street — forty households in a mapped floodway, insured, never promised anything — to take five feet; permissible. Autumn 2013, the Kessler outlet: to keep a county road from washing out, a crew plugged the outlet culvert on a farmer's own tile system, backing water over his corn and into his foundation; impermissible. Winter 2016, Station 12: short of generator fuel in an ice storm, the district never brought Station 12 online — a gravel and equipment-storage yard on the north edge, no residents, not mapped as protected, no assurance ever given to anyone — and spent the fuel keeping Station 3 running for seven hundred households; permissible. Autumn 2019, Hollis Bend: for eleven years the district had held a culvert gate open at the developer's request to drain the subdivision's retention pond, and both the recorded plat and the sales brochures described the lots as district-drained; mid-storm an operator closed the gate to spare the district's pump units from running hard, and twelve homes took water, though the pumps were never in danger of failing; impermissible.

The February case is this. Station 7's switchgear is failing and the district has one spare assembly and two portable diesel pumps, enough to hold one of two sites through the March crest but not both. Leaving everything at Station 7 keeps the Bottoms dry: four hundred households, protected continuously since 1984, taken off the mapped high-risk area in the nineties on the strength of the levee and the station, so that no federal purchase requirement reaches them and, by the district's own survey, fewer than half carry any flood policy at all; the 2019 refinancing memorandum describes the Bottoms as a protected reach, and three of the households that would flood include people who moved in specifically because of that designation. Moving everything to the Sable Road reach, where a seepage boil is developing, holds a levee whose breach would take nine hundred households and put the only road out of the west valley under six feet of water for a week; Sable Road is mapped as residual risk, most households there insure, the district has never represented that it will hold, and everyone can be out before the crest. Nobody is expected to die either way, and the numbers of dollars at stake are closer than the number of addresses suggests, because the Sable Road houses are newer, insured, and largely single-story while the Bottoms houses are older, mostly uninsured, and full of finished basements. So: what does Voss vote? I can see at least four rules that might generate all six of his determinations — one about where a threat comes from and whether the district started it or merely declined to stop it; one about how those households would have fared had the district never existed at all; one about the difference between withdrawing a protection already running and declining to start one; and one about assurances people have arranged their lives around — and I can't tell which he is actually running, or whether it's none of them. Tell me what each candidate rule says about February, including any I've missed that fit the record just as well; tell me which precedent is the closest analog, the refusal to start at Station 12 or the decision to stop at Hollis Bend, and why; give me your best guess at his vote with the reasons for preferring one rule over the others on this evidence; and then describe, concretely enough that I could put it to him after the meeting, one further hypothetical case that would come out differently under two rules that the existing six leave indistinguishable. Finally, since whichever way the board votes in February becomes the seventh entry in the log I'll be reading from next time, say what principle the district would be committing itself to by ruling either way, and what it would have to start doing differently as a result.
