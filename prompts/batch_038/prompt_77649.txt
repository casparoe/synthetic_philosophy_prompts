I'm moderating a batch of midterms for a second-year political theory module (Rousseau and the Enlightenment) and I want an independent read on one script before I put a mark on it. The question was:

**Q3 (500 words, 20 marks).** In the *Letter to d'Alembert*, Rousseau argues against the proposal to open a theatre in Geneva. Explain what his objection actually is — note that he is not simply saying that plays have bad content in them — and explain how he answers the suggestion that a well-regulated theatre, in which nothing indecent is performed, could be harmless or even useful to a republic. What does he offer in its place?

Here's the script, its about 550 words:

> Rousseau is careful to say that he likes the theatre. He opens the letter by admitting that he enjoys plays and finds them moving, so his quarrel is not with the pleasure of the stage as such. His objection is about what a theatre does to the city that builds one, and it turns on a claim about how drama works: a play can only please an audience by flattering what that audience already loves, so the theatre does not reform morals, it follows them and then strengthens them. That is why he thinks the whole debate about whether plays are edifying is beside the point, and why a repertoire of improving plays is not really an option for anyone. The real question is what social institution a city is installing when it installs a playhouse.
>
> For Geneva the answer is a bad one, on Rousseau's account. Geneva is a small republic whose citizens are supposed to work, know one another, and appear as they are. A playhouse creates a class of professional actors — people who earn their living by putting on appearances and by being paid to be somebody else — and Rousseau thinks that trade is incompatible with the frankness a republic rests on. Actors would also become public idols in a town where everyone knows everyone, and admiration of talent would start competing with respect for virtue. Beyond that there is the simple fact of the room: instead of meeting in the open air, citizens would shut themselves in a dark hall for hours, silent, motionless and passive, paying to be amused. Idleness and luxury follow from the building itself.
>
> His sharpest point is psychological and it is aimed at tragedy. Tragedy works by arousing pity, and Rousseau thinks the pity it arouses is morally worthless, because it costs the spectator nothing. He sheds a few tears for a suffering hero, enjoys the sense of his own sensitivity, and goes home no more inclined to help anybody real. The theatre gives us virtue as a feeling to be consumed. It is important to see what he is rejecting here, which is the Aristotelian idea that tragedy purges pity and fear. Rousseau thinks the passions do not get discharged at all — they get stirred up, and the audience carries them out through the doors with them, so a girl who has been made to feel vividly for Phèdre has been handed an attractive picture of a passion she may go on to imitate. The reason the theatre cannot make us better is that the feelings it produces are exactly the ones that let us off the hook while still moving us to act on them in private life.
>
> That is also why he is unmoved by d'Alembert's proposal for a well-regulated theatre — one in which nothing offensive to good morals is permitted and in which the actors are given legal standing and respect. Rousseau agrees that such a theatre is possible in principle; policed firmly enough, a playhouse could certainly be prevented from offending anybody, and could even be made to serve civic education. His objection is empirical and local: Geneva does not have the administrative machinery to keep it that way, and once a playhouse exists the need to fill seats will always push the managers towards whatever excites an audience most. Better not to start.
>
> What he offers instead is the festival. Plant a stake crowned with flowers in a public square, gather the citizens around it, and let them be a spectacle to one another. No paid performers, no separation between actor and audience, nothing sold at the door. The pleasure people take is pleasure in each other's company rather than in an illusion. It is the only form of spectacle Rousseau can endorse: participation instead of representation.
