I am drafting a chapter for an undergraduate volume on scientific realism, and its thesis runs as follows. Popper defined verisimilitude in terms of a theory's true content and its false content; Tichý, Harris, and Miller showed in 1974 that the definition is defective because on it no false theory can come out closer to the truth than another, and Popper conceded the point almost immediately. My claim is that this was a conceptual collapse rather than a merely technical one. Closeness to truth is a matter of having more true content and less false content, and since any false theory has some false content, ordering false theories by their distance from the truth cannot get off the ground in principle — so treating approximate truth as the aim of science is not a viable position, and later proposals to the contrary are just relabeling the same problem. Realists should therefore say that our best theories are true, without qualification, and drop the degrees-of-nearness business entirely.

Where does this go wrong? In particular: is the step from the failure of one definition to the impossibility of comparing false theories legitimate, and is the unqualified-truth fallback actually available to a realist who wants to answer the pessimistic induction?
