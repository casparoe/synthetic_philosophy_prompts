I cover science and ideas for a weekly magazine, which means anything with "genes" and "behavior" in the headline lands on my desk, which in turn means I've read the same deflationary paragraph about heritability roughly four hundred times. You know the one: its a population statistic, it's about variance not individuals, it tells you nothing about why your kid struggles with reading, and it *definitely* tells you nothing about why group averages differ. I mostly believe it. But I've started to suspect the paragraph overcorrects, and I want to write the piece argueing that — before I do, I need to know whether the argument can actually be defended or whether I'm about to be wrong in public.

So here's what I want an essay on: is the standard deflationary reading of heritability right, or does it overshoot? Work from a concrete case I've been chewing on (hypothetical, but shaped like something real). Country A introduces universal full-day preschool plus free school meals in 1998; Country B doesn't. Twenty-five years later, twin studies in each country put the heritability of a numeracy score at about .45 in B and about .75 in A. One of my sources, a quantitative geneticist, says that's exactly what you should expect: equalizing environments shrinks the environmental variance, so the genetic share of what's left goes up, and the higher number in A is a kind of compliment to the policy. Another source, an education researcher, says the comparison is a category error — the statistic is so hostage to local environmental variance that the two numbers can't be lined up at all, and neither one licenses any claim about individual kids or about A-versus-B differences. They can't both be right about how much work the number can do. Weigh them and land somewhere. I don't want "it's complicated," I want a verdict about which inferences survive and which don't, including whether the deflationary line itself sometimes smuggles in a claim it hasn't earned.

While you're in there, hit the stuff that usually gets skipped in the paragraph I keep reading: why the figure that falls out of a twin or ACE model tends to come in higher than the one you get from SNP-based methods on unrelated people, and what that gap does or doesn't imply; whether the variance-versus-causation gloss is fair, or a slogan that hides the fact that partitioning variance is itself a causal-ish claim with assumptions bolted to it; and whether high within-population heritability really is silent on between-population differences, or just a lot less informative than both sides pretend. No reading list and no citations, please — I'm on deadline and my editor cuts any paragraph that smells like a lit review. Just the reasoning, in prose I could steal sentences from, and tell me plainly where you think I'm wrong if you think I am.
