I am in the second year of a doctoral project that sits uncomfortably between two supervisors. One is a metaphysician who wants a non-trivial answer to the special composition question — when, if ever, do some things compose a further thing? The other works on knowledge representation and wants a criterion that could actually be applied to a complete description of a configuration of entities (their properties, their spatial relations, their causal interactions) in order to decide whether to posit one further object in an ontology that a robot uses to segment scenes for manipulation. Both are telling me to stop stalling and produce the criterion. I would rather produce the specification first: the inventory of things any candidate criterion must do before it is worth arguing over. My office-mate thinks a list like this is just a covert way of prejudging the answer in one's own favour, which is part of why I want it written down explicitly. To be clear about what I am not asking for: no survey of the existing answers, no reading list, no citations. I want the requirements, with the case or consideration that forces each one onto the list.

So what must an adequate answer to the special composition question deliver? Please lay out the classes of case it must adjudicate — things widely scattered across a region; things merely touching; things fastened, glued or bonded; the ladder of cases in which one moves by imperceptible steps from far-apart particles to a firmly unified artefact; swarms, queues, teams and other organised pluralities; a statue and the clay it is made of; the pressure from mentality, that is, the worry that if a person is composed of parts there are too many candidates for the thinker; and whether composites can survive the replacement or rearrangement of their parts. Then the distinctions it must not collapse: composition versus identity, the existence of a whole versus the obtaining of an arrangement among some things, synchronic versus diachronic composition, vagueness in our words versus vagueness in the world, and a genuinely informative restricted answer versus a restatement of the question in different vocabulary. Then whatever background results or commitments it must remain consistent with. Alongside your own list, please audit these eight constraints I have already written down, and say for each whether it is genuine, redundant with another, covertly question-begging, or the partisan property of one camp: (i) stability, in that the verdict must not flip under arbitrarily small perturbations of the arrangement; (ii) supervenience, in that nothing beyond the local qualitative and spatiotempotent distribution may enter the criterion; (iii) ideological parsimony, in that the criterion must be statable in an idiom a mereological nihilist could accept, deploying no notion of a whole, an object, or a unity in the analysans; (iv) causal non-redundancy, in that if the parts collectively already account for every effect, the criterion must not add an object doing extra causal work, unless it shows that object to be identical with them; (v) mechanical checkability, in that a competent judge given the complete description should be able to apply the criterion without appealing to what the configuration is for, or to anyone's interests or purposes; (vi) conservatism, in that it must not entail that ordinary belief about tables, chairs and organisms was systematically false, or must otherwise come with a worked-out error theory; (vii) harmony with the standard mereological principles, or else an explicit statement of which it rejects; (viii) explanatory fertility, in that it must say why composition, when it occurs, makes any difference to how things behave.

Three things matter to me more than the inventory. First, separate the genuinely non-negotiable requirements from the ones that are really the commitments of a particular camp — nihilist, universalist, organicist, contact-and-fastening theorist, four-dimensionalist permissivist — since I suspect that (v) is smuggled in from my engineering supervisor and (vi) from my metaphysics supervisor, and that they cannot both be honoured. Second, tell me whether any subset is jointly unsatisfiable. In particular I have been assuming that a criterion should be non-vague, should satisfy (i) and (iii), and should be restricted rather than trivial, and I cannot see how those four survive together; if the list is itself the interesting result, say so plainly, and say which requirement has to give and why. Third, identify the single desideratum that does the most work in excluding candidates. A closing paragraph of at most a few lines on how the main families of answer fare against the trimmed list is welcome, but keep the weight on the criteria rather than the scorecard. Please write throughout in British English.
