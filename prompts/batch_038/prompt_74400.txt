For the past year I have been in a weekly reading group on the semantics of indicative conditionals, and the group has settled into a stalemate that puzzles me, because everyone in it is well informed and nobody is being obviously stubborn. The occasion is Lewis's triviality results. Everyone grants the mathematics. If a conditional of the form "if A, C" expresses a proposition, and if the probability of that proposition is to equal the conditional probability of C given A — the Equation — not merely for one probability function but for every function obtainable from it by conditionalizing on further evidence, then the proposition is trivial: no non-degenerate space can satisfy the constraint. Everyone also grants the neighbouring collapse result associated with Gibbard, namely that a conditional operator which satisfies import–export, is supraclassical, and is stronger than the material conditional must collapse into the material conditional. And yet, given all of that shared mathematics, the group divides into camps that have not shifted in twelve months.

One camp holds that these results are devastating in a precise sense: no proposition expressible in the same language and living in the same probability space can play the role the Equation assigns to it, so indicative conditionals must be non-propositional — suppositional states rather than claims with truth conditions — or else their content must shift so violently with context that "what the conditional says" is not a stable thing at all. The other camp holds that the results are cheap, in the sense that they are hostage to idealizations nobody need accept: that the conditional is an event in the original algebra; that the Equation must hold for every conditionalization of the original function rather than only for the functions an actual agent with a fixed evidential context can be in; that probabilities are two-valued, classical, and real-valued rather than three-valued, void for a false antecedent, partial, or imprecise. This camp points out that once one of these goes — a conditional-event algebra, a trivalent treatment on which a false-antecedent conditional lacks a truth value altogether, coherent conditional previsions in place of unconditional measures, or an admissible class of probability functions restricted by context — non-trivial models of the Equation exist, and which relaxation is legitimate looks, from where they sit, like an entirely open question. A third, quieter position in the group says both camps are attacking a straw man: the Equation was a thesis about how rational agents should apportion credence, or about when a conditional is properly assertable, never a thesis about truth conditions, so the proofs do not address it.

My working hypothesis, which I would like you to test rather than endorse, is that this is not a disagreement about the formal results at all, and not quite a verbal one either: it is a disagreement about which idealizations are load-bearing and which are incidental, so that what presents itself as a theorem-driven debate is really a debate about the costs of idealization — and therefore one that no further theorem can terminate. Is that right? I want you to say what kind of disagreement this actually is, sorting out the parts that are verbal (about "the probability of a conditional," about what a semantics is required to deliver), the parts that are genuine formal questions nobody has yet settled, the parts that are two different questions travelling under a single name — one about propositions and measures, one about the norms of conditional reasoning — and the parts that are bedrock methodological commitments neither side can argue for. Ground the diagnosis in what each side actually claims rather than in a survey of theories of conditionals, which I have read; a description of the positions is not a diagnosis of them. Then tell me what would count as progress given your diagnosis: a specific formal result someone could in principle prove and which side it would favour, an empirical finding about how people assign probabilities to conditionals, a distinction that would let both camps keep what they care about, or a reason to think no progress should be expected because the question is ill-posed as stated. Do not give me a bibliography or a reading list — I am working offline and want the structure of the dispute, not references. Finally, please be explicit about the fragility of your own diagnosis: which argument or consideration, if I brought it to you, would most likely make you revise the verdict, and at which step your reasoning is most likely to fail — in particular, whether you have simply imported one camp's standards of success and dressed the resulting verdict up as a neutral diagnosis.
