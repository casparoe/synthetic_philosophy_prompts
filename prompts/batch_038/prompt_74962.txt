So my book club fell apart around month four but I kept going with the book anyway — Hofstadter's Le Ton beau de Marot, where he gets dozens of different people to translate the same tiny French poem, Marot's little get-well note to a sick young lady. I've been making my own English versions, trying to hold onto what he keeps insisting matters: the very short lines, eight syllables, grouped in threes and rhymed, plus the fact that the poem is a flirtatious social move made by a particular man to a particular girl on a particular afternoon. After about fifteen attempts I noticed I have a pile of gut verdicts about which of my versions are still translations of the poem and which are something else entirely, and they don't obviously hang together. Here they are.

1. A version that nails the line length, the tercets and the rhymes but quietly swaps the speaker calling himself her servant for calling himself her scribe — fine, that's still the poem.
2. A version that renders the sense of every line accurately in unrhymed four-beat lines — not the poem. It's a summary in a costume. Nothing factual is lost and I still say no.
3. A version that abandons verse altogether and turns the thing into a text-message thread — her: ugh, still in bed / him: get up, the whole court is asking after you — and I think this one basically works. It's the poem in a different genre. This is the one that wrecks my system, since it and #2 both throw away the verse form and I only accept one of them.
4. A version that hits everything but adds one extra line to a single tercet so a rhyme lands — not fine, even though it's a tiny local violation and the stanza reads better.
5. A version recast as a villanelle — a stricter form, but a different one. I call that a companion poem, not a translation.
6. A formally perfect version that changes the addressee from a young woman to a young man, because the rhymes only came out that way. I flip flop on this one constantly.
7. A formally perfect version where every single rhyme is bought with archaic inversion — quoth she, unto me, did part. I think this is worse than #8.
8. A version that misses exactly one rhyme in the whole poem and is otherwise ordinary contemporary English. Better than #7, which surprises me.
9. A literal line-by-line gloss with three pages of notes explaining precisely what couldn't be carried over. I learned more from this than from anything else I made and I still refuse to call it a translation.
10. A version a friend generated with a script that searched a huge space of wordings and found one satisfying every constraint I could write down, but the syntax is technically legal and no human would ever have written it, and the images are correct and dead. My gut says both yes (it did the thing) and no (nobody meant anything by it). Been going back and forth on this for weeks.

Is there a principle, or a short list of candidate principles, that delivers these verdicts? State it precisely and tag each clause with the case that forces it, because I suspect form is doing at least three seperate jobs in my mouth — prosody, genre and register, and the occasion (who's speaking to whom, on what afternoon) — and I want to know whether those need seperate clauses or whether they collapse into one. Then tell me which verdicts a simpler principle would overturn: if there's a clean account that gets nine of the ten, say which one I should give up and why I'm wrong about it. And give me the principle's verdicts on cases I haven't thought of, specifically — a version in a language whose natural short line is much longer than eight syllables, so the syllable count can't be carried over at all: is there a functional equivalent that counts, or does the poem just die there? A version set to a tune, where the melody picks up the load the rhyme used to carry. And a formally perfect, faithful version whose translator admits they never understood the poem — does the translator's comprehension belong in the principle at all? Last thing: Hofstadter's own position, as far as I can reconstruct it, is roughly that the form just is the poem. Do my verdicts commit me to that? #2 seems to require it and #3 seems to contradict it, so either I'm misreading him or my intuitions are inconsistent, and I'd like to know which.
