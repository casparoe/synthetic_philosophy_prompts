In three weeks I present a paper at a departmental colloquium on naturalism in ethics. My thesis is that Quinean holism, pushed consistently into the moral domain, yields neither skepticism nor expressivism but a position I call pragmatic web-revisionism. Moral principles are posits in the same fabric as statements about middling-sized physical objects; they are answerable to experience only through the whole system; and where two moral schemes compete, the choice between them is made by the same pragmatic maxims that govern theory choice in science — conservatism, simplicity, fecundity, empirical adequacy. Two commitments carry the argument. First, rivalry between moral schemes is objectively resolvable wherever the schemes entail different consequences for the empirical phenomena they were posited to systematize, such as the durability of cooperation, the incidence of suffering, or the stability of institutions, so a large share of apparent cross-cultural deadlock is empirical disagreement wearing evaluative clothing. Second, where the schemes really are empirically equivalent, choosing by pragmatic maxim is not a confession of subjectivity but exactly as truth-apt as choosing a physical theory, because the collapse of the analytic-synthetic distinction leaves no privileged class of questions on which truth outruns warranted assertibility.

The room will be hostile from several directions at once, and it is not a normal metaethics audience. There is a core of expressivists and quasi-realists. There are two naturalists who want moral properties doing explanatory work of the Harman–Sturgeon–Boyd kind and who think my account surrenders the only thing that made their realism worth defending. There is one non-naturalist realist of the relaxed stripe, who will insist that pragmatic success cannot be made to track moral truth. There are two philosophers of science who know Quine's corpus closely and will police the exegesis: whether I have conflated underdetermination of theory by all possible evidence with indeterminacy of translation, whether I am entitled to the pragmatic maxims as anything more than first philosophy in disguise, and whether Quine's own scattered remarks about value sentences are anywhere near as friendly to me as I assume. There is also a moral psychologist who doubts that anything in ethics issues predictions in the ordinary sense.

Prepare my question-period sheet. Give me eight to ten objections, ordered by the product of how likely this particular room is to raise them and how much damage they would do, each phrased in the idiom and cadence of the subgroup that will actually raise it rather than in a generic skeptic's voice. For each: a reply I can deliver in about two minutes, an explicit statement of where that reply is soft, what I should concede early to buy credibility, and what I cannot concede without the paper collapsing. Separate the exegetical objections from the substantive ones and tell me how much airtime each class deserves. Identify the single exchange that, if I fumble it, will define how the room remembers the talk. Close with the objection I am hoping nobody raises, and my best available answer to it. I know the literature, so no reading list, references, or suggested further work — I want the shape of the exchanges, not the sources.
