At the end of next month I am delivering a forty-five-minute seminar paper, with about twenty minutes for discussion, at a linguistics department whose pragmatics group includes three formal semanticists who work on scalar implicature within a broadly derivational framework, one general-pragmatics theorist in the post-Gricean tradition of quantity and informativeness scales, one avowed relevance theorist, and two psycholinguists who run reading-time and eye-tracking experiments on underinformative utterances. The paper is called "Implicature Without Content." I would like a preparation sheet for the question period, built to the specification below.

**The thesis I am defending.** Implicatures are not propositions. On the standard picture, a hearer who encounters an utterance such as *some of the applicants were shortlisted* computes, by reasoning about the speaker's observance of a quantity norm, the additional content *not all of the applicants were shortlisted*, and adds that content to what was said. I deny that anything of the sort is computed or added. What the hearer acquires is not a piece of content but a normative orientation toward the speaker: she is now to be treated as having committed herself not to be relying on the stronger claim, as answerable if it in fact turns out that all of them were, and as having forfeited the standing to later insist that she left the stronger possibility open. The implicature is a change in the speaker's standing, not an addition to what she conveyed.

**The work the thesis is meant to do.** Explicit cancellation stops being a puzzle: retracting a proposition conflicts with having asserted it, whereas withdrawing a normative status is a perfectly coherent move, which is why the *in fact, possibly all of them were* continuation is not a contradiction but merely a retraction. The symmetry problem disappears: the derivationalist needs a principled filter to stop the hearer from equally deriving *possibly not all* alongside *not all* from a bare *some*, whereas I generate no candidate contents to be filtered at all. On embedding, my position is more concessive — I hold that implicature-like effects do not compose inside the scope of negation, conditionals, or attitude verbs in the way content-based accounts require, and I treat the intuitive pull of the embedding data as an artifact of hearers adjusting what is said rather than of implicatures entering semantic composition. In the closing section I commit myself to one empirical consequence: the effects observed on underinformative utterances should track the hearer's assessment of whether the speaker can be relied upon, and should therefore vary with features of the social setting, such as power asymmetry between interlocutors and the hearer's prior experience of the speaker's scrupulousness, in ways that do not vary with the logical strength of the unexpressed alternative.

**What I want from you.** Draft the eight to ten objections this particular room will actually raise, as distinct from the ones a general philosophy-of-language audience would raise, and order them by the damage they would do to the paper if they landed. Phrase each in the register and vocabulary of the subfield it comes from — the way a formal semanticist, a relevance theorist, or a psycholinguist who lives in reading-time data would put it, including the technical terms each of them would naturally reach for. I expect at least: the compositionality objection from the semanticists, pressing on how a stance rather than a content could interact with semantic interpretation at all; the notational-variant objection, namely that normative standing is only a relabeled proposition and that I have bought nothing with the relabeling; the relevance theorist's charge that I cannot explain indirect rebuttal, irony, or the hearer's manifest surprise without smuggling the stronger content back in; and the psycholinguists' demand to know what measurable signature distinguishes my account from a content-based one, given that the additional-cost results on underinformative utterances are usually taken to favour local derivation.
