While traveling I picked up a popular science book about complexity, and one chapter argues that chaos theory forced a revision of the clockwork picture of the universe. The stretch that interested me reads as follows:

> In 1961 the meteorologist Edward Lorenz restarted a weather simulation from a number he had copied off a printout: 0.506 instead of the 0.506127 the machine had actually been carrying. He expected a nearly identical run. Instead the simulated atmosphere diverged completely from the earlier one. What Lorenz had stumbled on is that some perfectly deterministic systems amplify tiny differences in their starting conditions exponentially, doubling them again and again at a fixed rate until the perturbation is as large as the system itself. That is the butterfly effect. Nothing here involves chance: a chaotic system obeys exact laws, and given its state to perfect precision its future is fixed. The practical upshot is familiar — detailed weather forecasts are worthless beyond a fortnight or so. But the barrier is technological rather than fundamental: make your measurements of the current state a hundred times more accurate and you can forecast a hundred times further ahead. Poincaré had already exposed the root of the trouble at the end of the nineteenth century, when he proved that the three-body problem has no solution at all, no formula by which the motion of three gravitating bodies can be written down, which is why planetary positions can only ever be approximated. And the philosophical payoff is larger than meteorology. Chaos showed that a universe can be law-governed and still leave its future unsettled, because what the laws fix is never what anyone could ever get at. Determinism therefore lost the hold on the future that Laplace gave it, and the clockwork universe died in the twentieth century rather than the eighteenth.

I found this convincing at the time, and I came away with two beliefs: that deterministic and predictable-in-practice name a single property, and that chaos theory showed the future is not fixed. One thing makes me specifically suspicious of the claim about better instruments. I ran a double pendulum and measured how long a perturbed run tracked the unperturbed one before exceeding a fixed angular tolerance, for perturbations of 1e-6, 1e-9, 1e-12 and 1e-15 radians. Each thousandfold reduction in the perturbation bought me roughly two extra seconds of agreement — not a thousand times more. Separately, when I reordered the additions inside the integration loop, changing nothing mathematically, the run diverged on the same schedule.
