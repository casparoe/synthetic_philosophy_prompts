My father has intractable cancer pain and is being evaluated for an anterior cingulotomy. The surgeon's description unsettled me: afterwards, he said, the pain will still be there, it simply won't bother him. My father's answer was that he would rather go on being bothered than stop being the sort of person who minds. Waiting in clinics, I re-read *One Flew Over the Cuckoo's Nest*, and its ending — McMurphy brought back from the lobotomy and smothered by Chief Bromden — now strikes me as the same problem in a much darker key.

Please give me a serious reading of Kesey's novel, and of Forman's film where the two diverge, through the philosophy of pain and pain asymbolia: the dissociation cases Nikola Grahek built his account around, and the live disagreement over whether asymbolics feel pain that is no longer unpleasant, or feel unpleasant pain but have lost the desire or the bodily care to be rid of it. Work through three things. What does Chief's mercy killing presuppose about the badness of suffering, given that he never receives a report from the person it is meant to benefit? What of McMurphy's own pre-lobotomy relation to punishment, which repeatedly looks like harm absorbed without the aversion one would expect? And is Chief's feigned deaf-muteness, and the fog he attributes to his medication, a willed version of the same dissociation? Where the asymbolia frame genuinely illuminates the novel, say so; where it distorts — Kesey gives us an empty shell, not a feeler who doesn't mind — say that plainly. End that discussion by naming one thing the novel contributes that this literature structurally cannot, since its whole evidential base consists of patients who can still testify.

Work from the plot and scenes you know; please don't quote the text and don't give me citations or a reading list. Close with a single line of the form "Verdict: X", where X is one of *unpleasantness*, *caring*, *neither*, or *indeterminate*, naming what on your reading Chief takes to have been destroyed in McMurphy.
