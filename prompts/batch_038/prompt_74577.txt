I have spent eleven years reading LessWrong, the sequences, and the older rationalist forums, and my reflexes are the usual ones: steelman first, taboo the disputed term, then ask whether the disagreement survives. My day job, though, is payments architecture. I design clearing and settlement systems, and for the last three years I have been working on tokenized deposits and a stablecoin pilot, so my working vocabulary is the vocabulary of money as an engineered object: unit of account versus medium of exchange versus store of value, settlement finality, the double coincidence of wants, search and verification costs, the stamp that spares you the weighing, seigniorage, velocity, collateral haircuts, endogenous credit creation, reflexive peg dynamics, and the chartalist claim that a currency's demand is anchored by the state's willingness to accept it for taxes. I am also comfortable with the random-matching models in which money emerges spontaneously as a transaction-cost-reducing technology, and I have a strong prior — which I would like you to attack — that this apparatus is enough to hold essentially everything interesting that was said about money before 1700.

Our reading group, which is mostly ex-rationalists and one very patient classicist, spent the last two sessions on Aristotle's treatment of money: the discussion of reciprocal justice and exchange in Book V of the Nicomachean Ethics, and the account of household management and wealth-getting in Book I of the Politics. It went badly. Half the table concluded that Aristotle already had fiat money, and the other half concluded that he was confused, and the classicist accused everyone of anachronism without saying precisely which moves were anachronistic. So here is what I want from you. Take Aristotle's substantive monetary theses and restate them, one at a time, in the vocabulary I actually use, as faithfully as you can manage without a philology degree: the thesis that money exists by convention rather than by nature and is worth nothing in relation to the end it serves, so that its acceptance can be withdrawn and it can be rendered useless; the genealogy in which necessities are bartered, then intrinsically useful and transportable commodities are adopted, then a stamp is introduced to spare the measuring; the thesis that money functions as the single measure that makes heterogeneous goods commensurate, with need or demand as the underlying thing that holds the community together and money standing in for it by agreement; the distinction between using a thing and exchanging it, with the shoe as his example; the claim that household acquisition has a natural limit because its instruments serve a bounded end, whereas retail trade and money accumulation have no limit because their object is money itself; and the condemnation of interest as the most unnatural form of acquisition, on the grounds that money was invented for exchange and not for reproducing itself. For each, tell me what maps cleanly onto my categories, what maps only if you bend something, and what has no expression at all in the language of payments engineering and monetary theory.

Then give me the residue report properly, because that is the part I actually care about. Rank the untranslatable leftovers by how much they matter, and for each one say which diagnosis you favor: that my vocabulary is genuinely missing a piece of conceptual machinery — for instance, no way to register that a technique of acquisition could be a corruption of the purpose of the instrument used, as opposed to merely inefficient or legally prohibited — or that Aristotle's claim was resting on a teleology we now regard as unfounded, so that what looks like a disagreement is a difference in decoration. Be decisive rather than even-handed; I would rather have a wrong ranking than a diplomatic one. I want you to be scrupulous about not smuggling: whenever you reach for the three-function taxonomy, or for the assumption that money is a technology adopted by rational agents to cut transaction costs, or for the assumption that the relevant question is what a monetary instrument does rather than what it is for, flag it as an argumentative move that would beg the question if presented as a neutral translation. Finally, settle a side bet: some of my colleagues insist Aristotle is a proto-metallist because of his commodity-origin story, while others insist he is a proto-conventionalist because money's value rests on agreement and can be revoked by the users; tell me which the translation supports, or whether that dispute simply fails to be expressible in my framework. Two housekeeping notes. I cannot look anything up, so please do not give me a reading list, secondary citations, or Bekker-number apparatus — if a claim depends on a contested reading of the Greek, say so in one sentence and move on. And please write the whole answer in American English.
