I'm in the fourth year of a PhD in history and philosophy of science, and this term I'm co-teaching a seminar that puts Victorian and Edwardian literature up against the philosophy of biology. My week is the units-of-selection debate and I've decided to run H.G. Wells's The Time Machine through it, partly because the students will mostly have read it or seen one of the films, and partly because I have a a pet reading of it that I want pressure-tested before I stand up in front of a room and assert it out loud.

The pet reading. Whenever the book gets mentioned in this context it's treated as a group-selection story: two social classes, breeding separately for hundreds of thousands of years, diverging into the Eloi and the Morlocks, with the Morlock side coming out on top as the differential survival of groups. I want to say that's either wrong or at any rate the least interesting thing available in the novel. On my reading what Wells is actually staging is the *loss* of a unit rather than the emergence of one. The Eloi aren't the product of selection acting at a higher level; they're what's left once nothing much is selecting at any level — no predators, no scarcity, no competition for mates, no infant mortality worth mentioning, hence nothing about them that could be an adaptation to anything. The Morlocks are the lineage that stayed under selection, and the real bite of the book is that the only population still being shaped by anything is the one that ended up farming people. So the two species differ not in the level at which selection acted on them but in whether it acted at all.

What I'd like from you:

1. Tell me whether that survives contact with the distinctions people actually draw. I've been teaching the selection-of versus selection-for split, the MLS1/MLS2 way of separating group-structured populations from genuine group-level reproduction, trait-group models, and the replicator/interactor vocabulary, and I can't tell which of those my reading is leaning on or whether it quietly needs more than one of them at once. If my claim is best reconstructed as a statement about drift and relaxed selection rather than about levels, say so plainly.

2. Does the Morlock–Eloi relationship have any of the structural features that are supposed to mark a real higher-level unit — division of labour, reproductive specialisation, some kind of policing of within-group cheats? The Morlocks keep the Eloi fed and clothed and then eat them, which is a division of labour with the sign flipped. Is there any non-silly sense in which the pair is a candidate superorganism, or does calling it one just sound edgy?

3. Where does the novel resist the reading, in both directions? The Time Traveller is doing a retrospective inference from a snapshot of a present arrangement to a historical process, and I worry that makes his account the same sort of target that people attack as unfalsifiable storytelling about the past — which would mean the book is partly a satire on my own discipline. And conversely: is there anything Wells is doing with degeneration, class, and entropy that the units framework simply can't hold?

4. Two or three specific episodes I should point the class at, described rather than quoted, and what you'd have them argue about in each.

5. A straight answer on whether I'm doing violence to the novel, to the biology, or to neither.

Constraints: British English throughout, and a hard cap of 900 words — I'd rather you be selective than comprehensive. Lead with your bottom line on my central claim in two or three sentences before you work through the rest, since I want to know where I stand before I read the reasoning. Don't quote Wells and don't give me a bibliography, a reading list, or inline citations; I have the literature and it's the argument I'm after. Where a technical term is doing work, gloss it in a clause — some of these students are literature people who have never seen a fitness concept in their lives.
