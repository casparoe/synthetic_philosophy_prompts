I'm presenting a paper at an economics department seminar. The room is mostly empirical asset-pricing people and two market-microstructure theorists, plus one historian of economic thought who works on Hayek; no philosophers. Please draft my question-period prep sheet.

The thesis is that the Milgrom–Stokey no-trade theorem is standardly read as a curiosity about idealized rationality, but read correctly it is an impossibility result about market prices as aggregators of dispersed knowledge. My argument runs:

1. If a price fully summarized what traders privately know, that information would thereby be common knowledge, and the theorem's hypotheses would obtain.
2. Under those hypotheses nobody profits from trading on information, so the existence of sustained speculative volume is itself proof that prices are not sufficient statistics.
3. The usual repair — the Grossman–Stiglitz equilibrium, in which noise keeps prices only partly informative and so restores an incentive to acquire information — treats noise as a friction that caps informativeness. I argue the reverse: uninformed participation is a precondition of aggregation, because only traders who are not acting on what they know leave information for price to carry.
4. So the quantity that matters is not the amount of noise but what I call the epistemic discount: the share of what a price reflects that is there only because some class of participants trades for non-epistemic reasons (hedging, liquidity, index mandates, tax, regulation). A market's capacity to produce knowledge is a function of who is blind, not of who is informed.

Give me the eight or so objections this audience will actually raise, ordered by how much damage each would do to the argument, and phrased the way a microstructure theorist and an empirical researcher would respectively phrase them — they will not use my vocabulary. For each objection, supply a reply I can deliver in about two minutes, state which premise I should concede rather than defend, and note where the reply is softest. Then flag the single reply that has to land for the thesis to survive the hour, and tell me the question I should be hoping nobody asks.
