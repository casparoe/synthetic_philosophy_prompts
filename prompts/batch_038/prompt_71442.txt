Me and a mate spent about four hours on this last night over far too much wine and we both ended up shouting, so I want a third party who isn't invested in being right.

The episode: the 2016 UK referendum on EU membership. Not the campaign, not the economics — the franchise, i.e. the fact that a statute had to be passed deciding who counted as part of the demos for that particular decision, and how arbitrary-looking the line turned out to be. As I understand it (please tell me where I've got it wrong before you build on it): the vote was run under the European Union Referendum Act 2015, in the UK plus Gibraltar; Irish citizens resident in the UK could vote, and so could qualifying Commonwealth citizens; members of the House of Lords could vote even though they can't vote in general elections; UK citizens living abroad could vote only if they'd been on a UK register within the previous fifteen years; roughly three million EU nationals living in the UK — people who'd worked, paid tax and raised kids there for decades — were excluded entirely; 16- and 17-year-olds were excluded after the Lords tried to include them and the Commons knocked it back; prisoners were excluded; citizens of the Crown Dependencies were excluded even though the UK ran their defence and foreign policy; and the whole thing was advisory in law, with the government merely promising to implement whatever came out. Leave won 51.9 to 48.1.

So: run that through the boundary problem. Concretely, I want (1) a brainstorming pass first, nothing polished — list the candidate accounts of who should be in the demos (all-affected-interests, all-subjected-to-coercion, all-subjected-to-this-decision, some membership/participation account, plus whatever else you think belongs in the set) and just score each franchise rule against each account in a rough grid; then (2) the real analysis: which account best fits the rules that were actually chosen, and whether any of them — the Irish/Commonwealth inclusion, the fifteen-year cliff edge for expats, peers getting a vote, Gibraltar in but the Isle of Man out — are explicable by principle at all or are just sediment from the UK's existing electoral law; then (3) where the theories go silent or contradict themselves, without forcing them to say something they don't.

Two things I especially want pressed on. First, my friend keeps insisting the boundary problem is really an infinite regress rather than a circularity: the demos that draws the boundary is itself a demos drawn by an earlier boundary, all the way down, and he calls this "the bootstrap objection." Is he right that it's a regress, or is the standard complaint genuinely circular — a democratic procedure can't democratically legitimate its own scope — and if the two are different, does it matter which one you're facing here? Second, the aftermath: the rights of those excluded EU residents ended up being settled years later at a negotiating table between the UK government and the other 27 member states, which looks to me like the question of who decides got reopened and moved to a second, smaller demos after the vote. Does that count as a second-order boundary problem, and does it confirm Whelan-style pessimism that democracy can't fix its own edges, or does it show the problem is practically inert — a sovereign parliament just stipulates a line, the vote happens, and legitimacy gets manufactured retrospectively?
