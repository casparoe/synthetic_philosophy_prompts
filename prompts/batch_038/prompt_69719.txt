I'm on the methods committee of a health technology assessment agency. We write the reference case that tells evaluators how to score funding proposals; the scores feed a ranking and the ranking decides what actually gets built, so the scoring rule isnt a technicality.

Here's the fight. Outcomes are uncertain and they land differently on different people. Two candidate rules are on the table for the new reference case.

Rule A: score a proposal as a weighted sum of the individuals' expected utilities. The weights are fixed numbers, chosen up front and published, and they don't change depending on how the proposal turns out. They don't have to be equal — we've been toying with tilting them toward people who start out worse off in baseline health.

Rule B: for each possible outcome, rank people by how well that outcome goes for them, apply a weighting function that leans harder on the bottom of that ranking, and only then take the expectation across outcomes. So how much weight you get depends on where you land in the distribution in that particular scenario.

In the pilot round the two disagreed on a screening program that gives a large population a modest benefit in the likely case and rescues a small population from something catastrophic in an unlikely one. Rule B liked it a lot. Rule A didn't. That's the sort of case we'd be signing off on permanently.

Three members, three views. One with an econ background says Rule B is flatly inadmissable, because Harsanyi's aggregation theorem proves the social evaluation has to be a weighted sum of individual expected utilities and Rule B isn't one. A second says Rule B is obviously just Rule A with different weights, so the theorem doesn't touch it and the whole argument is a non-issue. A third says the theorem is question-begging — its Pareto assumption already builds in that unanimity is what matters, so it can't tell a body like ours what to do — and anyway we have never claimed to know how to compare wellbeing across people, which the sum in Rule A seems to need.

Draft the memo I'll circulate before the meeting. It has to come down on one rule and give the committee something to vote on. Specifically: say which of the three members is right about what, whether Rule B is or isn't a weighted sum in disguise, and — this is the part I care most about — whether the assumption the theorem leans on is one we'd genuinely be giving something up by dropping, or one we were entitled to reject from the start. I also suspect the ex ante / ex post business is where the real disagreement is hiding and I can't articulate it properly, so please get that out in the open and say which side of it our agency would be taking. And treat the strongest case against your recommendation honestly, the one that would make me nervous if a minister's office read the memo and then read the dissent.

No references or bibliography, I don't need them, just the reasoning laid out so a smart non-specialist on the committee can check it. Two pages is plenty.
