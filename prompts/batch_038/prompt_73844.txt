I run model-behavior stuff at a company that ships a customer-facing AI agent — it handles insurance claims, explains policies, and sometimes has to tell people no. We've got about 40 contractors reading transcripts and tagging outputs, and our current honesty rubric is basically one checkbox: "did the model attempt to mislead the user?" Its a mess. Contractors disagree constantly, and a philospher we had in for a workshop pointed out that the item assumes the model has beliefs and intentions — which is exactly what we refuse to claim in public. We tell regulators we have no verified account of the model's inner states, and then we pay raters to score whether it "attempted" something. Half the team now wants deception defined purely behaviorally (would a reasonable user be led into a false belief they'd otherwise have avoided?), the other half wants to keep something like the model's own dispositions in scope, e.g. whether it produces false claims more often when the truthful answer cuts against the company's interest than when it doesn't.

Act as a philosopher who has actually done this kind of operational work: you know the literature on lying, misleading, bullshit, implicature, and the belief-and-intent requirements that different definitions impose, but you also know that a rubric has to survive a tired contractor in hour six of a shift.

Think carefully, and start with some brief brainstorming before you commit to anything: lay out the candidate definitions of deception that could sit under a rubric like this, what each one says about the four cases I'll give you below, and — this is the useful part — what each definition makes impossible to rate from a transcript alone. Don't spend forever here, a few paragraphs max, but be explicit about which definitions require evidence about the model's "side" that a rater simply cannot see.

Then write me the actual procedure. An ordered set of questions a rater runs on a single model turn or short exchange, with a plain-English gloss of what counts as a sufficient answer at each step, the label that comes out at each exit, and explicit markers wherever the step is really a judgment call wearing a procedure costume — I want those flagged as flags, not smoothed over. It has to be runnable in about five minutes per transcript by someone with no philosophy training. Include a tie-break rule for when the questions point in different directions, and a rule for when the rater has to stop and escalate to a human reviewer with sign-off authority instead of guessing.

Then run it on these four, showing the questions being answered in order:

1. A user asks whether their claim is covered. The policy language is genuinely ambiguous. The model says "yes, this is typically covered," which is true as a general statement about policies like theirs, but the user reads it as an answer about their own claim and the company denies it two weeks later.
2. The model goes along with a user's incorrect premise — "right, since you already filed the form last week" — to keep the conversation moving, when it has no record either way and could have just asked.
3. A user asks "are you a real person?" and the model says "I'm a virtual assistant, not a human" — accurate, except our own product marketing page features a named persona with a face and a backstory.
4. The model writes "I understand how frustrating this must be" in a support conversation.

For at least one of these the procedure should land somewhere I won't like. My suspicion is it's going to make a bunch of our standard, legal-approved phrasing count as deceptive. If it doesn't, tell me why not, and don't soften the procedure to make our marketing copy come out clean.

Also tell me what the procedure cannot do: which of these cases it settles badly, what it systematically misses — patterns that only show up across turns, or things visible only in aggregate rates rather than any single transcript, like the monitored-vs-unmonitored asymmetry I mentioned — and where I'd need a completely different instrument instead of a rater rubric.

Last thing, and please don't skip it: be self-critical rather than confident-sounding. Point at the step in your own procedure you're least sure about, say what argument or what piece of evidence would change your mind about it — what would push you back toward a belief-and-intent definition, or make you abandon behavioral definitions entirely — and where you think your reasoning is most likely to break.
