I'm a second-year political theory PhD student and I've hit a wall on a chapter, so I'm outsourcing some of the thinking to you.

The thing bugging me is the architecture of the Paris Agreement. It's built entirely on pledge-and-review: every country picks its own target — its "nationally determined contribution" — and nobody else gets any say in how ambitious that target is. There are no sanctions for missing it. The legally binding parts are almost all procedural: you have to put a pledge forward, revise it every five years, each revision has to be an advance on the last one and reflect your highest possible ambition, and you have to report your emissions in a common format that gets expert-reviewed. Then every five years there's a global stocktake that collectively notes, politely, that the pledges add up to nowhere near the temperature goal. And the compliance body the treaty created is designed to be facilitative and explicitly non-punitive — it can't fine anybody or expel anybody. All of this was a deliberate reversal of the Kyoto Protocol, which did impose binding quantified targets on a fixed list of rich countries with an enforcement branch behind them, and which the US Senate had pre-emptively killed because it set binding targets for wealthy states while exempting developing ones.

So: what theory of cooperation does the Paris design actually presuppose? I can half game-theory my way through it — it looks like it's betting everything on an indefinitely repeated game with a long shadow of the future, on reputation, on reciprocity, maybe on converting a prisoner's dilemma into something more like a coordination or assurance game by making everyone's plans public and comparable. But I'm not sure I'm extracting that from the practice rather than reading my formal apparatus onto it.

Work through, in whatever order makes the argument hang together:

- what the design *strictly* commits you to versus what it merely sits comfortably with. Does pledge-and-review require that states are the right unit of analysis, or could the identical transparency machinery be run on cities, firms, and supply chains without changing the underlying assumptions?
- which rival accounts of why states cooperate it rules out, and which it doesn't. Deterrence-by-sanction is obviously gone. But what about the line that without a sovereign no promise actually binds? What about the view that cooperation is sustained by tit-for-tat rather than by anything resembling promise-keeping?
- the consistency question I actually care about. The whole edifice runs on countries honoring pledges they made at zero cost, with no penalty for breaking them. Is the engine here strategic (reputation, reciprocity, being frozen out of future deals) or normative (a pledge creates an obligation and states feel bound by it)? Those look like different motors and I can't tell whether the treaty is choosing one, or is just agnostic and hoping both show up. If it's leaning on promise-keeping as a motive, that's a big concession that the payoff matrix isn't the whole story — and I want to know whether it can avoid leaning on it.
- the transparency framework specifically. Publishing everyone's numbers only bites if being seen to underperform is costly, and that presupposes an audience. Other states? Domestic publics? Bond markets? Does the practice quietly assume those audiences want the same thing, which feels like it's doing an enormous amount of work for free.
- and what you'd have to rebuild if the implicit theory were swapped out. If the real view is that this is a stag hunt rather than a PD, or that the binding force is normative rather than strategic, which pieces of the architecture follow and which become pointless?
