I have no academic training in philosophy — no degree, no coursework — but I have been working on one question on my own for about two years now, and I have finally written down the things I actually believe. I would like you to take the role of an expert moral philosopher who has spent a career on the ethics of harming and of failing to prevent harm, and I would like you to audit my position rather than explain the debate back to me. I am not after a survey of the literature, and please do not give me references, attributions, or a reading list; I want to know whether everything below can be true at once, and where it cannot.

My commitments:

1. There is a genuine moral difference between doing harm and allowing harm. It is not a psychological quirk to be trained out of, and killing a person is substantially worse than letting that same person die. People tell me this is squeamishness dressed up as principle. I am not persuaded.

2. In the familiar sort of case — a runaway vehicle bearing down on five, a switch that diverts it onto a siding where it will kill one instead — throwing the switch is permissible, whereas pushing a heavy stranger off an overpass into its path to stop it is not. The difference, as I see it, has nothing to do with the arithmetic. In the overpass case the man's body is what does the work; he is used. In the switch case the one is killed by something already under way. What matters to me is whether the harm is the intended means or merely something foreseen alongside.

3. If a child is drowning in water you could stand up in, and the whole cost to you is ruined shoes and being late, then walking past is monstrous — very nearly as bad as pushing that child in. I do not think this is an extreme position and I do not expect to be argued out of it.

4. When a public body could prevent deaths at trivial cost and votes not to — a guardrail that was specified and then cut for savings, a naloxone program, a plowing route past a curve with a documented record — I put the officials responsible in roughly the same moral neighborhood as someone who shot the victims. Not identical. But the same neighborhood.

5. It is flatly impermissible to kill one healthy patient for organs to save five, whatever the arithmetic says. At the same time, when I give money away, I hold that the only thing that counts is how many lives each dollar buys, and I direct it accordingly, treating every stranger's life as weighing exactly as much as every other. I feel no pull toward saying that numbers stop mattering in the second situation.

6. A case of my own, which is what started all this. I own and manage four small rental properties. For years there were break-ins in the rear parking area and one serious assault. Last spring I put a guard on site from dusk until two in the morning, with floodlighting and a marked car. Incidents at my properties have gone to essentially nothing. The crime did not vanish, though: by the account of the neighborhood group it has shifted two streets over, including onto a block with a building full of elderly tenants. I have been telling myself this is just the switch again — the harm was already in motion, I redirected it rather than initiating it, and nobody is being used as a means to anybody else's safety. Said out loud, that does not sit right with me, and I cannot tell whether my principles are objecting or whether I simply dislike having the harm land on someone else's doorstep.

Please work through this carefully. Before you write the audit, map out for each significant pair whether the conflict is logical (they cannot both be true), normative (they cannot both be correct), or only verbal (they sound inconsistent because they are answering different questions). Show me that mapping and your reasoning, since I have no training and want to be able to check the work rather than accept it. Then rank the real conflicts by severity instead of listing them all as equally bad, and for each one give me the cheapest revision that would remove it along with what that revision would cost me elsewhere — I want to know what I lose, not merely that a fix exists. If it turns out that one of my stated principles is not doing the work I think it is doing, and that my verdicts are actually explained by some different principle I have not named, say so directly; that is the failure I most suspect I am making and cannot see. Finish by telling me which single change would clean up the most, and whether any revision at all lets me keep all six.

Two ground rules. Do not assume I am willing to give any particular item up; I genuinely do not know which one I would drop, and I would rather you not guess at my preferences. And if some part of this set is stable, say that too — I would prefer a discriminating audit to one that flags everything.
