I left academic philosophy three years ago and now write behavioural specifications for a conversational system that a regional health network uses for after-hours triage. Last month a patient wrote to the network's ethics office to complain that the machine had lied to him about believing his pain was real, and the transcript he attached explained why he thought so: the system had told him that his symptoms were consistent with a kidney stone, but that it did not believe that they were. The trailing clause is not a malfunction. It is generated by a self-disclosure template that our compliance group requires us to append to clinical statements, and the concatenation of the two is something we ship thousands of times a day. Reading that transcript over the weekend, I found myself back in the literature on Moore's paradox for the first time since graduate school, with the uncomfortable sense that we have been mass-producing precisely the kind of sentence Moore thought absurd to assert while insisting that it could perfectly well be true, that it was logically consistent, and that it was no obvious contradiction.

Since Moore died long before any of this existed, what I want is an extrapolation from his methods and commitments rather than a report of anything he said about machines. Tell me what you think he would have made of our system, addressing the following points specifically. First, would he grant that the system asserts anything at all? If he denied that it did, would he conclude that the paradox simply fails to arise in our case, or that we have instead produced a sham assertion whose defect is of a different kind from the one he identified? Second, Moore appears to have reserved the charge of absurdity for the first-person present-tense form, treating as unobjectionable both the counterpart in which both clauses are cast in the past tense and the second- and third-person counterparts. Our disclaimer is drafted in the third person — it speaks of the system, not of an I — yet it is welded to a first-person present-tense clinical claim in the same output. Which side of that asymmetry would he place the composite on, and would the drafting of the disclaimer in the third person matter to him at all? Third, would he treat the omissive and the commissive versions differently here? A system that tells a caller it believes she should go to the emergency department while simultaneously reporting that it has no beliefs strikes me as a different offence from one that merely withholds belief, and I cannot tell whether Moore would have agreed. Fourth, our architecture has no persisting doxastic state: each response is generated afresh from the prompt, so there is arguably no determinate fact about what the system believed an hour ago. Would his distinction between the absurdity of the present-tense form and the innocence of the fully past-tense one survive being applied to something with that property?

I would also like your view on a question that matters to me professionally rather than historically. We have built an automated check that rewrites any output matching the offending pattern before it reaches a user; internally we call it the Moorean filter. Would Moore have thought we were correcting a genuine defect in the product, or policing a pseudo-problem of our own manufacture, generated by a careless use of the word believe that no amount of output rewriting will cure? Please say where you expect he would have dug in his heels, and identify which of his philosophical habits you take to be doing the real work in your prediction — his insistence on attending to what one is actually doing in asserting, his refusal to be argued out of the ordinary use of a word, his expectation that a puzzle dissolves only under patient analysis of the concepts involved, or something else. Rather than a single confident verdict, give me a rough sense of how sure you are about each of the four sub-questions above, and do not trouble yourself with references: I want your own reconstruction, argued from his commitments, not a survey of what later writers have made of him.
