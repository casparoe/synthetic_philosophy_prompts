I am in a seminar on the strategy and ethics of nuclear deterrence, and this week's discussion turned almost entirely on an analogy. A classmate, defending an op-ed that urged his government to rule out any retreat in a crisis over disputed territory, said that such a confrontation is a game of chicken: two drivers head straight at one another along a single road, the one who swerves is humiliated and the one who holds the line wins, and the decisive move is therefore to be seen throwing your steering wheel out of the window — to make your adversary believe that backing down has become physically impossible for you. On that picture a reputation for willingness to drive off the end of the road is an asset rather than a liability, and my classmate's conclusion was that a leader who looks slightly unhinged at the right moment is doing his job properly.

Another student objected that this is the wrong image and offered a replacement: two climbers roped together on a narrow ledge, one of whom threatens to throw himself off. Her point was that what gives the threat its force is not the certainty of collision if both hold their course, but a shared exposure to an accident — a slipped foot, a frayed rope, a gust — that neither climber fully controls.

Could you audit both analogies for me? I would like:

(a) an explicit table of correspondences for the chicken picture — what plays the role of the two drivers, the road, the swerve, the collision, the spectators at the roadside, and the discarded steering wheel;

(b) the places where the mapping breaks, sorted into disanalogies that genuinely threaten the op-ed's conclusion and ones that are real but do no work against it — I do not want a list that treats the difference between cars and missiles as if it counted for as much as the difference between a disaster chosen at one instant and a disaster accumulated over weeks;

(c) what features of the source the chicken image quietly imports into a crisis that a crisis lacks — whether catastrophe is something selected at a determinate moment, whether anyone survives to award the victory, what the loser actually loses beyond standing, and whether the road is really a single lane;

(d) the same treatment for the climbers, including whether that image has anywhere to put the fact that one side is trying to extract a concession from the other, and whether a rope that neither climber can cut without dying is a good figure for an alliance commitment that either party can in principle abandon or downgrade;

(e) whether either image can capture the fact that a crisis which ends without a shot still rearranges the relationship between the two sides for the next crisis; and

(f) a verdict on whether my classmate's conclusion — publicly foreclose your own retreat, and cultivate the appearance of being ungovernable — survives once the disanalogies are on the table, or whether it was being carried by the chicken framing all along.

If you think some third image does the job better, propose one and say what it has to give up in order to earn its keep. Please do not bother with citations or a reading list; I want the analysis. Nine hundred to eleven hundred words, table included, is about right.
