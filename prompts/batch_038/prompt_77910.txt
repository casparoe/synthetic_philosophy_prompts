I work on long-horizon capacity planning for a regional power utility, which means I spend my days making decisions whose consequences land on people who have not been hired yet, and I have never taken a philosophy or an economics course. In trying to teach myself why the numbers I use look the way they do, I found that almost everything turns on a single parameter — a rate at which the future is discounted — and that the arguments about it are far older and stranger than I expected. I also read that the mathematician who first worked out the optimal savings problem in the 1920s considered it indefensible to discount the future merely because it is the future, and treated the impulse to do so as a failure of imagination rather than a finding. That has stayed with me.

I would like you to write a short story, between 2,000 and 2,800 words, that thinks this through in narrative form. Please write in American English, with American spelling and idiom, and give the story a title.

Use the following setting, which I have invented and which you should treat as fixed. In a near-future northern town, a charitable trust holds a physical endowment meant to see the town through a century of hard weather: cold-chain medical stock, seed, and banks of solid-state storage whose charge slowly leaks away. The endowment degrades. Its degradation rate has been measured in the field for sixty years, and it is the job of the story's protagonist, a maintenance engineer, to keep measuring it. The trust's charter ties the permitted annual drawdown to one rate, set at the founding partly from an estimate of the probability that the town itself would still be there to use the store. A new hydrological survey revises that survival estimate sharply downward, and the trustees want the rate raised so the endowment can be spent while there are still people to spend it on. The protagonist has to produce the number that will justify the decision either way.

I want the story to dramatize the following distinctions rather than explain them. A measured decay constant is a fact about a stock of goods; whether that fact licenses consuming the stock now is a different question, and the story should make someone feel the gap between the two. Discounting because the beneficiaries may not exist is not the same as discounting because they matter less, and the charter's founders appear to have slid from one to the other. If the people who inherit the store will be better off, the same unit is worth less to them — but that is a claim about the marginal value of consumption, not about their moral weight, and the projection of how well off they will be may simply be wrong in either direction. A rate fixed by one generation binds later ones only for as long as they let it, and the ease of overriding it should matter to somebody. And the arithmetic of compounding is brutal enough to be a plot event: a change of a few tenths of a point in that rate should, at some point in the story, visibly erase a century.

Two further constraints. First, do not name real economists, cite literature, or include a bibliography; let the trust's own documents, minutes, and instrument readings carry whatever authority the ideas need. Second, avoid a mouthpiece. The disagreement should run between two competent, decent people who are each right about something, and it should not be settled by a speech. Include at least one scene in which somebody does the calculation by hand or on a terminal and is either horrified or relieved by the result, and end on an action or a refusal to act rather than a summary of the argument.

One detail you may use if it helps: the town that would benefit in eighty years will not be populated by the same people who would have lived there had the trustees decided differently, so nobody in the story can quite name who is being wronged.
