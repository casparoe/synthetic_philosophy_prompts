Retired three years ago after a career in a water chemistry lab, and lately I've been reading philosophy to keep the old gray matter busy. But here's an actual thing from last weekend that I can't let go of, and it feels like a philosophy problem wearing a costume.

My granddaughter's school did a fundraiser, and one donor handed them two sealed envelopes with checks inside — one exactly twice the other, and the principal announced the amounts were round hundreds somewhere between $200 and $1,600. The kids got to pick an envelope for the school, blind. Emma picked one, and then the principal, being playful, offered to let her trade it for the other one. Emma made this arguement for trading: the other envelope is either double what I'm holding or half of it, fifty-fifty, so on average trading gains me a quarter of what I've got. Then she stoped mid-sentence and said, wait, but that same argument says I should trade straight back, which is obviously silly. She asked me where the mistake was and I couldn't tell her. I've spent thirty-five years hunting down errors in measurements, and here I can't find the bug — her arithmetic seems fine, and that's the part that bothers me.

So: is she actually wrong, and if so where exactly does the reasoning go bad? And given they knew the range, was there anything smarter than a coin flip available to them? Would it have changed things if she'd been allowed to open her envelope and look at the number first?
