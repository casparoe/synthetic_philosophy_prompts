I go to a reading group that's half philosophy grad students and half people from the vision lab down the hall, and it's usually pretty low-energy — someone reads out a paragraph, we argue about a footnote, we eat stale biscuits. Last week it turned into an actual two-hour fight and I sat there unable to referee any of it, so I'm outsourcing. The topic was cognitive penetration: whether what you know or believe can change what you literally see, as opposed to changing what you're willing to report that you see.

Nadia opened by saying the empirical question is basically settled and that philosophers who still hedge are behind the times. Her two exhibits: the face-lightness work of Levin and Banaji, where racially ambiguous face images with the same mean luminance were matched as darker the more they were categorized as Black, and Hansen and colleagues' fruit study, where a banana only looked grey to people once it was physically tinted slightly away from yellow. Same light in the eye, different appearance, and the difference tracks stored knowledge — so penetration is real. Ivo, who does psychophysics, said those are the two results he'd throw out first. On the faces: holding mean luminance constant isn't holding the image constant, the morph changes the features and the local surround around the region people are matching, and lightness is famously computed from local surround — so a purely bottom-up lightness computation might deliver the whole effect without any beliefs being involved. On the banana: adjusting until something looks achromatic is a criterion task, and the authors themselves describe the driver as high-level visual memory, which is arguably a resource inside the visual system rather than a belief about bananas — which is exactly where the firebreak is drawn, so nothing has been breached. He added that there's a whole review cataloguing this genre of pitfall: unequal stimuli, demand characteristics, and response bias getting scored as changes in appearance. Nadia said that's a moving target, and a revealing one: if every effect can be re-described as a low-level mechanism, the thesis is true by definition of where you put the border, and there's a standing circularity worry about cashing out "cognition" as whatever hasn't been shown to reach into vision — which makes the denial unfalsifiable rather than confirmed. She also said perceptual learning is the harder case for him: radiologists and chess players demonstrably segment scenes differently after years of training, and that's stored information structuring perception, not just biasing a report.

Ivo conceded maybe-some-penetration but said it buys nothing philosophically, because the claim people actually want is the epistemic one — that a penetrated experience justifies less. That argument, he said, assumes experience only justifies if it's belief-independent, which is the thing in question, and it has an absurd consequence: on that story an expert's beliefs make her see the nodule better and therefore her evidence gets worse, so the more you know the less your seeing supports you. Nadia said the downgrading isn't symmetric — what matters is whether the penetrating state is itself well-founded, so truth-tracking expertise can upgrade while a prejudiced perceiver's experience inherits the defect, which is precisely the case that makes the whole topic matter ethically and not just technically. Ivo's last move was that she's changed the subject: if the problem is that an unjustified belief transmits its defect into an experience, that's the generic problem of warrant transmission and you get it from ordinary inference off perception without any penetration at all, nobody has shown appearance rather than matching judgement shifted in the face case, and the inference from penetration to the theory-ladenness of observation in philosophy of science is a non sequitur even if everything she says is true.

Could you go through the six moves one at a time — Nadia's opening two examples, Ivo's two rebuttals plus his pitfalls point, her circularity/perceptual-learning reply, his epistemic-significance objection, her asymmetry reply, and his final bundle — and for each say whether the move succeeds, partly succeeds, or fails, and name the specific empirical or conceptual claim it's leaning on so I can check whether that claim actually holds? Then tell me who came out ahead overall, and separately, what both of them were ignoring — including what a clean test would have to look like if reports of appearance can't be trusted, and whether it matters that some of these effects come from attention or statistical learning rather than from belief proper.
