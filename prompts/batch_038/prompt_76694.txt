I'm setting the final paper for a fourth-year/MA epistemology module and I want one essay question on the regress problem that isn't just "expound and compare foundationalism, coherentism and infinitism". Half the cohort would reproduce their lecture notes in a different order and I'd learn nothing about whether they can actually think. So I want to hand them a little machine and make them say what it does.

Here's the machine, which I'd like built into the wording of the question itself:

S believes p. For every belief in the chain there is a further belief that is a reason for it, running back for ever, with no last link and no loop. But the reason relation weakens as you go back. The first reason makes p highly likely; the reason for that one makes it likely, though less strongly; the next weaker still, and so on, so that the strengths of support converge on a limit. The total support the chain confers on p settles somewhere around 0.9 — never reaching 1, never falling away to nothing either.

The question then has to press on three things:

(a) Is p justified for S, and how strongly? Where does the answer come from — the limit of the sequence, some aggregate over the individual links, or is the question malformed because it assumes justification is the kind of thing that could have a determinate value here?

(b) Suppose, more realistically, that S has only ever actually produced finitely many links, though she's disposed to produce a further reason whenever she's challenged. Then at any point where you stop her there is a last link with nothing behind it. Does that make her a foundationalist whether she likes it or not? And what exactly would an infinitist have to claim about dispositions — as opposed to performances — to say no?

(c) Which of the three positions is actually damaged by the machine, and which can simply absorb it? In particular, is a coherentist entitled to say the whole thing is beside the point, or does the machine have a version that bites on her too?

Some constraints on what you write:

- The question as it will appear on the paper: worth 25 marks, students get 75 minutes on it, closed book, and the scenario has to be stated in full inside the question so that it's answerable with no specialist reading and no exegesis of any particular author. They can name positions and standard moves, but nobody should need to quote anyone and there's obviously no bibliography.
- The difficulty has to come from the machine, not from the labels. A student who just sorts the three -isms into tidy boxes and mentions them in turn should not be able to scrape past a bare pass.
- Then a model answer of about 700 words. I want it to take a line on (a) rather than hovering, and to show what's really at stake in (b) — my own guess is that the crux is whether justification propagates along the chain link by link or whether it's a property of the whole structure, and that the answer to (a) changes depending on which picture you assume, but you may well disagree and I'd rather you argue it out than defer to me.
- Then a marking scheme totalling 25, split into five or six named moves with the marks each carries, and for each one a line on how students typically botch it. I'm expecting entries for things like reifying the limit and treating it as though it were one more link further back, and sliding between "the chain is infinitely long" and "the chain is completed" as though those came to the same thing. Add whatever else you think deserves catching.
- Finally, and separately, tell me what divides a first from a 2:1 on this question. Name the specific moves a competent but unimaginative answer will be missing, not just a general remark about depth or nuance.

Start straight in with the question as it will appear on the paper — no preamble, no "here is your exam question", and don't restate any of the above back at me. Then the model answer, then the marking scheme, then the note on the first/2:1 boundary. Nothing after that either; I don't need a closing comment on how you'd use it in class.
