And here's the submission. I've read it once and I'm deliberately not telling you what I made of it, because I don't want to steer you in either direction — I'm kind of worried about marking it wrong, definately.

> ¶1. Turing's first move is to refuse a definition. He points out that if the meaning of "machine" and "think" were settled by looking at how people ordinarily use the words, the answer to "can machines think?" would have to come from something like a Gallup poll, which he thinks is absurd. So he substitutes a question about a game. The imitation game is therefore a criterion, not a definition: he is not claiming that thinking just is passing, he is claiming that this is the nearby question worth investigating. That's an important difference, because a criterion can be a bad one without being a bad definition of anything.
>
> ¶2. The criterion is purely behavioral by construction. The interrogator never sees, touches, or hears the competitors and gets only typed answers, which is what lets Turing say that the game does not penalize the machine for things like failing to win a beauty contest or losing a race against an airplane. Nothing about what the machine is made of is allowed to matter. Only what comes out of the teleprinter.
>
> ¶3. The most familiar objection is Block's. Block asks us to imagine a system that is nothing but an enormous lookup table pairing every possible sequence of inputs over, say, an hour of conversation with a canned response, and he argues that such a system could get through a short interrogation without possessing any of the psychological states the interrogator thinks he is detecting. Block's other example makes the point even more sharply: a billion or so people organized so that each one plays the part of a neuron in the functional organization of a human body. Now, the Double Ask catches both of these. A lookup table has no record of what it has already said, so if you re-pose an earlier question in new words it will contradict itself, and the billion-person case is worse still, since none of the individual participants has any idea what the collective is doing, so a repeated question exposes them immediately. So the Double Ask filters out exactly the systems that the original criterion wrongly passes, which is why I think it does answer the objection.
>
> ¶4. Turing's prediction supports this reading. He expected that by around the year 2000 a machine with about a billion units of storage would fool an average interrogator seven times out of ten after five minutes of questioning. That is a very demanding standard, and it shows two things: the criterion was never meant to be a low bar, and Turing regarded passing as more or less sufficient for genuine thought, since a machine that deceives a judge 70 per cent of the time is thinking for all practical purposes.
>
> ¶5. So the Double Ask converts the game from a test of surface fluency into a test of whether there is a single coherent point of view behind the answers — which is what Searle's Chinese Room says the original game cannot detect. Searle's man in the room manipulates symbols he does not understand, and the systems reply says the understanding, if any, belongs to the room as a whole. The Double Ask settles that dispute empirically, because a system that merely manipulates symbols cannot keep its own story straight, whereas a system with a real point of view can.
