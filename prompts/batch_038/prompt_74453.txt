I am a postdoc in historical demography, and next week I send my senior co-author — call her H. — the draft of a chapter that argues a particular cohort should be treated as a genuine generation rather than as a statistical convenience. I need to predict her ruling before she makes it.

H. is not a theorist of generations in the abstract; she has a settled practice of ruling on proposed cohort labels, one at a time, and she has never written down the criterion. What she has said in print and in correspondence amounts to the following record.

She holds that the cyclical, archetype-based schemes sold to general readers are worthless, and that a generation is never an age-graded character type. Of the classical interwar sociology of generations she keeps only one apparatus: the triad that separates a cohort's mere common location in history, its becoming an actual generation, and the smaller group inside it that articulates the generation's self-understanding. She has said, of that triad, that the third term is the only one that ever does any explanatory work. She has also said, on a different occasion, that a change in what adolescence is happens whether or not anyone supplies a name for it. I do not know how she reconciles these.

Her rulings so far, as best I can reconstruct them:

1. The postwar baby boom: a real generation, and one generation, not two. She resisted subdividing it while conceding that its halves have quite different biographies.
2. The label circulated by advertising and market research for the boom's tail — those born from the late 1950s into the mid-1960s: not a generation. The people it names, she observed, continue to describe themselves either as boomers or as members of the cohort that follows, and nothing about their adolescence distinguishes them from those born five years earlier.
3. The cohort born from the late 1920s through the war years, popularly called the silent one: not a generation. She treats it as a residual category defined by negation; the name was imposed from outside by a magazine, was never taken up by the people it described, and their childhood was a continuation of the conditions of the cohort ahead of them.
4. The cohort following the boom: a real generation, whose boundaries are irreducibly vague — and she thinks the vagueness is evidence the category is tracking something, not a defect in it.
5. The proposed microgeneration for the cusp between that cohort and the next, the one described as having an analogue childhood and a digital adolescence: not a generation. She allows that it names something real, a stratum sitting on a boundary, but a boundary is not a kind.
6. The two cohorts after that — the one whose adolescence was shaped by the arrival of the networked home computer and the one whose adolescence was shaped by the handheld device and the feed: both real.
7. A national case she accepted: the Japanese cohort defined by having been schooled under the relaxed-curriculum reforms, a term used pejoratively and later reclaimed by its own members. Real, even though it does not line up with any cohort in the Anglo-American scheme, and she is untroubled by that. Of this case she wrote that a policy change counts as a generative event only because the cohort made a marker of itself out of it.
8. The youngest cohort now being given a name by consultancies: she declined to rule, saying only that it was too early, without saying too early for what.

The case she will have to rule on is my own field site. It is the Japanese cohort born roughly between 1971 and 1982, which finished schooling and tried to enter stable employment between the early 1990s and the mid-2000s, when regular-entry hiring had all but stopped. In Japanese public debate it is known by a term that translates roughly as the employment ice-age generation. That term was in use among the cohort's own members — in the newsletters of the temporary-worker associations they formed and in the campaigns and suits they organized — well before journalists and ministries picked it up, and it now appears in a government report on mid-career employment support. It has articulate internal factions that disagree with one another: one that casts the cohort as the injured party of a broken hiring system, one that recasts irregular work as a chosen alternative to the salaried model, and one that tries to organize the cohort as a voting bloc.

Equally important is what does not distinguish them. In technology, household size, schooling, and popular culture their childhood and adolescence were continuous with those of people born five years either side; the curriculum reform that defines the neighbouring cohort in item 7 overlaps the older half of them. What sets them apart is the condition of the labour market at the moment they entered it — and that condition persisted, so the same shock fell on several adjacent cohorts at different ages.

My own guess is that she will say no, but I cannot tell whether her no would be the no of item 2 and item 3 or a no I have not anticipated, and the difference determines how much of the chapter I have to rewrite.

Please give me the following, in this order:

- The single rule you think H. is actually running, stated precisely enough that it could be applied to a cohort nobody has thought of yet, together with the items in the record that most tightly constrain it.
- At least two further rules that fit every one of the eight rulings just as well as yours does. Do not pad this with strawmen: each alternative has to be something a careful demographer could hold and something the record genuinely fails to exclude. Say which item, if any, you were tempted to use against each of them and why it does not quite work.
- Your prediction for the ice-age cohort, with a rough confidence and the argument you expect her to make. Where her two recorded remarks about the third term in the triad and about unnamable change in adolescence pull in opposite directions, tell me which one governs this case and why.
- The one further case — a real or clearly describable hypothetical cohort that she has not ruled on — that would discriminate between your rule and your best alternative. State what each candidate rule predicts about it, so that I can see in advance why the answer is decisive and not merely suggestive.
- A recasting of my chapter's central claim that would survive under either of the two leading candidate rules, using the distinction she already accepts, and a plain statement of what my argument loses by that weakening.
- Finally: she will press the point that what I have described looks like a period effect falling on several cohorts at different ages rather than a cohort effect at all. Tell me whether that objection is internal to one of the candidate rules, a third rule of its own, or an objection she could make consistently with any of them — and which response to it concedes the least.

I am not asking for a survey of the literature on generations, for a definition of the term, or for references; everything you need is above, and I would rather have your reasoning about her than a general account of the field.
