I'm a fourth-year epistemology PhD student and I've somehow ended up co-running a cross-listed reading group between our department and the ML people down the hall; theme this term is self-improving systems and I'm the designated translator. My advisor thinks the whole thing is a category error, but the department buys the bagels so here we are. For the session in two weeks I want a handout that is literally a flowchart: "which view do you actually hold about bootstrapping?" It should start from the familiar shape of the problem — a subject takes a source's deliverances at face value, accumulates a record of them, then reasons from that record to the claim that the source is reliable (the gas gauge / suspiciously-red-table family of cases) — and end at a named position. Audience is half philosophy grads and half ML students who've never met the phrase "transmission failure," so every node has to be answerable by someone with zero exposure to this literature: phrase each node as a concrete case or a claim to react to, never as "do you accept principle X," and if you can't avoid a term of art, define it in a subordinate clause right there in the node. I'm thinking 9-11 nodes with two or three options each, landing on 8-12 leaves, and for each leaf give me (a) the standard name if it has one — dogmatism, entitlement, the no-bootstrapping principle, whatever — or a plain descriptive label if it doesn't, (b) one sentence on its most awkward commitment, and (c) a tiebreaker: the smallest possible case that separates it from whichever leaf sits nearest it in the tree. No bibliography, no page numbers, no suggested readings, no "see also" — I don't want anyone flipping to a paper mid-handout. It has to fit on two pages printed front/back because the printer in the grad lounge jams on anything longer.

The twist, and the reason I'm bothering: every version of this map I've seen sorts people only on the one-shot question, but the ML half of the room only cares about what happens under *iteration*, so build the tree so that the iteration joints are load-bearing rather than decorative. Three cases of mine that I want you to work into the nodes. First, the self-certifying archive: a pipeline classifies images and also writes, for each batch, a note about how accurate it took itself to be; a year later someone infers the pipeline is accurate from the notes plus the labels, where all of the labels were themselves produced by the pipeline. Is that structurally the same as one round of bootstrapping or a different animal, and does your view care? Second, the amnesiac's notebook: a driver keeps two columns, gauge reading and actual tank level, and the second column really was filled in from genuinely independent checks (odometer, fill-ups) — but years later she's forgotten that and reasons from the notebook believing the second column just came from the gauge. Does the conclusion inherit the etiology the premises actually have, or the etiology she thinks they have? Third, degradation: suppose each generation of the archive trains partly on the previous generation's output, so the record you reason from gets noisier and less informative over time even though the shape of the reasoning never changes. Does your view predict warrant decays across iterations, stays flat, or — bizarrely — compounds?

On the joints I want cut, roughly: whether warrant for a source's reliability can come from that source's own deliverances at all; if not, whether the culprit is a general constraint on reasoning or something about this particular evidence; whether what goes wrong is a failure to gain *knowledge* or a failure to gain even *justification* (I want that gap to be its own node, because I think a couple of positions live entirely inside it and get flattened by the usual maps); whether the reasoner's own view of where her premises came from matters or only the real history; and whether repeating an inference whose premises are products of earlier iterations can dilute or consume warrant, i.e. whether the account is monotone. Make sure the leaves include at least: the permissivist who says the reasoning is fine and the queasiness is a cost worth paying; the conservative/entitlement-style view on which warrant for a source can't be had from the source's own outputs; a closure-denying diagnosis; a no-bootstrapping-principle diagnosis; a reliabilist one; a coherentist one; the misrepresentation-flavored one where the reasoning goes wrong because the reasoner represents her evidence as better supported than it is; and the straight skeptic who takes all this to show there's no basic knowledge. Then, since you're building from the logical space rather than from a list of names, give me at least two leaves that are combinations nobody actually defends — including one that handles my archive case and one that handles the amnesiac — and for each say whether the cell is empty because the view is incoherent, because it's coherent but deeply unattractive, or because it's just unexplored and someone should write the paper. Finally, flag explicitly any pair of leaves your tree can't genuinely separate; I'd much rather have a note saying "these two come out identically on every node, the real difference is somewhere else" than a fake distinction dressed up as a branch. Oh and start the handout with a two-sentence "how to use this" note telling the reader the tree sorts by what they'd say about cases, not by what they'd call themselves — people otherwise just wander straight to whichever label they like and skip the questions.
