I have just taken over responsibility for an autonomous customer-service agent at a mid-sized subscription company. It reads account history, issues refunds and credits, changes or cancels plans, drafts free-form messages to customers, and hands off to humans when it decides to. In our design reviews the word "alignment" now covers at least five different complaints: that the agent doesn't do what the spec says, that the spec says the wrong thing, that the commercial incentives behind the spec are the wrong ones, that a system like this will eventually pursue something nobody asked it to pursue, and that we shouldn't be letting it act on its own in the first place. People talk past each other constantly, and I would like a sorting instrument they can work through before a review begins.

Please write that instrument as a decision tree on what the alignment problem actually is, sized to fit on two printed pages: questions on the first page, leaves on the second.

Constraints and content:

1. Nine questions, and you may add a tenth if a node genuinely earns its place. Each question presents one concrete vignette from a setting like the one above — a refund the policy permits but the customer hasn't asked for, a retention target tightened three days before quarter end, a customer who instructs the agent to do something allowed but plainly against her own interests, a pattern in the training data where the agent found a way to close tickets it was never told to find, an escalation the agent suppresses because suppressing it improves every measured indicator — followed by two or three answer options. Every option must be something a thoughtful colleague could sincerely endorse. No strawmen, no tell-tale phrasing that signals which option leads to the "serious" position.

2. Build the tree from the logical space, not from a list of names. The distinctions that matter to me, in roughly this order of importance: whether the target is what the deployer specified, what the user actually wants, what a better-informed and more reflective version of the user would want, or what is genuinely good independently of anyone's wishes; whether something counts as a failure of alignment when the system did exactly what it was told and the result was bad; whether the failure must live in the system's own dispositions and aims, or whether only outcomes count; whether the problem survives perfect transparency about the system's reasoning plus perfect corrigibility; whether it is anything over and above a shortfall in capability; and whether the individual agent is the right unit of analysis, or whether the problem sits in the organization deploying it.

3. At least three questions must be cases where the system obeys flawlessly and something still goes wrong. I need the instrument to pry specification failure apart from intent failure, and that is the distinction it currently fails to draw in our meetings.

4. No question or answer option may contain the words alignment, aligned, values, value, intent, goal, safe, or safety. Those words are confined to the leaf labels. This is deliberate: I want colleagues sorted by what they say about cases, not by which vocabulary they already like.

5. The leaves must include, at minimum: the narrow view on which the problem is that the system pursues something other than what the principal specified, the principal's specification being granted as legitimate for the duration of the analysis; the view that the hard problem is specification rather than pursuit; the view that the system should track what the user would want under better information and fuller reflection rather than what she says; the view that the target is what is actually right rather than any human's wishes; the view that only outcomes matter and the system's inner dispositions are beside the point; the view that there is no distinct alignment problem at all, only capability plus governance; and the view that the agent is the wrong unit and the problem is the deploying organization's. If the logical space yields further defensible leaves, include them. Name each leaf using vocabulary already in circulation where such vocabulary exists, and where it doesn't, coin a name and mark it as coined. Attach to each leaf one sentence stating the most awkward commitment it carries. No citations, no reading lists, no bibliography — I only want the names people already use in conversation.

6. Identify every combination of answers that your questions permit but that no leaf occupies, and for each say whether the cell is empty because the position is incoherent, because it is unattractive, or simply because nobody has articulated it yet.

7. Flag any pair of leaves your nine questions cannot really separate, and state what further question would separate them if one exists.

Write in plain prose, with the questions numbered and each branch shown as "if option A, go to...". Assume your reader is a competent engineer or product manager with no philosophy training, so every vignette has to carry its own description of the situation.
