Our philosophy-of-decision reading group met last Thursday and two members, Hana and Felix, ended up in a long argument that the rest of us could not resolve. I am writing this up as neutrally as I can, because I want an outside adjudication rather than more votes. Both cases under discussion were ones Hana had read out earlier in the evening, and I will restate them here in full so that the dispute can be assessed on its own terms.

**Case A (the mugging).** A being of near-perfect predictive ability flips a fair coin out of your sight. Had the coin come up tails, it would have given you $10,000 — but only if it had predicted that, in the heads outcome, you would hand over $100 on request. The coin came up heads. The being now appears and asks you for $100. Nothing it can do now will give you $10,000, and nothing you can do now will bring the tails outcome into existence; the only thing your choice could ever have affected was a payment in a branch that did not occur.

**Case B (the letter), a variant on the blackmail cases in the same family.** A letter arrives from a writer who claims to predict recipients' decisions very accurately. It says: *I have done nothing to your house and I will do nothing to it. My model tells me whether your house has been contaminated by a substance that will destroy it within the month. I have mailed this letter to exactly those addressees for whom precisely one of the following is true: (a) your house is contaminated; (b) you will wire me $1,000. If you pay, I keep the money; I promise nothing in return.* So the contamination is an ordinary physical fact, settled before you read anything, and the writer neither caused it nor offers to reverse it.

Here is the exchange, in order.

1. **Hana:** The two cases have one shape and one answer procedure. In each, a reliable predictor has correlated your decision with something that is not causally downstream of it. The right move is to pick the policy that looks best from before the correlation was installed: pay the $100 in Case A, refuse the wire in Case B.
2. **Felix:** Then your principle gives opposite prescriptions in structurally identical situations. In Case A you are told to treat a branch that will never happen as still on the books. In Case B you are told to disregard information that the letter genuinely carries about your house. At least one of those instructions has to be a mistake, and I would like to know which.
3. **Hana:** They are not identical. In Case A the payoff in the tails branch was keyed to your very act in the heads branch, so your act now is what fixes whether that payoff would have been yours. In Case B the contamination was produced by ordinary causes before you opened the envelope, and no act of yours reaches back into it.
4. **Felix:** That second half does all the work and it does too much work. What makes Case B safe to refuse is not that your act cannot reach the contamination — it is that the money buys nothing. Change one line: suppose the writer also promises to send a remediation crew to exactly those who pay. Now either a perfect predictor cannot keep its mailing rule intact, or the payer is strictly better off. So your verdict is tracking a contingent detail about whether the wire has any use, not a fact about news versus causation.
5. **Hana:** Fine — but that detail vindicates me rather than you. Compare the two decision algorithms across the worlds the writer could be in. A payer in a contaminated world gets no letter at all, since both (a) and (b) are then true, and loses the house without ever having spent the $1,000. A payer in an uncontaminated world gets the letter and spends $1,000 for nothing. A refuser never spends and loses the house exactly in the worlds where the house was already lost. Refusing is never worse and is sometimes better. Your evidential reasoning — that paying is good news about the house — is reasoning that only ever gets used by people who are already standing in the world where it costs them.
6. **Felix:** Notice what you just did. You dropped the news/causation vocabulary entirely and ran a dominance comparison over algorithms. That is a causal-style calculation. Now run it on Case A: what does the algorithm "hand over $100 when asked" get you in the branch you actually inhabit? Nothing. Case A only comes out the way you want because you are counting a payment that no one ever receives. Either such payments count, in which case the writer in Case B can build a version where paying is correct too, or they do not, in which case you should refuse in both cases.
7. **Hana:** They count, because before the coin was flipped you were the person who stood on both sides of that bet, and it is that earlier situation your present choice settles.
8. **Felix:** And who is that earlier person? If your answer is "me, viewed from behind the coin flip," you have not discovered anything about rationality — you have stipulated which of your temporal stages gets to speak for you. Updatelessness is a choice of bookkeeping, and you cannot then cite the bookkeeping as the reason the mugging comes out as it does.
9. **Hana:** The same objection lands on you. "Me, the one actually holding the letter" is also a stipulation about which stage speaks — and it is the stipulation that has a person wiring $1,000 to a stranger who caused nothing, offers nothing, and can do nothing.

Could you go through this move by move and tell me who is right at each step, rather than only who wins at the end? Specifically: (i) for each of the nine numbered moves, assign a rating on a five-point scale from clearly mistaken to clearly right, with a sentence or two of justification that engages with the content of that move and not just with the general debate; (ii) say explicitly whether Felix's fourth move — that Case B turns on the money's being useless rather than on any news/causation distinction — succeeds, and whether Hana's fifth move really is an answer to it or a concession dressed as one; (iii) say whether Felix's sixth move correctly identifies Hana's fifth as abandoning her own framework, or whether a policy-level comparison and an evidential/causal distinction can coexist; (iv) adjudicate the eighth and ninth moves, which look to me like the same objection pointed in two directions, and say whether they cancel, or whether one has force the other lacks; and (v) identify anything substantive that both of them leave out of the argument — including, if it matters, whether Case A and Case B really do belong in the same family at all, and whether the accuracy of the predictor in Case B can be made fully coherent given that its mailing rule refers to the addressee's own later decision.
