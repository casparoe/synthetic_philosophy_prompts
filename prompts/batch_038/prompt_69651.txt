Been chewing on the algorithmic fairness impossibility results and I want an essay that actually takes a position rather than surveying the landscape. My understanding of the setup: when two groups have different base rates, you can't simultaneously have calibration plus equal false positive rates plus equal false negative rates. But there's a catch that I think gets glossed over in the popular write-ups — you *can* have all three if you're willing to be useless about it. Score everyone at their own group's base rate, put the threshold above it, and now nobody gets flagged: FPR is 0 for both groups, FNR is 1 for both, and calibration holds trivially. So is the theorem a genuine normative tragedy, or is it just a statement about how much predictive power you have to buy with unfairness — an ordinary tradeoff wearing an impossiblity costume? Deal with that head on.

And then the second wrinkle: in the actual cases the label is generated by the system you're worried about (who gets stopped, who gets re-arrested, who gets screened), so the base rate gap isn't an exogenous fact about the world, it's partly a measurement artefact. Does that sharpen the conflict or dissolve it — if the true prevalences were equal, would the dilemma vanish, and do we have any way of knowing?

Finish by committing. Tell me what you'd actually require of a deployed risk score, which of the standard criteria you'd throw overboard, and why the one you keep is the one you keep.
