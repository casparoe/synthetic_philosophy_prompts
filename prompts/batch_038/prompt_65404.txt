I am in the final stages of a novel whose third act unfolds inside the People & Culture function of a mid-sized cold-chain logistics firm headquartered in Columbus, Ohio, and I need a piece of in-world documentary fiction I can quote at length. What I want is a satirical excerpt from that firm's employee handbook — call it Section 14, "Going Above and Beyond" — written entirely in the voice of the handbook, in deadpan corporate American English, roughly nine hundred words, formatted as numbered clauses, with a short sidebar of frequently asked questions and, at the very end, a one-sentence acknowledgment that the employee is required to sign. Please invent the firm's subsidiaries, its offices, its named managers, and its recognized employees. The target is a practice rather than a person or a real company: the practice of an institution trying to harvest moral credit from conduct that, by its own explicit doctrine, it may never require.

The engine of the piece is a distinction the firm has adopted on a consultant's advice and can no longer escape: certain acts are better than duty demands yet cannot be demanded of anyone, so that an employee who declines one has done nothing wrong and may not be asked, pressed, counseled, or "invited" to reconsider. Human Resources nevertheless wishes to celebrate such acts extravagantly, because celebration is cheap, and Section 14 must show, clause by clause and without ever confessing it, that every device the firm invents to encourage this conduct corrodes the category the conduct belongs to. I would like the following moves enacted rather than announced: a points-and-badge program that pays out for beyond-duty conduct, with the handbook visibly uneasy about whether an act performed for points is still beyond duty or has quietly become a wage; a cost-calibration procedure, on the reasoning that whether an act exceeds duty depends on what it costs the agent, which obliges the firm to audit each employee's finances, health, dependents, and commute, written as though this were merely prudent; the ratchet, whereby one formerly celebrated act hardens into an unwritten expectation, and Compliance's countermeasure of making the recognition deliberately cheap, ambiguous, and faintly embarrassing so that no one forms an expectation of it; a mirror-image category for conduct that is permitted yet somehow beneath one, administered by the same office and given its own demerit-free designation; an FAQ that must handle, with complete bureaucratic gravity, whether a manager may ask an employee to donate a kidney to a colleague, and whether the answer changes if the firm offers a reserved parking space; the firm's difficulty with its single most decorated volunteer, who has given away so much that he has no interests, opinions, or small talk left, and whose continued excellence is treated as a wellbeing liability; a form an employee may file to decline praise, the filing of which is itself beyond the call of duty and therefore may not be requested; and a standing clause asserting that nothing in Section 14 creates an obligation, which should reappear in progressively more strained formulations as the section proceeds.

Two craft constraints matter to me more than any individual joke. First, keep the irony legible but never break the mask: no philosopher's name, no imported jargon, no wink at the reader, no authorial aside, and no clause that simply states the objection the section is busy dramatizing. Second, the contradiction should be maintained rather than resolved — the handbook must never quite admit that it is requiring the unrequirable, and if it slips, the slip should be visible as a failure of nerve on the firm's part, not on yours. Aim for the register of managerial euphemism in which "we" means "you," and let the humor come from getting the ethics right, so that a moral philosopher reading it laughs rather than winces. Please put the signed acknowledgment at the point of maximum strain on the section's insistence that nothing here is required, and leave it to you how far it gives. After the excerpt, add a separate note of no more than one hundred and fifty words identifying which clause carries which problem in the theory it is parodying; I will not use that note in the novel, but it will tell me whether the satire is fair. The whole thing, excerpt and note alike, in American English.
