I'm a math undergrad sitting in on a history of science seminar this term (it fit around my other classes, and the room is a damp basement, but the reading has been genuinely fun), and last week we did Hobbes. A classmate used a line from the Leviathan to win an argument, roughly that geometry is the only science it has pleased God hitherto to bestow on mankind. I'm going from memory of the seminar and probably mangling the wording, and I don't have the book with me, so please don't bother with page numbers or a reference list — I want to know what the sentence can actually be claiming, not where it's printed.

Here's the work it was being made to do. My classmate's point was that Hobbes is a simple rationalist: mathematics is the one place real certainty lives, everything else is guesswork or opinion, so the lesson of the whole system is "do politics the way you do geometry" — fix your definitions and grind out the state as a theorem. That landed well in seminar, but I couldn't shake three things. First, that word hitherto seems load-bearing: if geometry is the only science *so far*, the line isn't a ranking of the disciplines at all, it's a claim about human progress, and Hobbes's own political philosophy is presumably candidate number two — which would make my classmate's use of it backfire, since he was citing it to show that politics is second-rate. Second, elsewhere Hobbes seems to say the reason geometry is demonstrable is that we draw the lines and figures ourselves, whereas with natural bodies we don't know their construction; that's an odd criterion, much closer to "we can demonstrate only what we cause" than to "math is about eternal truths", and it doesn't look like it puts geometry on top of a hierarchy so much as inside a small club that civil philosophy is also a member of. Third, the line turns up in a chapter about words and definitions, in the middle of a rant about men getting snared by their own vocabulary, so maybe it's less an epistemology than a jab at the universities.

So can you lay the readings out as a menu — each one stated as a precise claim, what it would take for it to be true, and whether it's a platitude, a substantive and defensible thesis, or just false? I especially want to know which readings actually support "do politics like geometry," which are neutral, and which are actively hostile to it; whether hitherto changes anything for each of them; and how much of the whole business survives the embarassing fact that the real geometers of his day thought Hobbes was hopeless (he believed he'd squared the circle, if I remember right). Does that even bear on the slogan, or is the slogan about mankind's achievements rather than about his own? For what it's worth I have a pet theory, which is that he's boasting: geometry is the only finished science and he intends to make it two. Tell me whether that's a genuine reading or just me wanting him to be consistent.
