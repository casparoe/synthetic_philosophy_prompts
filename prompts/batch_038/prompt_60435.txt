I am assembling case studies for a graduate seminar on the metaphysics of natural law, and I should like one of them written out properly before I circulate it. The case is the pair of anomalies that beset Newtonian gravitation between 1840 and 1915: one law, one kind of residual, two opposite outcomes. Let me supply the facts, so that nothing turns on your recollection of them.

Le Verrier's tables for Uranus left a residual, and in 1846 he computed the position of an unseen perturbing planet; Galle and d'Arrest, at Berlin, on the night of 23–24 September, found Neptune within about a degree of the predicted place. A particular was added to the ontology and the law was left untouched. Le Verrier then turned to Mercury, whose perihelion advanced faster than the perturbations of the known planets allowed — some 38 or 39 arcseconds per century on his 1859 reckoning, about 43 on Newcomb's later one. He again proposed an unseen body, Vulcan; a country doctor named Lescarbault reported a transit he said he had observed that March, Le Verrier travelled to inspect the instrument and endorsed the sighting, and then six decades of eclipse watches, plate surveys and transit hunts found nothing.

Meanwhile the other route was open, and it had been priced. In the mid-1890s Asaph Hall and Simon Newcomb showed that a force falling off very slightly faster than the inverse square — an exponent of roughly 2.00000016 — would generate exactly the missing advance; in 1906 Seeliger proposed instead a diffuse cloud of matter around the Sun, which had the incidental virtue of accounting for the zodiacal light. Einstein's field equations of November 1915 returned the residual with no parameter tuned to fit it. There is also a coda at the far end of the system: Lowell's Planet X, Tombaugh's discovery of Pluto in 1930, and the apparent closing of the residual anomalies of Uranus and Neptune — until Charon's discovery in 1978 showed Pluto to be far too small to have produced them, and Voyager 2's 1989 flyby revised Neptune's mass downward by roughly half a percent, whereupon the residuals simply evaporated.

For the exercise, call the first route *particular-channel repair* — leave the generalization, add to the ontology — and the second *law-channel repair* — leave the ontology, revise the generalization. I do not want the Duhemian observation that a hypothesis is never tested alone; I want to know what the metaphysics of lawhood adds to that observation or subtracts from it. Note that Mercury is a rare case in which both channels were quantified: Vulcan had an orbit and a mass, the exponent had a value to seven decimal places, Seeliger's cloud had a density.

The assignment runs in both directions. First, from theory to case. Set the episode against two accounts: the Lewisian best-system view, on which lawhood is the status earned by those generalizations that best trade simplicity against strength in a systematization of the total distribution of local particular matters of fact; and a necessitation-or-production view, on which laws are something over and above that distribution — Armstrong's second-order relation of nomic necessitation between universals, or Maudlin's primitive laws generating the mosaic. For each account: what was Newton's law of gravitation in 1880, with the searches running and the exponent proposal in the air? What makes Neptune a licensed particular-channel repair, the exponent tweak an unlicensed law-channel one, and general relativity a licensed law-channel repair? Can the account draw those distinctions from inside the episode, or only from the far side of 1915? Press this worry explicitly. On the Humean account the ranking of candidate systems is fixed by the whole mosaic, including its post-1915 portion; so at the moment of decision there was either no determinate fact about which generalization was a law, or a determinate fact that depended on the future distribution of matter and that no one in 1895 could consult. Is that an objection? And does the necessitation theorist escape it — if the necessitation obtained all along, what precisely were the astronomers wrong about for two and a half centuries, and what did November 1915 change?

Then run it back, from case to theory. Which account does the episode favour, and by what route? Is there any feature of it that one of them can describe only by contortion? Take the Pluto coda seriously rather than as decoration: a particular-channel repair that looked confirmed for nearly fifty years and then dissolved into an artifact of a misestimated mass. What can "the law was confirmed" mean, on each account, in a case like that? And if your conclusion is that the metaphysics of lawhood does no work in this episode at all — that everything of interest belongs to confirmation theory and to the sociology of astronomy, and that lawhood metaphysics is simply silent where I am asking it to speak — say so plainly and argue it, rather than manufacturing a verdict to please me. A clean negative result is worth more to me than an ingenious mapping.

House rules. Between 1,500 and 2,000 words, in three sections: the mapping (what counts as the mosaic, what the candidate law is, what the anomaly is, stated separately for each account); the two diagnoses; and the verdict on the case as evidence. Treat my facts as stipulated, and if you believe a detail is wrong, flag it in a bracketed remark and proceed with my version. No tutorial on the physics and no survey of the Humeanism debate; I want work that could not have been done without this episode. No bibliography and no citations — I am after your argument, not your references. If you think a third position, a powers or dispositional-essentialist account for instance, would handle the Mercury case differently, say so in a paragraph, but do not let it displace the main comparison.
