Been reading up on how the EU actually polices subsidiarity and something about it doesn't sit right with me. The substantive test in the treaty is purely about effectiveness: the Union may only act where the member states can't sufficiently achieve the objectives on their own and can be better served centrally, by reason of scale or cross-border effects. But the procedure for enforcing that test hands the judgement to the national parliaments — they get every draft, they have eight weeks to send in a reasoned opinion alleging a breach, and if a third of them object the Commission must review the proposal and justify itself, though it can carry on regardless. Regions and cities get no standing whatsoever, even though the principle is nominally about pushing decisions to the lowest level that can handle them.

So what theory of political legitimacy does that combination presuppose? One limb reads like a neutral efficiency calculation about which tier does the job best; the other reads like a jurisdictional bargain between rival polities, since the only parties entitled to complain are precisely the ones losing the powers. Reconstruct the implicit theory, and be careful to separate what the practice strictly requires from what merely happens to fit it — plus anywhere the machinery is loose enough that a quite different theory would license it just as well. Then tell me what would have to change, procedurally rather than rhetorically, if the efficiency reading were adopted in earnest, and what thier would be if the bargain reading were.
