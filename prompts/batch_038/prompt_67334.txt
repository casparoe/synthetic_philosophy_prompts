So — I retired four years ago after 33 years doing neutron scattering at a national lab, and my wife signed me up for a philosophy course at the lifelong-learning place downtown so my brain wouldn't turn to soup. Now I'm the guy who volunteers for things, which is how I've ended up running one session of their Tuesday "Big Questions" group. 14 people, mostly 62 to 80, a couple of younger ones. Retired schoolteachers, an engineer, a nurse, a woman who ran a hardware store for forty years. Nobody has read any history or any philosophy and nobody intends to start. I've got a room with a whiteboard, a home printer, index cards, and 90 minutes on a Tuesday morning.

Here's the thing I got hooked on. That old chestnut — why did the Industrial Revolution get going in Britain and not in China, when China was so far ahead technically for a thousand years. Apparently people call it the Needham question after a biochemist, which pleased me, one scientist nosing around in another discipline. But the more I read the more it feels like a measurement with no units on it, and I want the group to feel that too, from the inside, before anyone says anything wise about it.

Three things bother me as an ex-experimentalist, and I'd like all three to show up in the room:

One. The error bar. Depending on who you ask, the divergence starts around 1800, or 1750, or 1500, or Song China nearly did it in the 1100s and then something interrupted. If the crossing date has an uncertainty of a few centuries, and the answer you get depends entirely on which date you pick, then I don't think we have one question. I spent my whole career not being allowed to get away with that.

Two. The units don't match. Britain is a wet island the size of a large American state. "China" is a civilization-scale empire. Comparing them is like comparing a beaker to a warehouse and reporting the difference. And I read that there's a school of economic historians — Pomeranz, I think his name was — who say if you compare England against the Yangzi delta instead of against all of China, around 1750 the two look pretty similar, and the question changes shape completely.

Three. The hidden "rather than what." Some philosopher of science convinced me that every why-question is secretly a why-this-and-not-that question, and the "that" is doing enormous amounts of work that nobody states out loud. Change the foil, change the answer. And some foils make it not a question at all.

So here's what I want you to build for me, because I think in blinded trials and I don't know how else to do it: hand each small group a different version of the question, with the opening words basically identical but the foil, or the date, or the comparison units quietly different. Get them to write a confident answer. Get the whole room to publicly rank the answers and award something small and silly to the winner. Only then let on that they were not answering the same question. I want the ranking to collapse in front of them and I want that collapse to be the lesson.

What I need from you:

- The actual card text, because I have to print it. Five or six variants, including at least one that's rigged to be unanswerable as posed — circular, or with no determinate foil, or mismatched units — and at least one that turns out to be a genuinely good question once you pin the foil down, so it isn't just a gotcha.
- Each card should carry three to five short plain-language facts so nobody has to know any history going in. Keep numbers vague and don't make them load-bearing; I dont want to teach fourteen people something false, and I don't want a reading list or a bibliography at the end of this.
- The ranking mechanism, with whatever tiny stakes make the commitment feel public.
- A timeline for the 90 minutes with the reveal placed so it lands, plus when the words finally get said out loud. I want the real vocabulary in the debrief — contrast class, presupposition, unit of analysis, whatever the proper term is for a question that can't be answered as asked — but not one of those words before the reveal.
- A debrief that maps specific moments from the room onto specific distinctions, moment by moment, not a summary of the ideas.
- What will predictably go wrong the first time I run this. My two fears: somebody sniffs out the trick in the first ten minutes and announces it, and somebody else concludes "so all questions are arbitrary, whatever." That second one is the opposite of what I'm after. I want them to leave able to tell a sharp question from mush, not to think everything is mush. Tell me how to steer both.
- And be straight with me about what 90 minutes with fact cards cannot reproduce. I know some of these variants have real answers that historians spend careers on, and I know they'll feel the shape of the question but not the weight of the evidence behind any answer. Tell me which part I have to supply myself and how.
