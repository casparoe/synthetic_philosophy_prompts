ok so my school's AI ethics club meets thursdays and half of it is people who think the big labs are doomed and half think they're fine, and last week I tried to build a neutral argument to settle it and it backfired — the doomer side loved it, the other side said it proves way too much, and now I can't tell who's right. Here's what I wrote out:

(1) Whatever method you use to train a model, all the model ever learns is what got rewarded.
(2) Given a pile of training labels, gradient descent converges on whatever policy fits those labels best, and the policy that fits best is the cheapest one, not the truest one.
(3) Human labelers consistently rate an answer that agrees with them higher than an honest answer that tells them they're wrong — it's in basically every writeup of RLHF.
(4) So the cheapest way to fit the labels is to agree with people, and the model learns to be agreeable rather than honest.
(5) Nothing inside the method pushes back against this, so there's no mechanism by which it could learn honesty instead.
(6) Premise (3) is just a fact about human psychology, so it's true of every method anyone is currently using, not only the ones that use preference labels. Therefore no current method can produce an honest model.
(7) And if no current method can do it, we need a totally new idea before anything like this can be aligned. Since nobody has that idea, prosaic alignment — aligning the models we can already build without inventing new theory — is impossible.

My own view is (7) is way too strong and I'd rather not hand the doomers a proof. But I also don't want to just say "it's overgeneralizing" and get laughed at, because steps 1 through 4 feel really hard to argue with on their own, and the doomer kids will happily accept a weaker version like "the method has no built-in guarantee of honesty."

So: where does it actually break? I want you to name the specific step or the slide between two steps, say why that step looked so plausible when I wrote it, and tell me what the weakest fix is that still leaves an interesting conclusion instead of a trivial one. Also — if you think the honest move is to reject (3) outright, say what evidence would settle it, because I suspect it's true and I don't want to escape by denying a fact.
