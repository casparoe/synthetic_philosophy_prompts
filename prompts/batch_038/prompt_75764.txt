I TA a grad-level decision theory seminar and we're spending a week on dynamic choice — money pumps, transitivity, whether you need diachronic requirements on top of the synchronic ones. The trouble is the standard money pump story is useless for that week: the agent has cyclic preferences at a single moment, gets walked around the cycle, loses a penny a turn, and everyone in the room agrees the diagnosis is just "intransitivity is bad." Nobody has to think. I want to hand them a case where that diagnosis is off the table and they're forced to argue about whether there's still a rational failure left over.

So: construct a thought experiment in which an agent gets pumped — a run of small trades she accepts one at a time that leaves her back where she started minus real money — but at no single moment does she have intransitive or incomplete preferences. The cycle has to live across time rather than inside any moment: her ranking of the very same options genuinely reverses as the payoffs get nearer, and each trade she takes is strictly better than declining by her own lights at the instant she takes it. Constraints:

- one paragraph for the scenario, then the analysis; ordinary setting (a savings app, a delivery subscription, a freelancer chasing invoices), stakes in the tens of dollars, no demons, no neurosurgeons, no exotic physics;
- give the preference structure explicitly, not just the verdicts. I want a discounting rule or some equivalent description from which the reversals and the local dominance of each trade actually follow. If you have to just assert "on Tuesday she prefers this," the case is cheating and I'll know;
- she knows the whole sequence in advance, fee schedule included, so nothing turns on surprise or ignorance;
- she had a chance to precommit at the start (block the app, set an automatic rule) and didn't take it, and her not taking it has to be locally rational at that moment as well — not weakness of will, its not a mistake she knows she's making. She should never act against her own all-things-considered judgment at any node;
- every node is one she can walk away from.

Then show me the thing works. I want a step-by-step ledger: her ranking at each node, why the trade dominates there, and the running total, ending in the net loss. Check the transitivity constraint honestly — spell out her ordering at two or three of the moments and confirm nothing cycles within a moment. Say exactly where the money goes, given that no moment contains an intransitivity, and tell me what happens to the case if you swap her non-stationary discounting for a stationary one: does it collapse entirely, or does some residue survive, and if it survives, is it still a pump? After that, deal with the standard blocking moves — the sophisticated choice reply and the planning/disposition reply — and say whether either actually blocks this one given that precommitment was available and declined for locally good reasons. And give me the best attack a "synchronic rationality is all there is" theorist has on my setup, not a strawman.

Last thing: I want a verdict, not a menu of options. Tell me whether she's irrational, and if so at which moment or moments the failure is located — is it the first trade, the refusal to precommit, every trade, or the whole sequence considered as one extended object of assessment — and give your reasons for pinning it there. If your honest view is that nothing anywhere goes wrong and the sequence just happens to end badly, say that and defend it against the obvious objection that she foreseeably made herself poorer for nothing while remaining free to stop at every step.
