I am a professor of philosophy at a small college, and on Saturday I must deliver the eulogy for Judge Adeline Marchetti, who died last month at eighty-two after twenty-nine years on her state's intermediate appellate court. We met as freshmen in a required logic course in 1961 and remained close for the rest of her life; she asked me to do this, which her family considered an eccentric choice and which I now consider a trap. I would be grateful for your help in springing it.

What you need to know about her work, since it is the difficulty. Adeline was a textualist of a particularly unsentimental stripe. She held that a statute's words carry the meaning they had in ordinary usage at the time of enactment, read in the circumstances of their enactment; that a court may consult those circumstances but may not consult the legislature's further aims in order to displace what the words actually say; that where the text produces a gap or a hardship, the gap belongs to the legislature and the hardship is a reason to write to one's representative, not a reason to rewrite the law; and that this is a doctrine about the limits of her own authority rather than a theory about language in general. Before the bench she spent nine years drafting bills in the state legislature's counsel's office, and she used to say that she had watched purposes get traded away in committee and knew better than to look for them afterward. She was not a fool about it: she insisted repeatedly, and at length, that textualism is not context-blind, that the words of an utterance take their content from the situation of the utterance, and that what the doctrine forbids is the reader's substitution of his own purposes for the writer's. Everyone remembers her lone dissent in *Rourke v. Board of Assessors*, a case I am inventing for you here, in which a widow's application for a homestead exemption was postmarked March 14, reached the assessor on March 16, and was rejected under a statute requiring filing "on or before the fifteenth day of March," the office having been closed on the fifteenth. The majority read the deadline as directory. Adeline would not, and wrote, in a line I am also inventing and which you may alter or discard: the court had made the calendar kinder, but it had not been the court's calendar to make. The legislature fixed the statute two sessions later, which she regarded as the system working and her critics regarded as a confession.

The room. Roughly a hundred and forty people at her parish, the rector officiating; I speak between the opening collects and the psalm, so I have no liturgical latitude and I am not the presider. In the front row are her daughter Nora, who last year told me — not unkindly, and to my face — that her mother would rather have been right about a comma than good to a person, and Nora's two sons, aged eleven and fourteen. In the second row is retired Justice Emmett Doyle, eighty-eight, who wrote the *Rourke* majority and who has no idea that he is being seated within earshot of a vindication. Also present: four of her former clerks, now judges and law professors, who will recognize every technical thing I do; a delegation from the court; and her neighbors and the congregation, most of whom knew her only as the woman who cooked for and then ran the parish's Tuesday soup line for thirty years. Half this room will catch an interpretive-theory reference; the other half will not know what a dissent is, and neither half may be made to feel excluded or lectured.

The constraint she set. Two winters ago, over whiskey, she said that if I ever eulogized her I was to do it on her own terms and not purposively construe her: no supplying a rationale for what she left unexplained, no telling the room what she "really meant" when she had declined to say it, no converting the silences into evidence of a deeper intention. There are such silences. She never spoke of the end of her first marriage; she never said why she dissented alone in *Rourke* rather than joining a narrower concurrence that was available to her; her letters to me for sixty years were four pages long and contained no statement of feeling whatever. A conventional eulogy fills gaps exactly like these, and mine may not.

So: write the eulogy. It must comfort a church full of people, several of whom are grieving badly, and it must honor a constraint that appears to make comforting them impossible — for the honest way to answer Nora's charge seems to require explaining motives Adeline forbade me from imputing. I will tell you that I believe the distinction she herself drew, between the circumstances of an utterance and the purposes imposed upon it, does more work here than she ever claimed, but I want you to find the turn rather than be handed it, and I do not want it to be a cheat: the eulogy must not simply declare that love is an admissible context, must not have her recant or soften on her deathbed, must not have me declare her wrong, must not resolve the debate between her and Doyle, and must not become a seminar with a body in it. The doctrine should shape what the speech is permitted to say, not serve as its subject; a listener who has never read a judicial opinion should hear a particular and moving portrait of one woman. Include exactly one moment of levity, and make it intelligible to the eleven-year-old. Invent any words of hers you need, consistent with the character above, but attribute nothing to any real person.

Please write in American English, and do not include citations, references to real cases, statutes, or jurists, or suggestions for further reading; everything here is invented and I would rather you invent freely than guess. Organize your reply in exactly these three sections, in this order:

1. **The Eulogy** — the complete text, 1,100 to 1,300 words, as continuous prose to be read aloud, with paragraph breaks and no stage directions embedded in it.
2. **What the Constraint Permitted** — 250 to 350 words. Identify the three or four places where an ordinary eulogy would have supplied a purpose or filled a silence, say what you did there instead and why it is not a violation, and flag honestly any sentence you regard as borderline or as straining against what she asked of me.
3. **Reading It Aloud** — about 150 words. Where to stop and wait, which sentences are addressed to Nora and which to the second row, where to look up from the page, and the one place, if any, where you think I should depart from your text depending on how the room is behaving.
