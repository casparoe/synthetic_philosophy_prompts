I serve on the allocation committee of a joint computational research institute owned in equal shares by two partner organizations, a hospital system and a genomics center. Once a year we divide a fixed block of cluster capacity between the two partners' scientific programs, and for the past several cycles the division has been settled by haggling. I have proposed that this cycle we adopt a rule instead: treat the division as a two-person bargaining problem and take the Kalai–Smorodinsky solution, that is, the point on the Pareto frontier at which each partner's gain over its breakdown position bears the same proportion to its maximum possible gain. Two complications have surfaced, and I would rather they be on the table before we vote than after.

The first concerns the ideal points. The genomics center's submission claims a utility ceiling premised on a population-scale screening project that would require roughly four times the capacity we control, has no data-use approval, and was declined by its own external funder in the spring. The hospital's submission counts a workload that its steering committee defunded in March. Because the ideal point enters the arithmetic directly rather than merely bounding it, these fictions move the recommended split. A colleague on the committee says this is precisely the known weakness of the rule: it gives up independence of irrelevant alternatives, so adding an aspirational option to the feasible set changes the answer, whereas the Nash product would not budge. I take the point, but I also recall why we wanted to move away from Nash-style reasoning in the first place, namely that it can leave a partner worse off when its opportunities genuinely expand.

The second concerns the breakdown position. The hospital holds a standing commercial cloud contract; the genomics center has nothing comparable, so a breakdown would stall its program outright. Taken literally, that asymmetry gives the hospital a much higher floor and therefore a larger share of the surplus. But the contract was purchased with clinical revenue rather than institute funds, and by the hospital's own figures it delivers about sixty percent of the throughput at three times the marginal cost.

The committee votes in a fortnight, and there are six other members. Please draft the memo I will circulate to them. It must recommend one of three things: adopt the rule as written; adopt it subject to an explicitly stated restriction on how ideal points and breakdown positions are to be computed, in which case say exactly what the restriction is and how it would be applied to this year's two submissions; or abandon the rule for this cycle and specify what we should do instead. State the recommendation in your opening sentences and then give the reasoning, of a kind that a member who disagrees could actually argue with, including the strongest honest case for the position you reject rather than a caricature of it. Where the arithmetic matters, indicate roughly what the split would come out to under each variant and identify which features of the two submissions are doing the work. Write it as continuous prose, in the manner of a letter to colleagues: no headings, no bullet points, no boldface, no tables, and no citations or suggested reading, since what I need from you is the argument itself.
