So, slightly unusual request coming from a journalist rather than a teacher, but bear with me. I cover automation and labor for a general-interest magazine, and for about two years now I've been sitting on a half-formed idea I keep failing to land in print. Roughly: when you hand part of a job to a machine, the human's role doesn't shrink, it changes kind — from doing the thing to watching the thing get done. And watching is the one skill you get worse at precisely by exercising it, because the practice you get is all in the routine 95% the system handles fine, while you're only ever judged on the 5% it can't handle, which is by construction the part you've never rehearsed. I've written this sentence in three different articles and it always reads like a shrug. There's an old paper — Bainbridge, ironies of automation, early 80s — that makes something like the point, but nobody at a live event wants a citation. What I actually want is for people to feel it in their stomachs and then be told what they just felt.

The occasion: our magazine is running a public ideas weekend and someone has given me a 75-minute workshop slot for about 25 adults, total mixed bag — two ICU nurses, a longshoreman, a couple of grad students, two guys who run a small freight brokerage, some retirees, a few people who've been made redundant and are not shy about it. Nothing assigned in advance, no laptops for participants, and I need it to run on index cards, a whiteboard, a phone timer, and me. Design the game. I want a task with a real score — points, not money — that people can do competently by hand for a round or two, so they build up a feel for it and a little pride. Then the automated system arrives: a prewritten decision rule on a card, or a script, or volunteers playing the system, whatever you think actually works, and it should be right most of the time, not all of the time. The failure that matters has to come without warning and be diagnosable only by someone who still understands the underlying task, so that the teams who leaned on the system get a clean, undeniable, non-humiliating experience of getting it wrong and not knowing how they got it wrong. Nobody should be a plant and nobody should be made to look stupid in front of strangers — the game should lose against them, not against each other.

There's a second manipulation I've been toying with and I want your honest read on it. In the early manual rounds, every case has a person attached: a name, a one-line situation, something you have to read out loud before you decide. Once the system comes in, the same cases get stripped to numbers and categories because that's what the system can ingest. My suspicion is that deskilling and depersonalisation are two separate injuries and that a game like this will mush them into one undifferentiated bad feeling, which would actually undercut the thing I'm trying to show. So either give me a design that keeps them separable — maybe the order of rounds, maybe a condition where the names come back — or tell me to cut one and say which.

Then the debrief, which is where I usually flail. Map the specific moments of the game onto the distinction, tell me how much of it I'm allowed to explain before they've played versus what has to stay hidden until after, and give me the line where they learn there's a name for this. I also want the operational stuff: team size, round lengths that add up to 75 minutes including discussion, exactly what's on the cards, how scoring works so I'm not adjudicating 25 disputes, and what predictably goes wrong the first time I run it with a room I don't know — the longshoreman who refuses to trust the card, the team that games the score, the round that ends before anyone gets attached to the work. And be blunt about which feature of the idea the game structurally cannot reproduce, the part I'm going to have to supply by talking because cards and points can't do it. My guess is it's something about the time scale — real deskilling takes years and I have an hour — but tell me if the bigger hole is somewhere else.
