I am a postdoctoral fellow in a neuroethics group, and next month I am due to circulate a short position statement to an interdisciplinary reading group — two cognitive neuroscientists who run volition experiments, two legal scholars working on neuroscientific evidence in criminal law, and a philosopher of action who is, frankly, the one I am most afraid of. Before I send it around, I would like a consistency audit of the underlying package of views, because I suspect I have assembled it from several different arguments without checking that the pieces interlock. Please take on the role of an expert in the philosophy of action and the neuroscience of volition who is equally at home with the metaphysics of moral responsibility and with the methodological disputes surrounding the Libet paradigm. Think carefully, and work through the commitments pairwise on scratch paper before you write anything; a brief note on which pairs you considered is welcome, but the finished product should be an audit of my position, not a survey of the literature, and not a general lecture on compatibilism, which the group already knows inside out.

Here is the package, as I would state it publicly. (1) I accept the standard reading of Libet's timing results: the readiness potential onsets roughly 550 milliseconds before the movement, while the reported awareness of the urge to move arrives only about 200 milliseconds before it, so for on the order of three hundred and fifty milliseconds the act is already under way in the brain before the subject experiences anything that could be called deciding. (2) I also accept the deflationary reanalysis of the readiness potential — that the signal is largely what one gets by averaging backwards from movement over spontaneously fluctuating, slowly drifting neural activity, so that on any given trial there was no determinate unconscious decision waiting to be detected, only stochastic accumulation that happened to cross a threshold. (3) I do not think (2) rescues anything: substituting chance threshold-crossing for covert unconscious initiation is a downgrade for authorship rather than an upgrade, since a movement that emerges from noise is at least as alien to me as one that issues from a causal chain I did not start. (4) I hold a source view of responsibility rather than a leeway view: what matters is not whether I could have done otherwise in the exact same circumstances, but whether the springs of the action trace back to me in the right way; if factors wholly outside my control fully determine what I do, I am not its responsible author, however unimpeded I feel. (5) Following Libet's own fallback, I hold that the veto is sufficient to save responsibility: even if every act is initiated subpersonally, an ordinary adult retains the capacity to abort an initiated act during the final hundred to two hundred milliseconds, and exercising that capacity is a genuine exercise of agency — I have been calling my view terminal veto theory, and it is the label I want on the handout. (6) I think the reported-urge timings are the weakest link in the whole paradigm, because the clock task invites subjects to smear the onset of their urge and to reconstruct its timing after the fact, and I would not defend any of the figures in (1) to more than one significant digit. (7) I nonetheless maintain that we have reliable first-person access to the fact that we sometimes stop ourselves — the content of the veto experience, as opposed to its clock position, is not up for grabs. To force the issue, I also use a case of my own in the handout: the Silent Passenger, an agent who cannot initiate anything whatsoever, every act of hers being begun by subpersonal processes she cannot influence, but who is aware of each impending act in good time and retains an unimpaired power to abort any of them, which she exercises on exactly the grounds you and I would — she lets go of what she judges permissible and stops what she judges wrong. I want to say she is a responsible agent, praise- and blameworthy for what she allows, and I want my stated principles to deliver that verdict rather than merely to coexist with it.

What I need from you is an ordered audit, roughly a thousand words, that I can read in ten minutes. Rank the tensions you find by severity, most serious first, and do not present them as a flat list of equally worrying items; for each one, say precisely which commitment has to be dropped or weakened, what the weakened version looks like, and what that weakening costs me elsewhere in the package. Then set apart the pairs that only look contradictory and say why they dissolve on inspection — I expect there to be at least one, and I would rather you argued me out of a worry than manufactured one. After that, rule explicitly on the Silent Passenger using my own principles, and tell me whether (4) and (5) can jointly deliver the verdict I want her to get, or whether the case in fact drives a wedge between them; if it drives a wedge, say which side of it my position ends up on. Close by identifying the single revision that would repair the most damage at the least cost, and tell me in one or two sentences what I would then have to say to the reading group about the view I am left holding. Please do not give me a bibliography, a reading list, or any citations — I cannot verify them and they will only clutter the audit; if you want to attach a name to a move, do it in passing and spend your words on the argument itself.
