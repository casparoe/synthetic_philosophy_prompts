For the past several months I have been keeping a list, and I would like to know whether the things on it actually belong together. The list is of experiences I am willing to call pleasures. I am not a student of philosophy and I have not read the arguments; I have simply written things down as they happened and marked, honestly, whether I would count each one. Here is what I have.

Cold water after a morning of work outdoors in July: a pleasure, and one of the strongest on the list. The identical glass of water twenty minutes later, once I have drunk my fill: not a pleasure, faintly unpleasant, though nothing about the water has changed.

Scratching a mosquito bite: while I am doing it, among the keenest pleasures I know. But if you offered me that same scratching sensation with no bite underneath it, I would refuse it as worthless — and I would refuse it even though the feeling, so far as I can tell, would be the same. These two verdicts sit side by side in my notebook and I cannot get them to fit.

A particular piece of music that reliably brings tears to my eyes: I rank it among the greatest pleasures available to me, though the feeling it produces would be entered as pain in any other column of the list.

The burn of a chili pepper hot enough to make my eyes water: I call it a pleasure and keep going back for it, although if I described the sensation to a doctor without mentioning that I had chosen it, he would write down pain.

A free afternoon with nothing demanded of me, mildly agreeable from start to finish, uneventful enough that I would not remember it a week later: I do not want to call this a pleasure, and yet nothing else on the list is so completely free of discomfort.

Three hours of absorbed work — measuring, fitting, correcting, fitting again — during which I am aware of nothing at all, and after which I say that this was one of the most enjoyable parts of my week.

Learning that a man who cheated a friend of mine out of her savings has lost his job: a genuine pleasure in the moment of hearing it, and one I am ashamed of, though the shame arrives afterward and does not seem to have been part of it.

The first cigarette of the day for a man who is trying to quit: a pleasure he would sincerely deny is a pleasure, and I find myself agreeing with me against him.

Thinking back over a good meal a week after eating it: the memory is pleasant, but I have not put it on the list, and I am not sure why not.

My own tentative explanation was that pleasure is relief — the easing of some lack or tension — and that it therefore always presupposes something disagreeable to be relieved of. That handles the water and the bite and the cigarette. It plainly does not handle the music, which I do not listen to in order to be relieved of anything, nor the absorbed afternoon of work, nor the free afternoon that I refuse to count despite its being the most relief-like state on the list. So I have given up on it, or nearly.

What I want is the account that stands behind my verdicts, if there is one. Please state it precisely rather than gesture at it, and say which of the cases above does the work of motivating each part of it. Then tell me plainly which of my verdicts it cannot keep: I expect at least one has to be surrendered, and I would like to know which one you would bet against and what I lose by giving it up. I am especially interested in whether the scratching case and the chili case can both be preserved, since they seem to pull in opposite directions on whether a pleasure needs a bad state underneath it.

I would also like to know what the account says about three situations that are not on my list. A cow lying in the sun, apparently entirely content: is she pleased, and does it matter to the answer that she could not tell you so? A pill that produced in me exactly the feeling of cold water on a July morning, with no thirst beforehand and no glass in my hand: does taking it give me a pleasure, a counterfeit of one, or something else again? And a man who is warmed all afternoon by a piece of good news that turns out at dinner to have been a mistake: was it a pleasure while it lasted, and if so, what exactly was it a pleasure in?

Finally, I would like an answer to the question underneath all of this. Either pleasure is one thing that shows up in all these situations, in which case I want to be told what that thing is and how it survives the cases that seem to contradict it; or it is a collection of different things wearing one word, in which case I want to be told what holds the collection together and why it is a family rather than a coincidence. If you think some of the items do not belong on the list at all — if, say, the free afternoon or the absorbed work has been miscategorized by me — say so and explain what I was tracking instead.

Please write in American English, keep the technical vocabulary to what you define as you go, and give me the reasoning rather than a list of books or names to look up.
