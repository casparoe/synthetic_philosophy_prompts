Last month I spent most of a Saturday evening arguing with an old friend about whether a generous welfare state can survive open borders, and we ended the night precisely where we began. Her position is that the solidarity objection is either a falsifiable empirical claim that has been tested and failed, or a decorous way of saying that we would rather not share with strangers. Mine is that redistribution is not merely a transfer mechanism but a practice sustained by a sense of a bounded "we" whose members treat one another's claims as reciprocally owed, so that a purely fiscal accounting of what newcomers net contribute or cost misses the thing actually at issue. I suspect we were both guilty of asserting our conclusions instead of exhibiting them, which is why I want my students to run into the structure of the disagreement rather than inherit either verdict.

I teach a second-year political philosophy seminar of 18 students, meeting once for 80 minutes. They will have had one lecture on distributive justice and no assigned reading on migration. Design an in-class game — a repeated contribution game with genuine decisions and a payoff scheme that requires no money, only paper, tokens, or a whiteboard — that makes them feel the difference between two questions this debate routinely collapses into one: whether an additional entrant is a net contributor to the common pool, and whether admitting entrants alters the parameters on which everyone's willingness to contribute in the first place depends. The design requirement that matters most is that the second question must emerge from the rules rather than from anything I say aloud: participants should watch their own group's contribution rate move for reasons that have nothing to do with the arithmetic of the pool, and then notice that the responsible mechanism was itself something they chose and could have chosen otherwise.

Please supply: full rules and a round-by-round structure with timings; roles, including at least one that lets the group fix its own terms of redistribution before anyone knows who will be affected by them; a materials list; the payoff scheme; a debrief that maps particular moments in play onto the components of the argument and withholds the vocabulary until afterwards, including whatever name is appropriate for the claim that open borders, generous redistribution, and a demos capable of authorizing that redistribution are in tension; two rule modifications a critic of my position could propose to test whether the pessimistic outcome is an artifact of my design rather than a feature of the world, with a prediction of what each modification does; and an honest account of what the activity cannot reproduce and I will have to supply myself — including that the erosion parameter is stipulated by the facilitator rather than discovered by the players, and that real migrants are not exogenous shocks but agents with reasons of their own.

Two further requests. Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this and how to handle it in the moment. And tell me what I should be able to say — without overclaiming and without dismissing the question — to the student who has read something about whether ethnic heterogeneity depresses support for redistribution and wants the matter settled on the evidence.
