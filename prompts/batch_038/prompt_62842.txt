I'm trying to write up where I've actually landed on religious language before my reading group picks it apart, and I'd rather find out now whether the package holds together than discover it doesn't in the room. Here's the list, roughly in the order I hold them:

1. I take on board the Wittgensteinian point that a religious belief isn't a hypothesis. You wouldn't describe the believer as having weighed up the likelihood of God, and a change in the evidence wouldn't dislodge them — that's a feature of the belief, not a defect in it. I've been reading On Certainty lately and I'm tempted to add that these beliefs work like hinge propositions: outside the game of doubting and justifying, but holding the whole system up.

2. At the same time I think the resurrection is essentially historical for a Christian. Not "we'd be sad if it hadn't happened" — more that it's the whole edifice that would come down. Same sort of thing Paul says about preaching being empty if Christ wasn't raised.

3. I'm a realist about it. "God exists" is literally true, not metaphorical or expressive, and its truth doesn't depend on anyone's attitudes or way of life.

4. But I also want to say God isn't an item in the universe alongside the others, so "God exists" isn't a claim of the same kind as "Neptune exists", and the religious picture and the scientific one never actually collide.

5. What makes somebody a believer is what they do — praying, confessing, living as answerable — not an inner assent you could extract and inspect. Someone could be fully in the practice and hopeless at the metaphysics, and I'd still call them religious.

6. Even so, when a convinced atheist and I disagree I dont think we're just living seperate lives. I think they're wrong, and "wrong" isn't shorthand for "not to my taste".

7. I don't think there's any neutral standpoint from which to referee between the two pictures. You see the point of it from inside or you don't.

8. But I do think I could sometimes give a thoughtful atheist reasons that actually move them. Conversion isn't purely upbringing or grace.

9. One case verdict to keep me honest: when a friend left the faith over the problem of evil, I want to say she had already stopped practising and the argument arrived later as the story she told herself. But I also want to say the problem of evil is a genuine intellectual difficulty that deserves an answer, not a diagnosis of her spiritual state.

So: which of these genuinely pull against each other, and which only look like they do? Rank the conflicts by severity rather than handing me a flat list where everything is equally bad, and for each one say what has to give and what the cheapest fix costs me. Then tell me which single revision would clean up the most.

Do commit to a verdict — I'm not after a diplomatic survey in which every pair turns out to be reconcilable on a charitable reading, unless that's really the truth of it. And if you think the exercise is misconceived as posed — if I'm running two different notions of literal together between (3) and (4), say, or the hinge idea in (1) isn't available to someone who also holds (8) — tell me that plainly instead of politely auditing the list the way I've framed it. British English, please.
