A long-form magazine piece I read last week sums up Langdon Winner's 1980 essay Do Artifacts Have Politics? in the following terms: 'Winner showed that technological determinism applies to politics as much as to economics. Once an artefact is built, the social arrangements it demands follow automatically, so engineers wield political power whether or not they intend to. His famous case is the low overpasses Robert Moses had built across the Long Island parkways: too low for the buses that carried poor and Black New Yorkers, they excluded those citizens from Jones Beach without any law or sign having to say so. Winner generalises from this that every artefact has a politics, since every design decision settles who can reach what. He further argues that certain technologies are inherently political in the strict sense — nuclear power simply cannot be administered democratically, while solar power cannot fail to be decentralising. That is why the question of whether a recommender system is biased is a technical matter, fixed at design time and not reopenable by auditing or public consultation.'

I came to the piece already inclined to think that design choices are always political, and I now suspect it is simply telling me what I wanted to hear. How much of the summary is defensible? Please take it claim by claim, separating what Winner actually argued from fair simplification and from distortion — in particular whether the determinist framing and the strict necessity claims about nuclear and solar power are his, and whether the conclusion about algorithms survives once the rest has been corrected.
