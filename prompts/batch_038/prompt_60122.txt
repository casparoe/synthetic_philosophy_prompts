I retired four years ago after thirty-eight years as a physical oceanographer, most of them spent building coarse-resolution models of ocean heat transport. Philosophy has become a late hobby, and I approach it as I approached any new field: slowly, and with a standing suspicion of slogans. Recently I have been reading Rudolf Carnap, and one strand of his work has taken hold of me in a way I did not anticipate.

I mean the principle of tolerance, which Carnap introduced explicitly in the early 1930s and stated most famously in *The Logical Syntax of Language* (1934). Its content, roughly: when two parties work within different linguistic frameworks — different systems of rules fixing what counts as a well-formed statement and what counts as a valid inference — there is no question of fact as to which framework is the correct one. Frameworks are instruments, adopted and compared only by how well they serve the purposes we bring to them. Relatedly, in his 1950 essay *Empiricism, Semantics, and Ontology*, Carnap distinguishes internal questions, which are asked and answered within a framework and can be settled empirically or by proof, from external questions, which look like inquiries into whether the framework's entities really exist but which, taken seriously, can only be practical proposals about whether to adopt the framework at all.

What I cannot shake is how little consolation this offers when I set it beside the other half of Carnap's world. Several of his circle, Carnap included, were socialists, and the unity-of-science programme was for them in part a political project. His closest collaborator, Otto Neurath, argued for years that a planned economy could and should dispense with money and calculate in kind — in tonnes, kilowatt-hours, hectares, hours of labour — on the grounds that only physical magnitudes could express what a society actually requires. That, too, is a choice of framework. And it is a choice on which people's survival turns.

Here is what I should like you to produce.

**Your role.** For this task you are a philosopher of science with specialist knowledge of logical empiricism who is also an accomplished writer of literary short fiction: economical, concrete, closer to Borges or Le Guin than to a novelist who explains himself. Think carefully before writing. Do not settle for the obvious allegory — a story in which two bureaucrats argue about metaphysics will read as a lecture wearing a costume, and I have read enough of those.

**Step one — brainstorm.** Before any prose, give me three distinct premises for such a story, roughly sixty words each, each built on a different dramatic situation rather than a different setting. Then state which you choose and justify the choice in two sentences, naming the feature of Carnap's position that the chosen premise is best able to dramatize and the feature it will inevitably fail to dramatize.

**Step two — the story.** Write a short story of 1,800 to 2,600 words with a title, satisfying the following constraints:

1. Invent an institution charged with allocating something genuinely scarce. You may place it in a fictional country or an unnamed city, in the past or the near future. Do not use real historical figures, and do not attempt to reconstruct the actual socialist calculation debate; the interest lies in the structure of the disagreement, not in the history.
2. The institution must operate in one of two accounting languages — one that renders everything into a single monetary measure, one that admits only physical quantities — and the story must include the moment at which one of them is adopted, retained, or abandoned.
3. The dispute must be underdetermined by facts. Show the disputants in complete agreement about every matter of fact — a shared table of figures will do — so that the reader sees plainly that no further measurement could settle anything.
4. One reclassification must extinguish a particular person's claim on the common stock. Render it as paperwork, not as an illustration of an argument.
5. No character may lecture. The philosophy should live in forms, procedures, quarrels over what a given column of a ledger is permitted to contain, and in what people decline to say out loud. If anyone articulates the tolerance thesis at all, let it be a single sentence, and let it be rejected by another character for a bad reason that turns out to be a good one.
6. The ending must not resolve. I want a person obliged to choose with no tribunal standing above the choice, and a final movement that registers what is lost in each of the two options rather than in one of them.
7. Invent every document, memorandum, slogan, and line of dialogue. Quote no real text anywhere in your answer, and provide no bibliography, reading list, references, or footnotes.

One optional flourish, if you can manage it without announcing it: the same structure appears in my own old profession, where one decides which processes to resolve and which to parameterize, and where that decision settles in advance whether the model is capable of seeing the phenomenon at all. I would be glad to feel the echo. Do not, however, make the protagonist a scientist.

**Step three — author's note.** Close with a note of about 250 words, addressed to me as a reader rather than to a general audience. Say where the story's rendering of tolerance matches Carnap's view and where it distorts it for effect; take a position on the standing objection that tolerance collapses into a licence to adopt any framework whatsoever, and say frankly whether your story succumbs to that objection or escapes it; and identify one respect in which the fiction oversimplifies the relationship between framework choice and material consequence.
