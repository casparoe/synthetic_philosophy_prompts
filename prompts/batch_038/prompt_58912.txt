Next month my committee reads the central chapter of my dissertation, and my chair's judgment on a single claim will decide whether the chapter keeps its argument. Over three years of seminars I have accumulated her verdicts on what counts as an honest signal. The peacock's train: honest. The elongated outer tail feathers of the barn swallow: honest. Stotting in gazelles: honest, though she conceded it sits uneasily between a display and a demonstration. The low dominant frequency of a toad's advertisement call: reliable, but emphatically not a handicap, since a small male physically cannot produce it. The black bib of the house sparrow: cheap, and kept honest only because birds wearing a larger badge than they can defend get attacked. The fiddler crab's regenerated claw — same length, far weaker — flatly dishonest, and what fascinated her, she said, was how well it works anyway.

My own system is a lekking passerine whose males perform a wing display that, by my respirometry, consumes roughly a third of their daily energy budget and measurably shortens the breeding season. Females prefer it strongly. But last season's data produced three results: the energetic cost per bout is statistically indistinguishable between prime-condition and poor-condition males; essentially every male displays, at nearly the same intensity, including those who can least afford it; and display intensity is uncorrelated with the survival of the offspring sired. Nothing physically prevents a weak male from performing, and nothing punishes him for doing so.

Predict her verdict, and commit to one: honest handicap, reliable index, conventional badge, dishonest-but-effective, or something she would refuse to admit to the taxonomy. Her six verdicts are jointly consistent with more than one principle, and my case is where they come apart, so identify the candidate principles — cost as such guaranteeing reliability, cost falling differentially on quality, physical unfakeability, receiver-side enforcement, or one you think fits her record better — and say where they genuinely diverge here rather than merely redescribing the same outcome. Name the single further case she would raise that would settle which principle she is running. Then advise me: given your best guess at her principle, should the chapter claim honesty, claim a receiver bias carrying no information, or drop the question of the display's function altogether — and state what I would be conceding with each choice. I want a definite prediction with reasons, not a survey of the possibilities.
