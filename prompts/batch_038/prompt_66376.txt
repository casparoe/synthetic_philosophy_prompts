Working on an essay about what historical films and biopics owe the people they dramatize, and its stuck somewhere in the middle — I can't tell whether the machinery in the center is load-bearing or just decoration. Draft of the argument below (intro and ending are still TODO). Comments, please.

---

Begin with an asymmetry. A biopic solicits belief in a way a novel does not. Nobody assumes the dialogue in a novel was transcribed, so the novelist's inventions cost nobody anything; but the viewer of a film about a real person arrives with the genre's default setting switched on — what I'm watching happened, more or less. That default isn't a marketing trick, it's structural: the film gets its weight from the fact that the person existed. So the film spends credibility it never earned by argument, and the real question is what it's allowed to spend it on.

The standard framing — accuracy versus dramatic licence, licence tolerated up to some threshold — strikes me as wrong-headed, because it treats fidelity as a quantity. You can preserve every attested fact and still commit the distinctive wrong, and you can change a great deal and not commit it. So rather than sorting inventions into large and small, sort them by which gap in the record they're filling. Roughly:

(1) Events. Nothing attests that this meeting occurred; the film needs a scene, so it occurs.
(2) Speech. The meeting is attested; nothing records what anybody said in it. Every line is therefore invented — and this is by far the largest category in almost any historical film, which is exactly why it stops registering as invention at all.
(3) Motive. The action is attested, the reason for it isn't. The film shows us why, and usually shows us why in a way that makes the action intelligible — that is, in a way the record doesn't license.
(4) Persons. No such individual existed; the film hands us one as a witness, a servant, a colleague, a composite of three real people.

Here's the thesis I want to defend: what a film owes the dead isn't accuracy in any of the four, but non-foreclosure. The wrong isn't that the viewer is shown something that didn't happen; it's that the viewer is left unable to tell which kind of gap they're watching get filled, and so unable to hold the counterfactual open — unable to ask what the record would have said. A film that makes its joints visible doesn't thereby become less moving; it just stops converting its audience into unwitting believers. The obvious device is the seam: the character who admits she can't remember how the sentence ended, the scene that stops short of the motive, the composite signposted as a composite, the end-card that admits some scenes were imagined. On this view fidelity is a property of the film's epistemic posture, not of its content.

Two worries I haven't solved. First, seams seem available only where the record is thin in an ordinary way. For the recently dead, or for survivors of atrocity whose testimony is still contested in public, a visible seam may read as doubt cast on people who are alive and being believed or disbelieved right now — the film's honesty becomes a second injury. Second, non-foreclosure may be too weak a duty: it permits a film to invent a cowardly motive for a real person provided it flags the invention, and that person's descendants will take no comfort from the flag.
