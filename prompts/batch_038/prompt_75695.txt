I'm a third-year PhD student in political philosophy, and this term I'm co-convening a reading group on the ethics of taxation with a colleague from the economics department. After last week's session one of our members — a first-year, unusually quick — sketched the following argument on the board. Its stated target is the slogan that taxation is theft, but he now thinks it overreaches, and neither of us has found a clean exit. Here it is as he wrote it:

(1) An act is theft only if it violates an entitlement the victim has antecedently to, and independently of, the rules of the scheme under which the act is performed.
(2) There are no such antecedent entitlements: which bundle of holdings anyone is entitled to is fixed only by some scheme of property rules, and rival schemes fix rival bundles with nothing outside the schemes to adjudicate between them.
(3) Hence a scheme that taxes me takes nothing to which I was antecedently entitled; it partly constitutes what I am entitled to.
(4) So for any tax system whatever, the charge of theft is either false or lacks truth-apt content.
(5) But the same reasoning runs on takings generally: a robber who takes my wallet on a public street, an embezzler who drains a firm's accounts, and a legislature that confiscates ninety-nine percent of income and redistributes the proceeds at random all equally violate no antecedent entitlement, since in each case the content of entitlement is set by the rules in force.
(6) Therefore robbery is not theft, and no confiscatory scheme is criticisable on grounds of property right.
(7) (6) is intolerable: street robbery is the paradigm case of theft, and even a legitimate scheme seems subject to property-rights constraints.
(8) So (1) or (2) must go.

When I pressed him on (5) he offered a repair, which he dubbed the constitutor's exemption: the state differs from the robber in that the state is the agent whose activity constitutes the scheme in the first place, whereas the robber acts inside a scheme whose rules already fix my entitlement to the wallet — so the robber breaches a scheme-relative entitlement even though nobody breaches an antecedent one. I think the trouble lives in this repair, but I can't yet say why. My worry is that it either collapses back into (6) — for if the operative entitlements are just the scheme-relative ones, the constitutor can never be in breach of them and so can commit no property-rights wrong at all, which puts taxation and confiscation back on the same footing — or else it is unavailable to him in precisely the case he needs it for, since there the members of the scheme are formally its co-authors. To make that concrete I put a case of my own to him: a housing co-operative of some two hundred members whose general meeting writes the bylaws, sets the monthly dues, and spends them on the roof and the lift, so that the members are simultaneously authors of the dues rule and subjects of it, with no one outside the meeting whom one could describe as the constitutor. If the exemption requires a real gap between author and subject, it cannot be applied here; if it does not require such a gap, it cannot exclude (6).

So: which step is the first bad one, and is the argument as written valid, or does it trade on an equivocation? My instinct is that the pressure sits somewhere between (3) and (5), but I want that made precise rather than merely located. If the argument is invalid, give me the repair that makes it valid and say whether you can live with the repaired conclusion; if it is valid, tell me which premise is cheapest to surrender. Then price the alternatives: what does an entitlement theorist pay to keep (7), what does a theorist who denies any pre-institutional baseline pay to keep (2), and what does someone who thinks justice is practice-dependent pay — is there any position that gets to hold (2), the premise driving (5), and (7) all at once? I would rather give up (1) than (2), since I struggle to make sense of a pre-institutional moral baseline in holdings, but not at the cost of being unable to say that robbery is theft. Please do not answer by observing that everything turns on one's theory of property: tell me where the inference fails, or where the cost lies, and what each party must give up to avoid it.
