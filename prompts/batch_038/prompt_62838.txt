Six weeks ago I left an academic post in metaphysics for a knowledge-engineering group that maintains a hazard-classification database for industrial chemicals and battery materials. Nearly every substantive field in the schema is a dispositional claim — this substance is corrosive to steel, this electrolyte is thermally unstable, this powder is a sensitizer — and downstream users treat those fields as licensing conditionals: if it contacts moist steel, expect attack; if it passes this temperature, expect runaway. Our own data keeps defeating the conditionals. An inhibitor in the formulation suppresses an attack that would otherwise have occurred. A passivation layer forms on first contact and prevents the rest. Inert packaging blocks the manifestation entirely. A mixture partner changes the profile while the substance itself is untouched. My colleagues file all of this as data-quality exceptions and move on. I have come to think it is the central phenomenon rather than the noise around it, and that whatever semantics we adopt for the schema is an implicit answer to the question of what a disposition is.

Before I draft a proposal, I want the specification rather than the candidate theories. Write the desiderata: what any adequate account of dispositions has to deliver in order to be worth building on. Please do not give me references or further reading — I want the reasoning, not the literature.

Structure the response in exactly these sections, in this order:

**Bottom line up front** (~150 words). The three or four requirements that do the most work, the tension you take most seriously, and your one-sentence answer to whether a theory meeting all of them is available.

**What the theory is for** (~200 words). Restate the purpose as you understand it — an account good enough to underwrite a classification scheme whose entries stay true as surrounding conditions change — and tell me where that purpose distorts the general question, so I know which desiderata are load-bearing for me and which I could ignore.

**The desiderata** (~1,300 words). Numbered. For each: the requirement stated precisely enough that I could check a theory against it; the case that motivates it, drawn from both the familiar stock examples (the wire kept dead by a device that activates on contact, the fragile article packed in cushioning, the antidote already present in the bloodstream, the object with no such disposition that an interfering agent would break if it were struck) and, where you can manage it, my setting — if a stock case does not transfer to industrial materials, say so, since that is itself informative; my verdict on whether it is non-negotiable or a preference; and what a failure would look like in practice. Cover at least: the fink and mask phenomena and whatever asymmetry between them a theory owes us; the demand that the account explain why repackaging a substance leaves its dispositions intact while adding an inhibitor does not; graded and probabilistic manifestation; multi-track dispositions and the problem of specifying stimulus conditions; whether every disposition requires a categorical basis distinct from itself; whether dispositions must be intrinsic to their bearers or may be context-relative; whether dispositions do causal work or are displaced by whatever grounds them; and what blocks an account on which every property turns out to be dispositional all the way down.

**Sets that cannot be jointly satisfied** (~400 words). Identify any pair or triple of the above that you suspect no theory can meet at once, argue the incompatibility rather than assert it, and say which member should go and what is lost by dropping it. Then name the single requirement that knocks out the largest number of otherwise respectable views.

**Prejudices wearing the costume of requirements** (~300 words). Which things routinely demanded of a theory here are genuine constraints on dispositions, and which are artifacts of one particular research program — most obviously the program of analysing dispositions into conditionals, but also its critics. Explain why each of the bogus ones feels mandatory from inside the program that generates it.

**The contenders against the list** (~300 words). Briefly and by description of position rather than by name: the conditional analyses and their reformed descendants, the view that takes dispositions as irreducible features of the world, and any account that reaches for possibility or potentiality without routing through conditionals. Which survives the list best, and at what cost.

**What I should store instead** (~200 words). Given your conclusions: dispositions, manifestations, context-indexed conditionals, or something else — and what the honest semantics of my schema turns out to be.
