Three months ago I got moved sideways into the small team at my council that signs off on the risk models used in adult social care, and I've inherited an argument that predates me by about two years and has gone precisely nowhere. I've now been made the person who has to write the recommendation, so I need to understand what kind of stuck this actually is.

The setup. There's a triage score that ranks people coming through the front door so we know who gets an assessment within days and who goes on the waiting list. Two camps in the room.

Camp A, our data science lead: says calibration is the only defensible requirement, because a score is an estimate of a probability and it has to mean the same thing whichever group you're in. He points out that the incompatibility results from around 2016–17 show you can't also have equal false positive and false negative rates unless the base rates are equal or the prediction is perfect, so demanding both is asking for something maths rules out. His conclusion is that we make the score honest and let policy deal with the rest.

Camp B, the equalities lawyer and a couple of the practice leads: says calibration is a comfort blanket, because what a person experiences is the decision, not the score, and a perfectly calibrated score used with a threshold still flags roughly twice as many people from our most deprived wards, which is the thing the Equality Act is actually interested in. They add that the label is contaminated anyway — the outcome we calibrate against is whether a further crisis got recorded in the next twelve months, and whether a crisis gets recorded depends on who is already known to services. So we're calibrating to our own past behaviour.

There's also a third voice, a researcher seconded in, who says both sides are missing the point because fairness isn't a property of a score at all but of a whole decision process, and the metric war is a distraction from the fact that nobody has written down what the decision is for.

My hypothesis, which I'd like you to attack rather than flatter: this isn't really a technical disagreement, but nor is it just "values differ", because that phrase is being used as a conversation-stopper. I think the impossibility theorem has been conscripted as a veto. Camp A cite it as though a result about what can't be simultaneously true of a score settles what a public body ought to do, which looks to me like a category error; and Camp B tacitly accept that it has that force, which is why they spend all their energy on whether the premises hold rather than on whether the conclusion would follow from them. Both sides are letting one theorem do normative work it can't do. And I suspect there are three separate questions being run together under the single word fairness: one about the score as an estimate, one about the decision rule as a distribution of harms, one about the target variable as a measurement of anything real at all.

Second thing, and this one's mine. The theorem treats group base rates as given from outside. But our tool decides who gets an assessment, and assessments generate the recorded outcomes we retrain on next year. So the base rates are partly produced by the system, which would mean the impossibility isn't a static constraint but something with a time dimension that nobody in the room has engaged with. Is that a real objection or am I talking nonsense?

So: diagnose the disagreement. Is it verbal, about the word fairness? Is it about which thing is the proper object of justice — the estimate, the decision, the label? Is there a hidden empirical question doing all the work that could be settled with data we already have? Is it bedrock political commitments neither side can argue for? Or is the question simply ill-posed? Then tell me what would count as progress given your diagnosis — a distinction, a change of question, an experiment on our own referrals, a formal result — or argue that no progress should be expected and why. Please don't give me a survey of fairness definitions or a reading list, I've read the definitions and they aren't the problem. I need something I can take into a meeting on Thursday.
