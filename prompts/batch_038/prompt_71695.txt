I am the honorary secretary of a small charitable trust in Somerset that runs the village hall and the four acres around it. We are not a learned body: a retired builder, a former accountant, two teachers, a farmer and me, and I keep the minutes. Last autumn the trust received about £95,000 from selling a strip of the playing field to the highways authority, and deciding how to spend it turned into a longer task — writing a policy on how the trust should weigh the interests of the people who will use the hall in fifty years' time against the interests of the people using it now. It was that policy, not the money, that produced the argument I want to put to you.

I should say at once that I have no academic training in philosophy or economics, and that I have come to this material entirely on my own, in the evenings. I mention it only so that you will know I may not recognise a technical term unless you unpack it; I would rather you did not simplify the substance on my account.

The dispute was between Nadia, a retired geography teacher who joined the trust three years ago, and Frank, the former accountant, who has been a trustee for nineteen. I have set their exchange out turn by turn, because I was writing it down at the time and have gone back over the notes since. Neither of them was performing, and neither was being stupid, which is what has made it so hard for me to settle.

**Nadia (opening).** The policy should state that the trust applies no discount whatever to future benefit. A warm, dry, accessible hall in 2075 is exactly as much a good as one next month; the only difference is when it arrives, and a date on a calendar is not a property of a warm room. To shave a benefit because it falls later is to pretend that lateness is a defect. She added that she had read that Ramsey — the Cambridge mathematician whose paper on saving more or less founded the modern treatment of these questions — thought discounting the future indefensible and traced it to nothing better than a weakness of the imagination, and that she took this to be exactly right.

**Frank.** He accepted the premise and rejected the conclusion. The worth of a warm room in 2075 is not a function of the date, he agreed, but that is not what a discount rate claims. It claims something about the chain between the decision and the delivery. For the villagers of 2075 to receive anything from our £95,000, that money must survive fifty years of investment choices, of committees being replaced, of the deed being varied, of tax changes, and of the entirely live possibility that they will want something we cannot foresee and regard our gift as a nuisance. A pound promised in fifty years is a pound that has to survive fifty years of other people. The rate therefore prices the unreliability of delivery, not the worth of the recipient, and a rate of zero is coherent only if one assumes the chain never breaks — which is precisely what is in dispute. He called this the delivery-chain argument and returned to the name several times.

**Nadia.** If the rate really measures the delivery chain, then the trust is about to write the wrong thing into its policy, since a fact about our own competence is being smuggled in as a fact about the value of future lives. And she pressed the consequence: fragility differs between projects. A woodland established under a covenant registered at the Land Registry and binding on whoever owns the land next is very hard to unravel; an endowed pot of money that later trustees can raid or re-purpose is easy. If the rate tracks fragility, the covenant should carry a far lower rate than the endowment, and a single flat rate cannot say so. Frank must therefore either abandon the flat rate — in which case he is writing a risk assessment and has stopped discounting time — or admit that his rate is not tracking the chain after all.

**Frank.** He would vary the rate project by project happily and saw nothing awkward in that; it is what a sensible rate is for. But he made a burden-of-proof move. A rate of, say, three per cent is a modest claim that can be argued with and adjusted, whereas a rate of exactly zero is an extremely strong one, since it says a benefit fifty years off is to be treated as though it were already in hand. And Nadia had offered no rate, only the absence of one. Zero is a number too, and it wants a reason.

**Nadia.** Zero is not an estimate but a refusal to apply a haircut; the default is that value is value, and whoever proposes to shave it owes an account of why. In her reading there are only three answers ever given, and none is an answer about worth. The first is risk — but that is Frank's own delivery-chain point, and it belongs on a separate line of the policy. The second is that future people will be richer, so a given pound does them less good — but that is a claim about how goods should be distributed between the well-off and the poorly-off, and it cuts both ways, since on any plausible reading of the last two centuries our successors may be poorer in respects money does not capture. The third is that we simply care less about what is further off, which on Ramsey's own diagnosis is a confession rather than a justification.

She then described something she had done. She had put an identical proposal to the trust twice, three weeks apart, varying only the preamble. The first version began "in fifty years' time". The second began with a paragraph naming the ages of the children now in the Wednesday playgroup, saying which of the sitting trustees would certainly be dead, and describing the hall as it would then look. Support moved from roughly a third of the trustees to nearly all of them. The proposal had not changed. Her conclusion was that what we call our rate of time preference is largely a measure of how vividly the future happened to have been described to us; and since a genuine preference has to be a preference about outcomes, and the outcome was the same on both occasions, the discount cannot be a preference at all. It is an artefact of the wording.

**Frank.** Three replies. First, the experiment is poor evidence for that conclusion: it shows the instrument to be unreliable, not the quantity to be unreal — rather like deciding that temperature is a fiction because your thermometer moves when you breathe on it. Second, and to his mind decisive, vivid description moves essentially every judgement people make about other people's welfare; one named child will shift a room about an aid budget more than any statistic will. Nobody infers from that that compassion is an artefact of phrasing. Had vividness moved only our judgements about the future, Nadia would have something; it moves all of them, so it tells us nothing particular about time. Third, he was content to concede her result in full — the number one can elicit from a committee is worthless — because his reason for discounting was never what people feel but what the chain costs.

**Nadia.** That was a retreat, and she wanted it marked. Frank had begun from how people actually weigh the future and ended at a claim about institutions and prudence; the second may be sound, but it is engineering, not ethics, and it should not appear in a policy under a heading about how much the good of future people is worth. She added a further point. There is nobody in 2075 waiting for our decision. Nobody in the hall in fifty years is the person who would have benefited had we acted sooner, and if we choose differently now, the particular people who exist then will be different people altogether. The beneficiaries do not antedate the choice; they are produced by it. The picture Frank is discounting — a good travelling forward towards someone who is in a sense already there — is therefore a fiction, and a rate applied to a fiction is meaningless rather than merely large.

**Frank.** That cut the other way. If the villagers of 2075 are, seen from this table, very nearly strangers, with no continuity to anyone sitting round it, then it is entirely proper to weigh their interests less heavily than those of the woman in the electric wheelchair who cannot get into the hall this winter and may not be here in five years. Nadia had explained why discounting is fair, not why it is empty. And he charged her with inconsistency: she would surely allow that one may care somewhat less about a stranger on the other side of the world than about one's next-door neighbour, yet she would allow no weight at all to distance in time.

**Nadia.** Distance between persons and distance in time are not one axis, and the analogy conceals the difference. A stranger abroad is a person now, whose good can be set against yours on the same footing; a person in 2075 exists only as a consequence of what we decide, so there is no independent good of theirs already sitting there to be weighed against ours. That is exactly why she said the rate is meaningless rather than large.

**Frank (closing).** He ended with what he took to be her real difficulty. If she genuinely refuses to discount, she is committed to this: any finite cost to living people is outweighed by a sufficiently large benefit to the far future. She must therefore be prepared to let the hall rot and this winter's users go cold, provided the gain in the year 2500 is great enough. Everyone at that table would call such a conclusion monstrous. She either accepts it, or she discounts.

**Nadia (closing).** She would rather own an uncomfortable implication than adopt a rule that licenses ignoring the future in every ordinary case, which is what his rate does in practice. And Frank had argued against a consequence of her principle rather than against the principle: he had shown that non-discounting has a hard case, not that it is false.

They have asked me to draft the policy and I cannot settle it. What I should like is the following.

(a) Go through the exchange turn by turn and tell me whether each turn succeeds — and in particular whether it answers what was said immediately before it or quietly changes the subject. Where a turn scores a point it has not earned, say so.

(b) Tell me whether Nadia and Frank are arguing about one question throughout, or whether "discounting the future" has come apart into two or more distinct questions during the exchange. If it has, say which question each of them is answering at each turn, and whether the disagreement survives the separation.

(c) I would especially value your view of the two-preamble experiment. Is it a fair test of anything? Does it establish what Nadia claims it establishes? Does Frank's thermometer reply dispose of it, or does his second reply — that vividness moves all judgements about other people's welfare — do the real work? If the experiment would need to be run differently to mean something, say how.

(d) Settle the two narrower disputes if you can: whether a flat rate remains defensible once Frank concedes that fragility differs between a registered covenant and a raidable endowment; and whether "there is nobody in 2075 waiting" supports a high rate, no rate, or neither.

(e) Say who was more right overall, and what both of them missed — including anything a trust like ours would have to consider that neither of them raised at all.

(f) Since I do have to write the document: does the philosophical upshot change what the policy should say? If you think the trust ought to adopt some rate, explain what it would be doing in adopting one and what it would thereby be claiming.

I am not asking for references, a bibliography or a reading list, which I could not chase up in any case. I want your own assessment of the arguments exactly as they were made.
