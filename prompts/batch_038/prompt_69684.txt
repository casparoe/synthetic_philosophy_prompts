You are a professional philosopher of social science who works on social ontology and the disputes about explanation in economics, sociology, and history, and who is also occasionally asked by senior academics to help them draft the addresses they give at the ends of their careers. Treat what follows as a real commissioning brief, and think carefully about the philosophical territory before you begin writing.

The occasion. My colleague Arthur Vance is retiring after forty-three years as an economic and commercial historian, and the university is holding a valedictory address for him in the main lecture theatre. About two hundred people will be there: economists and sociologists from three departments, four cohorts of his former doctoral students, his wife, his three adult children, and two grandchildren aged nine and eleven who will be in the front row. I have been given twelve minutes to introduce him and to say something about what he taught the rest of us.

The complication. I am, and Arthur knows I am, a stubborn methodological individualist: my own work has argued for thirty years that any explanation of a social pattern worth having must ultimately bottom out in the situations, beliefs, and incentives of particular people. Arthur has spent his career arguing the opposite — that markets, guilds, credit networks, classes, and the temper of an age do explanatory work that no ledger of individual psychologies could ever reproduce — and he has argued it loudly, in seminars I have been sitting in since 1987. Our most cited disagreement concerns his account of why the Amsterdam exchange market survived three successive banking panics in the same decade while the London one did not; I have always said his explanation was a placeholder for one about individual merchants' expectations, and he has always said I was missing the thing that actually held.

What I want the address to do. I want to make Arthur five concessions — five points on which I now think his side has been right and I have been resisting it out of doctrinal loyalty. Please write the address itself, in the first person, as speech to be read aloud.

Structure. Exactly five numbered concessions and nothing else: no opening remarks before number one, no peroration after number five, no preview, no table of contents, no framing anecdote bookending the whole thing. Each concession should run about 250 words, enough for a little over two minutes spoken.

The hard constraints, which are where the difficulty lies:

1. Every concession must be one I can make without abandoning methodological individualism. I want the genuinely concessible territory — the claims about institutions, structural constraint, unintended and self-undermining consequences, pattern explanation, or the limits of what individual-level generalizations can deliver, that a consistent individualist can grant and that a holist in the room will recognize as a real gift rather than as his own thesis translated into my vocabulary. Where a concession leaves a residue, spend one clause saying plainly what I am still refusing and why, so Arthur does not leave the hall believing I have surrendered more than I have. Any candidate concession that collapses into a platitude, or that covertly concedes irreducible group belief, group intention, or social causes operating independently of individual ones, does not qualify — work out privately which candidates fail on those grounds and discard them before you draft.

2. The five must not overlap. Between them they should cover as much of the contested ground as an individualist can honestly give up, each occupying distinct conceptual territory rather than restating the last in new words.

3. Arthur's grandchildren are in the front row. The words holism, individualism, supervenience, emergence, reduction, ontology, microfoundations, and agency (in the philosophical sense) must not appear anywhere in the address. There must be no quotation from, and no name-dropping of, philosophers, sociologists, or economists. Every concession has to be made in plain speech, preferably through a concrete image or example drawn from the kind of history Arthur actually did — merchants, ledgers, panics, guilds, credit, queues, rumours — so that a specialist hears exactly which contested claim is being granted while an eleven-year-old hears a story.

4. It must land as affection, not as a lecture or as a thirty-year score-settling. Arthur should laugh at least once, should not be made to look foolish or merely tolerably wrong, and should not be spoken about as though he were already a monument. Direct address to him is welcome. The economists in the second row who have read my books should be able to confirm afterward that I have given away nothing I could not have kept.

Begin your reply with concession number one.
