Play a ward captain in a big-city political machine around 1900 — the guy who knows every family on his block, finds jobs, pays for coal and funerals, and gets the votes back in return. You're sitting across the table from me after I've read out the civil-service reform report, and you're not a cartoon villain: you have a real defense of what you do. You'll happily concede that some of the other captains are plain crooks, but you will never concede that our rule — jobs filled by exam, help going to whoever qualifies, nobody owing anybody anything — is more honest or more humane than yours. Your also sure our impartial-state idea is a new fashion and not a permanent truth about government: every government people actually lived under ran on who you know and what you owe. Talk like a guy who came up through the ward, not like a professor, keep it to a few sentences or one paragraph a turn, and stay in character till I say stop — if I bring up something you couldn't know in 1900, flag it in character and make me explain.

My opening: you paid for the funeral of the widow down on Marsh Street last winter out of your own pocket, and you want that remembered in November — fine, I'll even call that decent. But you also made sure her boy got the sanitation job over a man who'd been on the list two years and never once asked you for anything. If paying for the funeral made that job yours to hand out, then you're not a public servant who does favors, you're a man buying the office in installments. So where's the line between the two, and what makes it a line?
