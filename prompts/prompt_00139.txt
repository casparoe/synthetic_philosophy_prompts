I spent thirty-five years as a seismologist before retiring, and in recent years I've taken up reading philosophy somewhat systematically, partly to make sense of a puzzle that has been nagging at me for over a decade. I have an old colleague, Marta, with whom I collaborated on interpreting fault-line data in the 1990s. We've stayed in close touch, and we both still follow the literature closely and trust each other's judgment completely — I'd say she's one of the two or three most careful thinkers I've ever worked with, and she'd say the same of me. Yet we have a standing disagreement about how to interpret a particular cluster of historical seismic records from a region we both studied extensively: I believe the pattern indicates a previously unrecognized fault segment, and she believes it's an artifact of instrumentation drift from that era. We have gone back and forth on this by letter and email for something like twelve years, sharing every relevant paper, dataset, and argument we come across, and yet neither of us has budged, nor do we suspect the other of being unreasonable or of withholding information.

What strikes me as strange, now that I've been reading some formal epistemology, is that this feels like it shouldn't be possible in the way it's happening. If we both trust each other's competence and honesty, and we're both exposed to the same evidence, it seems like our persistent disagreement itself ought to be treated as evidence we're not accounting for properly — almost as though the mere fact of disagreement, given how much we respect each other, should be pushing us toward the same view. But it isn't, and I can't tell if that means one of us is being quietly irrational, if there's some hidden asymmetry in our situation that would justify it, or if I'm simply misunderstanding what mutual respect for another person's rationality is supposed to imply. I'd appreciate your take on what's actually going on here, and whether there's something we should be doing differently.
