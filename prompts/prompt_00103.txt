I've been reading about Bayesian confirmation theory and keep getting stuck on the problem of old evidence (the one Glymour raised in the late 70s using Einstein and the anomalous perihelion precession of Mercury). The textbook formulation seems almost paradoxical to me: standard Bayesian confirmation says E confirms H just in case P(H|E) > P(H), but if E is old evidence that the agent already knows with certainty, then P(E) = 1 and conditionalizing on it can't change anything, so P(H|E) = P(H). Yet obviously Einstein's contemporaries took the precession data as dramatically confirming general relativity even though the data had been sitting around, unexplained, for decades. Something in the naive picture is clearly missing.

What I'd like help with is less the historical detail and more the conceptual structure of the problem and the proposed fixes. Could you walk me through why this is a genuine problem for strict Bayesian conditionalization (rather than just a quirk of how philosophers phrase things), and then explain the main lines of response — e.g., Garber/Jeffrey-style moves that give up logical omniscience and treat the discovery of E's entailment by H as itself the new "evidence," versus counterfactual approaches that ask what your credence in H would have been had you not already known E. I'm especially interested in why neither move is fully satisfying: what's the residual worry that keeps this a live issue in Bayesian epistemology rather than something that got cleanly resolved decades ago?
