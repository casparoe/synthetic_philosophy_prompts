Write a dialogue between two equally informed interlocutors—Alistair and Beatrice—disagreeing over whether the backward induction paradox in the centipede game reveals a genuine normative failure or a reductio of an implicit epistemic assumption.

Alistair defends backward induction as logically valid: with perfect information and common knowledge of rationality, rational agents must stop at the first node. He accepts that this collapses cooperation, but insists that is the correct result, not a paradox. To avoid making the argument empirically empty, he introduces a specific refinement: backward induction applies only under a "terminal transparency condition"—the final node must be not merely logically terminal but epistemically accessible as a hard, publicly verified boundary at every prior stage. In physical or institutional analogues—sequential bargaining with ambiguous deadlines, or implementation delays—this condition fails, which explains why real agents continue without being irrational.

Beatrice rejects backward induction not by denying rationality, but by charging that it smuggles in an illicit "atemporal self-transparency" assumption. She argues that rationality at node n need not be fully transparent to the same agent's reasoning at node n-1, because preferences or epistemic commitments can shift in rational but not fully anticipatable ways. She uses this to explain continued cooperation without invoking error or bounded rationality. She also presses Alistair's transparency distinction: if the condition fails in every interesting real-world case, then backward induction governs only toy models, and saving it costs more than it is worth.

The dialogue must contain genuine back-and-forth: Beatrice should address Alistair's transparency condition directly, and Alistair should respond to her charge of atemporal smuggling rather than changing the subject. Neither should be a strawman. Include at least one moment where one interlocutor concedes a partial weakness in their own view but argues it is less costly than the opponent's alternative. Keep the exchange conceptually dense, do not invoke bibliographies or named texts, and do not ask for citations.
