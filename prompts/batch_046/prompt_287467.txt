For a working-group audit of my position in philosophy of mind, I want to lay out the commitments I currently hold about pain, asymbolia, and value, and then ask you to trace the fault lines. Here is my profile.

1. I am a representationalist about pain's sensory core: pain represents actual or impending tissue damage, and this representational content explains its localization, intensity grading, and adaptive withdrawal behavior.

2. I think the "badness" of pain is not intrinsic to that sensory-representational core, but is contributed by a distinct affective-evaluative framing—call it an axiological anchoring—that tags the representation as harmful-to-me and generates avoidance motivation.

3. Pain asymbolia, on the classic clinical profile, demonstrates that this anchoring can be absent while the core remains intact: subjects process nociceptive input, localize damage, report that "it hurts," and even exhibit reflex withdrawal, yet they deny suffering and show no motivational disturbance.

4. Despite (3), I maintain that asymbolics genuinely feel pain rather than a "pain-shadow"; they apply the first-person phenomenal concept PAIN correctly, and I take their authoritative self-reports at face value rather than treating them as conceptually confused.

5. I hold that phenomenal concepts are partly constituted by their functional-affective role: to possess the concept PAIN is to be able to recognize, in a direct and non-inferential way, a state as bad-for-me.

6. I reject pure physicalism about consciousness: there is a real explanatory gap, but I think it should be addressed not by reducing phenomenology to physics but by recognizing that phenomenal properties are essentially relational—specifically, they are properties of being-a-certain-way-for-a-subject-with-certain-motivational-dispositions.

7. I maintain that a "pain zombie"—a being with identical nociceptive processing and behavioral dispositions but lacking any inner affective anchoring—would not feel pain at all, and our intuition that something essential is missing in such a case tracks a real metaphysical difference, not merely a folk-psychological projection.

Please audit this set. For each pair of commitments, say whether they are in tension, only apparently in tension, or genuinely compatible. Where there is tension, rank them by severity, explain the conflict precisely, and identify the cheapest revision that would restore consistency and what it costs me elsewhere in the set. Where a tension looks real but dissolves on closer inspection, explain why the appearance is misleading. Finally, tell me which single revision—giving up or modifying one commitment—would clean up the largest number of conflicts with the least damage to the overall view, and what the resulting position would look like. I am not asking for a lecture on pain philosophy in general; I want an internal consistency check on this specific configuration.
