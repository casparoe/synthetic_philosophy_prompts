I am preparing a graduate seminar on philosophy of mind and artificial intelligence, and I want to move the discussion well beyond the introductory Searle-versus-functionalist script that most students have already encountered in survey texts. Consider Searle’s Chinese Room argument in its strongest form: not merely that computers run programs, but that formal symbol manipulation—no matter how sophisticated its syntactic rules—remains intrinsically devoid of semantic content, because syntax is not semantics and no purely computational process can bridge that gap from the inside. Now compare this with a hypothetical position I will call the Co-Referential Binding View. On this view, semantic content does not reside in any single processing node, nor in the syntactic rules governing local transitions, but emerges instead from stable co-referential binding relations that hold between distributed representational states across an entire system and its causal environment. The view concedes that any localized subsystem—analogous to the human operator inside the room—operates purely syntactically, yet it denies that this local syntactic isolation undermines the semantic status of the global architecture, on the grounds that semantics is a relational property of the binding structure rather than an intrinsic property of computation.

Compare the Chinese Room argument and the Co-Referential Binding View. Does Searle’s core intuition—that the room genuinely understands nothing—survive if semantic content is defined relationally across a coupled system rather than attributed to individual computational steps? Would the standard system reply to the Chinese Room succeed on this account, or does the binding view still leave an explanatory gap between syntactic correlation and genuine intentionality? Furthermore, which of the two positions offers the more defensible framework for explaining how a physically implemented system, especially one of modest scale lacking the massive distributed structure characteristic of modern neural networks, could come to mean something by its symbols? I am particularly interested in whether the relational move genuinely answers Searle’s challenge or merely relocates the mystery of intentionality to the architecture of binding itself.
