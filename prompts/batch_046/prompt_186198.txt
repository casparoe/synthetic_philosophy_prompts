Consider the risk-evaluation protocol adopted by the International Resilience Allocation Board (IRAB) for distributing climate-adaptation grants. For any proposed intervention, analysts first classify hazards into two bins. Where probabilities are well calibrated—for instance, the failure rate of a specific levee design under historical flood distributions—the board computes an ordinary expected-utility score using the project’s cost and benefit distributions. Where probabilities are structurally ambiguous—for instance, the likelihood of triggering an unknown feedback loop in stratospheric aerosol injection—the protocol switches to an ambiguity-averse aggregator: it computes the worst-case and best-case expected outcomes, assigns the worst-case a weight of 2 and the best-case a weight of 0.5, and takes the weighted average. Crucially, the board applies this ambiguity-averse aggregator only when experts judge the ambiguity to be epistemic (stemming from missing data) rather than ontic (stemming from genuinely indeterminate physical processes). Furthermore, every grant approved under the ambiguous-risk track must contain a “prior-lock” clause: the worst-case assessment used at approval may not be revised downward in light of subsequent data for at least ten years.

This practice seems to sit uneasily with several familiar positions. What philosophical commitments about rational decision-making, the nature of uncertainty, and the ethics of intertemporal commitment does the protocol actually presuppose? Must one accept that ambiguity aversion is normatively rational—not merely descriptively common—to justify the 2:0.5 weighting, or could a subjective Bayesian defend the same formula as a pragmatic risk premium? Does the epistemic/ontic distinction require an objective interpretation of probability, or can it be reconstructed within a purely subjectivist framework? And does the prior-lock clause presuppose a particular view of rational updating—perhaps that initial ambiguity assessments carry a special epistemic authority that later evidence should not override—or is it compatible with standard Bayesian conditioning?

In reconstructing the implicit theory, distinguish what the protocol strictly requires from what merely fits it well. Would a policy built on pure expected-utility maximisation, or on a thoroughgoing subjective Bayesianism, have to abandon every element of this design, or could it preserve parts of it? And is there a consistent philosophical position that licenses the differential treatment of ecological ambiguity versus economic ambiguity without smuggling in an ad hoc value distinction?
