My study group was arguing about running city zoning through predicition markets - you know, futachy, where people bet on outcomes and the policy with the best predicted welfare score gets adopted. Two friends, Leo and Nadia, got stuck and asked me to referee.

Leo said it sounds good for technical stuff but collapses once values enter. He said imagine the city uses a "flourishing index" mixing housing cost, green space, and cultural access. The market maximizes that. But Leo said picking the index is already a political choice about the good life, so futachy doesn't eliminate politics - it just hides it in the metric-selection stage. He said that's not liberal neturality, that's smuggling in values behind a technical curtain.

Nadia said Leo was treating the democratic layer wrong. She said citizens vote once on a thin constitutional metric - maybe just "minimize suffering plus autonomy" - and then the market figures out zoning. That separates ends from means, and it's actually more neutral because people don't have to argue over every building code. Leo said okay, but then you've admitted futachy doesn't solve value pluralism, it just quarantines it. And he said if you try to escape that by letting people bet on which metric is best - a meta-market - you hit an infinite regress, since that meta-market needs its own success criterion.

Nadia brought up something she called "reflective equilibrium markets" - traders bet not on outcomes directly but on correlations between different metrics and long-run citizen satisfaction surveys, so the system self-corrects without a fixed constitutional layer. Leo said that creates "value extraction loops": the market optimizes for people being satisfied with whatever metric is running, not for actual wellbeing. Nadia said that's too cynical, markets price real outcomes, not just feelings about measurements.

They asked me to call it. Can you go through their exchange step by step and say who's right about what? Specifically: is Leo right that metric-choice smuggles in values? Is Nadia right that separating constitutional ends from market means achieves neutrality? Does Leo's meta-market regress actually bite, or can Nadia's reflective equilibrium idea dodge it? And is Leo's "value extraction loop" a fatal flaw or just something you could engineer around? Also say what both are missing - maybe something about who designs the satisfaction surveys, or whether epistemic humility cuts both ways. Don't just split the difference; say who has the stronger point at each round, and whether there's a deeper mistake neither sees.
