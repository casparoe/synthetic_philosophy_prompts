I'm writing a paper for a graduate seminar on philosophy of science and public reason, and we've been working through Wittgenstein's On Certainty with particular attention to hinge propositions—those claims that "stand fast for us" not because they are justified by evidence but because they form the scaffolding that makes justification within a practice possible. I want to test whether this framework actually illuminates policy controversies or whether it collapses every deep disagreement into an incommensurable clash of frameworks. To do that, I'm applying it to the 1976 U.S. swine flu immunization campaign: after an outbreak of a swine-like influenza strain at Fort Dix killed one soldier and infected others, CDC leadership warned of a potential 1918-scale pandemic; President Ford endorsed a nationwide vaccination program that eventually reached roughly forty million people; the anticipated pandemic never arrived, but reports linked the vaccine to Guillain-Barré syndrome, prompting suspension of the program. I want you to analyze this episode through the lens of hinge epistemology. First, identify the specific hinge propositions operating for the advisory panel—claims about the reliability of serological identification, about the predictive value of historical analogies like 1918, about institutional authority to act under radical uncertainty—and contrast them with the hinges held by skeptical physicians and citizens who resisted (for instance, about the fallibility of epidemiological modeling, or about the default status of bodily integrity). Second, trace whether the non-occurrence of the pandemic functioned as a falsification of ordinary empirical claims within a shared framework, or whether it left the underlying hinges untouched, protected by ad hoc adjustments. Third, say whether the controversy was fundamentally a hinge-clash—each side reasoning correctly from different scaffolds—or whether there was genuine evidential conflict within a shared hinge system that Wittgenstein's model misses. Be disciplined about the mapping: specify which beliefs functioned as hinges versus ordinary empirical claims, note precisely where the framework is silent (for example, on normative questions of distributive risk or institutional accountability), and avoid treating every disagreement as automatically hinge-level. Finally, tell me whether this case ultimately supports the idea that public health controversies are best understood through hinge divergence, or whether it reveals that hinge epistemology is too blunt an instrument for controversies where normative obligations, not just epistemic structures, are at stake.
