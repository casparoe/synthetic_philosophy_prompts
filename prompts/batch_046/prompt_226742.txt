You're a decision theorist with some philosophy of economics background, and you know the rationalist blog discourse on agents and optimization. Before answering, brainstorm for a minute: list the nearest established concepts, then think about why each might miss the mark.

Okay, here's the pattern I've been noticing, and I don't have a word for it. There's the classic money pump—cyclic preferences, drained choice by choice. There's diachronic inconsistency—planning to save then blowing the budget. But I think there's a third thing.

Picture an agent with totally transative preferences—no cycles, ever, at any single moment. But they run a self-correction protocol: if they've held preference P for "longer than expected," they lower confidence in P and bump Q up a bit. It's not that Q gets more attractive; it's that the agent gets epistemically suspicious of their own stability. Now an adversary can slowly engineer things so the agent keeps hitting these "revision triggers." One version: controlling the pace of information so every quarter the agent checks "have I been too conservative?" and shifts retirement savings a little more aggressive, not because markets changed, but because the timer said "this consistency is suspicious." Another version: a negotiator who holds firm for "too long" and then voluntarily weakens their position, not because the other side offered more, but because they distrust prolonged deadlock. Over time resources ratchet one direction. The agent never violates local rationality, never directly reverses a preference, and can defend each step as "humility about my own models."

Is there an established term for this kind of second-order or meta-level pump? I know Dutch books target first-order preferences, and commitment devices solve ordinary dynamic inconsistency, but neither captures exploitation of the agent's own rationality-maintenance rules. If there's no standard name, coin one and draw the boundry: how does it differ from framing nudges, from ordinary learning, and from classic diachronic failure? I want the carving precise—what belongs inside, what sits outside, and where the edge gets fuzzy.
