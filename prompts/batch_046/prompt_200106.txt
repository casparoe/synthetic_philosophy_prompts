Write the desiderata that an adequate philosophical theory of legal self-reference must satisfy. The theory must explain how a legal system can contain rules that govern their own amendment or interpretation without either dissolving into logical contradiction—such as when a constitutional amendment clause is invoked to alter the very conditions under which amendments are valid—or collapsing into a purely political account of authority. Specify the full list of requirements: the cases it must correctly describe (including not only standard constitutional entrenchment but also recursive administrative frameworks in which an agency is empowered to revise both future regulations and the interpretive rules that govern its own authority); the distinctions it must preserve (for example, between a rule referring to its own propositional content and one referring to its own source of validity, between self-application and self-amendment, between logical impossibility and legal invalidity); the formal or jurisprudential results with which it must be consistent; and the normative judgments about democratic legitimacy and legal continuity that it must accommodate or explain away. For each desideratum, provide the motivating case and mark whether it is non-negotiable or camp-specific (e.g., legal-positivist versus interpretivist commitments). Then assess whether any subset of these requirements is jointly unsatisfiable—particularly whether accommodating both strict hierarchical closure and open-ended interpretive revision necessarily produces a contradiction, and if so, which requirement should yield. Keep the focus on the criteria and their justifications rather than on ranking existing accounts, though where the classic formulation of the self-amendment paradox bears on a requirement, indicate its relevance without citation.
