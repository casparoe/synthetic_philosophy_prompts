I have been working through Adam Smith's major ethical treatise for a seminar on eighteenth-century moral philosophy, and during our meeting a colleague offered this characterization of his position: Smith holds that all moral judgment is an exercise in sympathy, understood as a direct emotional identification with the feelings of another—so that to approve of a person's anger is literally to feel a matching anger oneself. According to this summary, the impartial spectator is best understood not as an imaginative projection but as an internalized social authority, the accumulated voice of one's community lodged within the breast, whose anticipated praise or blame serves as the real mechanism of moral regulation. The colleague then argued that because sympathy is just emotional resonance and the spectator is just society speaking inwardly, Smith's framework offers no independent critical standard and collapses ethics into communal conformity. I find this suspicious, partly because I recall that Smith distinguishes sharply between judging the propriety of a sentiment—where the spectator compares the agent's feeling with what he himself can share through imagination—and judging the merit or demerit of an action, where sympathy operates indirectly through the gratitude or resentment of the affected party rather than through direct identification. I also think the spectator is meant to be an idealized, not an actual, audience.

Could you audit this summary carefully? Please distinguish what is faithful to Smith's account, what is a common popular simplification, and what is a substantive distortion—particularly regarding whether sympathy requires emotional replication, whether the spectator functions as an internalized authority or as an imaginative construct, and whether the distinction between propriety and merit is preserved or erased. Finally, I want to know whether the colleague's conclusion—that Smith reduces ethics to emotional contagion and social conformity—actually follows from the accurate elements, or whether it depends on the misrepresentations you identify.
