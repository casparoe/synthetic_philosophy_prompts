Consider the institutional practice, employed in certain forensic and clinical competency assessments, of determining whether a patient remains the same person over time and retains legal standing primarily by evaluating the coherence and continuity of their first-person autobiographical narrative, rather than by testing episodic memory retention or physical persistence. Under this framework, an individual with severe retrograde amnesia but an intact, integrating self-story is often treated as fully continuous with their past self and capable of bearing contractual or criminal responsibility, whereas an individual with intact memory traces but a fractured, contradictory, or absent narrative self may be judged to lack the unified agency required for legal personhood. What philosophical theory of the self does this practice presuppose? Does it implicitly commit to the view that the self is best understood as a center of narrative gravity — an abstract focal point constructed from interwoven intentions, explanations, and temporal projections — rather than as a Cartesian substance, a Lockean memory-chain, or a biological organism? Reconstruct the implicit theory, distinguish what the practice strictly requires from what merely accompanies it, and say whether it could be sustained under rival accounts such as psychological-continuity or bodily-continuity theories. Finally, describe what concrete changes to assessment protocols, legal standards, or clinical interventions would be necessary if the institution replaced its narrative-based criterion with a purely physical or purely mnemonic one.
