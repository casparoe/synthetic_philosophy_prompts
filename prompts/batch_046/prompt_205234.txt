I am a PhD student in philosophy of science and technology studies, and I have been asked to deliver the opening remarks—about eight hundred words, spoken but formal—at a one-day workshop called “Reason and Its Discontents.” The room will be split evenly between historians of science who know the Enlightenment literature in detail and STEM graduate students who have never read Adorno or Horkheimer but are actively working on AI governance and open-source infrastructure. I want the remarks to set the terms for the day rather than decorate them: the Enlightenment should appear not as a settled historical verdict but as a live, structural tension between two legacies—emancipatory reason and instrumental domination—without letting either side win by definitional fiat. To keep this from being a standard “Enlightenment: good or bad?” framing, please inject a conceptual distinction that is not part of the usual debate: treat progress and domination not as opposing outcomes but as two incompatible temporal logics—progress as cumulative, revisable error-correction, and domination as recursive extraction that feeds on its own outputs—and apply that distinction to a single shared institutional practice familiar to both halves of the room (for instance, peer review or open-source collaboration). Show how the same practice can instantiate both logics simultaneously, so that the workshop’s discussions of AI and governance inherit an ambiguity that cannot be resolved by picking sides. The remarks must be precise enough that the historians do not roll their eyes, yet accessible enough that the STEM students are not lectured; they should close by stating explicitly what the workshop can claim to resolve given this framing and what it cannot. Do not include citations, bibliographies, reading lists, or attributions to specific texts—this needs to be generable from conceptual reasoning alone, with no reliance on web access.
