Having spent three decades in experimental physics before turning recently to philosophy of mind, I find the split-brain literature too quick to choose between one mind and two. I want your actual credence—not a literature review—in the following intermediate claim: in a classic corpus-callosotomy patient performing a bimanual conflict task (left hand drawing a circle, right hand a square, with verbal report confined to the left hemisphere), there is exactly one conscious subject whose phenomenal field remains globally present but whose access-structure is locally partitioned, neither fully unified nor duplicated. Decompose this into (a) the likelihood that phenomenal consciousness can fragment spatially without multiplying subjects, (b) the likelihood that verbal-report access is an unreliable proxy for subject-hood, and (c) any other factor you weigh heavily; combine them into an overall number, then state which component is weakest and what evidence would move it. Give me exactly five numbered items—each one or two sentences, no introduction, no conclusion—where item 1 is your overall credence, items 2–4 are the three decomposed probabilities with brief justification, and item 5 identifies the weakest link and the revision condition.
