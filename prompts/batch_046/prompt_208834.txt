I'm putting together notes for a seminar on disjunctivism and the argument from illusion and want to check if my package of views is actually coherent, or if I'm smuggling in contradictions without noticing.

Here's where I stand:
(a) I'm a disjunctivist: in a genuine case of seeing a red apple, the apple and its redness are literally constituents of my experience's phenomonal character, not just external causes.
(b) The argument from illusion is unsound because it illegitimately treats hallucination and veridical perception as falling under a common sensory kind—like treating knowing and merely believing as the same mental state with different external conditions.
(c) Phenomenal character is fully relational: it's fixed entirely by which objects and properties are actually present in the subject's environment, not by anything intrinsic to the brain.
(d) I also think BIV scenarios are genuinely possible, and that a brain in a vat could be in states molecule-for-molecule identical to those of someone actually seeing an apple.
(e) In those BIV/hallucinatory cases, there's literally no phenomenal experience—no what-it's-like—because there's no object to stand in relation to; there's just a blank neural event that gets misinterpreted.
(f) But I also think that when I introspect my own veridical seeing, I can directly apprehend that the tree is present in the experience itself—it's not hidden behind a veil.

Which of these commitments are actually pulling against each other, which pairs only look incompatible, and what's the least costly way to restore consistency? Rank the tensions by severity rather than listing them all as equally bad, and say if there's a single revision that fixes the most problems. Don't treat everything as equally bad—some of these probably dissolve on inspection.
