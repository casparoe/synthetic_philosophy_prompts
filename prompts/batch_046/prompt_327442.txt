I am currently taking a course on aesthetics and political philosophy, and we have been working through W. E. B. Du Bois's claim — developed particularly in his 1926 address on the criteria of Negro art — that all art is propaganda and that Black art ought specifically to serve as intentional propaganda for racial equality and human dignity. Rather than treating this as an abstract slogan, I want to test it against a concrete historical dispute that exposes its internal tensions.

Consider Du Bois's well-known review of Claude McKay's 1928 novel Home to Harlem, which appeared in the pages of The Crisis, the journal he edited. McKay's book had achieved considerable commercial success and was celebrated by many readers for its vivid, unromanticised portrayal of Black working-class life in Harlem; Du Bois, however, condemned it as degrading and useless to the race, arguing that it pandered to white prurience rather than advancing the cause of equality. Analyse this episode through Du Bois's propaganda framework: what does the theory say he was facing, what should it have led him to do, which features of the novel — its popularity with Black audiences, its refusal of a redemptive uplift narrative, its aesthetic autonomy — does the theory explain well, and which does it misdescribe or leave silent? Then say what the episode in turn shows about the theory: is it a confirmation, an anomaly, or a case it never properly anticipated?

I also want you to address a further complication drawn from Du Bois's own earlier work. His concept of double consciousness, articulated in The Souls of Black Folk, suggests that Black people always perceive themselves partly through the eyes of a dominant white world. If this is correct, then any Black art is necessarily produced under conditions in which the white gaze is already internalised. Does this collapse Du Bois's distinction between proper propaganda (consciously race-affirming) and improper art (degrading or pandering), since all Black art is already negotiating with that gaze? Or does double consciousness actually strengthen his demand that artists take conscious control of the propaganda they produce? Apply this specifically to McKay's novel: was Du Bois right to see it as failed propaganda, or does the concept of double consciousness reveal that McKay was engaged in a different, equally necessary form of racial expression that Du Bois's framework was structurally unable to recognise? Please do not provide bibliographies, citations, or reading lists; I want the analysis to stand on conceptual grounds alone, and I am particularly interested in where the theory is silent rather than where it can be forced to speak.
