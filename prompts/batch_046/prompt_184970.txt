1. I think Buridan's ass is misdiagnosed - the problem isn't starvation, it's the false assumption that rational choice requires a strict comparative preference ordering.
2. I buy the small-improvement argument: if you're genuinely indifferent between A and B, and A gets even a hair better, you should switch to A, which suggests true symmetry is unstable.
3. I think coin-flipping randomization is rationally permissible in symmetric cases, but never rationally required - there's no norm forcing you to do it.
4. I reject value incommensurability, but I still think there are real tie-breaking situations where reasons run out without leaving values incomparable.
5. I think arbitrary choice without reason isn't irrational, but delaying choice until new reasons show up - the classic Buridan-style escape - is conceptually sound even if psycholgically unrealistic.

I'm worried about 2 and 3 pulling apart: if symmetry is always unstable, then there are no real symmetric cases, so what's the point of saying randomization is permissible? And 1 vs 5 looks tense too - if preference ordering isn't required, why do we need the delay tactic at all? Is 4 just contradicting 1?
