I'm preparing notes for a transitional-justice workshop at my school, and before presenting I'd like an audit of whether my commitments actually hang together. Here is roughly where I've landed:

(1) I think South Africa's Truth and Reconciliation Commission was right to grant conditional amnesty for gross human-rights violations, because democratic consolidation in that context required breaking cycles of vengeance, even at the cost of pure retributive justice.

(2) At the same time, I think Argentina was correct to annul its transition-era amnesty statutes and prosecute former junta members, because amnesty for crimes against humanity is void under non-derogable international norms.

(3) I also think Rwanda's gacaca courts were preferable to relying solely on the International Criminal Tribunal for Rwanda, because embedding accountability in local linguistic and communal frameworks can restore social fabric in ways distant international tribunals rarely replicate.

(4) I want to extend conditional-amnesty logic to what I call "omissive perpetrators"—mid-level officials who did not actively commit atrocities but knew of them and failed to intervene—provided they offer complete disclosure, on the grounds that transitional justice should distinguish culpable omission from active commission.

(5) I think the traditional distinction between "political offenses" and "ordinary crimes" that underpins many amnesty frameworks is conceptually unstable in post-authoritarian settings, since authoritarian regimes politicize all violence.

(6) I believe truth commissions should possess subpoena power to compel testimony, but they must be structurally barred from referring evidence directly to criminal prosecutors, because if disclosure can trigger prosecution, rational actors will withhold information and the epistemic purpose of the commission collapses.

My worry is that (1) and (2) pull in opposite directions: if amnesty for crimes against humanity is legally void, then the South African model was not merely pragmatically risky but morally wrong. Meanwhile, (4) seems to rely on a stable distinction between types of culpability that (5) undermines, since if all violence is politicized, omission and commission may be harder to separate than I assume. There may also be tension between (2) and (6): if prosecution is morally required for certain crimes, insulating testimony from prosecutors might obstruct justice rather than serve it. On the other hand, perhaps (3) and (6) are only apparently in tension—local justice might actually depend on testimony protections.

Please go through these commitments and tell me which pairs are genuinely in tension, which only appear to conflict, and what the least costly revision would be. Rank the tensions by severity, and for each genuine tension specify whether one commitment must go, or whether a conceptual refinement can save both. Don't treat them all as equally bad—I want to know which ones actually threaten coherence and which are surface-level.
