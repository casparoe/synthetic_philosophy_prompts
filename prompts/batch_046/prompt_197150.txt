In a seminar on moral psychology, someone dismissed an argument I was making by saying it involved “one thought too many.” The context was this: I had suggested that a parent who saves her own child over two strangers need not be failing to maximize welfare, provided she does not actively weigh the lives against each other, and that this absence of explicit weighing might be compatible with her still recognizing, in some background sense, that two lives have greater value than one. The reply was that even holding that comparative judgment—while still acting on direct concern—is already “one thought too many,” and therefore any moral framework that permits, or requires, such recognition corrupts the attachment it describes. I want to know exactly what is being charged. Could you lay out the distinct precise theses that “one thought too many” might express, organized as a menu? For each reading, specify: (a) whether it targets an explicit mental act (calculation or weighing), a background belief (acknowledging comparative value without acting on it), or a structural feature of the theory (the demand for impartial justification); (b) whether it applies only within intimate or familial attachments or extends to any non-instrumental commitment; (c) whether it is meant as a psychological claim (direct concern is psychologically incompatible with the belief), a normative claim (the agent ought not hold it), or a rational-coherence claim (the two attitudes are conceptually incompatible); and (d) whether the reading is trivial (unnecessary mental arithmetic is inefficient), defensible, false, or misattributed to the integrity objection. Crucially, say which readings actually support dismissing my specific argument—that the absence of explicit weighing saves the agent from corruption—and which instead undermine that dismissal by allowing the background belief without the weighing. If any reading depends on a non-standard interpretation of the integrity objection, flag it.
