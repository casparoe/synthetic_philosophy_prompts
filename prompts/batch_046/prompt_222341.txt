For a feature I'm writing on metaphysics and public understanding, I need to audit a comparison that dominates popular accounts of David Lewis's modal realism: the claim that possible worlds are "parallel universes" — physically real realms branching or coexisting with ours, separated only by inaccessible dimensions, much like multiverse scenarios in theoretical physics. The analogy is typically deployed either to render Lewis's view intuitively concrete or to dismiss it as empirical science fiction. Can you lay out the explicit correspondence — what plays the role of our world, the other worlds, their inhabitants, and modal truths — then identify precisely where the multiverse framing breaks down: spatiotemporal relations, causal isolation, the indexical status of "actual," and the absence of branching dynamics? Next, tell me which conclusions the analogy was carrying — whether modal realism is ontologically extravagant or philosophically parsimonious — and whether those conclusions survive once the disanalogies are registered. Finally, Lewis himself likened remote possible worlds to remote planets in actual space rather than to parallel dimensions; is that astronomical analogy more faithful to his ontology, and does substituting it for the multiverse image change whether the view reads as speculative cosmology or as an analysis of modal language?
