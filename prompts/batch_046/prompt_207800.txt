I am presenting a paper at an interdisciplinary workshop on moral psychology and legal theory in which I defend a modified Strawsonian account of moral responsibility. My thesis is that reactive attitudes—resentment, gratitude, indignation—are not merely dyadic interpersonal responses but are scaffolded by what I call normative affordance spaces: institutional and relational structures that make specific affective reactions available and intelligible to participants. From this I argue that determinism does not undermine moral responsibility because the scaffolding is practical and social rather than metaphysically libertarian; however, I further claim that when these spaces are systematically distorted—for instance, by carceral institutions that suppress reactive capacities in some groups while amplifying punitive ones in others—the conditions for genuine responsibility are undermined not at the individual level but at the structural level, producing what I term distributed moral disablement. The audience includes orthodox analytic Strawsonians who regard any structural extension of reactive attitudes as a category mistake; legal theorists who justify punishment through deterrence and treat reactive attitudes as epiphenomenal; cognitive scientists of emotion who are skeptical that scaffolding adds explanatory value beyond standard situated-cognition models; and one prominent critic of Strawson who maintains that the reactive-objective distinction collapses under empirical psychological scrutiny. Draft my Q&A prep sheet: list the objections in the order they are likely to arise, beginning with the Strawsonians, then the legal theorists, then the cognitive scientists, and finally the critic. For each objection, phrase it as that interlocutor would actually voice it, specify what I should concede up front, what I must hold, provide a brief reply appropriate to the time available, and flag the weakest point in that reply. Identify the one question I am hoping nobody asks—specifically whether my view entails that members of systematically oppressed groups lack moral agency altogether—and draft the reply I should have ready if it is raised. Conclude by pinpointing the single weakest point in my overall position that I should defend actively rather than hope the audience overlooks.
