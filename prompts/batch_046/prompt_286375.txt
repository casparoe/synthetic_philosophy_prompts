In Plato's Laws, the Athenian Stranger proposes a striking institutional design for Magnesia: citizens are not prohibited from wine but are required to attend regulated symposia overseen by a sober symposiarch, whose explicit purpose is pedagogical and diagnostic—deliberate, supervised intoxication is meant to expose hidden passions, test the stability of the soul, and provide a controlled environment for moral training. At the same time, the same legal framework treats public drunkenness as a serious civic failure and punishes the loss of rational control with severe penalties, as if the temporary suspension of reason were both an essential civic exercise and a dangerous breach of civic order. I have been trying to understand what philosophical theory of the soul, reason, and moral responsibility makes this dual practice coherent rather than contradictory, and whether Plato's institution actually needs that theory or is merely compatible with it.

What precise commitments about the architecture of the psyche does the symposion-as-institution presuppose? Does it require that the rational part can be genuinely separated from the appetitive part—switched off like a lamp—so that wine reveals a hidden nature obscured by reason, or can it be sustained on a model where reason is always present but merely modulated in strength? Reconstruct the implicit theory: is the practice committed to a partitioned soul in which reason and appetite compete for sovereignty, and if so, how does that square with the legal system's insistence that the citizen remains fully responsible even when reason is pharmacologically suspended? Further, distinguish what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely accommodates—could a continuous, graded view of rational control license the same regulations, or does the very idea of a sober supervisor who tests others depend on a discrete boundary between rational and irrational states?

Finally, if we replaced the underlying view with a non-partitioned psychology—say, one in which intoxication is not the revelation of a hidden appetitive self but merely a shift along a continuum of cognitive control—what would have to change in the institution itself? Would the symposiarch become unnecessary, would the diagnostic purpose collapse, and would the severe punishment of public drunkenness become incoherent? Say whether the practice is philosophically underdetermined, allowing several theories of the soul to license it, or whether it quietly rules out rival views in ways that Plato never explicitly defended.
