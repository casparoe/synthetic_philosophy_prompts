I am a retired experimental physicist who has only recently begun studying philosophy of science in earnest, and I now take part in a philosophy-of-physics reading group of about fifteen people—mostly philosophy graduate students together with a few working scientists—who meet for seventy-five-minute sessions. We have been discussing Bell's theorem and the challenge it poses to local explanations of quantum correlations, but I find that the formal derivation of the Bell bound, clear as it is, rarely makes participants feel the constraint from the inside. I would like you to design a runnable in-class simulation for this exact group: a structured game with explicit rules, simple physical materials, and a collective non-monetary payoff scheme that makes pairs of separated participants attempt to reproduce quantum-style correlations using only shared hidden instructions, so that they run into the failure of local realism before anyone names the result.

The game should proceed in two phases. In the first, each pair receives an envelope containing pre-agreed local strategy cards that specify deterministic responses for four possible measurement directions, but the partners are physically separated and may not communicate once a round begins; a random mechanism selects one setting for each partner, they record outcomes, and after many rounds the group computes an aggregate correlation score. The collective payoff—say, points toward a shared coffee voucher—depends on reaching a target value that exceeds the Bell bound of 2, which the participants should discover is impossible with any local card strategy. In the second phase, allow one partner to observe the other's setting before choosing a response, thereby violating parameter independence, but still forbid any direct influence on the partner's outcome; the group should then see that even this limited signaling does not restore the quantum predictions, driving home the distinction between parameter independence and outcome dependence. Include a debrief that maps each phase onto these formal distinctions, predicts what will predictably go wrong on the first run—such as participants secretly signaling, confusing statistical correlation with causal influence, or treating the card deck as a literal physical mechanism—and explains how to correct it without lecturing. Be explicit about which aspect of quantum mechanics, specifically the entangled state-vector formalism, the activity cannot replicate and that the facilitator must supply verbally.

Please separate what is well established in the interpretation of Bell's theorem—such as the mathematical impossibility of reproducing quantum correlations with local hidden variables—from positions that remain genuinely contested among specialists, including whether the result requires abandoning local realism altogether, whether it bears on determinism, and how it relates to the no-signaling principle. Do not present any one of these contested interpretations as the consensus view. Write your response in American English, in a formal and polished register suitable for an academic workshop design.
