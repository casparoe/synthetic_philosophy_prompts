I'm a PhD candidate in comparative moral philosophy presenting a 25-minute paper at an interdisciplinary workshop on folk moral structures. The audience includes empirical moral psychologists, cultural sociologists, and philosophers of action—few of whom specialize in normative ethics. My thesis is that American and German folk morality do not differ merely in how they weigh individual conscience against rule-following, but in second-order structure: American moral cognition treats conscience as a direct first-order override of rules, whereas German Ordnungsmoral treats conscience as legitimate only when mediated by a second-order commitment to the stability of the rule-system itself. I further argue that this produces a revisability asymmetry—the American framework adapts faster to rapid institutional change (for instance, emergent technological risks) because its rules are implicitly treated as procedural commitments to revisability rather than as substantive commands, while the German framework risks pathological rigidity when rules conflict with risks that outpace formal revision mechanisms.
