I've been working through the tension between Margalit's ethics of collective remembrance and Rieff's critique of therapeutic culture—particularly his suspicion that modernity has lost the capacity for proper mourning and the disciplined art of letting certain wounds remain open without letting them consume the living. A colleague pushed me into an argument that now seems to prove that any robust duty to remember grave historical injustices is structurally self-defeating, and I cannot locate the exit without abandoning commitments I want to keep: namely, that collective forgetting can be a genuine moral failure, and that remembrance is genuinely obligatory rather than merely sentimental.

Here is the chain. (1) If a community bears a duty to remember a grave injustice, then that duty must be dischargeable by the individuals who constitute the community; otherwise it is an obligation without agents. (2) Collective memory, however, is not a mental state distributed among individuals but exists only in shared practices, monuments, rituals, and narratives—what we might call mnemonic infrastructure. (3) Therefore, for an individual to discharge the collective duty, she must personally internalize and actively sustain the corresponding affective and cognitive disturbance; she cannot merely maintain the infrastructure while remaining personally untouched. (4) If the wrong is grave enough, such internalization without the possibility of narrative closure produces a permanently unhealed wound—what we might term affective foreclosure—damaging the individual's capacity for present moral engagement. (5) So each individual must either reject internalization and fail the duty, or accept the damage and become a diminished moral agent. (6) But a duty that systematically undermines the very agency required to fulfill it is structurally incoherent, analogous to an obligation to destroy one's own capacity for obligation. (7) Hence there is no genuine collective duty to remember grave wrongs—only permission, or perhaps a prudential recommendation to maintain monuments. (8) Yet this conflicts with the strong intuition that willful amnesia about certain atrocities is not merely imprudent but blameworthy, and that communities can be held accountable for it.

I want to preserve (1) and the claim that forgetting can be morally blameworthy, but I also want to avoid saying that moral life requires perpetual, unhealed grief distributed across every member. Which step should I reject, and what does rejecting it cost—particularly regarding whether collective mnemonic infrastructure can satisfy a duty without demanding full individual affective internalization?
