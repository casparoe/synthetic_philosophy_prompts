I just finished a long, exhausting argument with a close friend about procreative beneficence and liberal eugenics, and we kept circling the same sticking point: if parents have a reason to select the best child they can, does that reason survive once we treat the child's future social identity—not just their health or capacities—as partly constituted by the genetic traits being selected and by the relational environment those selections help create? I want to turn this into a closed-book essay exam for an upper-level undergraduate philosophy and bioethics course. Please write one exam question that students could answer in about 45 minutes. The question should force them to examine whether the principle of procreative beneficence can be consistently combined with a liberal eugenics framework that respects what we might call the relational constitution of personal identity, specifically the idea that selecting for socially embedded traits (such as specific cognitive profiles or social dispositions) does not merely improve an individual life but may reshape the very relational conditions in which that life becomes valuable to the person living it. The question must not be answerable by simply summarizing standard objections about state coercion or by reciting the standard formulation of procreative beneficence; instead, it should require students to locate the precise conceptual crux where beneficence reasoning conflicts with a liberal commitment to an open future.

Please provide all of the following, written in American English and without any request for citations, bibliographies, or reading lists:

1. The exam question itself, with the framing above built directly into its wording.
2. A model answer of approximately 500 words.
3. A marking scheme out of 20 points, divided into five specific analytical moves that distinguish an A-level answer from a B-level answer. For each move, state the point value (the five values must sum to 20), describe exactly what the answer must do to earn those points, and include a one-line note on the typical mistake that costs marks on that move.

The assessment should depend only on concepts students would have encountered in a standard upper-level course; inject the niche element—the tension between selecting for traits and altering the relational context of identity—into the question rather than assuming familiarity with obscure technical literature.
