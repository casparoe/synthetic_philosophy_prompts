As a retired physicist who has only recently begun to read philosophy systematically, I have become preoccupied with karma not as a religious narrative but as a putative theory of moral desert—an attempt to explain why suffering and flourishing are distributed in ways that track moral conduct across temporal boundaries that exceed a single life. I would like you to write out the desiderata that any theory of karma, understood in this strictly normative and desert-based sense rather than as a mere causal regularity, must satisfy in order to count as philosophically adequate. For each desideratum, please state the motivating case or conceptual pressure that makes it seem necessary, indicate whether you take it to be non-negotiable or merely the preference of a particular camp (for instance, naturalists, libertarians about free will, or those who reject metaphysical personal identity), and keep the weight on the criteria themselves rather than on scoring particular historical doctrines.

The list should address at least the following tensions: first, an account of diachronic personal identity robust enough to make it coherent that an agent now deserves reward or punishment for actions performed by a prior embodiment, without collapsing into an impersonal calculus of aggregate welfare; second, a principle of proportionality linking the quality and severity of prior moral conduct to present conditions of suffering or flourishing, together with an explanation of how such proportionality can be maintained without an omniscient administrator; third, consistency with the rejection of an unjust primordial distribution—that is, the theory must not simply push the problem of unequal initial conditions back into an infinite regress of prior lives, but must either justify initial disparities, explain them away, or deny that they exist; fourth, a clear boundary between moral desert and moral luck, particularly regarding circumstances of birth, social position, and innate temperament that an agent did not choose; fifth, either full compatibility with the causal closure of the physical world, or a principled argument for why moral causation necessarily transcends physical law; sixth, the status of forgiveness, atonement, or grace—whether these operate as genuine cancellations of accumulated desert or merely as alterations of future karmic trajectory; and seventh, whether the theory ought to respect something analogous to a physical conservation law—call it moral conservation—whereby the total quantum of moral debt or credit within a closed system of agents remains constant unless transformed by specific, identifiable mechanisms. If any subset of these requirements proves jointly unsatisfiable—for example, if strict proportionality, naturalistic closure, and the denial of an initial cosmic ledger cannot all be maintained—please identify the contradiction explicitly and indicate which desideratum is the least defensible.

Please write in a formal, analytical register appropriate to a seminar specification. I should also ask you plainly: if this request rests on a confused premise—such as treating a normative theory of desert as if it were an empirically testable hypothesis, or if the task cannot be executed without covertly importing contested metaphysical commitments that render the desiderata circular—say so directly rather than accommodating me politely. I am not asking for bibliographies, reading lists, or citations; a respondent without access to external sources should be able to address the conceptual architecture from first principles.
