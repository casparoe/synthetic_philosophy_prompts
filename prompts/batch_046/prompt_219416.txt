I'm writing a feature on whether Douglas Hofstadter's strange-loop theory of the self—the claim that the first-person pronoun functions as a self-referential symbol generated by recursive patterns in a physical substrate—remains a live research program or has become a philosophical curiosity. Rather than rehearse the familiar narrative, I want to know where the serious intellectual work is now. Among the open questions this framework raises, which deserve sustained attention over the next decade, and for what kind of researcher?

I'm weighing four lines that sit at different depths. First, whether strange-loop selfhood requires the specific biological architecture of cortical recursion, or whether functionally equivalent loops could emerge in distributed, non-neural substrates—for instance, in self-modifying artificial systems that maintain a persistent indexical pointer without anything resembling a brain. Second, whether the strange-loop account can be reconciled with predictive-processing and enactivist models that treat the self not as a symbol but as a dynamic control-model; resolving this may determine whether Hofstadter's view is an alternative to, or a special case of, embodied cognition. Third, the empirical program: whether neuroscience can identify specific recursive neural dynamics that correlate with first-person reports, and whether a failure to isolate them should weaken the theory or merely relocate the loop to a different scale. Fourth, the downstream normative implication: if the "I" is genuinely a symbol without a substrate-independent bearer, does this dissolve traditional notions of moral agency, or does it require us to relocate responsibility inside the loop's dynamics rather than in a metaphysical soul?

But I want a defended prioritization, not a survey. Which two or three of these are genuinely upstream of the others? Which are tractable for philosophers and theorists working without laboratory resources, and which depend on empirical findings unlikely to arrive soon? What criteria should govern the ranking—dependency, tractability, or the scale of conceptual payoff? And what specific result or argument in the next few years would cause you to reorder them?
