I've been tinkering with this weird hybrid view for a character in my novel — she's a labor historian with a grudge and a tendency to overtheorize. The position she's defending is that the Protestant work ethic didn't just glorify labor; it accidentally manufactured a kind of moral property right over one's own leisure, but only when that leisure is visibly earned through disciplined vocational calling. So the 35-hour week isn't a victory for labor rights but a kind of bureacratic mismeasurement — it treats leisure like a fungible resource that can be portioned out evenly, ignoring that in Weber's framework leisure only carries moral weight as the direct fruit of called work. And karoshi? In her telling it's not simply death by overwork; it's the structural collapse of the leisure-right mechanism, the point where the boundary between called labor and uncalled rest dissolves so completely that the worker loses not just health but the very moral status that made their suffering legible in the first place. I'm writing the scene where she gets challenged in a seminar. What would actually count against this? Not just generic appeals to rest or human dignity — I want to know which intuitions, which historical counter-cases (maybe Japanese salaryman culture doesn't map onto Weber at all?), which formal arguments about distributive rights, or which rival theories of leisure (the Aristotelian idea that leisure is the highest good irrespective of labor) would genuinely wound it. Which defeaters would she be forced to concede, and which could she dismiss as missing her specific point? Rank them from fatal to merely awkward. And be honest: is this mostly insulated by definitional sleight-of-hand? I want to know exactly where it's exposed.
