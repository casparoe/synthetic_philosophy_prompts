I recently experienced a brief dissociative episode following a minor concussion, which has made the architecture of consciousness suddenly personal: I found myself questioning whether there is a single, continuous "observer" inside my head, or whether what felt like sustained inner attention was actually something more fragmented. A neuroscience essay I encountered addresses exactly this tension by contrasting Descartes's Cartesian theater with Daniel Dennett's multiple drafts model. The author summarizes the comparison like this: Descartes held that consciousness requires a central theater in the brain—specifically the pineal gland—where all sensory inputs are projected onto a unified screen and inspected by a non-physical soul, making him the originator of the Cartesian theater idea that contemporary neuroscience rejects. Dennett's multiple drafts model replaces this with a decentralized neural architecture in which the brain produces many parallel, competing narratives of experience; there is no canonical first draft, no central audience, and therefore no intrinsic moment at which a particular sensory event becomes conscious. Because the model eliminates any unified point of view, it treats subjective experience as an illusion generated after the fact by editorial processes, which means clinical reports of continuous self-observation during dissociative states are merely misinterpretations of overlapping drafts as a single stream. The essay then concludes that Dennett's account is incompatible with sustained inner observation during dissociation, and that medicine should therefore retain a modified Cartesian theater.

Before I let this framing shape how I understand my own experience, I want to audit its fairness. Which parts accurately capture Dennett's technical notion of "drafts" and Descartes's actual claims about the pineal gland, and which parts conflate Dennett's rejection of a central observer with a denial of subjective experience altogether? Does the assertion that Dennett regards consciousness as a pure post-hoc illusion follow from his distributed model, or is it an overstatement? Where does the summary slip from Dennett's specialized meaning of "draft" into an everyday sense of preliminary revision? Finally, does the clinical argument about dissociation genuinely undermine the multiple drafts model, or does it implicitly assume the very theater-like unity that Dennett's view is designed to reject?
