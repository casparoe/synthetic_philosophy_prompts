I’ve been chatting with two mates from my moral philosphy course — Raj and Maya — about Augustine’s take on lying, and they got into it over a pretty specific case: a hospice nurse who tells a dying patient that their estranged child is on the way, knowing full well the child is actually dead, just to ease the final hours.

Raj kicked off by saying Augustine’s view is dead simple: in De Mendacio he lays out eight grades of lies, but every single one is sinful because lying corrupts the speaker’s soul and violates language’s purpose of revealing truth. The grading only tracks how bad the sin is, not whether any lie is permitted, and no amount of suffering prevented can change that. Maya pushed back that this misses something in how Augustine actually orders the grades — lies told to save someone’s life or chastity rank lower than lies told for fun, which only makes sense if Augustine already thinks the external harm or good done affects moral weight. She said there’s a hidden tension: if the soul-harm is constant but the culpability varies with circumstance, then Augustine is smuggling in a kind of proportional reasoning he officially rejects, and the nurse’s lie might fall into that lower-grade zone he implicitly treats differently. Raj answered that Maya is just reading consequentialism into a hierarchy of guilt — comparing a small lie and a big lie is like comparing petty theft and grand theft, both are wrong, and Augustine never says lower culpability equals permissibility. He also pointed out that Augustine’s semiotic view in De Doctrina Christiana — that signs exist to convey truth — makes the wrongness about breaking the sign-system itself, not about outcomes, so the nurse’s kind intentions don’t fix the broken sign. Maya replied that if the wrongness is purely semiotic and outcome-independent, then Augustine shouldn’t care whether the lie saves a life or not, yet he clearly ranks them differently. She said this shows his absolutism is actually unstable: either he has a hidden exception for protective deception, or his grading system is incoherent because it tracks outcomes he claims don’t matter.
