I have been working through the conceptual architecture of Gödel, Escher, Bach, particularly Hofstadter's distinction between frame messages, outer messages, and inner messages, and I am trying to apply it to a problem in institutional design rather than in formal logic or cognition. Specifically, I am examining hypothetical constitutional amendments that contain fully internalized suspension conditions: clauses that define, within their own text, the precise circumstances under which they cease to bind future interpreters, with no appeal to an external meta-document. This seems to create a strange loop in the legal domain analogous to the self-referential structures Hofstadter analyzes.

Please describe the most important implications of the frame/outer/inner message distinction for interpreting such a self-modifying provision. In particular, can the interpretive frame—the meta-rules that govern how the clause is read—ever be completely codified within the document itself, or must there always remain a residual external frame that escapes formalization? How should we understand the interaction between the outer message of original legislative intent and the inner message of the clause's logical structure when the suspension conditions are satisfied? And does this framework suggest that self-referential constitutional provisions can achieve stable binding authority, or does it imply that they necessarily generate the kind of interpretive instability and paradox that Hofstadter associates with strange loops? I want a conceptual analysis rather than a survey of legal doctrine, and I do not need citations, reading lists, or references to specific case law.
