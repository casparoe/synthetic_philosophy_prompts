I've been studying A.J. Ayer's logical positivism in Language, Truth and Logic, particularly his treatment of religious and metaphysical language, and I recently came across his 1988 near-death experience, which he recounted in the Daily Telegraph after choking on smoked salmon in hospital. In that account he describes being confronted by an exceedingly bright red light responsible for the government of the universe, along with two creatures acting as ministers of space who had failed to maintain cosmic order. What's puzzling me is how this experience relates to his earlier claim that talk of life after death is not false but literally meaningless, since it fails the verification principle. It seems to me that if Ayer genuinely experienced consciousness persisting while his brain was clinically inactive for several minutes, then he possessed a first-person empirical datum that should have satisfied the very criterion he demanded: direct observation of a state that, if reported accurately, verifies post-mortem awareness. Yet he apparently maintained that logical positivism was untouched, and in a subsequent Spectator postscript he said only that his inflexible attitude had weakened, not that his belief in no afterlife had changed. This makes me think he must have been operating with an unstated physicalist identity theory all along—treating any seeming post-mortem report as necessarily reducible to residual neural firing—so that the verification principle was never the real engine of his rejection, but rather a linguistic cover for a metaphysical commitment to mind-brain dependence. Otherwise, why wouldn't his own phenomenological report count as the kind of empirical verification that renders talk of life after death meaningful and possibly true? Could you lead with the bottom line: was Ayer's position genuinely verificationist, or was he implicitly relying on a hidden physicalist metaphysics? Then explain the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of the question.
