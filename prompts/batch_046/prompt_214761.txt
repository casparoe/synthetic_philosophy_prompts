As a retired field ecologist who has only recently begun auditing courses in philosophy of science and mathematical logic, I have been trying to discipline my intuitions about where Gödel's incompleteness theorems genuinely travel beyond arithmetic and where they are merely borrowed for rhetorical weight. I would like to know whether there is a unifying principle behind my scattered judgments, or whether I am simply inconsistent.

My reactions to analogies are these. First, the familiar argument that human cognition transcends computation because minds can recognize arithmetical truths that no formal system can prove: I find this suggestive but not decisive, since it seems to depend on an unstated claim about mental access that the theorem itself does not supply. Second, an extension of that reasoning to ethics—claiming that because moral evaluation involves self-reference, no formal ethical framework can be complete—strikes me as an obvious overreach; the analogy collapses once we notice that ethical reasoning lacks the precise syntax and consistency conditions Gödel requires. Third, a legal-theoretic version, in which statutes are Gödel-numbered and shown to contain legally undecidable cases, feels more structurally apt, yet my verdict remains that it is at best an illuminating metaphor rather than a logical derivation, because legal systems tolerate inconsistency in ways formal arithmetic does not. Fourth, I recently encountered in a theoretical-biology seminar a more niche claim: that evolutionary dynamics constitute a self-referential formal system, since organisms construct predictive models of the very selective pressures that shape them, and therefore no complete evolutionary theory can exist—there will always be adaptive outcomes the theory cannot derive. I want to reject this as a confusion between empirical open-endedness and logical incompleteness, but I hesitate, because biological self-reference seems structurally closer to arithmetic self-reference than legal self-reference does. Fifth, in physics, the suggestion that a final Theory of Everything is Gödel-impossible—because any formalism describing its own measurement apparatus can be arithmetized—leaves me genuinely oscillating; sometimes it strikes me as a category error (physics describes nature, not numbers), sometimes as a profound constraint.

Is there one principle, or a small family of precisely stated conditions, that distinguishes analogies inheriting Gödel's logical force from those that only appropriate its prestige? For each candidate principle, state which of my five verdicts it accommodates and which it must overturn, paying special attention to the evolutionary-biology case and the physics case where my judgment is split. Then apply your best principle to an unconsidered case: the argument that any complete generative grammar for a natural language must contain grammatically well-formed sentences that the grammar itself cannot classify as grammatical or ungrammatical. Finally, be explicit about whether accommodating all my reactions requires an ad hoc complexity that should lead me to revise one or more of them.
