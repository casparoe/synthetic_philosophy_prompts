The problem that interests me is overdetermination and multi-causal explanation in history—specifically, the observation that major historical outcomes (the collapse of a particular political order, the widespread adoption of a technological standard, or the shift in a scientific paradigm) are routinely explained by historians through long lists of economic, cultural, institutional, and individual causes, each of which might seem sufficient on its own. In my scientific background, I encountered a structurally similar puzzle in inverse problems: when observational data can be reproduced by many different parameter configurations, the system is degenerate, and one must invoke independent measurements or prior physical constraints to break the symmetry. I want to know whether historical overdetermination is genuinely analogous to this kind of degeneracy, or whether the multiplicity of historical causes belongs to a different logical category altogether.

My preliminary notes are as follows. First, historical explanations are often overdetermined only in a weak sense—several individually sufficient narratives can be constructed for the same event—but this does not entail that all the cited causes were actually operative together; narrative selection may obscure rather than reveal joint causation. Second, the analogy to inverse-problem degeneracy is instructive but limited, because historical causes are not quantitative parameters that can be varied continuously; they are framing-dependent descriptions whose relevance depends on the granularity of the historian’s account. Third, a closer analogy might be biological redundancy, where multiple pathways can produce the same phenotypic outcome, except that in history there is no “wild-type” baseline or experimental control against which to measure deviation, so we lack an independent criterion for pruning redundant explanations. Fourth, consequently, multi-causal historical explanation is not epistemically defective per se, but it requires explicit articulation of the counterfactual dependencies among the cited causes; without this, multiplicity collapses into vagueness rather than robust overdetermination.
