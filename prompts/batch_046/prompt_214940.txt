I am a high school senior in an advanced philosophy seminar, and last Tuesday two classmates—Jordan and Riley—became deadlocked over a conceptual puzzle about material constitution. They constructed a hypothetical installation called Flux: a large clay sculpture whose creator mandates that exactly one-tenth of its clay be replaced each year, with the removed clay fired and laid into the gallery floor. Jordan and Riley asked me to referee the exchange I reproduce below, and they want a point-by-point verdict rather than a vague overall opinion.

Jordan began with a strict identity thesis: at any moment the statue just is the particular clay arranged statue-wise, so gradual replacement eventually destroys the original and substitutes a numerically different object. He added that four-dimensionalism fails to rescue persistence, since on that view Flux is not one enduring thing but a scattered spacetime worm of disconnected clay-stages—hardly the unified artwork we evaluate. Riley replied with the standard modal argument—the clay could survive being squashed into a ball, but the statue could not, so they are not identical—and proposed a constitution view combined with a phase-sortal analysis, treating sculpture like tadpole or caterpillar: a temporary phase of an artistic entity unified by the creator's design protocol. Thus Flux survives replacement governed by the protocol, while the fired bricks fall outside it because they belong to the functional sortal floor-tile rather than sculptural-phase. Jordan countered that Riley smuggles intentionality into ontology: if the artist's plan determines membership, then constitution collapses into conventionalism, with no mind-independent fact about whether the bricks belong to Flux, and a proper metaphysics should depend only on spatiotemporal and causal relations. Riley answered that artifact kinds are inherently mind-involving—no purely physical feature distinguishes a statue from a random lump—so Jordan's demand for pure ontology is unrealistic; she also noted that his four-dimensionalist picture cannot explain why we hold the artist responsible for the completed work if it is never one object across time, but only a succession of stages.

Please adjudicate move by move, rating each argument from clearly mistaken to clearly right and briefly justifying each rating. Specifically, assess whether Riley's phase-sortal proposal genuinely solves the problem or merely relocates it; whether Jordan's charge of conventionalism hits its target; and whether the fired bricks create a scattered-object problem for either view. Also identify what both miss—particularly regarding sortal essentialism and design-dependent unity—and say who is more right overall. I ask that you write your answer in British English; I have composed this prompt in American English.
