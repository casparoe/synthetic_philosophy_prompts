I'm drafting an essay on whether Daniel Dennett's intentional stance can be coherently adopted toward large language models, and I'd like comments on the argument's coherence, its interest, and a specific conceptual move I'm trying to make.

Here are the notes for the essay's core line. Rather than asking whether LLMs "really" possess beliefs, I examine whether attributing beliefs to them yields predictive leverage that isn't already available from the design or physical stance. With LLMs, knowing the transformer architecture, the training objective, and the token-probability distribution already allows highly accurate prediction of outputs. The intentional stance therefore appears redundant: it redescribes statistical patterns in mentalistic vocabulary without adding predictive power.

But I think there's a deeper failure, which I call the problem of inferential anchoring. In human cognition, attributing one belief constrains which other beliefs can be consistently attributed, because human reasoning operates with a rough global consistency constraint. LLMs, by contrast, can be induced to affirm contradictory propositions in adjacent contexts without registering any internal tension. So belief-attribution to LLMs fails not merely because it duplicates the design stance, but because the attributed beliefs don't form a stable network: they are locally anchored to specific prompts but globally unanchored across contexts.

I then address an objection: we already apply the intentional stance instrumentally to artifacts like chess engines ("it wants to protect its queen"), and those attributions are also locally anchored. I reply that chess-engine attributions are harmless fictions confined to tightly bounded domains, whereas LLMs are deployed across open-ended domains. The lack of global anchoring therefore becomes a practical liability rather than a benign instrumental fiction.

Finally, I propose that this suggests a revision to the intentional stance itself. Predictive success may be necessary but insufficient; the stance might also require what I term cross-contextual stability—the expectation that an attributed mental state will constrain behavior in contexts that are not specifically engineered to trigger it.

I'd welcome focused feedback on three points. First, is the distinction between predictive redundancy and inferential anchoring genuinely distinct, or does it collapse into familiar skepticism about machine mentality? Second, does the comparison with chess engines unfairly downplay the domain-specificity of human belief? Third, and most specifically, is cross-contextual stability a defensible refinement of Dennett's framework, or does it smuggle in an essentialist requirement that the intentional stance was explicitly designed to avoid? I am also unsure whether the essay is interesting, or whether it merely restates well-known criticisms of anthropomorphizing AI in unnecessarily technical language. If the framework holds, what should it predict about belief-attribution to retrieval-augmented systems whose knowledge is partitioned across an external database rather than encoded in weights?
