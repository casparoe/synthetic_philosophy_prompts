I'm preparing for an independent study session on value theory, and one of my peers offered a summary of what she called "lexical threshold negative utilitarianism" together with its stance on the commensurability of pleasure and pain. She said the view starts from the denial that pleasure and pain can be mapped onto a single cardinal scale, but she insisted this is not a metaphysical claim about qualitatively different kinds of experience; rather, it is a structural feature of value aggregation that activates only once suffering crosses a specific intensity threshold. Below that threshold, she explained, ordinary aggregative reasoning applies without restriction: pleasures and pains can be summed, compared, and traded off in the familiar utilitarian fashion. Once the threshold is crossed, however, suffering acquires lexical priority, so that no quantity of pleasure, however vast, can justify permitting or causing suffering of that magnitude. She then argued that this hybrid framework neatly dissolves the classic extinction objection to negative utilitarianism, because once all suffering has been suppressed below the threshold there remains no further moral mandate to eliminate sentient life altogether, leaving us free to pursue positive goods. Finally, she concluded that the thesis of incommensurability is therefore purely pragmatic—a boundary condition on rational choice—rather than a deep ontological statement about the nature of affective experience. I am inclined to think she has made the view sound more coherent than it is, but I do not have the philosophical vocabulary to locate the errors precisely. Would you audit her summary for me? Please separate what is faithful to the position as serious defenders present it, what subtly conflates lexical ordering with threshold conditions or mischaracterizes the extinction argument, and whether her conclusion about the purely structural, non-metaphysical status of incommensurability actually follows from the premises she laid out.
