So I was at this rationalist meetup last thursday — well, more like a post-meetup dinner at a noisy diner — and Jordan and Riley got into a pretty intense back-and-forth over Browning's study of Reserve Battalion 101 versus Goldhagen's argument about eliminationist antisemitism, but they weren't rehashing the usual situation-versus-culture summary. Jordan said Browning's whole point is essentially situationist: peer pressure, incremental orders, bureaucratic distancing, all the classic social psych mechanisms. So Goldhagen's claim that ordinary Germans were already soaked in a deep cultural script is redundant — the situation explains the killing without needing some uniform eliminationist belief. Riley pushed back hard. She pointed out that Browning's own data shows about 10-15 percent refused to participate, and refusal was usually tolerated with no real punishment. If the situation was genuinely determinative, she asked, why did anyone opt out? Riley thinks whats actually going on isnt "character" in the virtue-ethics sense, but something like moral self-conception stability under role fragmentation — the capacity to keep your narrative identity intact when an institution splits action from intention. She said Goldhagen gets the cultural availability right but treats culture as a uniform field rather than a selective filter; the variance in behavior comes from this stability trait, not from differences in antisemitic conviction.

Jordan came back saying Riley is just smuggling interactionism back in under a fancy name. Browning explicitly rejects trait explanations, and even that refusal rate is exactly what situationism predicts if you allow for individual differences in situational resistance — thats still person-situation interaction, not proof of deep belief driving the choice. Then Riley said Jordan is conflating situationism with interactionism, and that if you actually look at the refusal patterns they cluster around people who had prior non-Nazi social identities they could fall back on, which sounds more like narrative stability than generic resistance. Jordan replied that falling back on a social identity is itself a situational resource, not an internal trait, so Riley hasn't escaped the framework she claims to reject.

They turned to me and asked who was right, and I honestly couldn't call it. Can you go through thier exchange step by step and say who's right about which specific point — especially whether Riley's "moral self-conception stability" idea is doing real explanatory work or just relabeling situationism with extra steps?
