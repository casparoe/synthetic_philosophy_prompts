I am convening an advanced seminar on the ethics of public memory, and I should like you to write a formal essay addressing a specific controversy within that field. In recent debates over commemorative statues and street names, discussion has tended to oscillate between a conservative appeal to historical continuity and a radical-democratic demand for the removal of symbols that perpetuate hermeneutical injustice. I want you to examine a less discussed intermediate proposal—what might be called "mnemonic zoning"—according to which a municipality retains controversial monuments and toponyms in place, but surrounds them with counter-monumental installations and dynamically updated interpretive framing that responds to evolving community sentiment through participatory, non-commercial protocols. Your essay should weigh whether this approach genuinely remedies the moral wrong of commemorative exclusion, or whether it merely aestheticises injustice by substituting adaptive signage for structural transformation. You should consider objections drawn from the ethics of recognition, the politics of spatial justice, and the risk that algorithmically mediated memory-work depoliticises collective grievance.

Reach and justify a clear conclusion on the moral legitimacy of mnemonic zoning as a policy for contested urban heritage. Please compose your response in British English, and do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citations; I am concerned solely with the rigour and originality of your argument.
