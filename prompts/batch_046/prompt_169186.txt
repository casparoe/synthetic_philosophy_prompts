In debates about whether philosophy contributes anything to scientific practice, critics have often relied on analogies rather than detailed arguments. One recurring version, associated with Feynman, compares the philosopher of science to an ornithologist studying birds: the scientist is the bird in flight, the philosopher watches from the ground, and the flight proceeds perfectly well without the observer. Critics in a similar vein—Weinberg treating philosophy as an obscuring hindrance rather than an illuminating tool, Hawking describing it as a dead discipline superseded by physics—have pressed the same conclusion from different images. Defenders reply with analogies of their own: navigation, cartography, or conceptual debugging.

Your task is to audit the ornithology analogy specifically, not to defend philosophy in general. First, lay out the correspondence table precisely: what plays the role of the ornithologist, the bird, flight, the evolutionary history being studied, the observational instruments, and the absence of feedback from study to flight? Second, identify which correspondences are structurally accurate and which are quietly imported: for instance, birds do not study ornithology, but scientists do sometimes study philosophy; ornithology describes flight mechanisms, yet philosophy of science often concerns norms of inference and conceptual coherence rather than descriptive taxonomy; flight succeeds or fails independently of observation, yet scientific practice is partly constituted by the very concepts and distinctions it employs. Third, separate the disanalogies that actually undermine the conclusion—that philosophy is irrelevant to scientific practice—from those that merely show the analogy is imperfect. Fourth, compare the argumentative work this analogy does with the images used by Weinberg and Hawking: does an obscuring-hindrance analogy import a different set of assumptions about interaction (interference versus irrelevance), and does the dead-discipline analogy rely on a historical-progress narrative that the ornithology analogy avoids? Can any of these three analogies support the dismissal they are used to license once the disanalogies are made explicit? Finally, say whether a better analogy is available to either side—one that captures the actual relation without smuggling in false features of the source domain—and whether, once all three are audited together, any of them survives as a reason to exclude philosophy from scientific training or institutional support.
