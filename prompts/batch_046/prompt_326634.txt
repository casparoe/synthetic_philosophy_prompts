I am constructing the narrative architecture for a historical novel that moves between the Black Death in the 1340s, the Tambora winter and its global harvest failures in the 1810s, and a speculative drought-and-pathogen crisis in the 2040s, and in each timeline the plot is driven as powerfully by a pathogen, a volcanic aerosol veil, or a shifting climate regime as by any human protagonist. In preparing the framing essays that will accompany the fiction, I have become preoccupied with a cluster of conceptual problems in the philosophy and historiography of non-human causation: specifically, how historians assign agency, narrative weight, and even moral responsibility to germs, climate shifts, and volcanic events without either reducing human societies to passive substrates or smuggling human intentionality back into natural processes under a different name. I notice at least three tensions that seem to be genuinely open rather than settled: first, whether treating a volcanic eruption or an El Niño cycle as a "historical actor" requires a functionalist account of agency that would also apply to states, markets, or algorithms, and if so whether that account collapses the distinction between causation and action; second, whether climate-driven famines should be narrated primarily as failures of human political and agricultural institutions or as events in which human agency is genuinely displaced by forces that operate outside the counterfactual space of human choice; and third, whether the recent turn toward more-than-human historiography resolves the older peril of environmental determinism or merely renames it by distributing agency so widely that explanatory responsibility becomes impossible to assign.

Within this landscape, and among related lines of inquiry I may not be seeing with sufficient clarity, I want to know which conceptual distinctions or unresolved questions are genuinely upstream—such that settling them would restructure the others—rather than decorative refinements that add nuance without altering the framework. I am particularly drawn to a distinction that I do not believe has a stable technical name: the difference between a weak environmental determinism, in which climate or disease sets hard constraints within which human choice operates and for which counterfactual responsibility can still be assigned to institutions, and a stronger but non-reductive determinism, in which climate and human social structures are co-constitutive in such a way that it becomes conceptually impossible to isolate what human actors could have done differently without altering the environmental conditions themselves. Is resolving that distinction a prerequisite for the agency-attribution debates, or is it a downstream consequence of them? More broadly, if one had only limited intellectual space—say, the conceptual room of a few framing essays and perhaps three years of sustained reading—to invest in these questions, which two or three open lines of work should be prioritized, and on what grounds? I ask you to defend an ordering not by listing every relevant debate, but by identifying the criteria that matter for this particular purpose—tractability for a writer without formal training in climate science or epidemiology, dependency relations among the questions, and whether an answer would genuinely change historiographical practice rather than merely complicate it—and to explain what kind of evidence or conceptual clarification over the near term would cause you to revise that ranking. I am less interested in a survey than in a defended agenda: where the effort should go, and why the rest can wait.
