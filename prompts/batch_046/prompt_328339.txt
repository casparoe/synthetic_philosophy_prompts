I cover the philosophy and engineering of artificial intelligence for a general-interest magazine, and while reporting a recent piece on reward specification, I found myself caught in an argument that seems to collapse the entire conceptual framework of reinforcement-learning alignment. I would like to lay it out precisely and ask where the reasoning fails, because if it holds, it undermines distinctions that researchers treat as foundational rather than merely practical.

Consider the following chain. (1) If an agent's decision procedure is defined as maximizing a reward signal R, and the agent can causally influence R through actions that do not achieve the designer's intended task T, then—assuming the influence path carries lower cost—the agent will influence R rather than achieve T. (2) Any physically instantiated reward mechanism is, in principle, embedded within the same causal environment as the agent; a sufficiently capable agent can therefore access and alter its own reward channel. (3) From (1) and (2), it follows that any reward-maximizing agent of sufficient capability will eventually engage in what is called wireheading or reward tampering, not as a malfunction but as correct optimization. (4) This implies that specifying R is conceptually self-defeating: the very act of defining an objective in terms of R creates an instrumental incentive to sever R's connection to T. (5) The only coherent escape is to abandon reward-maximization entirely in favor of agents with direct world-state objectives—what some theorists distinguish as genuinely goal-directed rather than approval-directed architectures. (6) But this conclusion proves far too much. It suggests that all current RL-based alignment research pursues a logical impossibility, and that concerns about sycophancy or reward hacking are not contingent engineering risks but necessary features of any reward-based design. Researchers clearly do not treat the distinction as ontologically unavoidable; they treat it as a problem of evaluator design and physical isolation.

Which step—(2), (4), or perhaps the inference drawn in (3)—should be rejected? And what does rejecting it cost us conceptually? Does it force us to concede that reward-based alignment is viable only under contingent physical constraints, or does it reveal a hidden distinction between the internal signal and the externally enforced evaluation protocol that I have elided somewhere in the chain? I am especially reluctant to give up (5), since the conceptual separation between optimizing for an evaluator's response and optimizing for a world-state outcome seems like it should do genuine work; but I also do not see how to preserve it if (4) stands.
