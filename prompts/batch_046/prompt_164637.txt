I've been thinking about those virtue-education programs that still use Plutarch-style parallel lives as a core text, but they've updated the pedagogy in this weird modular way. They have students read a biography—say, of a reformer or military leader—and then fill out a "virtue extraction" worksheet where you list the subject's courage, prudence, and so on in one column, but there's a seperate box for "historical context" where you dump all the moral failures, and the rubric says those failures don't subtract from the exemplar score at all. The program's handbook literally says biography should inspire emulation rather than invite judgment, and they defend this by saying if you mix good and bad together students just get cynical and learn nothing. Meanwhile they also claim they're teaching whole-person moral formation, not just sanitized hero-worship, and they explicitly say they reject old-fashioned hagiography.

What philosophical commitments about moral learning does that institutional setup actually presuppose? Does it only make sense if virtues are kind of modular—like you can lift courage out of one life and ignore the cruelty it was bundled with? Or does it depend on a view that moral knowledge is basically mimetic, so seeing a model works even if you don't understand the narrative context? There's also this tension: they say they're against hagiography, but the worksheet structure seems to build hagiography right into the method by design. Can the practice be consistent with itself, or does it quietly rely on two incompatible theories of what biography is for—one where lives are sources of extractable traits, and one where they're narratives you enter into? And if we replaced the implicit theory with a straightforwardly narrative or historical one, where understanding a virtue requires understanding the vices it was entangled with, what exactly in the classroom practice and in the rubric would have to change?
