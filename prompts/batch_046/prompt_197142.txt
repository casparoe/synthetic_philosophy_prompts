I've been arguing with my roommate about police abolition and I keep hitting this same wall in the argument that I don't have a word for. The position is that we should eliminate police departments entirely, but also block private security firms, armed landlords, or neighborhood militias from filling the gap - so it's not just breaking the state's monopoly on violence, it's refusing to let violence become privatized or marketized either. What's left is this strange empty instutional space where coersive force is supposed to just... not exist? But then when I ask what stops a corporation from hiring thugs to break a strike in that world, the answer is usually something like "that wouldn't happen if workers owned the means of production" - which feels like it's solving an instutional problem with a sociological precondition rather than a mechanism. I see the same shape in prison abolition debates where people reject both state prisons and private detention centers, leaving nothing but transformed social relations to prevent harm.

Is there an established concept for this specific move - not just "power vacuum" since that implies someone rushes in to fill it, and not exactly "utopianism" since the claim is about institutional design? I'm wondering if there's a term for the double rejection where you abolish both the state institution and its private substitutes, then defend the resulting gap by saying the conditions that required enforcement will disappear. How is that seperate from ordinary functional replacement, and does it have a specific name in political philosophy? If not, what should I call it?
