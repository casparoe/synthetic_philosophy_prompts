For a seminar on well-being and the experience machine, I have been working with a modified case study that moves beyond Nozick’s original plug-in scenario. I call it the Parallel Archive: a neuro-adaptive system that does not replace waking life, but instead generates immersive, emotionally complete episodes—reconciliation with an estranged child, the completion of a long-abandoned creative work, moments of unambiguous moral courage—during sleep and other interstitial periods of consciousness. The user wakes with intact memories of these episodes and full knowledge that they were synthesized from their own psychological profile; they can modulate frequency and intensity, but not the specific narratives, which adapt dynamically to unconscious needs. Users consistently report that waking life feels comparatively thin, yet they score very high on global life-satisfaction measures, because the Archive supplies the narrative peaks and emotional resolutions that ordinary biography rarely provides.

Please write a short story—approximately 600 to 900 words—about a single long-term user of the Parallel Archive. The narrative should turn on a concrete decision: whether to maintain, reduce, or abandon the Archive in response to a crisis in unsimulated life, such as a real relationship that demands sustained, unrewarded effort or a professional failure that cannot be redeemed inside the system. The story should embed, through action and dialogue rather than exposition, at least two competing theories of well-being: for instance, a hedonist defense that values the Archive for its reliable production of positive mental states; an objective-list objection that synthesized achievements and relationships lack the causal history and external reality that make goods genuinely beneficial; and a desire-satisfaction concern that knowing the episodes are artificial undermines their value even when first-order desires are fulfilled. Let the protagonist’s choice—or refusal to choose—illustrate whether well-being is best understood as an aggregate of discrete experiential episodes, as a property of an integrated diachronic narrative, or as something that requires causal interaction with an independent world. Do not resolve the philosophical tension with an explicit moral; instead, allow the story’s formal structure—its distribution of narrative weight between archived and waking scenes—to carry the conceptual argument. I will use the best responses to ask students whether the protagonist’s life is going well, and on which theory.
