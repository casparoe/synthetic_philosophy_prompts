I am preparing a lecture for an interdisciplinary seminar on economic ethics, and I need to test whether classical frameworks can still illuminate emerging institutional forms. Consider a transnational clearing mechanism—operating without human creditors—where sovereign currencies are pooled into algorithmically governed liquidity baskets; parity is maintained not by interest-bearing loans but by continuous automatic rebasing, diluting or concentrating holdings in real time according to trade-flow imbalances. Proponents argue this eliminates chrematistic profit, since no party lends at interest, yet participants accumulate exchange-value purely through algorithmic positioning rather than productive labor. Drawing on Aristotle's distinction between household management and the art of wealth acquisition, his view that money is a convention for exchange rather than a source of unnatural growth, and his account of justice in exchange as proportional reciprocity, reconstruct how he would likely assess this arrangement. Would he consider the absence of explicit interest sufficient to render the practice natural, or would he identify a distinct form of unnatural acquisition in the extraction of surplus from circulation itself? Address the strongest counter-argument—that because the mechanism facilitates necessary exchange without hoarding metallic currency, it fulfills money's proper telos—and present the reconstruction as reasoned speculation rather than historical attribution.
