I am preparing a 90-minute workshop for an upper-level undergraduate seminar in moral philosophy — about sixteen students, seated in a movable-seating room — and I want them to feel the joint bite of Hume’s is-ought gap and Moore’s naturalistic fallacy rather than merely reciting the labels. A colleague who teaches formal logic remarked that students can paraphrase the guillotine without ever sensing where the blade actually falls, so I would like a runnable classroom activity in which the group must collectively author a binding “code of fair allocation” governing a tangible shared resource: specifically, a budget of tokens that fixes the order in which each student may choose a final-presentation topic, with the hard constraint that no one may be assigned a topic they have already ruled out. The rules must permit participants to justify every normative clause using only empirical, descriptive premises — evolutionary patterns, psychological measurements, neurological correlates, or observed cooperative behaviour — and the design should include a hidden mechanism: each student receives, without disclosure, a private “calibration card” that assigns a different normative weight to the very same empirical fact (so that elevated oxytocin, for instance, might read to one card as “trustworthy, hence deserving priority” and to another as “merely biochemical, hence irrelevant to desert”). This should force the group to discover that descriptive premises alone underdetermine the normative conclusion and that any bridge they construct requires smuggling in an unstated value. Please provide the full rules, the materials required, a precise timeline for the session, the minor-stakes payoff scheme, and a debrief that maps specific moments in the activity onto Hume’s distinction between relations of ideas and matters of fact on the one hand, and Moore’s open-question argument on the other. I also want an honest note on what will predictably go wrong — for example, participants collapsing the distinction by appealing to “human flourishing” or “what works” without noticing the normative load — together with advice on how to handle it in the moment, and a frank admission of which feature of the gap or fallacy the simulation cannot reproduce and the facilitator must supply explicitly. The answer should be written in British English.
