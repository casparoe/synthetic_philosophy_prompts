Draft an adversarial-collaboration statement that a committed textualist and a committed purposivist could both sign, restricting the collaboration to the ontological problem of what a statute is when the mental states and collective intentions behind its enactment are epistemically inaccessible to the interpreting court. First, provide a numbered list of agreed claims about enacted texts, about whether collective legislative mental states exist, about the distinction between observing textual properties and inferring unobservable purposive properties, and about the general limits of judicial access to such hidden states. Second, formulate the residual disagreements in wording that each camp would accept for its own side, specifically addressing whether a statute’s identity conditions depend on properties that are in principle unobservable, and whether interpreting the statute requires identifying an object whose ontology is partly constituted by those hidden properties. Third, identify the cruxes; take them to include whether legislative “purpose” operates as an observable feature of the text and its context or as a theoretical posit required to fix the statute’s ontology, and whether breaking symmetry between equally textually and purposively plausible readings demands an ontological commitment that textualism can genuinely avoid. Fourth, for each crux, present one concrete interpretive scenario or conceptual argument that both sides agree is relevant, recording in advance what each camp predicts the correct resolution should be. Fifth, present the remaining disagreements in a table with columns for the crux, the textualist formulation, and the purposivist formulation. The statement should not request citations, bibliographies, reading lists, or references to specific judicial opinions; reason instead from the conceptual architecture of statutory interpretation and from the epistemology of identifying objects through inaccessible information.
