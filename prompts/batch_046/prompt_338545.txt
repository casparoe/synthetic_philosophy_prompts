I cover the ethics of public memory and commemorative naming for a general audience, and I have encountered a recurring argumentative pattern that I cannot place in the existing literature. The pattern arises in disputes over whether to retain a street name, monument, or institutional title tied to a figure whose legacy now includes actions widely regarded as morally grave.

In these disputes, defenders of retention often argue that the name has undergone what linguists call semantic bleaching: for present residents, “Mercer Street” denotes only a geographic location, not the person after whom it was named, so the honorific charge has dissipated and retention is morally neutral. Yet the same defenders frequently resist substituting the name with that of a different figure—say, a local civil-rights organizer—on the ground that such a substitution would constitute an explicit political judgment, whereas keeping the original is allegedly apolitical. Critics then reply that if the original name is truly bleached, replacing it should be harmless, since the geographic reference can be preserved under a new label. At this point, defenders sometimes revert to treating the original name as honorifically loaded, warning that removal would constitute a deliberate repudiation of the figure. Thus the name is treated as symbolically inert when that inertia supports keeping it, but symbolically charged when that charge supports resisting its replacement.

This is not simple hypocrisy, because the two claims operate in different argumentative contexts—preservation versus substitution—and each can be defended with locally plausible premises. Nor is it exactly the familiar motte-and-bailey maneuver, since the two positions are not a defensible core and an indefensible extension; rather, they are contradictory assessments of the same symbolic property applied asymmetrically according to the direction of proposed change. Does this configuration have an established name in philosophy, rhetoric, or cultural studies? How precisely does it differ from ordinary strategic ambiguity about symbolic meaning, and if it lacks a specific term, what formal conditions—perhaps relating to the directionality of substitution—would be needed to distinguish it from generic argumentative inconsistency?
