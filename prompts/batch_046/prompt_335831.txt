I'm writing the final for an upper-level philosophy of mind and computation seminar. I need one essay question that students can answer in about 90 minutes (target length around 800 words). The point is to see whether they can think through a constructed case rather than recite summaries of physical computation or symbol-grounding debates.

Please embed the following hypothetical scenario directly into the exam prompt: There is a substrate called careenium, composed of nodes that can be in either a bound or free state. Within it sit discrete spherical tokens called simmballs. Each simmball carries a symbolic value—say, 0 or 1—but is physically identical regardless of value: same mass, charge, and mechanical coupling to the nodes. The nodes transition according to deterministic dynamics that read the symbolic value of any adjacent simmball: when the value is 1, the node releases thermal energy into the lattice; when 0, it absorbs energy. The symbol itself has no physical properties beyond those carried by the token.

The exam question should ask students to evaluate whether, in this setup, the symbol genuinely "pushes the substrate around"—that is, whether the symbolic content is causally responsible for the heat-flow pattern—or whether all causal work is done by the physical properties of the nodes and the token's presence, with the symbol being only a descriptive overlay. The question must require students to do three things: (i) distinguish causal relevance at the implementation level from causal efficacy of the symbol qua symbol; (ii) assess whether the scenario poses a serious problem for substrate-independent theories of computation or for non-reductive physicalism, and say what a defender of each view would have to claim; (iii) identify one point in this area that is genuinely contested among specialists—such as whether downward causation requires a non-physical causal power, or whether computation is necessarily multiply realizable—and one point that is largely settled, being explicit about which is which and never presenting the contested point as if it were consensus.

Please deliver:
1. The exam question itself, clearly worded with the careenium/simmball scenario included.
2. A model answer of about 800 words.
3. A marking scheme built around five must-hit points, with the marks each is worth (total 20), plus a one-line note on how candidates typically miss or misunderstand each point.

Constraints: Do not ask for bibliographies, citations, or reading lists. Do not quote specific texts; paraphrase any reference to standard positions. Keep the difficulty in analyzing the constructed case, not in recalling niche literature. In both the model answer and the marking scheme, clearly separate what is settled from what is genuinely contested, and do not present any contested interpretation as the consensus view.
