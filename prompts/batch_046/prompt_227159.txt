In debates about skill and cognition, a familiar analogy compares knowing-how to riding a bicycle and knowing-that to owning a repair manual: one is an embodied capacity that operates without explicit representation, the other is a discursive resource you can consult, share, or forget independently of your body. This comparison is frequently used to defend a sharp divide—procedural knowledge is non-propositional, resistant to full verbal articulation, and acquired only through practice—while propositional knowledge is symbolically encoded and transferable by testimony alone. Audit this analogy rigorously. First, provide an explicit correspondence table: what in the target domain plays the role of the rider, the bicycle, the manual, the mechanic, the sense of balance, and the crash? Second, identify precisely where the mapping fractures—for example, expert cyclists often can and do verbalize rules, manuals can become so thoroughly internalized that they function like reflex, and some propositional knowledge (e.g., knowing that one city lies north of another) seems as automatically available as balance. Third, ask which features of the source domain the analogy quietly imports: does it smuggle in an individualist picture of the knower, a false body-symbol dichotomy, or an unjustified privilege for first-person access? Fourth, determine whether the conclusion—that knowing-how is irreducibly distinct from and not reducible to knowing-that—survives once these disanalogies are fully registered, or whether the analogy itself was carrying the argumentative weight. Finally, if the bicycle-manual comparison collapses under scrutiny, sketch a better analogy for the relationship between the two forms of knowledge, one that accommodates both their differences and their frequent entanglement in actual human competence.
