I serve on the Legislative Defense Reform Panel reviewing the proposed Armed Neutrality and Civic Obligation Act. The bill would abolish universal conscription and replace it with a tiered “Strategic Latency” framework: a small professional force maintains peacetime readiness; a selective reserve of technically trained citizens holds active status; and the general citizenry bears a dormant mobilization obligation that activates solely upon a formal, executive-certified breach of our armed neutrality. The design explicitly borrows from Swedish selective conscription, Finnish territorial reserve structures, and Swiss militia decentralization, yet it eliminates the Swiss baseline of universal service and substitutes a conditional, probabilistic burden for a direct one. The Panel must issue a formal recommendation—approve, reject, or approve with binding conditions—within thirty days, accompanied by an implementation timeline. The philosophical tension is whether democratic equality in defense requires that the risk of service be distributed uniformly across citizens, or whether strategic efficiency and individual liberty can justify a system in which most people bear only a latent obligation whose actualization depends on an executive determination rather than a legislative declaration. Draft the Panel’s decision memo: state a clear recommendation with specific conditions or phases if approval is conditional; engage seriously with the argument that latency preserves both neutrality credibility and civic equality by making the burden universal in principle but differentiated in practice; and address the strongest counter-argument that a dormant obligation lacks the transparency, reciprocity, and predictability required for genuine democratic consent, particularly when activation hinges on a contested judgment about the status of neutrality rather than an unambiguous act of aggression.
