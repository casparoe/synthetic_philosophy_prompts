In my seminar on collective memory and the politics of defeat, we have been comparing how militarily defeated groups construct origin myths—specifically the Confederate Lost Cause, the Masada narrative in Zionist historiography, and the Kosovo Field myth in Serbian nationalism. In each case, the course framework identifies a shared structure: the defeat is not denied but inverted, so that the enemy’s victory is attributed to overwhelming force or betrayal, turning loss into evidence of moral exceptionalism. This term I have also been examining the British commemorative framing of Dunkirk, which is consistently described not as a defeat but as a strategic withdrawal that preserved the army. Despite this, I keep encountering comparative scholarship that treats Dunkirk as part of the same analytical family of “defeat myths” that includes Masada and Kosovo. This strikes me as incoherent. If the category of lost-cause mythology requires an actual defeat to be inverted, then Dunkirk—which was an evacuation rather than a lost battle—should be excluded; yet if scholars do include it, then the category must depend on something other than defeat itself, perhaps on the structural move of converting extreme vulnerability into founding virtue regardless of military outcome. So my question is this: is the concept of lost-cause mythology actually anchored in defeat, or is it anchored in the transformation of trauma into national virtue? And if it is the latter, does the category not collapse into a general theory of national mythmaking that would also cover victorious origins—say, the American Revolution—whenever they contain sufficient trauma? I am trying to determine whether my confusion stems from misreading the comparative literature, or whether the literature is indeed using “lost cause” in two incompatible senses, one tied to defeat and one tied to vulnerability.
