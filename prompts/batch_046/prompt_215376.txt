Next week I present to a philosophy of economics workshop my defense of a triadic reconstruction of Keynes: alongside risk and radical uncertainty, there is structural ambiguity, defined as the situation in which agents share the same probability distribution over outcomes but embed it in incompatible causal structures, so that interpersonal aggregation fails not for epistemic but for ontological reasons. The audience is precisely composed of three interlocutors: a Bayesian decision theorist who specializes in imprecise probabilities and hierarchical modeling, a historian of economic thought who demands strict textual fidelity to Keynes's Treatise on Probability and rejects anachronistic formalizations, and a macroeconomist who builds DSGE models with stochastic volatility and regards Keynesian uncertainty as a tractable friction parameter. I need you to prepare me for the question period by listing the objections in roughly the order they will come, phrased in the voice of each specialist, together with what I should concede, what I should hold firm on, the weakest point in my own reply that I must defend rather than hide, and the single objection I am praying nobody raises. In doing so, clearly distinguish what is settled in the literature from what remains genuinely contested among knowledgeable interpreters, and do not present a disputed reading as if it were consensus. Deliver the result as exactly five numbered items, each consisting of one or two sentences, with no introductory or concluding text.
