After thirty years in condensed-matter physics, I have recently turned to philosophy of science and find myself returning repeatedly to the fine-tuning argument—not as a theologian, but as someone struck by the conceptual architecture of probabilistic inference across domains. I want to understand which open conceptual questions in this debate are genuinely upstream, in the sense that resolving them would reshape how we think about observer selection, physical law, and scientific explanation, rather than merely deciding which side “wins.” To avoid the usual rehearsed moves, I am framing the problem through a specific analogy that I have not seen developed in the literature: cosmological observation may be subject to a selection effect structurally analogous to Berkson’s paradox in epidemiology, where sampling conditions distort apparent correlations. If we observe constants compatible with life only because such regions alone permit observers, does that undermine the inference to design or multiverse, or does it merely mirror the routine selection biases we correct for in cohort studies? Alongside this, I consider two further tensions central: the circularity involved in defining “life-permitting” ranges when our sole sample is carbon-based and Earth-conditioned; and whether invoking a multiverse to dissolve fine-tuning commits us to a probabilistic framework—specifically a conceptually coherent solution to the measure problem—that does not presuppose the very physical laws that vary across worlds.

Given this framing, I am asking you to advise a small interdisciplinary working group—one philosopher of science, one cosmologist, and one evolutionary biologist—who have three years and limited funding. Which three open conceptual questions or lines of work should they prioritize, in what order, and why? I want a defended agenda, not a survey. Please lead with your bottom line: state the ranked priorities and the single governing criterion for the ordering in your opening paragraph. Then, in no more than 500 words total, defend the ranking by explaining for each item: (i) how it is upstream of the others; (ii) whether it is tractable for philosophers and scientists without requiring new empirical data; and (iii) what kind of conceptual result or argument over the next few years would cause you to reorder the list. Do not provide a bibliography, reading list, or citations; I want reasoning that stands on its own. I am particularly interested in whether the Berkson-analogy selection-effect problem should be treated as a preliminary methodological correction or as a deep challenge to the argument’s validity—so please address that explicitly in your defense.
