I keep noticing a pattern in risk assessment and forecasting debates that I cannot map cleanly onto any term I already know. An analyst predicts an outcome for a specific case—say, that a particular startup will fail within five years—by appealing to a broad reference class with a well-known base rate. Once the outcome is known, if it contradicts her prediction, she does not defend the broad class; instead, she relocates the case into a narrower, equally real subclass—“early-stage biotech with that specific capital structure”—where the opposite result is the statistical norm, thereby preserving her reasoning framework intact. Had the outcome matched her prediction, she might have retained the broad class, or switched to an even narrower one that highlights her foresight. The subclasses are not invented from whole cloth; they are genuine distinctions that are selectively activated after the fact, and the migration consistently protects the analyst’s prior judgment from falsification. This is not exactly the Texas sharpshooter fallacy, since the categories exist independently and are not fabricated solely to fit the data point; nor is it ordinary hindsight bias, because the move is an explicit statistical reframing rather than a mere feeling of inevitability; and while it touches the reference-class problem, that problem is usually framed as a prospective ambiguity about which class to apply, not as a strategic, retrospective oscillation between valid classes. Is there an established concept—perhaps in forecasting methodology, epistemology, or decision theory—for this protective, post-hoc reference-class migration? If there is no precise name, can you coin one with a clear definition and boundary conditions, specifying exactly how it differs from the Texas sharpshooter fallacy, from hindsight bias, and from the standard reference-class problem, and identifying what feature distinguishes it from plain ad hoc rationalization?
