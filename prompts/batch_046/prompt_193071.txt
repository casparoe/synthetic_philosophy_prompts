For a graduate seminar on risk ethics and long-term futures, I want the full specification before I assess any particular framework. What must an adequate theory of suffering risks—risks of extreme, large-scale, or structurally novel suffering—actually deliver? List the desiderata: the boundary cases it must cover (biological sentience, non-biological substrates, distributed experience architectures, and what I will call depth-scaling cases in which suffering transforms qualitatively rather than merely quantitatively); the conceptual distinctions it must preserve (between risk magnitude, risk type, moral urgency, and the identity conditions of a sufferer); the results from philosophy of mind and value theory with which it must remain consistent; and, for each, whether it is non-negotiable or camp-specific. In particular, the framework must address fragmentation risks, where suffering is instantiated across functionally disconnected modules rather than within a unified subject. Identify the single desideratum that eliminates the largest number of popular approaches—such as standard expected-utility aggregation or longtermist quantitative frameworks—and explain why. A table flagging negotiability is welcome. Only after establishing these criteria, briefly score a rights-based threshold account, a preference-satisfaction model, and a capacity-focused sentience framework against them; keep the analytical weight on the requirements themselves, not the scorecard.
