Suppose you are advising a small philosophy research group with three years of funding and no empirical laboratory, whose explicit aim is to clarify the conceptual architecture of lexical-threshold negative utilitarianism and the related problem of whether pleasure and pain are commensurable across different kinds of subjects. The group can pursue theoretical work only—conceptual analysis, formal modeling of decision rules, and normative argumentation—and they want a defended agenda rather than a literature survey.

Identify the three to five open conceptual questions or lines of work at this intersection that most deserve their effort, ordered by priority. For each, explain why it is upstream of the others, how tractable it is for philosophers working without empirical data, and what kind of conceptual result over the next few years would either elevate or demote it. In particular, weigh the following competing considerations: whether suffering and pleasure can be mapped onto a common cardinal scale even within a single subject; whether a defensible lexical threshold should be indexed to raw affective intensity, to meta-cognitive suffering (suffering about one’s own suffering), or to some other structural feature of experience; whether lexical-threshold negative utilitarianism entails that any world containing even one instance of sufficiently severe suffering is worse than any world with arbitrarily large pleasure but none of that suffering; and how these answers bear on practical prioritization between reducing extreme suffering in present biological organisms and preventing potential suffering in future digital or synthetic minds that may lack the same meta-cognitive architecture.

Defend your ordering with explicit criteria—dependency on other questions, tractability for pure conceptual work, neglectedness within normative ethics, and relevance for longtermist resource allocation—and specify what argument or conceptual distinction would cause you to reorder the priorities. Do not provide bibliographies, reading lists, or citations.
