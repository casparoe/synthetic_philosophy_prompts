I am currently in my final year of secondary school, and after an extended discussion with my friends about evolutionary ethics, I have developed a research idea that I wish to formalise into a proposal for a one-semester independent study project of approximately 5,000 words. Rather than rehearsing the familiar argument about whether Darwin was a moral realist or an evolutionary anti-realist, I want to examine a more specific tension within Darwin's own account in The Descent of Man. I propose that Darwin treats the 'moral sense' not merely as an instinct refined by social selection, but as what I call a 'normative buffer': a second-order cognitive mechanism that converts group-beneficial instincts into judgments experienced as categorically binding. At the same time, Darwin explains religious belief as the misapplication of very similar instinctive machinery toward supernatural objects. My central question is whether this structural symmetry undermines the authority of moral judgments: if both morality and religion arise from the same evolutionary source, but Darwin dismisses religion as an erroneous projection while retaining moral authority, does his framework contain the resources to justify that asymmetry, or does it collapse moral bindingness into mere social utility?

Please write the proposal. It should state the question precisely, explain why existing interpretations of Darwin — whether as a straightforward evolutionary ethicist or as a simple reductionist — fail to settle this particular tension. Outline an approach that proceeds philosophically rather than empirically: for instance, analysing the conceptual architecture of Darwin's distinctions between instinct, habit, and moral reflection, and testing whether the 'normative buffer' can be coherently separated from the 'projective error' he attributes to religion. Describe what a successful result would look like — perhaps a reconstructed Darwinian position that either preserves moral authority without supernatural grounding, or demonstrates that Darwin's own premises commit him to a more radical scepticism than is usually recognised — and state clearly what would count as failure, such as the argument collapsing into a generic evolutionary debunking claim that ignores Darwin's specific texts. Finally, provide an honest assessment of whether this is too large for a single semester, and suggest a realistic scope that keeps the project tractable without reducing it to a summary. I will add citations and bibliographic references myself, so please do not include a reading list. The answer should be written in British English.
