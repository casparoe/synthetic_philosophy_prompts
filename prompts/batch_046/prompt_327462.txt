I am preparing a graduate seminar on collective identity and historical ontology, and I want us to establish the criteria of adequacy for any theory that assigns a continuous existence or a specific birth date to a nation before we turn to the familiar literature. By a theory of national chronology I mean an account that determines, for a given nation, whether it is correct to say it existed at a particular historical moment, and if so whether the entity then was the same as the one named today—especially where the population lacked the concept, the institutional form, or the imagined horizontal comradeship associated with modern nationhood.

Please write the desiderata that such a theory must meet. Your specification should cover the cases it must get right (for example, claims about France before 1789, Ethiopia before its modern consolidation, England in 1066, and post-colonial states whose borders were drawn without reference to prior collective identity); the distinctions it must not collapse (for instance between a state apparatus and a nation, between ethnic continuity and national continuity, and between retrospective recognition and contemporaneous self-identification); the historiographical results it must respect without contradiction (including Hobsbawm’s arguments about invented tradition and Anderson’s analysis of imagined communities as tied to print-capitalism and modular sovereignty); and the conceptual puzzles it must dissolve—above all the problem of the presumptuous historian: if a theory permits us to say that a twelfth-century peasant was French in the same sense as a twenty-first-century citizen, what precise error does that commit, and how must the theory block it without simply denying all pre-modern collective identity?

Then flag any pair of desiderata you suspect are jointly unsatisfiable, indicate which demands are non-negotiable and which are preferences of particular camps, and identify the single desideratum that does the most damage to both essentialist and purely constructivist accounts.

Please organise your response in British English as follows:

1. The framing and purpose (approximately 150 words): what the theory is for and why adequacy is relative to that purpose.
2. The desiderata (approximately 450 words): the full list, with the motivating case or consideration behind each requirement.
3. Tensions, negotiability, and the most exclusionary criterion (approximately 200 words): which requirements genuinely conflict, which are camp preferences, and which one excludes the most candidates.
4. Brief diagnostic (approximately 150 words): how Hobsbawm’s invention-of-tradition thesis and Anderson’s modular-nation model fare against your list—not a full scorecard, only enough to show where each stalls.

Do not provide a bibliography, reading list, or citations. Do not quote verbatim from any text; paraphrase in your own words wherever reference is needed.
