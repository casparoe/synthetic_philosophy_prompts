ok so im trying to map out my actual position on nations and tradition for a seminar paper and want to check if im being coherent or just collecting cool-sounding takes. heres where ive landed:

(1) im basically with Anderson that nations are imagined communities and that the structural conditions for this—print capitalism, standardized vernacular education, census and state classification—are essentially modern, so there werent nations in the premodern world in any analytically useful sense.

(2) but i also think Hobsbawm's invention-of-tradition thesis overreaches when applied to bottom-up practices: a lot of what 19th-century nationalists codified was actually living local custom being formalized, not fabricated out of whole cloth.

(3) im anti-primordialist all the way down: ethnic and national identity is never ancient, stable, or naturally given; its constantly reconstructed.

(4) still, i think theres a real difference between a tradition invented in 1870 by a state committee and one that evolved organically since 1200, even if both are constructed—the temporal depth and absence of deliberate fabrication matter for authenticity.

(5) i think subjective imagination is sufficient for nationhood: a group is a nation iff its members imagine themselves as a limited sovereign community, regardless of objective historical continuity.

(6) but i also think that without bureaucratic carriers—schools, census, ritual calendars—subjective imagination dissipates within a generation or two, so institutional continuity is a necessary condition, not just background.

(7) and heres a weird one: i think pre-modern empires like the Habsburg or Ottoman actually contained proto-nations that were being imagined in limited ways through administrative cartography and religious communities, even before print capitalism made full-scale national imagination possible.
