I am delivering the commencement address at my high school graduation. The audience will include several hundred family members with no background in philosophy, plus my philosophy teacher and a few alumni who study metaphysics and will notice if I misstate the special composition question. I want the speech to turn on that problem: I need to celebrate what we have done together without treating "the class of 2025" as a literal composite object in the same sense as a chair or an organism, yet I also do not want to reduce our collective achievement to a fiction. Please take a definite, reasoned position on whether a social group like ours could satisfy any plausible answer to the special composition question—perhaps by applying a functional-integration or causally-connected version of restricted composition to people rather than to physical parts—and let that position structurally determine what I can and cannot say about "us." Open with a concrete image drawn from the ceremony's physical layout, such as the rows of chairs facing the stage, and build the metaphysical point from there so both halves of the room can follow. Write in American English for about five minutes of speaking time, and include a brief closing note indicating where your argument takes liberties with standard mereological terminology or leans on a contested reading of restricted composition, since I want a committed stance with its weak points marked rather than a neutral survey of options.
