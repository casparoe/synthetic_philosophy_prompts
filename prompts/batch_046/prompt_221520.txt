At a philosophy reading group, two members got into an extended argument about Douglas Hofstadter's Platonia dilemma and the real-world Luring Lottery he later described. I was the one taking notes and I do not lean either way; please adjudicate the exchange as it actually unfolded.

The first speaker, Jordan, defended superrational reasoning in Platonia: with twenty people sharing identical reasoning and no cooperation permitted, each should send a telegram with probability 1/20, and anyone who sends deterministically ignores the symmetry that binds all agents together. Jordan said the Luring Lottery's disastrous outcome—where Scientific American readers submitted astronomically large numbers, collapsing the prize—does not refute this framework, because superrationality requires common knowledge of logical omniscience and an identical reasoning algorithm across agents, conditions that were obviously absent in an anonymous mass-mailing contest with no transparency. Jordan then introduced a more niche claim: that in genuinely symmetric games, an agent should apply what Jordan called "indexical neutrality," treating her own reasoning process as an evidential sample from the class of all identically structured reasoners. On this view, your mental state carries information about others' mental states not through causation but through structural correlation, which justifies a low-entry strategy even when you have no causal influence over strangers.

The second speaker, Sam, pushed back on four fronts. First, Sam accused Jordan of making superrationality unfalsifiable: every empirical failure is dismissed as a breakdown of background conditions rather than evidence against the theory. Second, Sam argued that the Luring Lottery is not Platonia scaled up; its payoff structure is competitive rather than purely coordinative, so applying Platonia-style reasoning is a category error. Third, Sam drew a sharp distinction between evidential and causal decision theory: in the Luring Lottery, your entry count causally affects the prize pool size but does not causally affect others' submissions, so causal reasoning recommends submitting as many entries as possible. Any correlation between your choice and others' choices is spurious in such a large anonymous population. Fourth, Sam claimed that Jordan's "indexical neutrality" is essentially magical thinking—without an actual causal or informational link, treating your mental state as evidence about strangers' mental states is no more rational than assuming a telepathic bond.

Jordan replied that Sam begs the question against evidential reasoning: if the game is genuinely symmetric, the correlation is not spurious but structural. Jordan also noted that even with a large population, if agents reason symmetrically, the correlation coefficient is diluted but not eliminated, so evidential considerations still favor restraint, just with reduced weight. Sam answered that this concedes the central point: once the correlation is diluted below the causal effect size, causal reasoning dominates, which is exactly what happened in the actual lottery, where rational agents submitted huge numbers.

Please adjudicate this exchange move by move. For each of Jordan's and Sam's major arguments—the defense of Platonia symmetry, the unfalsifiability objection, the category-error claim, the evidential-versus-causal distinction, the charge of magical thinking, the dilution reply, and the concession argument—rate it from clearly mistaken to clearly right and briefly justify your rating. Then say who is more right overall about the relationship between Platonia-style superrationality and the Luring Lottery, and identify what both speakers are missing, particularly whether there is a principled way to determine when evidential correlations are strong enough to override causal reasoning in large-population symmetric games. Write your answer in American English and keep the analysis focused on the arguments as actually made rather than delivering a general lecture on decision theory.
