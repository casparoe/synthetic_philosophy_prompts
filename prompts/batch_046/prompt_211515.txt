I'm observing a debate between two graduate students in a philosophy of value seminar who are trying to apply Samuel Scheffler's conjecture about the "afterlife"—the idea that much of what gives our lives meaning depends on the expectation that humanity will continue after our deaths—to the question of why human extinction is bad beyond the direct deaths it entails. The first student argues that extinction represents a distinctive structural harm: it severs the temporal chain that gives our present commitments, projects, and institutions their point, so that even a painless extinction annihilates the value-framework within which human lives are lived, regardless of whether anyone experiences grief about it. The second student counters that this so-called structural harm collapses into either a psychological harm—anxiety about legacy among the living—or a harm to specific individuals who care about posterity, and therefore does not establish that extinction is objectively worse than the sum of the deaths involved; on this view, Scheffler's conjecture merely describes a widespread human preference for continuity, not an independent source of disvalue.

To press the point, the second student introduces a hypothetical: suppose that before extinction, humanity succeeds in transferring its cultural, scientific, and artistic achievements into a self-sustaining, evolving digital system—something like a continuously developing artificial civilization that preserves relationships, creates new works, and maintains institutional memory for millennia, though no biological humans ever exist again. If Scheffler is right that what matters is the persistence of a future in which human values continue to unfold, then this digital successor should largely neutralize the distinctive badness of biological extinction; but if the first student insists that only embodied, mortal human lives can constitute the relevant "afterlife," then she has smuggled in a biological essentialism that Scheffler's original argument—centered on expectation and value-structure rather than on carbon-based biology—does not obviously support. The first student replies that embodiment and mortality are not incidental but constitutive of the relationships and commitments Scheffler describes: an AI archive, however dynamic, lacks the vulnerability, reciprocity, and generational replacement that make human projects meaningful. The second student responds that this defense now makes the badness of extinction depend on a highly specific, contested conception of human nature, which undermines the conjecture's broader appeal and leaves it unable to explain why extinction should matter to those who do not share that conception.

As a bystander who has not taken sides, I want an adjudication of the actual arguments as they stand. First, is the "afterlife" harm genuinely distinct from the aggregate of individual deaths and psychological legacy-concerns, or has the second student shown it to be reducible? Second, does the digital-successor scenario successfully expose an undefended essentialism in the first student's position, or is there a principled reason—available within Scheffler's framework—to restrict the relevant future to biological human lives? Third, what are both disputants missing? In particular, does either address the asymmetry between the non-existence of future people (who cannot be harmed by never being born) and the destruction of an expected future that currently structures living values? And if you think neither position fully captures the wrongness of extinction, what adjustment—perhaps involving false expectations, institutional betrayal, or the collapse of meaning-structures—would best repair the account? Please address each round specifically, indicate where each party is right and where they overreach, and state what your repaired view predicts for the case in which humanity knowingly chooses extinction in order to leave behind precisely such a digital successor.
