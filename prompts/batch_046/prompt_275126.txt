As a novelist drafting a philosophical dialogue, I have constructed the following argument and now find it suspicious because it dissolves all penal authority. (1) Camus rejects capital punishment because the state can never possess the absolute moral certainty required to permanently extinguish a human life; judicial fallibility makes such finality intolerable. (2) I propose a principle—retrospective accessibility—holding that for any punishment to be compatible with fallibilism, the punished subject must remain within the reach of future exoneration or moral revision. (3) Execution violates this principle by annihilating the subject; life without parole violates it by permanently removing the subject from the social conditions in which revision could become meaningful. (4) Therefore, if fallibility truly bars execution, it should also bar any punishment that irreversibly severs civic membership. (5) Yet the standard interpretation of Camus treats imprisonment as legitimate, apparently assuming that biological survival alone preserves accessibility. (6) That assumption seems arbitrary: one who lives yet is permanently excluded from public life is, for purposes of moral revision, as inaccessible as one who is dead. (7) So either fallibility does not specifically prohibit execution, or it prohibits all irreversible punishments—including long-term incarceration. (8) Since the latter is unacceptable, the argument must fail at some earlier step. I am inclined to defend (2) but am unsure whether the error lies in equating social exclusion with annihilation, or whether saving the argument requires conceding that imprisonment is also unjust. Which premise should I reject, and what does that rejection commit me to?
