I'm preparing for a session at the workshop on Agency and Responsibility at the University of Edinburgh, where I will defend a diachronic revision of the Principle of Alternative Possibilities. My thesis is that Frankfurt-style counterexamples defeat only a synchronic reading of PAP—the claim that an agent must have been able to do otherwise at the exact moment of action—while leaving intact a diachronic version that requires what I call architectural openness: the agent's preference-formation process must not have been deterministically closed to revision across the relevant developmental window, even if the particular action was locally inevitable. I argue that in canonical Frankfurt cases, the counterfactual intervener operates at the moment of decision but does not penetrate the prior formation of the agent's values, so the agent retains the kind of openness that grounds responsibility. I extend this to distinguish shallow Frankfurt cases, where only the act-token is blocked, from deep cases, where the intervener has shaped the agent's hierarchical preferences from the outset; only the deep cases genuinely threaten responsibility, and they do so precisely by violating diachronic openness rather than by removing branching alternatives.

The audience will be about twenty analytic philosophers of action and moral psychologists, mostly compatibilists who take Frankfurt cases to have settled the debate, but with a vocal minority of libertarians and several empiricists who treat readiness-potential findings in decision neuroscience as decisive against any robust alternative-possibilities condition. They are technically sharp, skeptical of distinctions that look like saving the phenomenon by redefining it, and inclined to ask for concrete criteria that separate shallow from deep intervention without collapsing into circular judgments about responsibility itself.

Please prepare me for the objections I will actually face, ordered by how likely they are and how damaging each would be if I mishandle it. For each, tell me: how they will phrase it in their vocabulary, whether it carries real force, what I should concede up front, what reply must land, and where that reply is weakest. Include the objection I am hoping nobody raises—specifically, the one that forces me to say whether my diachronic condition applies to agents whose preferences were formed through ordinary socialization in a deterministic universe, and if so, whether it entails that almost nobody is responsible. Keep the analysis disciplined: map who is the player in each exchange, what counts as the evidence, and where my theory is genuinely silent rather than being forced to speak.
