Consider the following argument, developed in a seminar on well-being, about Nozick's experience machine. The premises each seem defensible, yet the conclusion is implausible, and we cannot isolate the error. (1) The standard objection to life inside the experience machine is that it severs contact with a reality that exists independently of one's mental states. (2) Call this the Reality-Contact Requirement: for mental states to contribute positively to well-being, they must stand in appropriate representational or causal relations to an external world. (3) If this requirement holds, then well-being is not an intrinsic property of consciousness but a relational property of mental-world pairs. (4) Apply the requirement consistently within an ordinary life: a subject holds false beliefs, pursues failed intentions, and harbors unfulfilled desires—mental states that lack corresponding world-states. (5) By the Reality-Contact Requirement, these mental states contribute nothing, or negatively, to well-being, regardless of their phenomenological quality or functional role in the subject's psychology. (6) Thus well-being reduces to an objective inventory of veridical representations and successful achievements, with subjective experience playing no independent role. (7) This makes well-being radically alienated from first-person experience: two individuals with identical subjective lives could differ maximally in well-being based solely on hidden external correspondences they never access. (8) Because this consequence is more implausible than accepting the experience machine, the Reality-Contact Requirement must be false, and well-being is purely phenomenological. (9) Therefore the experience-machine objection collapses entirely.

We would prefer to preserve (1) and reject a later step if possible, but we are unsure where the damage is done. Identify the precise step or inference that fails, explain why it initially looked sound, and price the cost of rejecting it. In particular, is there an equivocation or scope shift between applying the reality-contact condition to a life taken as a structured whole versus applying it distributively to each individual mental state? And if one saves the objection by restricting the requirement's scope, what does that restriction commit one to about the value of false belief, failed ambition, or imaginative experience within an otherwise reality-connected life?
