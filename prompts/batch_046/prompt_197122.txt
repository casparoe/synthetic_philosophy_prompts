I am drafting a proposal for a one-semester independent study (approximately 800 words) and should be grateful for assistance in sharpening its framing. The subject is the paradox of deontology, which has become personally urgent since I recently joined a committee tasked with designing decision-procedures for clinical resource allocation under absolute constraints—specifically, rules that prohibit denying certain treatments to defined groups regardless of aggregate outcome. In observing our deliberations, I have begun to suspect that the familiar paradox—that universal adherence to deontological rules might produce worse outcomes than universal consequentialism—rests on an unexamined assumption: namely, that all deontological reasons are agent-neutral prohibitions applying uniformly across cases.

My half-formed hypothesis is that we ought to distinguish two registers of deontological reason. I propose to call them 'threshold-guarding' reasons, which protect the moral status of agents and resist aggregation altogether; and 'directional' reasons, which govern choice among permissible options and may still be weighed in some non-consequentialist fashion. If the distinction holds, the standard paradox may dissolve for threshold-guarding reasons, since they do not compete on an outcome scale, but intensify for directional reasons, since competing directional claims appear to require a selection mechanism that risks looking like maximisation.

Please structure the proposal with the following elements: (1) a precise statement of the research question—does this two-register distinction resolve, relocate, or deepen the paradox?—without reliance on specific textual citations; (2) an explanation of why existing treatments of the paradox leave this angle open, particularly their tendency to treat deontological constraints as a single class; (3) a method setting out what conceptual analysis I would undertake month by month, using constructed allocation cases rather than empirical data, and specifying how I would test whether the distinction collapses into familiar consequentialist or contractualist frameworks; (4) a clear account of what success would look like (for instance, a formalised taxonomy of deontological reasons with a conflict-resolution procedure internal to each register) and what failure would look like (for instance, the distinction proving redundant or incoherent); and (5) an honest assessment of scope for a single semester, including the risk that the project becomes either too abstract or too closely tied to applied ethics.

Please write in British English. In accordance with the workshop guidelines, do not include any bibliographies, reading lists, or citation strategies—I shall compile those separately.
