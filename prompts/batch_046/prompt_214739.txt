Two people in a seminar on conservative political theory argued about whether Edmund Burke's defense of prescription and G.K. Chesterton's principle of the fence apply to proposals for abolishing the Electoral College. I would like you to adjudicate their exchange move by move.

(1) The first speaker said that Burke's preference for inherited practice over abstract rights, together with Chesterton's warning that one should not tear down a barrier without knowing why it was built, supports keeping the Electoral College. Its survival for over two centuries indicates a stabilizing function that reformers cannot fully map, and removing it risks destabilizing federal balance in unpredictable ways.

(2) The second speaker replied that Chesterton's rule is procedural rather than prohibitive: it requires understanding an institution's purpose before altering it, not preserving it indefinitely. The Electoral College was designed as a mediated filter between popular and elite judgment, a function that modern party nominations and national campaigns have rendered obsolete; once the original purpose is documented and shown defunct, the barrier can be removed. She added that Burke's defense of prescription applies to organically evolved social practices—local associations, family structures—not to engineered constitutional mechanisms, so applying Burke here is a category mistake.

(3) The first speaker countered that this draws an artificial line between organic and engineered institutions. Through prolonged use, constitutional mechanisms become embedded in social life and acquire prescriptive authority; the Electoral College is now woven into the fabric of American federal identity. He also argued that the second speaker's understand-then-remove standard is impossible to meet for any complex institution, since complete causal knowledge is never available, which means her position secretly collapses back into his caution.

(4) The second speaker said this interpretation makes Chesterton's fence meaningless: if understanding is always insufficient, then no reform is ever permissible, which conflicts with Chesterton's own willingness to support reforms he endorsed. She also noted that Burke justified the American Revolution precisely because British colonial institutions had lost their prescriptive legitimacy when they violated established rights, showing that Burkean prescription is conditional rather than absolute.

Please rate each of the four moves from clearly mistaken to clearly right, with brief justification for each rating. Identify who is more right overall, and point out what both sides miss—whether about Burke's actual arguments, Chesterton's intent, or the logic of institutional change. Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then give the reasoning; include no preamble and do not restate the question.
