I'm preparing a recorded interview for a philosophy podcast, and next week's guest is an unnamed philosopher who defends Benatar's asymmetry—the claim that the absence of suffering is good even when there is no subject to enjoy that absence, whereas the absence of pleasure is not bad—to argue that bringing sentient beings into existence is morally wrong. The subject has become personally urgent: I recently became a parent, and I cannot dismiss the thought that by creating my child I may have introduced avoidable suffering without benefiting anyone who was previously deprived of pleasure.

I want eight to ten polished interview questions that do not ask the guest to restate the view or provide a bibliography, but instead press where the literature actually presses. Please include: one question on the replacement problem—why, if non-existence avoids suffering, it is not obligatory to replace an existing suffering being with a happy potential one; one on adoption versus biological procreation, asking whether adopting an already-existing suffering child is permissible or even required, given that the asymmetry offers no reason to create but may offer reasons about existing subjects; one on preimplantation genetic screening, probing whether preventing the birth of children who would suffer implicitly commits the defender to preventing births that would yield only neutral or mildly positive lives; and one that anticipates the reply that love and meaningfulness can outweigh suffering, with a follow-up asking whether those goods, being available only to existents, supply exactly the symmetric countervailing reason the asymmetry denies is present for pleasure.

Additionally, include a final question asking what kind of conceptual shift—rather than empirical discovery—could cause the asymmetry itself to collapse, since I want to understand whether my own discomfort is genuinely philosophical or merely emotional. For each question, briefly indicate the answer the guest is most likely to give and supply the sharper follow-up that makes the deflection harder. Write in a formal register appropriate for an educated but non-specialist audience.
