I'm trying to figure out where my gut draws the line between legend and actual history, using cases I keep going back to. Here are my verdicts, which feel mostly consistent but probably aren't:

The Trojan War: I think it's got a real historical kernel. Schliemann's digs at Hisarlik turned up a destroyed city from about the right period, and the Mycenaean world in Homer's poems lines up with archaeology. Obviously the gods and the wooden horse are myth, but I think there was probably some conflict there.

King Arthur: Still pure legend for me. There's that late reference to a warlord named Artorius, and some Welsh poems mention battles, but it's all centuries after the fact and way too thin. The round table and Merlin are obvious later embroidery, so I don't buy that there's a specific guy behind it.

The Exodus: Also still mostly legend. There's that Egyptian inscription mentioning Israel, and people talk about the Hyksos, but I can't get past the scale problem - no archaeological trace of hundreds of thousands of people wandering the Sinai for forty years. So I put it with Arthur, not with Troy.

But here's the tension that bugs me. The Minotaur story is tied to Knossos - Evans found the palace, there are bull-leaping frescoes, the layout is maze-like. So there's a real place and real rituals behind it. But I still think the Minotaur itself is pure legend; there's no kernel event. Meanwhile I allow a kernel for Troy even though we don't have proof of Hector or Achilles specifically. So why do I treat them differently? Maybe because the Trojan destruction layer is an actual event, while Knossos is just a setting?

Another wierd case: Prester John. Europeans spent centuries treating him as a real Christian king in Asia, but he turned out to be a misinterpretation of actual rulers in Ethiopia and Central Asia. I think he was never history - just a legend that accidentally mapped onto real people. But that feels different from Arthur, who might be based on one real guy. So my principle needs to seperate "legend with a real backdrop" from "legend that misinterprets real figures."

Also, what about the Amazons? I haven't made up my mind, but now there are finds of warrior women in Scythian graves. Does that mean the Amazon legend has a kernel? Or is that just a real cultural practice that got mythologized?

So what's the principle? Maybe: a legend becomes history when there's independent, contemporary material evidence of the specific event the narrative describes, not just of the general setting or culture. By that rule, Troy barely makes it (the destruction layer exists but we can't tie it to Greeks specifically), Arthur fails (no contemporary evidence), Exodus fails (no trace of the migration), Minotaur fails (no evidence of the creature or ritual), and Prester John fails (no single person matching the description).

But then why do I still feel Troy is closer to history than Arthur? Maybe I'm smuggling in "scale of claim" - big claims need big evidence. Or maybe I should just drop one verdict.

Can you lay out the best principle that saves most of my calls, say which one it forces me to revise, and tell me what it predicts for the Amazon case? And be honest about whether I'm just using complexity to save a contradiction.
