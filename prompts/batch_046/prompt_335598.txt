For as long as I have followed philosophy of history, there has been a standoff over whether large-scale outcomes—say, the rapid industrialization of a particular region or the collapse of an empire—are genuinely causally overdetermined by economic, geographic, and institutional factors working in concert, or whether that apparent multiplicity merely reflects our epistemic ignorance of a dominant causal chain. The positions feel frozen in place: structural determinists treat redundancy as an ontological feature of the world, contingency advocates treat it as an artifact of incomplete information, and a middle group insists that "overdetermination" is really just a failure of causal selection norms. I am trying to diagnose whether this is a substantive disagreement about historical ontology, a verbal dispute about what counts as an explanation versus a cause, or whether the camps are actually answering different questions—one about whether counterfactual dependencies hold in the actual world, another about whether a coherent narrative can be constructed without explanatory redundancy. Specifically, I wonder if the hidden work is being done by an unexamined commitment that historical causes must be individually necessary for some outcome, or whether there is a formal result—perhaps from interventionist causation or causal graph theory applied to non-repeatable systems—that could break the deadlock by distinguishing causal overdetermination from what we might call "interventionist underdetermination" and "explanatory overabundance." Are the sides differing about facts, about standards of evidence, about which historical cases count as data, or about starting intuitions that neither side can argue for? Is the impasse a sign that the question is ill-posed, or merely that it is hard? Give me a diagnosis rooted in what determinists, contingency theorists, and selection-criteria theorists actually say about concrete cases—such as the diffusion of agricultural practices or the decline of specific imperial systems—together with an account of what would count as progress given your diagnosis: a conceptual distinction that separates the three phenomena, an empirical study of how working historians weight causes, a formal framework for measuring explanatory redundancy, or an argument that the framing should be abandoned in favor of a different model of historical explanation. Please answer in American English.
