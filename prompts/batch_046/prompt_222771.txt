I have noticed a recurring argumentative move in discussions about whether killing is morally worse than letting die, and I want to know whether it corresponds to an established concept or whether it needs a new label. Here is what I see, described concretely.

First instance: In medical ethics, someone defends the act/omission distinction by saying that actively administering a lethal drug is wrong because it causes death, whereas withholding a ventilator is permissible because one is merely allowing the underlying condition to take its course. But if you point out that turning off an already-running ventilator requires the same physical movement as turning on a switch, the defender shifts to saying that the relevant factor is not bodily motion but whether one is ceasing an artificial intervention or allowing nature to proceed. The "natural course" is then identified with whatever state existed before the medical team arrived—yet that prior state was itself produced by earlier interventions, and no independent reason is given for why that particular baseline should carry moral weight.

Second instance: In environmental ethics, turning off pumps that keep a reservoir filled is defended as "stopping harm to the river," while refusing to build the pumps in the first place is defended as "not interfering with nature," even though the ecological outcome—a dry riverbed versus a wet one—is symmetric. The baseline that determines which action counts as "interference" tracks whatever infrastructure is already present, not any feature of the ecosystem itself.

The pattern is not simple equivocation, because both formulations—"causing death" versus "allowing nature"—seem sincerely held. Rather, it looks like the defender is using an unstated, context-sensitive default state to classify actions as active or passive, then using that classification to justify the moral distinction. The default shifts depending on prior human involvement, but the principle selecting the default is never articulated.
