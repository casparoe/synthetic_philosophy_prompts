I'm working through an argument about artistic originals that I constructed for a chapter in aesthetic ontology, and I worry it proves far more than I intend. The argument runs as follows.

(1) The artistic value of a work supervenes on its perceptible formal properties together with the experiential responses they warrant; causal history—who made it, when, and under what conditions—contributes to value only if it affects these perceptible or experiential features.

(2) A perfect forgery of a painting duplicates every perceptible formal property of the original, and therefore warrants identical experiential responses.

(3) So the forgery and the original share the same artistic value.

(4) If two objects share all value-relevant properties, then rational preference between them must rest on non-value-related considerations (scarcity, sentiment, market convention), not on artistic merit itself.

(5) Applying this consistently: if every museum replaced its originals with undetectable replicas, no artistic value would be lost, since every warranted response remains available.

(6) By the same token, if an artificial system produces an image perceptually and structurally identical to a lost painting, that image possesses the same artistic achievement-value as the lost original, because achievement-value depends on the result's formal excellence rather than on the causal path that produced it.

(7) Extending further: the destruction of a unique historical artifact—say, a carved relief that is also a primary source for an extinct script—is not a loss of cultural value if a perfect physical and informational replica survives, since all value-conferring properties are preserved.

(8) But this seems wrong in a deep way. We treat the destruction of originals and unique artifacts as irreparable losses that replicas cannot remedy; and we distinguish sharply between artistic creation and replication in ways that affect evaluation.

(9) Therefore, (1) is false, and causal history must make an independent contribution to artistic value.

My difficulty is that I want to keep something like (1) for its explanatory economy—value should depend on what we can actually encounter and evaluate—but I also want to preserve (8). Where does the slide occur? Is there an equivocation between artistic value, achievement-value, and artifact-value? Or does the argument illicitly extend a claim about experience-value to domains where causal history is constitutive rather than merely instrumental? I am especially unsure about step (6): does it confuse the value of a result with the value of an achievement? If I reject (6) but keep (1), what do I owe to the relationist about artistic value? And what principle, if any, distinguishes the cases in (5) and (7) without collapsing back into a mysterious essentialism about originals?
