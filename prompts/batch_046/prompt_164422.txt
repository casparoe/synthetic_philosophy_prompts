I'm serving as the student representative on my university's Committee for Public Memory and Cultural Funding, and I'm currently enrolled in a graduate seminar on nationalism, collective identity, and the politics of historical narrative. Next Thursday we must vote on a proposal that has sharply divided the committee, and I've been asked to draft the formal opinion memo that will circulate in advance of the meeting.

The proposal requests $180,000 to support a three-year Restoration and Revitalization of what the applicants call the Ancient Covenant of the River Valley, a ceremonial gathering they describe as an uninterrupted 400-year-old practice that anchors the identity and mental well-being of a regional diaspora community now settled near our campus. Our staff historian's preliminary report, however, documents that while scattered seasonal gatherings existed in the region, the specific ritual structure, symbolic vocabulary, and collective origin narrative of "the Covenant" were assembled in 1968 by a cultural revivalist association and then standardized for public performance only in 1998. The applicants do not deny this archival record; they argue that for three generations the ceremony has been lived sincerely as ancient, has generated genuine solidarity and documented community-health benefits, and that exposing its constructed pedigree would cause concrete cultural harm without advancing any legitimate educational aim. Some members want to reject the grant on the grounds that institutional funding would implicitly endorse a misrepresentation of history; others want to approve it precisely because the tradition's present social reality now outweighs questions about its fabricated origin.

Please draft the memo I should circulate: it should recommend a concrete course of action—approve, reject, or approve with binding conditions and a defined review timeline—rather than leaving the matter ambiguous. The reasoning should take seriously both the concern that invented traditions should not be presented or funded as timeless custom, and the recognition that large-scale communities depend on shared narratives that are in some sense always constructed, so that historical fabrication and lived collective reality are not straightforward opposites. Give an honest account of the strongest argument for the position you reject. Finally, please state explicitly what evidence or moral consideration would change your recommendation, and identify precisely where your reasoning is most vulnerable—whether in weighing archival accuracy against present community need, or in treating institutional funding as equivalent to institutional endorsement of a particular historical claim.
