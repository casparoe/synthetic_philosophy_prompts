I'm a novelist working on a historical novel about the 1848 revolutions, and I got into a long argument with my historian friend over drinks. I think there's a defensible middle ground between Ranke's old idea of showing how things actually happened and the full scepticism you see in Carr and Hayden White. Definately, historical narratives are shaped by rhetorical tropes and present concerns — Carr and White are right about that part — but there's also what I'd call narrative friction: points where the archive genuinely resists the shape you want to impose, not just because evidence is missing, but because the traces have an internal coherence that doesn't align with your plot. So objectivity isn't about holding up a mirror, but about being accountable to these friction points.

What would actually count against this view? I want an honest map of the exposure: which arguments from White's tropology or Carr's presentism undermine the idea that there's any meaningful resistance, and am I just smuggling Ranke back in through the back door? Also, are there specific historical cases where this friction idea clearly falls apart? I'm not asking for a bibliography — I just want the defeaters ranked from fatal to merely awkward, and whether a committed defender of this middle ground should concede anything serious.
