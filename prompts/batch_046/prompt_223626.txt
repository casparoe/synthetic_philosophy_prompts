I serve on the technical review board for a municipal water-management authority, and we are increasingly asked to approve multi-decade capital commitments—reservoir expansions, flood barriers, drainage overhauls—based on hydrological models that embed climate projections. The board is split between members who treat long-range model divergence as evidence that the underlying physical system lacks determinism, and therefore that long-range planning is conceptually inappropriate, and members who treat the same divergence as merely an information-limit problem that better sensors or computation could eventually solve. I need you to turn the philosophical distinction among ontological determinism, chaotic dynamics, and epistemic predictability into a reusable decision procedure that my board can actually run during proposal review. The procedure should move through ordered questions: first, identifying the specific failure mode of the model in question; second, distinguishing structurally unstable divergence from merely information-sensitive divergence; third, determining whether the institutional commitment being proposed requires ontological determinism, practical predictability, or only a weaker condition of bounded risk. For each step, specify what counts as a sufficient answer, what reasoning or data is required, where the procedure stops with a clear recommendation, and where it must hand the decision to human judgment because the remaining question is normative rather than technical—such as whether a community should bear costs now for benefits that depend on an unpredictable forecast. Then apply the procedure to three cases: a reservoir expansion justified by a rainfall model that diverges sharply after year five; a flood-barrier investment rejected because storm-frequency projections show exponential error growth; and a maintenance-deferral plan that the procedure approves despite high divergence, an outcome I expect the board will dislike. The procedure should be honest about its own misfire points—specifically, where philosophical distinctions collapse into ordinary engineering judgments, or where the cost of delaying action exceeds the epistemic cost of acting under uncertainty. It must be usable by board members without philosophical training, but it should not water the concepts down to slogans like chaos means we cannot know anything. Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citations; reference the conceptual distinctions precisely, but do not depend on specific named papers or obscure formalisms that a practitioner could not reconstruct from the procedure itself.
