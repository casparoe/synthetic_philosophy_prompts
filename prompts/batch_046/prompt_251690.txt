I've been trying to sort out my own view on blame and free will after hearing people discuss those thought experiments where someone has a hidden backup ready to force your hand, and I want to check whether my commitments actually hang together before I talk about them with friends. Here is where I have landed:

(1) I think moral responsibility genuinely requires that you could have done otherwise in some real, not merely felt, sense—there has to be an open path you actually declined.
(2) At the same time, I am persuaded that if a hidden mechanism is poised to stop you but never actually activates, and your action flows entirely from your own reasoning, then you are still fully responsible; the backup's mere presence does not erase your authorship.
(3) So I accept that you can be responsible even when every alternative has been preemptively blocked, provided the blocker never steps in and your will is the actual cause.
(4) I apply this in ordinary life: if a colleague stays late to help me finish a project, I still thank her sincerely even if I later learn our manager had secretly arranged a contractor to take over had she left; her choice remains genuinely hers.
(5) I think the same logic extends to artificial systems: if a language model produces harmful output only because an internal safety filter failed to trigger, but the filter was never actually needed to block that specific output, then the model's response traces back to its own parameters without being overridden, so it bears a structural analog of responsibility.
(6) But I also hold that if physical determinism is true—if every brain state follows necessarily from prior states with no gap—then there is no will distinct from the mechanism, and responsibility collapses entirely, hidden blockers or not.

Which pairs are in genuine tension, which only appear to be, and what is the least costly revision? I am particularly unsure whether (1) can survive alongside (3), whether (5) is a legitimate extension of (2) or a category mistake, and whether (6) undermines (3) rather than complementing it. If the set is inconsistent, should I drop the determinism worry in (6), restrict the hidden-blocker idea in (3) to human agents, or abandon the "could have done otherwise" requirement in (1)? Please rank the tensions by severity, specify exactly what must be revised in each case and at what cost, and tell me which single change would clean up the most problems.
