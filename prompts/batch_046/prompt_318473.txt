I study the preservation of scientific archives and have noticed a recurring pattern in digitization projects that I lack a precise term for. An institution scans its analog holdings—say, laboratory notebooks and draft reports from the 1970s—and produces high-resolution, searchable images. Yet the scanning protocol systematically strips away a specific class of material evidence: staple holes that reveal which pages were once physically grouped together; coffee stains and wear patterns that indicate which sections were consulted repeatedly; differences in paper weight or ink bleed that distinguish original drafts from contemporaneous carbon copies. The archive is thus made more complete in accessibility and textual fidelity while becoming epistemically thinner with respect to provenance, use-history, and temporal layering. This is not the intentional destruction of records, nor is it random physical decay; it is a silence generated by the preservation technology itself, shaped by institutional priorities about what counts as "the record." Historians and archivists have written about Foucauldian archival silences and about accidental data loss, but this seems different—an absence produced precisely by the act of comprehensive preservation. Does this phenomenon have an established name in archival theory or in science and technology studies? If a concept exists, how does it differ from ordinary "loss of metadata," from "archival silence" in the critical-theory sense, and from simple "media translation error"? If there is no precise term, can you coin one, define its boundaries, and specify what it is not—particularly distinguishing it from both intentional erasure and unintentional deterioration? I am not seeking a bibliography or reading list; I want the carving of the concept itself: what the phenomenon is, where its edges lie, and which neighboring ideas it must be separated from.
