I have been working through some long-range predictive theories in a philosophy of science seminar—particularly the recurring debates about whether Malthusian dynamics can ever be genuinely falsified, or whether they are merely right but early. A pattern keeps appearing that I cannot quite map onto the standard vocabulary, and I want to know whether it already has a precise name.

Consider the following argumentative structure. A theory predicts that some underlying pressure—population growth relative to agricultural yield, for instance—will eventually produce a decisive crisis. For decades, perhaps centuries, the crisis fails to materialise; instead, living conditions improve, thanks to technological or institutional innovations that seem to have solved the problem permanently. Defenders of the original theory do not usually respond by inserting an explicit exception clause stating that the mechanism applies except where innovation intervenes, nor do they revise the core mechanism. Rather, they treat the very postponement of the crisis as confirmation that it has been deferred rather than averted. They argue that the temporary relief has allowed underlying pressures to accumulate beneath the surface, so that the failure of the prediction to arrive on schedule is reinterpreted as evidence that the reckoning will be more severe and more inevitable when it eventually comes. I have seen this move deployed not only in discussions of population and subsistence, but also in certain long-range energy-depletion forecasts and in arguments about secular economic stagnation, where each temporary reprieve is framed as a dangerous delay.

This seems distinct from ordinary Popperian immunisation, because no ad hoc auxiliary hypothesis about conditions is being added; the mechanism remains intact and the timeline is simply rendered elastic. It also differs from a Lakatosian protective-belt adjustment, since the auxiliary claims are not about when the theory applies, but about the temporal location and accumulated severity of the predicted event. Is there an established concept—perhaps in the literature on long-run prediction, falsification, or rescue strategies—that captures exactly this conversion of empirical delay into accumulated inevitability? If so, how does it differ from nearby ideas such as predictive deferral or temporal immunisation? If there is no adequate term, please take a definite position: propose one specific label with clear boundary conditions, explaining precisely what distinguishes this pattern from plain unfalsifiability and from legitimate theoretical refinement, and defend your choice rather than offering a list of alternatives.
