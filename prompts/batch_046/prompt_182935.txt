For more than a decade, the literature on democratic backsliding—particularly with reference to Hungary, Poland, Turkey, and the United States—has circled the same set of arguments without clear resolution. One camp treats backsliding as an observable institutional process: executive aggrandizement, judicial capture, media restrictions, and electoral manipulation that can be tracked through event-based or expert-survey indicators. Another camp insists that labeling these changes as backsliding presupposes a liberal-democratic baseline that populist governments reject; on this view, the same institutional shifts represent democratic deepening or majoritarian correction rather than decay. A third position holds that the concept has become primarily a partisan cudgel—deployed selectively against right-populist regimes while similar practices in other contexts are ignored—so that the disagreement is not about institutional facts but about who gets to apply the label and with what normative authority.

Despite extensive empirical work, the conceptual debate seems stalled: every standard argument—about whether Hungary remains an electoral democracy, whether Poland’s judicial reforms cross a constitutional threshold, whether American polarization constitutes backsliding or mere gridlock, and whether Turkey’s trajectory was inevitable or contingent—is well rehearsed, and scholars largely talk past one another. I want you to diagnose why. Is this a substantive disagreement about institutional facts and normative standards? A verbal disagreement about what democracy or backsliding means, where different scholars use the same terms for different phenomena? Or are there actually two or more distinct questions being collapsed under one heading—for instance, a descriptive-comparative question about regime classification and a normative-political question about legitimacy—such that progress requires disentangling them?

As part of your diagnosis, please consider a specific framing distinction that is rarely made explicit in the literature but may be obscuring the disagreement: the difference between threshold backsliding, in which a regime crosses a discrete boundary from democracy into some form of competitive authoritarianism or hybrid regime, and vector backsliding, in which a state moves continuously along a multi-dimensional democratic-quality axis without necessarily crossing any categorical line. Some analysts implicitly adopt a threshold model when they ask whether a country has ceased to be a democracy, while others implicitly adopt a vector model when they discuss erosion or deconsolidation without specifying a tipping point. Could it be that part of the impasse arises because proponents of different models are answering different questions—one asking whether a line has been crossed, the other asking in which direction and how far a system has moved—while using identical vocabulary?
