hey, i'm a backend engineer with basically zero formal phil training—just read random stuff on my commute and argue with friends. i got thinking about Buridan's ass, y'know the donkey that starves between two identical bales because there's no reason to pick either, and i tried to formalize it and now it feels like it proves free will is fake in close-call moral choices, which is obviously nuts. here's the chain i wrote down:

(1) if option A and B are tied in every relevent respect, then there's literally no rational basis for picking one over the other.
(2) a choise with no rational basis isn't rational—it's just noise, like a flipped bit.
(3) so in a genuine tie, a rational agent should be paralized, exactly like the ass.
(4) but in computing we never paralize; we break ties with deterministic rules—load balancers pick the lower server ID, hash tables chain buckets, RL agents use epsilon-greedy seeds. none of that is "stuck."
(5) humans do the same in ties: we flip a coin, go with gut feel, pick the left one. we're running some hidden tie-breaker.
(6) so either human "choice" in ties is just deterministic execution of hidden rules (neural noise, subconscious cues), or there was never a real tie—there's always some hidden asymmetry.
(7) infact if there's always hidden asymmetry, then "choice without preference" is literally impossible—every decision has a reason, we just don't see it.
(8) extend that to moral dilemmas: when we think we're genuinely stuck between saving person A or B (identical twins, same distance), there's actually a hidden factor breaking the tie, and our "decision" is just that factor playing out.
(9) so moral agency in close cases is an illusion—we're just deterministic tie-breaking machines.
(10) but that's absurd; we clearly blame people for those close calls.

i feel like the slip is somewhere between (4) and (6), maybe an equivocation between "no rational basis visible to the agent" and "no causal mechanism at all"? but i can't name it. if i have to save something, i'd really like to keep (1) and (3)—the idea that true ties should paralize a purely rational agent—because that feels right. which step do i actually reject, and what's the cost? does giving up (6) save moral agency but make Buridan's ass impossible?
