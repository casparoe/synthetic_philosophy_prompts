I've been in this philosophy of mind reading group for like three months and we're stuck on the exact same loop every week. The topic is whether global workspace theory actually explains phenomenal consciousness, or if it only gets you access consciousness and leaves the "what it's like" part untouched. Block's distinction is pretty clear in our discussions — phenomenal is the raw subjective presence, access is availability for report, reasoning, and control — and the workspace people (Dehaene, Cohen, that crowd) say that once information is broadcast across the prefrontal-parietal network with the right temporal integration, you've got both. Block's side replies that broadcasting explains reportability and cognitive access just fine, but it doesn't even begin to close the explanatory gap for the intrinsic feel. Meanwhile the GWT defenders say phenomenal consciousness just is a specific mode of global availability, maybe tied to a particular ignition profile or sustained activation window, so there is no leftover "hard problem" once the architecture is mapped.

What drives me crazy is that nobody in the room denies the neuroscience. We all agree on the broadcasting dynamics, the ignition events, the P3-related markers, all of it. The empirical picture hasn't changed, and yet neither camp has moved an inch. My own hunch is that the disagreement isn't hidden in the data at all — it's about what counts as a complete explanation. One side thinks explaining the functional architecture is explaining consciousness full stop; the other thinks there's a residual first-person fact that functional descriptions necessarily leave out, no matter how detailed the broadcasting model gets. But I also wonder if we're mixing up two different things under the label "phenomenal consciousness": an instantaneous presentational state that exists right now, versus a diachronic capacity for self-reference that unfolds over a temporal window. If that's the mix-up, maybe the two sides are actually answering different questions — one about synchronic presence, one about sustained access-over-time — without realizing it.

So I'm trying to figure out: is this stall a substantive disagreement about hidden empirical facts, a verbal dispute about the word "explanation" or "consciousness," a clash of bedrock intuitions about subjectivity that neither side can argue for, or just two research programs talking past each other? And if you had to diagnose the structure of the impasse, what — a sharper conceptual distinction, a hypothetical experiment that actually separates the two profiles, a formal result about integration windows, or maybe just accepting that the question is ill-posed — could actually move it? Or is this one of those debates where progress shouldn't be expected and we should just pick our intuitions and move on?
