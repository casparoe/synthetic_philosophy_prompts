I'd like an in-the-weeds explanation of the boundary problem in democratic theory—specifically the reflexive variant where membership in the demos is supposed to track being affected by collective decisions, but the legitimate domain of those decisions is itself defined by who belongs to the demos. Standard discussions tend to collapse this into either territorial closure or a vague "all affected" slogan without addressing the circularity. Could you walk through why this is a conceptual knot, not just an institutional design problem? In particular, distinguish ex-ante affectedness—who is projected to bear costs or benefits under a proposed rule—from ex-post affectedness—who actually ends up bearing them once the rule reshapes incentives and behavior. Then apply this to a somewhat niche framing: imagine a supranational climate body that assigns deliberative weights according to dynamic vulnerability indices rather than citizenship. Because the indices are themselves functions of the policy outputs, the boundary of the demos and the scope of authority become co-determined. Please don't present any single resolution—say, a pragmatic closure rule or an open-ended stakeholder model—as the settled view. Instead, separate what's conceptually uncontroversial (that the circularity exists and that both ex-ante and ex-post criteria fail to close it without auxiliary assumptions) from what's genuinely contested among democratic theorists (whether legitimacy requires breaking the circle, or whether we should accept an arbitrary but transparent boundary condition).
