I'm in an upper-level metaphysics course and we're supposed to come up with practice final questions for our study group. I need an essay question on material constitution — the statue and the clay thing — but I'm bored with the standard David/Phidias examples that every textbook recycles. I want something that forces people to actually think about persistence conditions instead of just naming the distinction.

So here's the scenario I want baked into the prompt: there's a museum piece called 'Persistent Form' made entirely of living bio-clay that slowly regenerates and repairs itself. Over a decade, conservators replace every original biological molecule with synthetic polymer, but they do it in tiny increments so that at no point does more than 30% of the material swap out at once. The artist left instructions saying if the original biological substrate is fully replaced, the piece is 'dead' and must be destroyed. By year ten it's 100% synthetic, but the shape, weight, and visible properties never changed.

The exam question should ask whether the statue survives, whether the original clay survives, and whether the destruction condition is met — and students shouldn't be able to answer it just by reciting that statue ≠ clay. They need to engage with whether temporal parts, coincident objects, or functional continuity explain what's going on, and they should deal with the objection that artifact identity depends on authorial intent in a way that natural-object identity doesn't. Please write the question, a model answer of about 600 words, and a marking scheme that seperates an A from a B by naming the specific philosophical moves each must contain (like distinguishing de re persistence conditions from de dicto descriptions, or explaining why the gradual replacement doesn't save identity for the clay). Make it suitable for a 45-minute exam, 25 marks, no citations or reading lists needed — just reasoning.
