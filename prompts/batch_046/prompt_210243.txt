I'm preparing a seminar on structural pathologies of democratic deliberation, and I've become convinced that the three canonical Athenian failures—the Mytilene debate's emotional reversal, the Sicilian expedition's demagogic overreach, and the trial of Socrates as post-catastrophe scapegoating—are not isolated moral failures but expressions of a single recurring arc: under acute collective anxiety, direct democratic assemblies collapse the temporal and cognitive distance required for cost-benefit reasoning, substitute charismatic urgency for institutional caution, and then, once disaster unfolds, redirect collective shame onto the very voices that had resisted the initial folly. I want to apply this "triad arc" framework, which I take to be implicit in Thucydides' narrative architecture though rarely formalized, to a modern democratic episode that is well documented and structurally analogous: the 2003 United States congressional authorization for military action in Iraq, followed by the professional and political marginalization of dissenting intelligence analysts, diplomats, and legislators who had warned about the absence of WMD stockpiles and the risks of prolonged occupation.

Please analyze the Iraq authorization and its aftermath through the lens of this Athenian triad. First, lay out the explicit correspondence table: who plays the role of the demagogic orator driving emotional urgency, who plays the role of the prudent warning voice arguing for delay and risk assessment, and who eventually becomes the Socratic scapegoat once the predicted failures materialize; then identify the "Mytilene moment"—whether there was a rapid suppression of deliberative procedure or an emotional reversal that foreclosed careful weighing—and the "Sicily moment," meaning the imperial or strategic overreach fueled by inflated collective self-confidence and the dismissal of expert warnings about occupation and sectarian fracture. Next, examine whether a "Socrates moment" occurred in the post-invasion period: did democratic publics and representatives redirect blame not only onto executive deception but onto the dissenters themselves, treating prior warnings as disloyalty, naivety, or failure of patriotic resolve? Crucially, say where the mapping genuinely breaks: modern representative democracy lacks the direct face-to-face assembly, the institutional separation between intelligence agencies and political decision-makers differs from the Athenian fusion of deliberation and execution, and the geopolitical scale is not identical. Finally, assess what the episode does to the theory itself—does it confirm that the triad describes a general democratic psychology under stress, or does it reveal that the framework applies only to direct, unmediated assemblies and therefore misses the specific pathologies of bureaucratic, media-mediated modern democracy? Be disciplined about marking where the theory is silent rather than forcing it to speak.
