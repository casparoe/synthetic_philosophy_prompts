"Bradley's regress argument starts with the idea that if two terms are related, the relation itself needs to be related to each term, which requires further relations, and so on forever. So relations can't be ultimately real. This also creates a problem for propositions: in the judgment 'Socrates is wise,' if Socrates and wisdom are separate terms that need a relation to stick them together, then that relation also needs sticking, so the judgment never achieves real unity - it's just a heap of terms.

Bradley concludes that reality is the Absolute, an undifferentiated whole where all distinctions disappear into pure experience. The relations we see are just appearances.

Russell responds by saying that relations don't need relating because they are internal to the terms they connect. Once you see that being-related is part of the nature of the terms themselves, the regress never gets started. This solves the problem for propositions too, because the terms already contain their connections internally, so the proposition is naturally unified.

But I think Russell's answer is weak, because if relations are internal, then everything is necessarily connected to everything else, which is just Bradley's Absolute all over again. So Russell ends up accepting Bradley's conclusion without realizing it. I don't see any real parallel to grounding debates here, since grounding is about dependence not about infinite regresses."
