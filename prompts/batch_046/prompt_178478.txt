Write a proposal for a one-semester seminar paper of approximately 5,000 words on whether the distinction between authentic moral preferences and distorting biases can survive once we acknowledge that moral learning operates through normative sedimentation—the gradual, often pre-reflective layering of evaluative commitments through repeated practical engagement rather than through explicit endorsement or choice. State the precise question: is there a non-circular criterion for identifying a given moral preference as bias-driven rather than autonomy-driven when both kinds of commitments are formed through structurally identical sedimentation processes? Explain why standard procedural, coherence-based, and genealogical accounts fail to settle this—particularly because each relies on a normative baseline that is itself vulnerable to the same sedimentation dynamics. Propose an approach that introduces counterfactual narrative rupture—the degree to which abandoning a commitment would fracture an agent's self-interpretive continuity—as a diagnostic tool for distinguishing sedimented preference from sedimented bias, and defend this approach against the more obvious alternative of appealing to idealized rational choice or informational abundance. Specify what a successful result would demonstrate (for example, that the rupture criterion applies asymmetrically to commitments that serve adaptive self-deception versus those that serve genuine value exploration) and what would count as failure (for instance, if the criterion applies symmetrically to both progressive and conservative moral revisions). Conclude with an honest assessment of scope: which conceptual arguments can be completed within one semester, and which empirical questions about the psychology of moral revision must be bracketed rather than resolved. Do not include a bibliography or literature review by citation; reference positions and arguments generally, and note where specific sources will be added later.
