What would an adequate philosophical account of knowing-how have to achieve in order to be genuinely distinct from an account of knowing-that? Give me the full specification: the concrete cases it must accommodate (the expert performer who cannot articulate her method, the learner who picks up a skill purely through imitation without grasping rules, the phenomenon where explicit theoretical attention degrades fluent execution), the conceptual distinctions it must preserve (knowing-how versus mere behavioral ability, versus knowing-that one is able, versus having a tacit disposition), and the empirical constraints it must respect—particularly findings about procedural memory consolidation and the dissociation between declarative and procedural cognitive systems. For each requirement, state the motivating consideration and whether it is non-negotiable or a camp-specific preference (for instance, the anti-intellectualist demand that knowing-how be irreducibly practical, versus the intellectualist allowance for inarticulate propositional knowledge). Identify which single desideratum does the most damage to reductionist views that treat knowing-how as merely propositional knowledge with practical content. Also examine whether any subset of these requirements is jointly unsatisfiable—say, if accommodating imitation-based learning requires genuinely non-propositional content, while accommodating expert verbal failure is compatible with hidden propositional representations—since that tension is itself a result. Keep the weight on the criteria and their motivating cases rather than on cataloguing particular philosophers.
