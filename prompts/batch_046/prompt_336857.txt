I'm preparing a moderated colloquium Q&A for a philosophy podcast series. The guest is a philosopher defending a strict, extended version of the anti-aggregationist position associated with John Taurek in rescue ethics: specifically, they hold that in any distributive decision involving lives, the mere fact that more people are on one side never strengthens the collective claim, and that the only permissible response to a tie is a fair lottery among all affected individuals. The guest distinguishes this from utilitarian aggregation by arguing that "structural aggregation"—pooling resources to make rescue more reliable for everyone—is permissible, but "numerical weighting"—treating five lives as carrying five times the moral urgency of one—is a category error that treats separate persons as parts of a composite suffering unit.

Write me a sequence of eight to ten interview questions that avoid biography and simple restatement, and that press the actual content of the position.

Requirements:

- Include at least one question confronting the intuition that saving the greater number is obviously correct, paired with a follow-up keyed to the likely reply that this intuition tracks social coordination rather than moral weight.
- Include at least one question on why a fair lottery is the appropriate tie-breaker rather than proximity, kinship, or temporal priority, with a follow-up addressing the objection that randomization itself ignores morally relevant differences between candidates.
- Include at least one question on heterogeneous rescue groups—say, five elderly individuals with low survival probability versus one child—anticipating the reply that introducing qualitative weights collapses back into aggregation, and then pushing whether that reply makes the view implausibly rigid.
- Include at least one question extending the view beyond physical rescue to institutional resource allocation, such as public-health triage or disaster-response protocols, asking what practical decision procedure follows and where judgment must replace rule.
- Close with a question asking what institutional or empirical evidence—such as audit data from triage protocols or behavioral studies of rescue decision-making—would genuinely count against the claim that headcount should never be weighted. The follow-up should ask whether the defender would concede that widespread, stable moral disagreement about the lottery method itself could be a defeater, or whether they would dismiss it as a failure of moral education.

Keep the tone formal but conversational. Do not ask for citations, bibliographies, or reading lists.
