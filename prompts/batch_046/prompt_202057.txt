At a recent philosophy of science workshop, two colleagues debated the implications of Quine’s naturalism and ontological commitments for moral philosophy. The first argued that Quine’s rejection of the analytic-synthetic distinction and his web-of-belief model entail that moral judgments are continuous with empirical claims: they occupy nodes in our overall conceptual scheme, are revisable in principle by scientific or anthropological findings, and must be interpreted within Quine’s physicalist ontology, which admits no non-natural moral properties. From this, she concluded that Quine is best read as a moral naturalist for whom ethical disagreement is largely a matter of descriptive divergence about human practices and dispositions, rather than irreducible normative conflict. The second respondent objected that this misapplies epistemological holism. Quine’s point, he said, concerns the underdetermination of physical theory by sensory evidence; moral judgments serve practical and expressive functions rather than explanatory-predictive ones, so they do not enter the web of belief in the same evidential role as scientific hypotheses. He further argued that Quine’s criterion of ontological commitment—accept only what our best overall theory requires—does not automatically exclude irreducible normative elements if they figure indispensably in practical reasoning, and that Quine’s own scattered remarks on values suggest cultural variability rather than reduction to physical description. The first speaker countered that practical reasoning itself can be naturalized within Quine’s behaviorist psychology, leaving no residue of irreducible normativity. The second replied that behaviorist psychology describes dispositions to assent or dissent, not the justificatory structure of moral argument, and that conflating the two collapses ethics into sociology without accounting for the internal logic of normative debate.

Please adjudicate their exchange point by point. For each major move—(1) the extension of holism to ethics, (2) the ontological argument from physicalism, (3) the interpretation of Quine’s own remarks on values, and (4) the behaviorist naturalization of practical reasoning—say which speaker has the stronger Quinean ground and why. Then say whether either party is missing a third option that Quine’s framework actually permits, and whether the disagreement ultimately reveals an ambiguity in Quine’s philosophy or a genuine disagreement about its scope.
