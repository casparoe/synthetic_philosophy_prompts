After a long and somewhat fractious argument with a close friend about whether deflationary theories of truth are genuinely explanatory or merely notational devices, we tried to adjudicate the dispute by constructing a specific test case we call endorsed disquotation under modal uncertainty. The scenario involves a scientist who says, "If the quantum-gravity conjecture is correct, then it is true that the quantum-gravity conjecture is correct," and who insists that the embedded clause carries a distinctive normative commitment—an explicit endorsement of the conjecture's epistemic authority—that the bare conditional lacks, even though she denies that "truth" denotes any substantive property like correspondence to reality. We want to know which deflationary framework best accommodates the intuition that the disquotational move here functions as a genuine act of endorsement rather than a redundant repetition, without smuggling in a substantial truth-property or collapsing into a minimalist equivalence schema that renders the two formulations interchangeable. Please rank the following five positions from 1 (best accommodates the intuition) to 5 (worst), and assign each a score from 1 to 10: (1) Ramsey-style redundancy theory, (2) Horwich's minimalism centered on the equivalence schema, (3) Grover-Camp-Belnap prosententialism, (4) Quine-style disquotationalism, and (5) Strawson's performative theory. For each entry, provide a single sentence explaining whether its core machinery allows the endorsement-like force to survive disquotation, or whether it must treat the speaker's intuition as a purely pragmatic overlay with no semantic weight.
