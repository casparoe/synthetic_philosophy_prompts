I am a senior at my high school and I edit the philosophy interview column for our school's academic journal. For our upcoming issue I will be interviewing a philosopher — I will refer to her as Dr. Elena Voss — who defends what she calls "asymmetric conventionalism about micro-obligations." Her view is that everyday practices such as tipping in American restaurants, queuing in British public spaces, and observing pedestrian signals are not merely social habits but can generate genuine moral obligations, though the strength and kind of obligation depend on the agent's relationship to the local normative framework. For permanent residents, she holds that these conventions create derivative duties of fairness because residents benefit from the coordination scheme. For visitors, however, she argues that the same practices generate only prudential recommendations unless the visitor crosses what she terms a "temporal-commitment threshold" — specifically, residing or working in the locale for more than six months — at which point the visitor acquires the same derivative duties as a local. She extends this analysis to jaywalking: where pedestrian law functions primarily as a coordination mechanism rather than a safety mandate, a visitor who crosses against the signal does not wrong any particular individual, but she claims the act degrades what she calls a "predictability pool" that locals rely on for efficient coordination.

I need eight to ten interview questions that go well beyond asking her to restate this framework. I want at least one question focused specifically on her choice of a six-month temporal cutoff rather than an intentional-commitment marker, such as signing a lease or accepting paid employment; please include a follow-up keyed to her likely response that temporal boundaries are administratively tractable. I also want one question that presses her on the predictability pool idea in the jaywalking case: if the pool is non-rival and non-excludable, why should one tourist's deviation constitute a moral failure rather than merely statistical noise? For that one, anticipate her probable reply that cumulative expectation shifts erode the pool, and then include a follow-up asking how she distinguishes such erosion from a mere collective preference for uniformity. Finally, I want a question comparing the moral status of a tourist who refuses to tip in the United States with one who cuts in a queue: does the monetary dimension of tipping create a categorically different wrong, or are both violations simply instances of free-riding on a local coordination scheme? Throughout, the questions should be formal enough for publication in a school journal yet sharp enough that she cannot answer them by repeating her opening summary.
