As a journalist preparing an in-depth feature for a science-and-ideas magazine, I want a diagnostic decision tree—phrased as a taxonomy of positions rather than a list of named philosophers—to sort thinkers on Peter Singer’s drowning-child analogy and the rescue obligations it implies. The instrument should start from the commitment that geographical distance does not, by itself, diminish moral urgency, then branch through three concrete, answerable distinctions that sit in the weeds of the debate. First, the causal-proximity split: does obligation diminish with the length and opacity of the causal chain, and can institutional intermediaries restore or even amplify it despite adding links? Second, the supererogation-boundary split: is there a principled threshold—perhaps defined relative to basic capabilities or life-plans—beyond which rescue becomes optional, and does that threshold apply differently to one-off versus repeated demands? Third, the collective-action split: when many agents could rescue, does duty distribute evenly, collapse onto the nearest agent, or become conditional on coordination; include here the niche view I will call distributed complicity, which holds that in large groups individual duty is not proportionally reduced but transformed into a duty to support collective mechanisms, even if that support is less efficacious than direct action. Each branch should be a question answerable without knowing the label it leads to. The tree must locate at least six distinct leaves—including a strict consequentialist, a threshold deontologist, a virtue-ethical moderate, the distributed-complicity position, a libertarian refusal, and one unoccupied combination that you flag as incoherent or unexplored—with brief diagnoses of which answer-patterns reach them. Note any pair of leaves that the tree cannot cleanly separate without an extra premise. Please present this in formal, polished British English suitable for a magazine feature, without bibliographies, reading lists, or requests for citations, and rely only on widely established philosophical attributions that require no verification.
