I am a retired evolutionary biologist who has only recently begun reading philosophy of medicine in a structured way, and I am now preparing a single ninety-minute session for a reading group of fellow retired natural scientists—engineers, chemists, and clinicians—none of whom have studied philosophy formally. The concept I want them to grasp is the conceptual tension between naturalist and normativist accounts of disease: on one side, the biostatistical idea that disease is statistically subnormal functioning relative to a species-typical design; on the other, the claim that biological dysfunction alone is insufficient without an accompanying harm or failure of well-being. I am not asking for a lecture on the debate itself, but for a detailed teaching plan. Specifically, I need the dependency-ordered sequence in which these ideas must be introduced—what conceptual foundation must be laid before statistical typicality can be understood, and why the normative component cannot be presented first without generating confusion; the one carrying example that should carry the entire session, together with explicit justification for its selection and a clear statement of why more familiar illustrations such as sickle-cell trait or clinical hypertension should be set aside at this stage; the predictable misconceptions this audience will bring or construct—particularly the identification of statistical rarity with pathology, and the belief that adding a normative condition reduces disease to a social label—along with the precise moment in the sequence where each should be anticipated and defused; a concise check for understanding, preferably in the form of a single diagnostic question, that reveals whether the participants see why the biological and evaluative dimensions are jointly necessary rather than treating the distinction as a mere taxonomy; and an explicit list of what should be omitted here—particular philosophers’ full biographies, the historical development of the debate, or any request for bibliographic references—together with a brief rationale for each omission.

To keep the example precise and slightly unfamiliar to this group, I propose to build the session around the loss of endogenous vitamin C synthesis in humans due to the GULO pseudogene: a clear mammalian statistical deviation that is not experienced as disease when diet is adequate. Please construct the plan around this case, showing exactly how it exposes the gap between biological dysfunction and experienced harm without collapsing either conception into the other, and indicate at each step how a retired scientist’s instinct to treat statistical deviation as automatically pathological should be gently corrected without dismissing the biological data.
