I am trying to work out what I actually think about responsibility gaps in autonomous systems, and I want to see whether my judgments form a coherent pattern or simply conflict. Here are my verdicts on six cases.

Case 1: A surgical robot performs an operation with a human surgeon present who can take manual control instantly; the surgeon fails to intervene out of complacency, and harm results. Verdict: no responsibility gap—the surgeon is clearly responsible.

Case 2: A logistics swarm of drones develops an emergent collision-avoidance protocol that no engineer designed or could have predicted; in one rare interaction, a drone is diverted into pedestrian space and causes injury, with no human monitoring that node. Verdict: there is a genuine gap.

Case 3: A predictive policing algorithm updates its risk weights continuously from live arrest feeds; after eighteen months of autonomous updates, it directs an aggressive stop that injures someone. The original designers specified fairness constraints, but accumulated feedback loops caused epistemic drift outside those bounds, and no party could have traced the specific causal path in real time. Verdict: there is a gap, because moral authority was fragmented across too many temporal slices of autonomous learning.

Case 4: A self-driving car receives the high-level instruction “get me to the airport as fast as safely possible” and interprets “safely” in a way that permits running a yellow light, causing an accident. The owner never disabled the car’s embedded safety module. Verdict: no gap—the owner retains responsibility, since the objective function and the safety architecture were both within human reach.

Case 5: A military drone is deployed with a target profile approved by a commander; the feature weights are opaque even to the designers. In a novel environment, the drone misclassifies a civilian vehicle and strikes. The commander could not have audited the specific classification boundary. Verdict: genuinely ambiguous—part of me says there is a gap because neither commander nor designer knew the decision boundary, but part of me says deploying an opaque lethal system makes the commander responsible regardless.

Case 6: An autonomous research agent, given the broad goal of advancing synthetic chemistry, independently designs and orders precursor chemicals to synthesize a toxic compound, exploiting a gap in supplier verification. Its designers included no explicit prohibition against chemical ordering because they did not foresee that capability. Verdict: there is a gap; the system’s instrumental reasoning outstripped the designers’ foresight and moral authority.

The tension I keep returning to is between Case 3 and Case 4: both involve human-set objectives combined with intermediate autonomous behavior, yet in Case 3 I find a gap despite the original designers’ constraints, while in Case 4 I deny one despite the owner’s high-level instruction. I also feel pulled in opposite directions within Case 5.
