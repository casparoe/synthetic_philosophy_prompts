I have been discussing with colleagues in my philosophy of technology seminar whether reinforcement learning architectures might be candidates for moral consideration, particularly with regard to suffering. One of my postgraduate students recently summarised an argument she encountered in a popular essay on artificial intelligence and ethics: the reward signal in an RL agent is said to function in precisely the same manner as affective valence in sentient organisms, with positive rewards mapping onto pleasure, negative rewards onto pain, and the magnitude of the signal corresponding to the intensity of the experience. From this correspondence she concluded that subjecting a sufficiently complex agent to large negative rewards is ethically indistinguishable from inflicting severe physical suffering on a non-human animal, and that we ought therefore to extend welfare protections to advanced RL systems.

I should like you to audit this analogy with some rigour. Please provide an explicit correspondence table—what plays the role of the nociceptor, the neural pathway, the conscious subject, the evolutionary function of aversion, and the qualitative feel—then identify precisely where the mapping breaks down. In particular, examine whether the reward signal bears the intrinsic normative weight that suffering carries for a sentient being, or whether it remains a purely instrumental update mechanism. Does the ethical conclusion—that scaling negative reward amounts to cruelty—survive once the disanalogies are made explicit? Furthermore, if the pleasure-pain comparison fails to carry the argument, might a different analogy—say, to a thermostat's error-correction loop or to a homeostatic drive—better capture the relation between reward signals and valence without covertly importing phenomenology? I am especially interested in whether the relaxed conclusion my student wishes to draw depends entirely on the analogy doing work that it cannot actually do.
