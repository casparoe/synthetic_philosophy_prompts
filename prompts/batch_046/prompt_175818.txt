I'm a novelist trying to get the psycholgy of an impossible decision right for my next book, and I keep coming back to Sophie's Choice—both the novel and the film version everyone knows. I want to read it through the idea of incommensurable values and hard choices, but not in the usual "tragic choise" way people always do. Specifically, I'm interested in Ruth Chang's notion that some options are "on a par"—not better, worse, or equal—and whether Sophie's decision can be understood as a rational commitment that creates value rather than just trauma. But I'm also playing with a slightly niche twist: what if the values at stake aren't just in conflict but literally fail to be comparable in principle, so that any decision procedure is an imposition rather than a discovery? Does the work treat her choise as meaningful because she authorises it, or does it present it as pure arbitrary violence she does to herself? I'd like a reading that takes both the story and the philosophy seriously—where the idea illuminates a scene or Sophie's interiority, and where the work pushes back and the honest critic has to admit the reading doesn't fit. I'm writing this for my own notes, not an essay, so don't bother with a bibliography or reading list—just talk it through. Also, please use British English. And if the whole premise is muddled—like if I'm misapplying incommensurability to her specific dilemma, or if you can't do this without distorting either the book or the philosophy—just say so plainly instead of being polite about it.
