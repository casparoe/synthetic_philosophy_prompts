Draft the joint statement of an adversarial collaboration between a Universal Calibration theorist and a Dominance-Resistant Pluralist on the problem of ascription universality and epistemic dominance. The Universal Calibration theorist holds that practices of ascribing mental states, moral status, and epistemic authority can be evaluated against cross-contextual functional and normative criteria, and that treating every local variation as equally valid collapses into an untenable relativism that obscures genuine error. The Dominance-Resistant Pluralist holds that any purported universal calibration framework necessarily encodes the normative commitments of historically dominant epistemic traditions, that ascription is constitutively shaped by local practices of recognition rather than by discoverable universal conditions, and that imposing universal criteria operates as epistemic violence that erases plural forms of life.

Your deliverable must contain four parts. First, a numbered section of agreed claims—statements both sides can sign—covering the reality of cross-cultural variation in ascription practices, the documented history of epistemic dominance in colonial and institutional contexts, and the present limits of both empirical anthropology and normative philosophy in resolving these disputes. Second, a disagreements table with two columns, one phrased so Universal Calibration advocates would accept their own position and one so Dominance-Resistant Pluralists would accept theirs. The table must cover at least: whether a non-parochial universal standard for correct ascription exists; whether the act of universalizing can ever be separated from the exercise of epistemic dominance; and whether local ascription practices are incommensurable with one another or merely differently calibrated. Third, identify the cruxes—sub-questions such that settling them would move at least one side. Include specifically: (a) the reflexivity problem, i.e., whether the universalist framework can apply to itself without circularity; (b) the non-substitutability thesis, i.e., whether thick descriptions of local recognition practices can be translated into universal functional terms without normative loss; and (c) the predictive divergence case involving a concrete hypothetical society in which attributions of individual belief are systematically refused in favor of collective epistemic attribution. Fourth, for each crux specify one concrete case, argument, or empirical pattern both sides agree is genuinely relevant, and record each side's explicit advance prediction about what that case will show. For crux (c), specify exactly what conceptual or observational finding about that distributed-cognition society each camp predicts.

Format the agreed claims as a numbered list. Format the disagreements as a table. Keep every formulation precise and neutral—no straw-manning, no rhetorical loading, and no dependence on specific bibliographic citations, author names, or dated texts. The document should read as something both parties could put their names to, with the residual disagreement made exact rather than dissolved into verbal dispute.
