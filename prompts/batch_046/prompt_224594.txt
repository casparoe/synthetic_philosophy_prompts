Student answer: Wittgenstein's hinge propositions—such as that the earth has existed for a long time or that I have hands—are best understood as the most secure tier of our empirical knowledge. They are propositions so thoroughly confirmed by experience and embedded in our cognitive practices that they have become practically immune to revision, functioning as fixed points around which particular doubts turn. Unlike Cartesian foundations, they are not justified by rational intuition or clear and distinct perception; rather, their security comes from massive empirical corroboration over time. Unlike Moore's common-sense claims, however, they are not merely pieces of knowledge that happen to enjoy extraordinary confidence; Wittgenstein relocates them to a structural role, making them conditions for the possibility of justification rather than results of it. Because they stand outside the ordinary game of giving and asking for reasons, they are not known in the standard propositional sense—knowledge requires the possibility of doubt, and hinges are precisely what we do not doubt. Nevertheless, since they lack evidential support, Wittgenstein must regard them as arbitrary commitments that we adopt without reason. We could, in principle, revise them through rational reflection if we chose to, just as we revise scientific theories when the evidence shifts, though in practice our form of life makes such revision extremely unlikely. Furthermore, when Wittgenstein says that having two hands is not a belief he holds, this should be read as a psychological claim: people do not experience these propositions as occurrent mental states, even though they remain tacit empirical assumptions operating in the background of our reasoning.
