Two colleagues in a graduate seminar on comparative historical sociology have been arguing over whether “collapse” is a coherent analytical category or merely a modern projection onto past transformations. In the first round, one—call her A—argued that applying the term to the Classic Maya or to Easter Island is anachronistic ethnocentrism. She pointed out that after the southern Maya lowland cities were depopulated, populations persisted in the northern Yucatán and highlands with modified but continuous cultural practices, and that writing, though diminished in the lowlands, was never fully extinguished. For Easter Island, she noted that the shift from the moai-building ancestor cult to the Birdman competition represents not disappearance but religious reorganization, and that labeling it “collapse” implicitly privileges large-scale monument-building as the only legitimate form of social complexity.

Her interlocutor, B, replied that this is semantic evasion. He conceded that populations did not vanish, but insisted that losing roughly eighty to ninety percent of urban settlement, seeing the near-total cessation of monumental construction and the localized disappearance of a writing system, and enduring centuries without the previous institutional framework constitutes catastrophic failure by any meaningful metric, not neutral change. He added that on Easter Island the deforestation and subsequent shift to warfare and cannibalism marked the breakdown of a prior social contract, not a voluntary reorganization.

In the second round, A invoked the adaptive-cycle framework from ecological resilience theory—sometimes called panarchy—to claim that societies, like ecosystems, pass through phases of growth, conservation, release, and reorganization. She argued that what B calls “collapse” is better understood as the “release” phase, a rapid but structurally normal transition that makes renewal possible. She further claimed that calling it failure smuggles in a normative preference for centralized, large-scale polities, and that without direct testimony from ancient participants we have no right to say they experienced these shifts as disasters rather than liberations—perhaps the end of coerced moai labor was welcomed.

B acknowledged the descriptive utility of the adaptive cycle but denied that it resolves the normative and causal questions. He introduced the idea of an “infrastructure of expectation”—the shared set of commitments about security, meaning, and continuity that a society maintains for its members—and argued that collapse should be defined not by total extinction but by the rapid, simultaneous destruction of that infrastructure across interdependent domains faster than it can be rebuilt. He applied this to the Late Bronze Age Eastern Mediterranean, where palace economies, long-distance trade, and literacy collapsed in synchrony within a generation, producing a centuries-long interruption. Even if populations eventually reorganized into smaller polities, he said, the breakdown was so severe and, where textual hints exist—as in the Ugaritic correspondence—experienced as traumatic loss that “collapse” remains the appropriate term.

In the third round, A pushed back that B’s criterion is irremediably subjective: we lack psychological reports for the Classic Maya or Easter Islanders, so invoking an “infrastructure of expectation” projects modern sentiments onto silent archaeological records. She proposed a stricter, observable standard: call it collapse only when there is irreversible extinction of a cultural lineage—language death or total demographic replacement. By that standard, she said, neither the Maya nor Easter Island qualifies, and B’s framework would implausibly deny that the Western Roman Empire collapsed, reducing it instead to “specialization” into smaller kingdoms.

B responded that her standard sets the bar so high that it makes the concept useless for historical analysis. He argued that the Bronze Age and Maya cases share a specific structural signature—synchronous, interdependent breakdown of trade, administration, and symbolic systems triggered by external shock or internal fragility—that distinguishes them from gradual evolution. He concluded that A’s adaptive-cycle framing obscures this causal specificity by treating all rapid change as internal renewal, and that her demand for direct testimony is an impossible evidentiary standard that would prevent us from diagnosing any past societal crisis.

Please adjudicate point by point: is A right that the adaptive-cycle framework undermines the concept of collapse, or does it merely redescribe it? Is B’s “infrastructure of expectation” a legitimate analytical addition or an untestable projection? Does A’s extinction-only criterion save the concept of transformation at the cost of making “collapse” empirically empty? And finally, what should we say about Easter Island—does the shift to the Birdman cult represent a failure of the prior society, or is that judgment precisely the anachronism A warns against?
