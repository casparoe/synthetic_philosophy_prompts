I'm taking a philosophy of mind seminar focused on Douglas Hofstadter's account of the self as a strange loop—specifically, the idea that an I is not a fixed substrate but a pattern of causal feedback that can, in principle, persist across alterations in its medium. Our professor has reconstructed a consistent Hofstadter-inspired position through her rulings on five distribution scenarios we've discussed this term. First, in the classic split-brain case where the corpus callosum is severed but both hemispheres remain in one body, she held that a single I persists, though its control is bifurcated; there are not two selves because the bodily loop remains unified. Second, when both hemispheres are transplanted into separate clone bodies, she ruled that the original I is destroyed rather than duplicated—there may be two new centers of narrative gravity, but neither is the original self. Third, regarding gradual neuron-by-neuron replacement with synthetic equivalents preserving all functional relations, she affirmed continuous persistence; substrate independence holds so long as the loop's causal structure is maintained. Fourth, she denied that a complete digital scan running on a computer without biological embodiment preserves the I; the loop requires living, embodied feedback, so the copy is a simulation, not the self. Fifth, in a case where a recipient receives a partial hippocampal graft carrying another person's autobiographical memories, she said the recipient remains one I; the alien memories are incorporated data, not a second strange loop.

Now consider this new case. Subject X gradually enters a bidirectional neural bridge with two other adults, Y and Z, over several months. X's autobiographical patterns are distributed across all three brains, interwoven with Y's and Z's prior narratives. The bridge permits real-time co-consciousness: when connected, they can jointly deliberate and share a present experiential field, but they can also disconnect and function independently, each retaining the distributed fragments. Eventually X's original body dies; the patterns survive only within Y and Z, who can reconnect to reactivate a unified perspective or remain separate.

Predict her verdict: does X's I now persist as a single distributed self spanning Y and Z, is it fragmented into hybrid successors, or is it extinguished? Identify the implicit rule or rules—perhaps substrate-independent pattern continuity, embodied loop closure requiring a single organism, or narrative-center gravity—that best explain her five prior rulings and that generate divergent predictions here. Give your best guess with reasoning, and specify which further hypothetical case would best discriminate between the leading rules you identify.
