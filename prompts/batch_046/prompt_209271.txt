I'm in my early thirties and recently married, and my partner and I have been actively discussing whether to have children. In the course of these conversations we've worked through some of the population-ethics literature, particularly the procreation asymmetry, and I've found that my gut reactions to specific scenarios feel more stable than any formal theory I've encountered—yet they don't line up neatly. Here are my verdicts, which I hope reveal a hidden principle or expose an inconsistency I need to resolve.

If a couple knows they could conceive a child who would live an extraordinarily flourishing life—deep relationships, meaningful work, robust health, and sustained joy—and they simply decide not to, that strikes me as entirely permissible. There is no failure of duty, not even a minor one. By contrast, if a couple knows they will conceive a child who will suffer from a severe, untreatable condition and live in constant pain, that strikes me as clearly wrong. The asymmetry feels real: suffering creation is prohibited, but happy creation is not required.

My reactions get murkier around the middle and in comparative cases. Suppose a couple undergoing IVF has two viable embryos: one that will develop into a person whose life is just barely worth living—mostly neutral, with occasional hardship and only modest joy—and one that will develop into a clearly flourishing person. They arbitrarily select the barely-positive embryo. I feel this is wrong, though less gravely wrong than creating the severely suffering child. Yet I also feel no obligation to conceive the flourishing embryo at all if they had not planned to have children, which seems inconsistent: why should the presence of an alternative potential child generate a comparative duty? Another tension involves rescue versus creation. I think it is wrong to conceive a barely-happy child even if doing so is the only way to save five existing people—say, the child is needed for a bizarre but effective organ-donation chain. The intuition that we should not manufacture a servant-person for others feels strong. But if five people already exist and one must suffer to save them, I think the rescue is permissible, perhaps required. So my asymmetry applies to creation but not to harm among the living, and I cannot tell whether that boundary is principled or ad hoc. Finally, there is a case that keeps returning: a couple could, at essentially zero cost, prevent conception of a severely suffering child—by selecting a different genetic combination in IVF—but they fail to do so. I think they have done something wrong. But if they could, at the same cost, cause conception of an additional flourishing child—by implanting an extra embryo—I feel no corresponding pull. The absence of a positive duty to create feels right, yet the presence of a negative duty combined with this comparative selection-duty seems to smuggle in an obligation to maximize welfare over potential lives that I otherwise reject.

Is there a single principle—perhaps involving person-affecting reasons, a threshold for harm, or the moral relevance of the non-identity problem—that can accommodate all of these without collapsing into either simple welfare maximization or a bare "no obligation either way" symmetry? If not, which of my verdicts should I abandon: the comparative wrongness of selecting the worse embryo, the resistance to creating a servant-person, or the strong asymmetry between not-creating-happy and not-creating-suffering lives? And what does the best available principle predict for a case I have not mentioned—say, a society that could, through universal genetic screening, eliminate all severely suffering births but also, as an unavoidable side effect, reduce the total number of births by half?
