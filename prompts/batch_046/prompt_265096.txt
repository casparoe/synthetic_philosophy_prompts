I sit on the advisory board for our university's anthropology museum, and we're finally dealing with the three Benin plaques that have sat in storage since the 1980s. They came to us in 1978 as part of a larger bequest from a private collector who bought them at a Sotheby's sale in 1953 — there's paperwork, but the provenence trail basically stops at "acquired in London, circa 1952." Meanwhile, a Nigerian national museums commission has asked not just for physical return but for something they're calling "epistemic restitution" — the idea that returning the objects without also transferring curatorial authority and interpretive sovereignty just recreates colonial power dynamics in a different register. Our director needs a concrete reccomendation by next Friday's meeting: do we return the plaques unconditionally, return them with some shared-custody agreement, or hold them pending further research?

I'm drafting the memo for circulation and I'm kinda stuck between the legal risk and the moral argument. Our legal counsel says any return before 2026 could violate the donor-agreement terms, and half the board thinks that's just a technicality we should ignore. The other half thinks epistemic restitution is too vague to operationalize, but they still want to take it seriously rather than dismiss it out of hand. Please write the memo recommending one course of action with a clear time frame, treat the epistemic-restitution argument honestly even if you end up rejecting it, and include the strongest consideration for the side you're arguing against. Make it something the board could actually vote on Thursday — not just philosophy, but a decision with concrete steps.
