I'm trying to understand Douglas Hofstadter's claim that an "I" is not confined to one brain but is essentially a dynamic pattern—something like a strange loop of symbol and self-reference—that can be sustained, in partial or degraded form, in other brains that have internalized it, so that fragments of a person's self might persist after biological death in friends, students, or readers who continue to "run" aspects of that pattern.

Restate this position as faithfully as you can in the vocabulary I actually use: treat the self-pattern as analogous to a developmental program that can be horizontally transmitted or maintained across multiple bodies rather than vertically through a germ line, and describe its persistence in others using the language of colony-level selection, distributed nervous systems, and the evolutionary conditions under which a trait can be decoupled from the genome of its original bearer. Then give me the residue report: which parts of Hofstadter's view map cleanly onto biological talk about multicellular integration or shared cognitive architectures, which parts require me to distort biological concepts—for instance, by treating cultural transmission as if it had replication fidelity comparable to DNA, or by attributing intentional states to a colony in ways that violate standard evolutionary individuation criteria—and which parts seem to have no biological counterpart at all, especially any phenomenological or normative claims about what it feels like to be that distributed pattern or about the moral significance of its persistence. Finally, tell me whether the residue points mainly to a limitation in evolutionary biology's ability to capture subjective continuity, or whether it suggests that Hofstadter is smuggling in metaphysical commitments that do not actually depend on his "pattern" metaphor and that the biological translation exposes as ungrounded.
