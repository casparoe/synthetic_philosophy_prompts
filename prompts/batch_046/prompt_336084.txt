Since retiring from experimental physics I have been working through philosophy of mind, and I recently heard the slogan "there is no view from nowhere" invoked to dismiss the possibility of an objective neuroscience of consciousness—on the grounds that every observer is necessarily embedded inside the very phenomenon being studied. I want it unpacked into its distinct precise theses: the trivial claim that all observation has a standpoint; the stronger claim that first-person experience is epistemically irreducible; and the radical claim that third-person science is systematically distorted by its outsider position. For each reading, say whether it is defensible, whether it confuses the inside/outside distinction with a failure of objectivity, and whether it actually licenses the anti-scientific inference my interlocutor drew. I am especially interested in how these interpretations apply when a scientist studies her own mental states: does the slogan then imply that self-observation is uniquely authoritative, uniquely unreliable, or neither, and what does that do to the demand for epistemic modesty?
