Here is an argument I built for a seminar and now find suspicious because it seems to dissolve the very capacity I'm using to evaluate it.

(1) Darwin's strange inversion is that complex biological competence—adaptive design—can be fully produced by natural selection without any comprehension, foresight, or representational grasp of outcomes on the part of the selecting mechanism.

(2) Turing's strange inversion is that behavioral and cognitive competence—passing as intelligent—can be fully produced by a discrete-state machine manipulating symbols by formal rules without any internal comprehension, consciousness, or semantic understanding.

(3) So in both cases, observed competence is explained by an underlying mechanism that itself involves no comprehension whatsoever.

(4) Human scientific reasoning, moral judgment, and first-person self-understanding are observed competences.

(5) By the same explanatory logic, these human competences should be accounted for by underlying neural, evolutionary, or computational mechanisms that lack comprehension.

(6) But then to say "humans genuinely comprehend" is either false or redundant, since every appearance of comprehension is just another mechanistic competence to be explained away without residue.

(7) Therefore comprehension is never a valid explanatory category; it is always an illusion projected onto competent behavior.

The conclusion is absurd, since formulating and assessing this very argument seems to require comprehension. Where does the chain break? Which step is least costly to reject, and what does giving it up commit me to—perhaps a principled distinction between levels of explanation, between origin and current functioning, or between third-person mechanism and first-person access? I would prefer to keep (1) and (2) intact if possible.
