I teach an upper-level philosophy of mind and artificial intelligence seminar. I want one final essay question that students can answer in 90 minutes (approximately 800 words) on whether reinforcement learning agents can suffer, with the specific focus on the structural analogy between reward signals and affective valence. The question must be designed so that it cannot be answered by reciting the standard claim that agents lack consciousness and therefore cannot suffer. Instead, it should force students to engage with a specific conceptual tension: reward in RL is typically characterized as a scalar feedback signal that updates value estimates through prediction-error mechanisms, whereas affective valence—especially in embodied and predictive-processing frameworks—is often characterized as an interoceptive evaluation mode tied to allostatic regulation and homeostatic set-point maintenance. Ask students to evaluate whether this functional-structural analogy can be sustained even under a non-substance-dualist, functionalist account of suffering, and whether the absence of an embodied allostatic architecture in standard RL agents undermines the analogy at the mechanistic level rather than merely at the substrate level. Please provide the exam question, a model answer of about 700 words, and a marking scheme built around five must-hit points (with marks assigned to each, totaling 20 points), plus a one-line note on how candidates typically miss each point. The difficulty should come from requiring students to separate the question of phenomenological consciousness from the question of functional organization, and to say precisely where the analogy between reward and valence breaks down—or survives—without appealing to unargued intuitions about silicon versus carbon.
