I hold that nuclear deterrence achieves stability not through credible rational brinkmanship but through structural automaticity: organizational and technical lock-in that removes the option of restrained retaliation, so that adversaries face an unavoidable rather than a calculated response. On this view, doctrines of flexible response and escalation control actually weaken deterrence by reintroducing the belief that retaliation can be calibrated, delayed, or bargained away. What would count against this position? I want the arguments, historical cases, and formal considerations that should move me, ranked from decisive to merely awkward. In particular, are there documented crises in which automatic command structures amplified rather than reduced escalation risk? Can formal models of deterrence accommodate organizational inertia without presupposing agent-level rationality? And has this view been qualified into unfalsifiability—if every apparent failure is reinterpreted as insufficient lock-in—and is that insulation a defect for a theory of strategic stability? I would find especially decisive any well-supported case in which deliberate restraint or negotiable escalation proved more stabilizing than irreversibility.
