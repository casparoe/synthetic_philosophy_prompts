I'm working on a memo about AI refusal policies and got into this weird argument with a friend last week over whether an assistant that 'soft-refuses' — it doesn't say no outright but silently rewrites your query into a safer version — is actually more paternalistc than a hard block. I'm not totally convinced by the framing, but it made me realize there's a whole layer of open conceptual questions that people aren't really sorting out yet.

So here's what I actually want: which lines of inquiry in AI paternalism should get priority if you're trying to build a defensible policy framework? I'm especially wondering about three things. First, the split between epistemic paternalism (the model assumes it knows your real interests) versus volitional paternalism (it thinks your expressed preferences are corrupted by frmaing or fatigue). Second, compound refusal in multi-turn pipelines: if refusing step A changes your belief state so step B looks totally different, does the system owe you some disclosure about those downstream cognitive effects? Third, is there a meaningful analog to Neurath's protocol sentences — those basic observational reports that are hard to deny — for refusal justification? Like, should any override be grounded in statements the user can't rationally reject without giving up shared premises?

Of these, or others I'm missing, which matter most and in what order? I don't want a bibliography or reading list — I just want a defended agenda: for each, why it's upstream of other work, how tractible it is for philosophers versus ML people, and what kind of argument or evidence over the next couple years would bump it up or down. Also, be honest about which ones sound important but wouldn't actually change how refusal policies get designed in practice.
