I'm a doctoral student in political philosophy working on democratic boundary problems at the subnational level, specifically on climate-adaptation committees in agricultural regions where seasonal non-citizen laborers are affected by irrigation and land-use decisions but hold no formal membership in the municipal demos. At a workshop last month, after I raised the concern that extending voting rights to temporary residents might generate overlapping jurisdictions and accountability gaps, a senior scholar interrupted me with the line: "Affectedness fixes the boundary; citizenship is just an administrative proxy." The remark was being used to dismiss my worry as a category mistake—treating an empirical administrative feature as if it could constrain the normative boundary of the demos.

Please disambiguate the precise theses that sentence could be expressing. Lay them out as a menu running from the trivial to the radical: for each reading, indicate whether it is a platitude, a substantive and defensible democratic principle, a contentious normative claim, or false; and specify which readings actually license dismissing jurisdictional-overlap objections about temporary residents, and which leave my concern untouched. In particular, distinguish (i) descriptive claims about how existing institutions happen to align citizenship with affectedness, (ii) normative claims that affectedness is necessary and sufficient for inclusion, (iii) conceptual claims that citizenship just is affectedness under another name, and (iv) any stronger metaphysical or methodological claims about what "fixes" a boundary. I need to be able to ask: if the scholar meant reading X, does that genuinely answer my objection, or does it merely change the subject?
