Consider the following verdicts I hold about vagueness across different kinds of cases. I want to know whether they can all be captured by a single principle, or whether I must reject at least one.

(1) Hairline case: A man has exactly 5,000 hairs, distributed in the usual pattern across his scalp. I think it is genuinely indeterminate whether he counts as bald; there is no hidden fact about a precise cutoff that I am merely ignorant of.

(2) Sculptural ruin: A classical statue has lost precisely 50 percent of its original marble mass, evenly distributed across the form. I think there is no fact of the matter whether it remains "the same statue" or has become "a ruin." At the same time, I think that if an art-restoration charter were to stipulate a sharp cutoff at 49.9 percent loss, that stipulation would be conventional rather than metaphysically revelatory.

(3) Mid-spectrum color: A printed color patch sits exactly at the calibrated midpoint between standardized red and orange on a neutral color wheel, viewed in standard lighting. Here I am inclined toward an epistemic view: I suspect our color-categorization mechanisms do have a sharp boundary that we cannot introspect, so the patch is determinately one or the other, and my uncertainty is purely epistemic.

(4) Geological reclassification: A hill was called a "mountain" by local usage for centuries, then a geological survey reclassifies it using a precise elevation criterion (say, 600 meters). During the transitional period, I think the term is genuinely unsettled; but once the survey is adopted by the relevant institutions, the boundary becomes sharp and conventional, not metaphysically vague.

(5) Hidden-criterion height: In a medical study, "tall" is defined by a precise cutoff at 183.0 cm, encoded in the study protocol but unknown to a lay speaker assessing a subject who measures exactly 182.999 cm. I think the speaker's claim "he is tall" is determinately false—the hidden protocol fixes the fact—even though the speaker's ignorance is excusable.

These five feel consistent to me, but (1) and (5) seem to pull in opposite directions: (1) denies a hidden sharp boundary for baldness, while (5) asserts one for height. Meanwhile, (2) and (4) both involve conventions, yet (2) treats the convention as not resolving metaphysical indeterminacy, whereas (4) treats adoption of the convention as removing vagueness entirely.

Is there one principle—perhaps distinguishing kinds of vagueness (ontic, semantic, epistemic) or kinds of predicates (natural-kind versus institutional)—that delivers all five verdicts? If so, state it precisely, show which clause is motivated by which case, predict its verdict on a further case (for instance: a digital artwork reconstructed by an algorithm that replaces exactly 50 percent of its pixels with generative fills, where no restoration charter exists and no community has settled usage), and say honestly which of my five verdicts it cannot accommodate without strain. If no single principle works, present the two simplest competing candidates, assign each the verdicts it saves and the ones it sacrifices, and say which trade-off you think is better.
