I am working through a difficulty with G. E. M. Anscombe's critique of the atomic bombings of Hiroshima and Nagasaki—specifically, her claim that directing lethal force against non-combatants is murder regardless of any consequentialist justification—and I worry that the principle she deploys generalizes into an implausible paralysis of practical reasoning. I want an honest diagnosis of where the following argument fails, if it does.

(1) If an agent chooses an act that belongs to a kind that is intrinsically wrong—namely, the direct killing of innocents—then that agent commits murder, irrespective of any good that results.

(2) In ordering the atomic attacks, the relevant agent chose an act of precisely that kind: civilians were killed not as an accidental side effect of destroying military targets, but as a direct means to breaking Japanese resistance.

(3) Hence that agent committed murder.

(4) The moral force of (1) turns on the formal structure of the choice—intending the death of the innocent as a means—rather than on the magnitude of the harm.

(5) Now consider a state public-health agency that mandates a vaccination program knowing, with statistical precision, that a small, identifiable subset of healthy children will suffer fatal anaphylactic reactions, and knowing further that these deaths are an unavoidable feature of achieving the herd-immunity threshold required to prevent a far greater number of fatalities.

(6) The agency chooses a policy that foreseeably and structurally incorporates the death of specific innocents as a necessary condition for achieving the greater good.

(7) By the formal structure appealed to in (1) and (4), the agency therefore also commits murder.

(8) Yet standard moral judgment does not treat such public-health mandates as murderous; indeed, they are typically judged obligatory.

(9) So either (1) is false, or ordinary governance routinely involves murder.

Which premise or inferential step should be rejected, and what does rejecting it cost? In particular, can an Anscombian distinguish (2) from (6) without invoking a consequentialist weighing that her position officially forswears? Does the distinction between direct and indirect killing do the work, and if so, why does it not collapse once we notice that both cases involve precise foreknowledge of specific victims? I am also open to the possibility that the argument is valid and that the conclusion—however uncomfortable—must be accepted, together with its implications for practical reasoning. Please distinguish distinctions or objections that are genuinely decisive from those that are merely face-saving, and say what defenders of the position would themselves concede as counting against it.
