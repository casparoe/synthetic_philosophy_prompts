Having retired from a career in physics and only recently begun reading philosophy in earnest, I am drafting a proposal for a six-month independent study—perhaps pitched to a philosophy-of-science reading group or as a preliminary dissertation prospectus—on a question that sits at the boundary of logic and scientific practice. The question concerns Skolem's paradox: specifically, whether the paradox's demonstration that first-order formalizations of set theory can possess countable models containing "uncountable" sets reveals something structurally similar to the way physical theories invoke continuous, uncountably infinite spaces—real-valued fields, smooth manifolds—even though every actual measurement is finitely resolved and discrete. I want to determine whether physicists, in effect, live with a practical analog of Skolem's paradox, where formal entities exist within the model but cannot be mapped onto any countable domain of observation, and whether this analog undermines, clarifies, or leaves untouched realist commitments to continuous structure.

Please write the proposal with the following elements: a precise statement of the question that makes it determinately answerable rather than merely metaphorical; an account of why existing philosophical treatments—the standard logical resolution of Skolem's paradox on one hand, and broad instrumentalist or realist stances in philosophy of science on the other—leave the specific analogy unsettled; the methodological approach for a six-month timeline, including which physical modeling contexts you would examine and which conceptual distinctions (for instance, between model-relative and absolute cardinality, or between representational and ontological commitment) must be sharpened; an honest description of what a successful conclusion and a failed conclusion would each look like; a realistic assessment of the point at which the scope risks exceeding six months; and the one predictable misconception—likely that countability is an intrinsic property of a collection rather than a model-relative one—that must be defused for the argument to proceed. I will supply citations and a reading list separately; do not include a bibliography. The framing should be rigorous enough for someone accustomed to formal scientific models, but it should not assume specialist training in axiomatic set theory beyond familiarity with first-order logic.
