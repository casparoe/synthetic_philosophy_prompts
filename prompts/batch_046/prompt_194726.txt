I've been trying to formalize my stance on the surprise exam paradox for an epistemology workshop and need a consistency audit before I present it. Here is my current package of commitments: (1) I regard the backward-induction argument as formally valid—if the students know by Thursday evening that the exam has not yet occurred and that it must occur by Friday, then Friday is eliminated as surprising, and the same reasoning applies recursively to earlier days. (2) I analyze surprise in epistemic terms: an exam is surprising on a given day just in case the students lack knowledge of its occurrence on that specific day, not merely in case they feel unprepared. (3) I endorse rational closure without exception: if an agent rationally believes the premises of a valid argument, they ought to rationally believe the conclusion, and this holds even for temporal reasoning or reasoning about one's own future epistemic states. (4) I maintain that the teacher's announcement is fully coherent and can be satisfied: there is a possible scenario in which the exam falls on Wednesday, the announcement was truthful, and the students are genuinely surprised when it arrives. (5) I think the induction is blocked by a failure of common knowledge rather than by a logical mistake—each student can see that applying the induction requires mutual transparency about applying it, and the surprise condition precisely undermines that transparency, so the premises never achieve the status needed for closure to operate. (6) I deny that there is any stable distinction between knowing "there will be an exam next week" and knowing "it is Wednesday" once Wednesday is the only remaining day in the logical space; once the possibilities have been narrowed by deduction, general knowledge collapses into specific knowledge.
