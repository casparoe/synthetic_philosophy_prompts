I am a software engineer with no formal training in philosophy, though I have recently been discussing with colleagues whether it is rational to fear one's own death. In particular, I have been trying to understand the Epicurean argument that death is not bad for the one who dies, because the dead do not exist to suffer any harm; I have also encountered the symmetry argument—that the eternity before our birth was not harmful, so the eternity after death should not be either—but I am unsure how these abstract points hold up against the emotional reality of facing mortality. To think this through, I want to read Ingmar Bergman’s film The Seventh Seal through this lens. The plot, as I understand it, involves the knight Antonius Block returning from the Crusades, playing chess with Death to postpone his end, and attempting to perform one meaningful act—saving a traveling family of performers—before the plague takes everyone.

My request is not for a summary or a bibliography, but for a philosophical reading that takes both the film and the Epicurean position seriously and allows each to challenge the other. Does Block’s terror and his willingness to bargain for more time show that he is making a conceptual error about where harm can occur, or does Bergman suggest that Block is actually afraid of something the Epicurean argument does not address—namely, the interruption of an unfinished life-project rather than the state of being dead? I am especially interested in whether the chess match can be interpreted as a visual dramatization of the symmetry argument: Death treats Block’s continued existence as a temporary delay of an inevitable, indifferent outcome, much as Lucretius treats posthumous non-existence as the mirror of prenatal non-existence. At the same time, does the film resist this reading through Block’s confessions of doubt, the senselessness of the plague, or the escape of the young family? I want to know whether The Seventh Seal ultimately supports the Epicurean view that death is nothing to the dead, undermines it by showing that the value of life lies in its continuation, or reveals that the argument misses a distinct kind of harm—perhaps one that applies not to the dead person but to the living who must witness the collapse of meaning.

If you take this on, please do not provide citations or a reading list; I am trying to see whether the philosophical idea and the film can illuminate each other without external authority. I would also like the analysis to be honest about where the film refuses the philosophical framing: for instance, if the religious and existential dimensions of Block’s quest simply do not map onto the Epicurean concern with experiential harm, say so explicitly, and explain what that resistance teaches us about the limits of the argument.
