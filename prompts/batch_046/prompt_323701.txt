I write fiction about civic life in failing states, and I have settled on a view I want tested: that voting is not primarily an instrument of preference aggregation or a discharge of civic duty, but an act of narrative co-authorship in which the voter becomes jointly responsible for the collective story the polity tells about itself. On this account, choosing the lesser evil is not a pragmatic compromise but a moral-aesthetic corruption: the voter writes herself into a narrative she finds repugnant, destroying the expressive integrity that alone could justify the act. This seems to undermine both compulsory-voting arguments grounded in duty and strategic-voting defenses grounded in harm reduction. What would have to be true for this to be wrong? I want the defeaters mapped honestly: which empirical patterns of voter behavior, which analogies from artistic or contractual collaboration, which institutional designs, and which normative arguments about collective responsibility would actually threaten the core claim rather than merely complicate it? Rank them from fatal to awkward. Tell me what a committed defender should concede as genuinely damaging, not just what critics allege, and assess whether the view can be qualified—say, by claiming that every ballot is always already a narrative act—into unfalsifiability, and whether that insulation is a virtue or a vice for a position of this kind.
