In software engineering we distinguish failure modes: fail-fast (immediate crash on constraint violation), graceful degradation (maintain core function despite partial damage), and circuit-breaker (halt operations and demand an external reset). Some colleagues map these onto moral cultures—honor culture as fail-fast (instant retaliation for insult), dignity culture as graceful degradation (equal intrinsic worth that slowly erodes with social failure), and victimhood culture as circuit-breaker (status injury triggers collective intervention and moral reset)—to argue that dignity culture is morally superior because it optimizes for long-term stability rather than retaliation or dependency. Audit this triad: build the correspondence table (engineering mechanism, cultural mechanism, moral outcome), identify which imported values—efficiency, recoverability, system-level optimization—are illegitimate when applied to human dignity, separate decorative disanalogies from ones that actually break the comparison, and determine whether the analogy can genuinely support the claim that one culture is morally superior, or whether it merely redescribes different optimization targets that are not morally commensurable. I have no formal philosophy background, so frame the audit in terms of what the analogy actually carries versus what it quietly assumes.
