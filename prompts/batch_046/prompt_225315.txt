As someone who spent forty years in experimental physics working on rotational dynamics and precision calibration, and who has only recently turned to philosophy, I find myself puzzled by the substantivalism–relationism debate, especially Newton's bucket. Rather than asking for a standard exposition, I would like you to translate both positions—substantivalism about absolute space and relationism about relations among bodies—into the vocabulary of laboratory practice: calibration chains, reference-frame conventions, rotational invariance in instrument design, and the operational distinction between measuring a physical state and assigning coordinates to it. Do this systematically, then provide a residue report: which claims about absolute rotation and spatial ontology survive this translation intact, which require distortion to fit within metrological concepts (for example, whether "absolute acceleration" corresponds to anything more than a particular class of instrument failure), and which have no counterpart whatsoever in how experimentalists actually stabilize measurement protocols. I am particularly interested in whether the philosophical residue points to an ontological commitment that calibration science implicitly suppresses, or whether the debate concerns distinctions that laboratory procedure treats as purely conventional.
