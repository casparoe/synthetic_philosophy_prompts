I am a high school student preparing notes for my philosophy club's journal, and I recently read a summary of Leibniz's moral philosophy on an unnamed undergraduate philosophy blog. The author writes: "Leibniz believed that morality is essentially applied theology: God computes the best possible world through a calculus of perfection, and moral rules are merely the instructions he issues to rational creatures so they can align with that cosmic optimum. Justice, for Leibniz, is the charity of the wise—understood as an impartial distribution of benevolence across all beings without preference. Happiness consists in the clear cognitive perception of universal harmony, so moral improvement is mainly a matter of sharpening one's mental clarity about the universal order. Consequently, an action is right if and only if it maximizes overall cosmic perfection, and moral obligations bind us only because we are ignorant; the wise, perceiving harmony directly, have no need for rules whatsoever."

Some of this resonates with what I have encountered about Leibniz's rationalism and his formula caritas sapientis, yet other elements sound suspiciously like an anachronistic projection of modern consequentialism onto his system. Would you audit this summary for me? Please separate, claim by claim, what is faithful to Leibniz's own distinctions—especially regarding whether moral truths depend on divine will or on eternal reasons, how charity actually operates in the wise person, and whether the evaluation of individual action reduces to cosmic optimization—from what is a common simplification, a conflation of two claims he keeps apart, or a direct misrepresentation. I also want to know whether the author's final conclusion, that the wise have no need for rules at all, actually follows from the accurate portions of the summary, or whether it depends on the errors embedded earlier. I am not asking for a bibliography, reading list, or verbatim primary-source quotations; I need an evaluation precise enough that I can use it to correct my club notes.
