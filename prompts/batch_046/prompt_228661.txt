I am a third-year undergraduate reading Classics and Philosophy, and I have been asked to deliver the opening remarks at a one-day workshop entitled "The Architecture of Collective Decision", hosted jointly by our university's Department of Ancient History and a local council's deliberative-governance unit. The audience will be mixed: doctoral researchers working on Athenian political institutions, practising local-government officers who run participatory budgeting schemes, and a few retired magistrates. I want the speech to be genuinely welcoming and framing, not merely ceremonial, and I need it written in British English.

The conceptual backbone should be drawn from three well-known failures of Athenian democracy—the Mytilene debate, in which the assembly voted for mass execution and then reversed itself within twenty-four hours; the Sicilian Expedition, in which democratic enthusiasm overrode strategic caution and led to catastrophic defeat; and the trial of Socrates, in which a majority jury condemned philosophical dissent to death. But I do not want these treated as a simple morality tale about democracy gone wrong. Instead, please structure the speech around a specific interpretive claim I have been developing in my coursework: that each failure manifests a distinct temporal distortion in democratic judgement—retroactive emotional correction in Mytilene, prospective scale-blindness in Sicily, and synchronic moral compression in the trial of Socrates—so that the three cases form a kind of diagnostic triad rather than a list of anecdotes. This framework should do real structural work: it should determine which comforting platitudes the speech must avoid (for instance, that democracy naturally self-corrects, or that expert oversight is a simple remedy), and it should shape the tone so that the practitioners in the room are invited to recognise analogous distortions in modern participatory processes without feeling accused.

Please write the speech—approximately eight to ten minutes when delivered—so that it lands for the specialists without alienating the non-academic attendees. It should open with a brief, concrete image rather than an abstract thesis, weave the three historical moments into the argument rather than presenting them as historical digressions, and conclude with a precise, limited claim about what democratic institutions can and cannot be expected to correct on their own. Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citations; I want the audience to engage with the ideas directly rather than being directed to sources. The speech should feel intellectually serious but orally deliverable.
