A colleague summarized the debate over historical objectivity like this: Ranke sought to recover past events as they actually occurred by stripping away the historian's present judgments, treating archives as transparent windows onto reality. Carr responded that facts never speak for themselves—historians inevitably select and arrange them according to present concerns—so the ideal of a neutral reconstruction is a myth, though rational debate over interpretations remains possible. Hayden White then argued that every historical account is shaped by one of four deep literary structures—romance, tragedy, comedy, satire—that determine explanation prior to any examination of evidence, turning history into a form of rhetoric rather than empirical science. More recently, the archival turn has shown that archives are themselves constructed texts shaped by institutional power, which my colleague reads as final proof that no unmediated access to the past exists. From all this, the colleague concludes that Ranke was naively empiricist, Carr was an inconsistent half-skeptic who undermined the rational standards he still wanted to preserve, and White together with archival studies demonstrates that historical knowledge is ultimately indistinguishable from fiction, leaving no meaningful gap between a chronicle and a myth. Is this a fair summary? Does White's analysis of emplotment and tropological prefiguration actually license the collapse of history into fiction, or does it allow for a more limited structural objectivity? And does the archival turn genuinely confirm White's position, or can it be accommodated within Carr's more restrained skepticism?
