I'm preparing material for a graduate seminar on collective action and moral responsibility, though the issue first arose in an extended discussion among peers who unanimously accept both the scientific consensus on anthropogenic climate change and the ethical seriousness of industrial animal agriculture, yet remain entirely unmoved on whether individual choices—dietary abstention, electoral participation, or consumption patterns—bear any genuine moral weight. The deadlock is strikingly durable. One camp, which I will call the inefficacy position, maintains that in large-scale causal architectures—global carbon markets, democratic electorates numbering in the millions, industrial supply chains—the marginal effect of any single token action is effectively zero; therefore moral assessment should be directed at collective agents, institutional designs, or structural conditions rather than at individuals. The opposing camp concedes the near-zero marginal causal impact but insists that participation in harmful mechanisms carries expressive and normative significance: it shapes social norms, signals complicity, and affects the agent's own moral standing. Over repeated exchanges in both academic and informal settings, neither side has yielded ground; the inefficacy advocates treat appeals to symbolic value as a category mistake that confuses emotional consolation with causal analysis, while the participatory-responsibility advocates treat the inefficacy argument as a reductio of any moral framework that requires counterfactual difference-making. I am increasingly convinced that the impasse is not empirical—both sides share the same climate models, supply-chain data, and voting statistics—but conceptual, and I want to test a specific hypothesis. The hidden fracture, I suspect, concerns two incompatible models of contribution: one that analyzes harm as continuous flow governed by token-level probabilistic overdetermination (where every unit incrementally sustains the mechanism regardless of discrete thresholds), and one that analyzes harm as discrete choice within a threshold-governed collective action (where only contributions that cross a causal boundary count as genuine participation). Given this framing, is the disagreement substantive, verbal, or an instance of the two sides answering different questions—one about causal attribution, one about expressive meaning—under the single rubric of "individual moral responsibility"? And given your diagnosis, what would actually constitute progress: a formal model distinguishing participatory from marginal causation, a conceptual clarification of when an action counts as "part of" a collective harm, or an argument that the question is ill-posed for individual-level ethics?
