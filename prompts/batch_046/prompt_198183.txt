Consider the following argument I constructed for a graduate seminar on moral status, which now strikes me as suspicious because its conclusion undermines commitments I am not prepared to surrender. (1) Species membership is not a morally relevant property in itself; being a member of Homo sapiens confers no intrinsic moral privilege. (2) Moral status derives solely from intrinsic capacities—specifically, the capacity for conscious suffering and for autonomous preference-formation. (3) These capacities are functionally defined and therefore substrate-independent: a synthetic system that realizes the exact functional dynamics of a mature pig's cognitive architecture possesses those capacities in precisely the same manner and to the same degree as the biological pig. (4) Therefore, such a synthetic being—call it S—has exactly the same basic moral status as the pig it functionally duplicates. (5) Now consider a continuous spectrum of synthetic beings, S1 through Sn, where S1 duplicates the functional profile of a human infant and Sn that of an adult human genius, with every intermediate gradation instantiated. (6) Because these profiles form a continuum with no natural discontinuities, there is no non-arbitrary functional threshold that includes all biologically human beings and excludes all other beings on the spectrum. (7) Consequently, moral status cannot be assigned categorically; it must vary continuously with the degree of realized capacity. (8) This implies that some biologically human beings—those with severe cognitive impairments—would occupy lower positions on the moral spectrum than certain synthetic or non-human beings with more developed functional profiles. (9) Hence, if we wish to preserve the categorical equality of all human beings, we must reject (1) and allow species membership to function as a protective boundary; but if we maintain (1), we must accept a hierarchical ordering of moral status within the human community. Where does this reasoning fail, if it does? Identify the specific step or implicit inference that is weakest, explain why it seemed plausible, and price the alternatives: what do we pay if we reject (3), what do we pay if we reject (6), and whether there is a way to preserve (1) and (2) without accepting the hierarchical conclusion in (9). I am particularly inclined to keep (1) and (2), but I want to understand what preserving them commits me to.
