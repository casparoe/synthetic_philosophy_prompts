Recently I stumbled across the Chinese School of Names and the white horse dialogue—the argument that a white horse is not a horse, because horse picks out the form or shape, while white picks out the color, so combining them produces something that no longer belongs purely to the category of horse. That struck me as an oddly precise way to ask whether Gregor Samsa, once he carries the form of a vermin, can still be called Gregor, or whether his family is correct to treat the name as misapplied.

Does Kafka treat Gregor's humanity as an essential form that survives despite the insect appearance—which would make the family wrong to rename him as an it—or does the story show that once visible properties change, the name loses its grip, and social recognition is what actually fixes the category? I'm especially interested in the furniture-removal scene and Grete's final refusal to call him by name: do these moments function like the School of Names' separation of properties, where stripping away the human context is what dissolves the category, rather than the physical transformation itself?
