I'm preparing a philosophical profile on moral worth and motivation for a seminar, and I want a consistency check before presenting. Here is where I stand: (1) moral worth attaches only to actions done for the right reason, understood as recognition of the action's moral necessity rather than its attractiveness; (2) if an agent's dominant motivation is the pleasure derived from acting well, the action lacks full moral worth, even when the right reason is present; (3) moral education should cultivate spontaneous, pleasurable virtuous dispositions—a second nature—so that good agents act without struggle; (4) such spontaneity does not undermine worth, provided the pleasure is a byproduct and not the causal driver; (5) we can test for byproduct status counterfactually: the agent must still recognize the action as necessary in a scenario where the pleasure is absent; (6) actions done with spontaneous pleasure therefore carry equal moral worth to those done with effort; (7) nonetheless, the highest degree of moral worth belongs to actions that overcome strong contrary inclination, because such cases display maximal modal robustness of the right reason. Which pairs are genuinely in tension, which only appear to be, and what is the least costly revision? In particular, does (5) actually secure (4) against (2), or does spontaneous pleasure inevitably become the dominant explanatory factor? And can (6) and (7) be reconciled without collapsing moral worth into a purely evidential notion? Rank the tensions by severity and identify the single revision that cleans up the most.
