Argue that the primary harm of human extinction is not the deprivation of future lives, nor merely the psychological loss of meaning for present agents, but the dissolution of what can be called transgenerational warrant conditions: the implicit normative structure through which present commitments—scientific, artistic, moral, institutional—derive their point from being embedded in an open-ended human future that can recognize, continue, or contest them. Defend the claim that this dissolution is an objective axiological damage, not reducible to aggregate welfare loss, and address the replacement objection by explaining why substitutability by non-human successors fails to preserve the specific kind of diachronic intelligibility at stake. Conclude by distinguishing your position from both standard consequentialist accounts and from the framing that treats the value of posterity as analogous to a personal afterlife, showing why the dependency of present value on future existence is structural rather than sentimental.
