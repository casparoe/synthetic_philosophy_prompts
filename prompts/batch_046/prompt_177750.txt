Here are my gut verdicts on a few translation puzzles, mostly riffing on Quine's gavagai stuff but with some twists I haven't seen in the usual writeups. I want to know if there's one principled account behind them, or if I'm just being incoherent.

Case 1 — the classic: natives yell "Gavagai!" whenever a rabbit runs past. I say the translation is genuinely indeterminate between "rabbit", "undetached rabbit-part", and "rabbit-stage". No hidden fact of the matter fixes it.

Case 2 — there's a word "blit" used for red things in sunlight and green things in moonlight, but never for red in moonlight. I actually think "blit" determinately means something like "object that looks warm-colored in present lighting". It doesn't feel like gavagai; I think the analytic hypotheses can't split this correlation without breaking the manual elsewhere.

Case 3 — a speaker says "floop" whenever a cup breaks, and also right after waking from a dream about a cup breaking. I think "floop" means "cup-breaking event, real or dreamed", and that there's no further indeterminancy hiding inside — it's not like it could secretly mean only real breaks with some analytical trick.

Case 4 — here's the one I keep flipping on. There's a term "gronk" applied to every bridge a king has crossed, and also to structurally identical bridges with no royal history, but the natives keep detailed genealogies. Part of me wants to say "gronk" determinately tracks the royal-crossing history. But then I think: couldn't the manual just translate it as "bridge of structural type K" and treat all the genealogy talk as part of the analytic hypotheses? So maybe it's indeterminate after all? I'm genuinely torn.

Case 5 — in a language with no tense marking, there's a word used every morning for "the sun is rising", and also for "a new leader is emerging". I think the political use is determinately metaphorical, derived via analytic hypotheses about social structure, not an independent ambiguity.

So my pattern seems to be: indeterminancy in 1, maybe in 4; determinacy in 2, 3, and 5. Is there a coherent Quinean account — maybe something about when analytic hypotheses can be locally adjusted vs when holism forces a global constraint — that saves all five? Or do I have to drop one? In particular, what's the principaled difference between case 1 (where I want indeterminacy) and case 3 (where I want determinacy despite the dream/reality split)? And what does your principle predict for a case I haven't listed — say, a word used for "fire" both when there's actual flame and when there's just smoke with no heat? Please also say how confident you are in whatever you claim, and single out the point you're least sure about — I bet it's whatever you say about case 4.
