I was reading this old essay about Whig history - you know, the thing where historians write the past like it's all just marching toward us and our modern liberal democracy or whatever. And I was wondering if you could make the case that this isn't actually a mistake we can fix, but more like a structural feature of how normative political philosphy works? Like, if you're trying to justify why we have the institutions we have now, you kind of have to tell a story where the past was groping toward them, even if you dress it up as 'contingency' or 'path dependency'. I think Herbert Butterfield was the guy who named it, but I'm not asking for a bibliography or anything.

The thing is, everyone says 'don't be Whiggish' like it's just bad scholarship, but maybe it's inevitible whenever you combine descriptive history with a theory of justice. So argue that Whig history is unavoidable for any political theory that needs to ground its legitimacy in historical developement rather than pure reason, and that the real problem isn't the teleology itself but that we pretend it's neutral. You could even bring in the idea that evolutionary narratives in biology have the same structure - we tell just-so stories about adaptations - and ask whether that makes them equally 'Whiggish' or whether there's a difference because biology doesn't have normative commitments. Anyway, don't worry about citing specific pages; I just want the argument laid out, maybe with some acknowledgment of where it gets shaky.
