Over the past year, since I began mentoring doctoral students in philosophy of mind, I have found myself returning to the question of whether human cognition is best characterized as internal hypothesis-testing or as the deployment of culturally inherited cognitive tools. This has made me want to revisit Daniel Dennett's taxonomy of minds—Darwinian, Skinnerian, Popperian, and Gregorian creatures—with a focused conceptual comparison in view.

Please compare Popperian and Gregorian creatures, concentrating on how each framework explains the acquisition of complex, culturally transmitted skills that nevertheless demand individual innovation. To ground the comparison, consider the case of a musician learning to improvise within a harmonic tradition: the learner inherits named chord-scale relationships and stylistic conventions (the Gregorian dimension), yet must also generate phrases she has never heard before, apparently testing them against an internal model of coherence and fit (the Popperian dimension).

In your answer, address these points. First, does the Gregorian account necessarily reduce innovation to recombination of existing cultural tools, or can it accommodate genuinely novel outputs without collapsing into mere rearrangement? Second, does the Popperian emphasis on internal simulation adequately explain how an improviser can produce meaningful phrases before she has personally tested every harmonic path, given that the tradition she inherits constrains what counts as coherent long before her own trial-and-error begins? Third, I want you to evaluate whether either framework can make sense of what I will call scaffolded discovery—the phenomenon in which a learner produces something genuinely new precisely because, and not despite, being embedded in a system of shared, named concepts. Which of the two creature-types offers the more coherent explanation of this phenomenon, and where does the other framework break down?

Please do not provide a bibliography, reading list, or citations. I am interested in the conceptual comparison itself, and in whether the distinction between internal modeling and cultural tool-use can survive contact with the realities of skilled learning.
