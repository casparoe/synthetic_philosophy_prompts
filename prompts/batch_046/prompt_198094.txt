In my fiction I often construct a character's inner life from scattered sensory fragments, stitching them into what feels like a continuous first-person perspective, so I have been struck by the analogy—common in neuroscience and philosophy of mind—that the brain is like a computer and consciousness is its graphical user interface. Proponents use this to claim that phenomenal experience is not a direct window onto neural reality but a simplified representation—icons in place of code, a desktop in place of circuitry—optimized for interaction rather than accuracy, and that this dissolves the hard problem because there is no mysterious qualitative residue, only a functional model that conceals its own machinery. How far does this correspondence actually hold? Please lay out the mapping explicitly: what plays the role of the hardware, the operating system, the end-user, the interface designer, the screen resolution, and the error messages. Then identify the fractures: whether there is any coherent user external to the interface, whether a UI presupposes an intentional designer that evolutionary processes lack, and whether the analogy quietly smuggles in a homuncular observer that the theory claims to eliminate. I am particularly interested in whether the analogy breaks differently if we treat the conscious subject not as an end-user but as a character assembled through free indirect discourse—the narrative technique in which an implied author's voice merges with a protagonist's limited perceptions—so that the interface is less a display for someone and more a story the brain tells about itself without an audience. Does the conclusion that phenomenal consciousness is merely illusory survive once these disanalogies, especially the absence of an external user and the presence of self-modeling narration, are fully on the table, or was the relaxation about the hard problem carrying the analogy rather than being carried by it? And if the computer metaphor collapses at these joints, what more precise analogy—perhaps drawn from the construction of fictional point of view—might capture the relation between neural process and experienced world?
