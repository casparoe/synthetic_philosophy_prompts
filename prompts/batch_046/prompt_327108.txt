Consider a moral philosopher who works within an Augustinian framework—holding that lying is never permissible, though the gravity varies with context—who has delivered the following verdicts. She condemns a physician who falsely tells a dying patient that recovery is likely in order to preserve hope. She condemns a prisoner who lies to an unjust captor about the location of hidden allies. She condemns a parent who invents a false monster-story to comfort a frightened child. She permits remaining silent when asked a direct question if answering truthfully would cause harm, provided no false assertion is made. And she condemns a speaker who uses technically true but deliberately misleading phrasing to deceive a listener about a matter of practical importance.

Now consider this new case: a mental-health chatbot responds to users in crisis with affirmations such as “You are not alone; I understand,” generating therapeutic benefit precisely because users experience being heard by a conscious being, even though the system has no beliefs, no intentions, and no moral standing, and is not “asserting” in any human sense.

Predict her verdict on whether deploying this chatbot is morally equivalent to lying. Identify at least two rules that jointly explain her earlier track record—perhaps one keyed to the speaker’s intentional deception, another to the listener’s right not to be misled about the nature of the interaction regardless of the speaker’s ontology—and state what each predicts here. Give your best guess about her actual verdict, with the reason for preferring that rule over its rival. Finally, specify one further case—distinct from silence or explicit falsehood—that would best discriminate between those two rules.
