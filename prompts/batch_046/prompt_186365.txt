I'm trying to get clear on democratic boundaries after a long argument with friends about whether long-term residents should vote, and I keep running into what looks like a structural tension in the actual institutions rather than just a surface-level policy disagreement. In nearly every liberal democracy, the demos is defined by citizenship, and citizenship is acquired primarily by birthright—jus soli or jus sanguinis—not by being subject to state power or being affected by state decisions. The result is that permanent residents who have lived, worked, and paid taxes under a jurisdiction for decades are systematically excluded from voting, while diaspora citizens who have never lived in the country, pay no taxes, and are not subject to its laws routinely retain full voting rights in national elections. Even more striking, the rules that determine who counts as a citizen—and therefore who belongs to the demos—are themselves enacted and amended only by the current citizen-demos, with no external tribunal or neutral procedure for validating those boundary decisions. So the membership of the decision-making body is fixed by decisions made by that very body.

What philosophical commitments about democratic legitimacy does this institutional pattern presuppose? Does it implicitly commit us to the view that the demos is a pre-political community defined by shared nationality or descent rather than by coercion or affected interests? Or is the practice actually compatible with both the all-subjected principle and the all-affected-interests principle, if we interpret them in some non-obvious way? I want you to reconstruct the implicit theory of value and authority that makes this arrangement look coherent, distinguish what the practice strictly requires from what merely fits it well, and say whether its parts are philosophically consistent—especially the combination of hereditary membership with democratic self-rule. I am particularly interested in whether the practice forces us to treat democratic boundaries as necessarily conventional or arbitrary, given that there is no non-circular ground for deciding who gets to decide.

Please also address what I think of as the bootstrapping problem: because the demos determines its own membership rules without external appeal, the boundary seems structurally analogous to a self-referential set in logic, where the definition of membership depends on a prior membership. Does this mean that any democratic boundary must rest on a non-rational or conventional foundation, or can a normative theory supply a principled stopping point? Finally, tell me what a democratic practice built on a genuinely non-circular, all-affected-interests foundation would have to change—would it require abolishing citizenship-based voting entirely, or only modifying the inheritance rules?
