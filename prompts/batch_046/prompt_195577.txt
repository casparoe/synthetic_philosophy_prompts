Write a philosophical dialogue of approximately 900 words between Dr. Alistair Venn and Dr. Mei-Ling Zhou. Venn defends a strict reading of the Principal Principle — the normative claim, associated with David Lewis, that rational credences ought to defer to known objective chances — and insists that this deference remains binding even when an agent possesses indexical information. Zhou argues that once self-locating or centred evidence enters the picture, the Principal Principle either yields contradictory guidance or must be abandoned in favour of a purely evidential updating rule.

Frame their debate around an invented but precisely specified thought experiment: the Dormant Observer protocol. In this scenario, one thousand physically and psychologically identical observers are sealed in identical chambers. Outside, a fair coin is flipped. If the result is heads, exactly one observer — selected uniformly at random — is awakened and informed only that she has been awakened; she receives no data about the coin's outcome or about the status of the other 999 observers. If the result is tails, all remain dormant. The awakened observer knows the protocol in full.

Venn should argue that, because the objective chance of heads was 0.5 and she knows this, her rational credence in heads must remain 0.5, regardless of the selection mechanism that brought her to consciousness. Zhou should counter that her very awakening is evidence: she knows that, were the coin tails, she would almost certainly still be dormant, so her centred evidence — "I am awake" — shifts her credence away from 0.5. She should press Venn on whether deference to chance requires treating her own existence in a particular branch as evidentially inert, and whether that commitment is consistent with standard Bayesian conditioning.

The exchange must be a genuine dialogue: each speaker should address the specific claim just made by the other, not deliver parallel monologues. Neither should be a strawman. Venn must engage seriously with the selection-effect objection rather than dismissing it; Zhou must engage seriously with the normative force of deference to objective chance rather than treating it as a mere heuristic. By the conclusion, the disagreement should be precisely located — ideally, on whether the Principal Principle applies only to uncentred propositions or can be extended to indexical ones without generating incoherence.

Write in British English, using formal philosophical vocabulary but remaining accessible. Do not include a bibliography, citations, or references to specific texts beyond the mention of the Principal Principle itself. The dialogue should stand alone without requiring external sources.
