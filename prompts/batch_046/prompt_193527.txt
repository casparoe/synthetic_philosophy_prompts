Historians have long debated presentism — the risk of judging past societies by the standards of one’s own era. A more stubborn problem is whether historians can ever fully escape the conceptual and normative frameworks of their present, even when they strive for contextual understanding. Consider three interrelated lines of inquiry. First, the epistemic question: can a historian genuinely adopt a past actor’s conceptual scheme, or does every translation into modern analytical language leave a residual presentist frame? Second, the normative question: can moral evaluation of historical agents be bracketed without undermining the explanatory coherence of the narrative, or does causation itself require attributing intentions that invite judgment? Third, a less discussed but structurally similar issue we might call metric presentism: quantitative historiography — applying modern GDP equivalents, climate anomaly baselines, or welfare metrics to pre-industrial societies — does not merely describe the past but embeds present-day judgments about what counts as significant change, loss, or improvement within the measurement framework itself.

If you were advising a small interdisciplinary research programme — philosophers of history and practising historians with about three years and no obligation to produce empirical archives — which of these open questions should take priority, and in what order? Please do not provide a bibliography or name specific scholars. Instead, defend your ranking by identifying which problem is conceptually upstream of the others, which is most tractable with current philosophical and methodological tools, and which, if settled, would least alter actual historiographical practice. Also indicate what kind of conceptual result or methodological demonstration over the next few years would cause you to reorder them.
