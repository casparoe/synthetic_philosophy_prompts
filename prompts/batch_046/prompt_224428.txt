In discussions comparing evolutionary biology and artificial intelligence, a common analogy pairs Darwin's account of natural selection with Turing's account of universal computation as parallel "inversions of reasoning": Darwin is said to have shown that apparent design in organisms requires no designer, just as Turing is said to have shown that apparent intelligent behavior requires no inner comprehension. The pairing is often used to argue that both cases license the same ontological deflation—treating competence (adaptive form, problem-solving) as fully separable from any corresponding mental or intentional cause. Set out the correspondence explicitly: what plays the role of the designer, the mechanical substitute, the competence being explained, and the hidden process that produces it? Then identify where the mapping fractures—particularly regarding whether evolutionary blindness and symbol-manipulation are genuinely analogous in lacking comprehension, whether Turing's framework actually eliminates understanding or only displaces it onto the programmer and interpreter, and whether Darwin's process involves normative or functional evaluation that computation lacks. Finally, assess whether the analogy's intended conclusion—that biological design and machine cognition should be treated with the same eliminative attitude toward internal states—survives once these disanalogies are on the table, or whether the comparison was carrying the argument all along.
