I work as a software engineer without formal philosophical training, and I am trying to understand the conceptual architecture behind a specific EU institutional pattern. The Union rests on treaty commitments to an ever-closer union, yet it also maintains permanent opt-outs and enhanced cooperation frameworks that let subsets of states integrate more deeply in domains like monetary policy, border control, or patent law while others remain outside indefinitely. At the same time, the European Court of Justice asserts that EU law takes precedence over conflicting national statutes, but several national constitutional courts reserve the right to review whether EU acts violate core constitutional identity. Standard commentary treats this as a democratic deficit, but I am interested in what theory of sovereignty and democratic legitimacy actually makes the dual structure coherent, if any. Does it presuppose that sovereignty can be partitioned like a distributed system—separate nodes retaining root authority over their own domains—rather than pooled into a single locus? And does it require a view of legitimacy generated once by state consent to the treaty framework, or one demanding continuous transnational popular authorization? If we replaced the underlying commitments with a unitary, majoritarian conception of sovereignty and democratic legitimacy, which mechanisms—enhanced cooperation, constitutional identity review, the Commission's infringement powers—would become conceptually unsustainable? I want to know whether the practice is philosophically consistent or whether it quietly depends on incompatible theories of authority.
