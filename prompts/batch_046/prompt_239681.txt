I have been tracking the long-running dispute over whether genuine moral dilemmas are possible—cases in which an agent faces two or more all-things-considered obligations that cannot jointly be satisfied, not through any failure of her own. The standard map is familiar: one camp, running through Bernard Williams and certain virtue-theoretic accounts, treats post-choice agent-regret as evidence that a genuine obligation was violated regardless of which horn was taken; the opposing camp, including Kantian and many deontic-logical accounts, holds that conflicting all-things-considered obligations are conceptually impossible, since practical reason must deliver a unique verdict and the principle that ought implies can blocks the coherence of unavoidable wrong-doing. The arguments have not moved in decades.

My hypothesis is that the stalemate is not mainly about moral psychology or formal deontic consistency, but about competing criteria for theoretical adequacy: whether a moral framework must preserve the phenomenological reality of tragic loss, or whether it must preserve the action-guiding uniqueness of practical reason. I want a structural diagnosis rather than another survey. Is this disagreement substantive, verbal, or a case of two distinct questions—logical consistency of normative systems versus descriptive fidelity to moral experience—being pursued under a single heading? Specifically, does the conflict depend on an ambiguous use of moral dilemma to cover both structural conflicts built into the norms themselves and contingent conflicts generated only by circumstance? If so, does that ambiguity dissolve the disagreement or merely relocate it?

Please diagnose what each side actually commits to about agent-regret, about the uniqueness of rational verdicts, and about whether practical reason can be locally suspended. Then tell me what would count as progress given your diagnosis: a formal result in non-classical deontic logic, a conceptual distinction that separates structural from contingent conflicts, an empirical finding about decision conflict, or an admission that the dispute rests on bedrock intuitions neither side can rationally compel. I am not asking for a literature review or a bibliography; I want the anatomy of the stall and an honest assessment of whether anything should be expected to break it.
