For my senior-year philosophy of mind independent study, I have been working through analogical reasoning in tightly bounded domains—particularly the kind of letter-string transformations Douglas Hofstadter used to illustrate conceptual fluidity in projects like Copycat, where a shift from abc → abd to mrrjjj → mrrjjk either succeeds or slips in ways that feel productive rather than erroneous. My instructor wants me to design a diagnostic instrument rather than a standard essay, because she thinks the debate about whether concepts are genuinely fluid or secretly rigid is usually settled by slogans rather than by what people actually say about concrete cases. I need a decision-tree taxonomy—roughly seven or eight terminal leaves, each carrying a one-sentence description of its most awkward commitment—that classifies positions on conceptual slippage within these microdomains.

The root node should present a concrete slippage case: a system recognizes abc → abd and ijk → ijl as instances of the same alphabetic increment, but when faced with mrrjjj → mrrjjk it must decide whether the repetition pattern belongs to the same concept or breaks it. From there, branch first by whether this mismatch is treated as a correctable error (opening paths toward formalist repair views), as evidence that the original concept never had a sharp boundary (opening paths toward fluid-concept views), or as proof that the microdomain is too structurally poor to support analogies at all (opening skeptical or eliminativist leaves). A second layer should split each path by whether hidden deep structure is invoked to explain the slippage, whether the slippage is valued as creative, and whether the observer accepts that concepts can stretch without ever becoming incoherent. One branch must incorporate the framing I developed for this project: rather than describing slippage as logical relaxation, model it as thermal diffusion of conceptual boundaries across a lattice of perceptually similar cases, and ask whether adopting that physical analogy pushes a view into a different leaf—say, from emergent creativity into a named diffusive fluidism—or whether it collapses back into an existing position.

Please label every internal node with the concrete claim or case that separates its children, not with abstract philosophical jargon, so that someone who understands analogies but has not studied this specific literature can navigate the tree without knowing the position names in advance. Each leaf should carry either a recognized label—such as rigid analogical formalism, Hofstadter-style fluid conceptualism, microdomain eliminativism, or diffusive fluidism—or be marked unnamed if the combination is coherent but apparently unexplored. Also flag any pairs of leaves that your tree cannot cleanly distinguish—for example, whether a view that accepts productive slippage but denies hidden structure remains genuinely distinct from pure emergentism or whether the distinction is merely verbal. Avoid asking for bibliographies, named scholars as authorities, or citation lists; the instrument should work entirely from the concrete letter-pattern cases and the thermal-diffusion framing. I am especially interested in whether there is an unoccupied region in the logical space—perhaps a view that accepts thermal diffusion but insists on absolute boundary preservation elsewhere—that seems incoherent or merely unattractive, and I would like that gap diagnosed in one sentence.
