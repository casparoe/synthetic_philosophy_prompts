hey — i'm writing a feature on whether Bostrom's simluation argument actually puts the brakes on far-future fanaticism, the kind where people talk about locking in values for trillions of digital minds. I keep running into two interpretations that seem to talk past each other.

First there's the 'reach-collapse' argument: if we're probably in an ancestor sim, then our causal chain into the 'real' far future is broken or at least severely attenuated. So the expected value of extreme present-day sacrifice for distant outcomes plummets, and longtermist fanaticism loses its rational footing.

Second, there's a view I kind of made up but that feels like it should exist — call it 'stack-indifference longtermism.' The idea is that simulation nesting doesn't matter ethically because obligations are indexed to observers at every level. If the simulators run longtermist reasoning too, then sacrificing for the far future is still rational, it's just that 'the far future' now includes the simulators' timeline and whatever timelines sit above thier. So the argument doesn't dampen fanaticism; it just redistributes it across nesting levels.

Can you compare these two? I want to know which one actually derives from the simulation premises without sneaking in hidden ethical assumptions. Does reach-collapse confuse epistemic uncertainty about being simulated with causal impotence? And does stack-indifference smuggle in a kind of universalizability that Bostrom's argument never licensed? There's also an asimmetry I can't shake: if we're simulated, our distant future might literally be the simulators' near present. Does that collapse the longtermist time-horizon in a way that breaks stack-indifference but leaves reach-collapse intact?

Keep it conceptual — no need for citations or reading lists, i'm just trying to think this through for the piece.
