I've been trying to apply the money-pump argument for intransitive preferences across time, and I think standard treatments of diachronic rationality miss a structural parallel. The synchronic version is clear: if at one moment I prefer A to B, B to C, and C to A, a bookmaker can cycle me through trades and extract unbounded payments. Now consider what I'll call a "diachronic loop" with no new information: on Sunday I strictly prefer saving $500; on Tuesday, with identical options and knowledge, I prefer spending it on a concert ticket; by Wednesday I prefer having saved and suffer anticipatory regret, though I can't reverse the purchase. To avoid this, I might pay a Sunday precommitment fee to lock in saving. My question is: since philosophers discuss diachronic rationality in terms of planning consistency or avoiding self-defeat, why don't they treat my Sunday→Tuesday→Wednesday shift as literally the same A→B→C→A cycle spread over a temporal index? If I pay fees on Sunday and regret on Wednesday, haven't I been money-pumped across time? And if so, doesn't that imply that any preference reversal without new information is just hidden synchronic intransitivity, making "rational revision" over time conceptually incoherent?
