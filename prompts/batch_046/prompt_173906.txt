Here is roughly where I've landed on self-deception, and I'd like to know if the package hangs together: (1) I think self-deception is genuinely possible—agents can mislead themselves about things they care about, not just suffer bias or ignorance. (2) I reject explanations that require literally holding contradictory beliefs (P and not-P) simultaneously; that's psychologically incoherent. (3) I also reject homuncular or partitioned-mind accounts—there are not separate sub-personal belief-boxes running in parallel. (4) For it to count as deception rather than mere error, the agent must have some cognitive access to the unwelcome truth they are avoiding. (5) I hold that belief is normatively governed by evidence, so maintaining a belief in the face of recognized counter-evidence is a rational failure, not a skillful strategy. (6) Yet I think people routinely do exactly this—sustain flattering self-images despite abundant contrary evidence they can articulate when pressed. Is this jointly tenable? Specifically, can (1), (3), and (4) be satisfied without smuggling contradiction back in through "tacit belief"? Does (5) collapse (1) and (6) into plain irrationality, leaving "self-deception" with no distinctive work to do? And is there an apparent tension between (2) and (4) that dissolves once we distinguish doxastic belief from non-doxastic awareness? Order the tensions by severity, identify the weakest link, and tell me what I would have to give up to restore consistency and what that costs.
