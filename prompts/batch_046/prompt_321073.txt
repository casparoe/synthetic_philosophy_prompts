I'm in a philosophy of history seminar and we got into a huge argument about tearing down statues of Columbus and Churchill and whether Jefferson gets a pass. My friend laid out this argument that I can't shake but it feels like it proves way too much. He said: (1) You can only blame someone for doing X if X was actually wrong for them at the time they did it, not just wrong by our lights. (2) Something is wrong for an agent only if it's wrong according to the best moral framework that agent could have accessed. (3) Columbus, Churchill, and Jefferson all lived in enviroments where anti-colonialism and racial equality weren't serious intelectual options — no one around them was arguing for them in a way they could grasp. (4) So those actions weren't actually wrong for them. (5) If we reject this and say no, slavery was wrong in 1790 regardless of what Jefferson thought, then we're saying moral truths don't depend on what's accessible, which means they're objective and timeless. (6) But then consistency demands we apply not just past standards retroactively, but future standards prospectively: if morality is timeless, then whatever we'll discover is wrong in 2100 is already wrong now, and we're all blameworty for it already. (7) So either we accept that Columbus wasn't blameworthy, or we accept that we're all constantly guilty of future sins we haven't figured out yet. (8) Nobody accepts (7), so (1) is false and cross-temporal blame is impossible? I feel like there's a hidden slide between "accessible framework" and "objective truth" but I can't name it. Which step do I actually reject? I'd like to keep the idea that Jefferson was genuinely wrong about slavery, but I don't want to be on the hook for every future moral discovery.
