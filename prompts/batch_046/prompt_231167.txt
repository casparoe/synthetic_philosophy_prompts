Consider the institutional architecture of inheritance taxation in modern democracies that simultaneously maintain three features: a nominally progressive estate tax, a provision that steps up the tax basis of inherited capital assets so that unrealized appreciation escapes taxation entirely, and broad exemptions that allow family enterprises and agricultural holdings to pass largely intact across generations. These same jurisdictions publicly justify extensive education and social-mobility spending in the language of equality of opportunity and the neutralization of unearned advantage. Reconstruct the implicit philosophical theory—of justice across generations, of desert, of the moral status of family units versus individuals, and of what opportunity requires—that renders this triad coherent rather than contradictory. Does it presuppose that inherited wealth is a form of earned reward for the decedent's labor, that intergenerational transmission is a legitimate plural good independent of distributive fairness, or that equality of opportunity demands only a sufficient floor rather than any constraint on dynastic accumulation? Distinguish what the institutional design strictly presupposes from what it merely happens to accommodate, and specify which concrete provisions—tax base, exemption thresholds, or the treatment of unrealized gains—would need to be altered if the underlying view were replaced either by a strict luck-egalitarian commitment that treats all inherited advantage as brute luck to be neutralized, or by a communitarian view that treats long family continuity as an independent social value worthy of preservation.
