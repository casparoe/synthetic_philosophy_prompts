Here is an argument I built after working through the paradox of phenomenal judgment, but it now strikes me as proving far more than intended—perhaps even undermining the very reasoning that motivates it.

(1) If phenomenal properties are causally inert, then no belief about one's own phenomenal state is caused by that state itself, but only by its underlying physical substrate.
(2) If a belief is never caused by the state it represents, then there is no causal connection capable of justifying it.
(3) So under epiphenomenalism, all first-person phenomenal judgments lack justification.
(4) Now suppose a neural simulator reproduces the physical substrate of a pain-state in a subject while suppressing the phenomenal property (if such suppression is coherent).
(5) The subject still forms the belief "I am in pain," caused by the substrate alone, yet the belief is false.
(6) Because the causal chain is identical in the genuine and simulated cases, the subject has no internal means to discriminate them.
(7) Therefore first-person phenomenal judgments are not merely unjustified but systematically unreliable.
(8) All third-person attributions of consciousness depend, directly or indirectly, on analogical inference from one's own phenomenal judgments.
(9) So if epiphenomenalism is true, we are unjustified in attributing consciousness to anyone, including ourselves in any reflective mode.
(10) But the very argument for epiphenomenalism rests on phenomenal judgments about what consciousness seems to do—e.g., that it appears to have no physical effects.
(11) Hence the view undermines the evidential basis for holding it.
(12) By parity, any theory that severs mental causation—including certain forms of parallelism or emergentism—falls to the same reductio.

This seems to show not just that epiphenomenalism is false, but that denying mental causation is self-defeating in a way that should trouble even critics of the view. Where does the slip occur? Is it in (2), treating causal connection as necessary for justification? In (4), assuming we can coherently suppress the phenomenal property while preserving the substrate? In (8), in the dependence of other-minds reasoning on first-person authority? Or elsewhere? I would most like to keep (1) and (10) intact, but I am willing to pay the price elsewhere. Which rejection is least costly, and what does accepting it commit me to?
