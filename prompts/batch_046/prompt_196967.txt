I have come to hold a specific synthesis of Amartya Sen's entitlement approach: famines are never natural disasters in the morally and causally relevant sense, but are always politically mediated entitlement collapses, and when they occur within states that possess functioning administrative capacity and available food reserves they ought to be classified as institutional crimes rather than agricultural misfortunes. I take the Bengal famine of 1943, the Irish famine of the 1840s, and the Great Leap Forward famine of 1959-61 as central confirming instances, and I extend the claim to cover regimes with democratic or bureaucratic capacity where distribution or market failures nevertheless produce mass starvation, such as the 1974 Bangladesh famine. I also accept the related corollary that democratic accountability paired with a free press functions as a robust structural preventer, so that a sustained, large-scale famine in an open democracy with functioning markets would be not merely improbable but conceptually anomalous for my view. Tell me what would genuinely count against this position, sorted by how decisive each defeater would be, from fatal to merely awkward. Which actual or hypothetical cases—perhaps involving climatological shock in the absence of any available surplus, or colonial settings where local entitlement structures are destroyed yet the metropole exercises no direct administrative presence—would a committed defender be forced to concede? Which conceptual distinctions, for instance between direct entitlement failure rooted in production loss and trade-based failure rooted in exchange collapse, or between omission and commission in institutional resource distribution, would defenders dismiss as missing the point rather than undermining the thesis? I want an honest assessment of whether the view has been qualified into unfalsifiability: if I restrict the claim to apply only where state capacity is present, have I insulated it, and is that insulation a problem for a thesis framed as a causal and moral generalization? Address also whether findings in adjacent domains—say, about collective responsibility in diffuse bureaucracies, or about the moral weight of unintended structural outcomes versus deliberate policy—would weaken the framing of famine as crime rather than disaster.
