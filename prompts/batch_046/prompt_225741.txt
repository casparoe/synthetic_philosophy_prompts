I have been trying to work out my own view on whether visitors are bound by local conventions such as tipping, queuing, and traffic norms, and I want to test whether my intuitions cohere around a single principle or require me to abandon some verdict.

Here are my judgments on concrete cases.

First, a tourist in New York who refuses to leave a gratuity for restaurant staff: I think this is morally wrong, not merely ignorant.

Second, a pedestrian in Berlin who crosses an empty street against a red signal at three in the morning: I do not think this is morally wrong, even though locals wait.

Third, a visitor to Tokyo who pushes onto a commuter train without joining the orderly queue: I think this is wrong.

Fourth, a traveller in a market where bargaining over price is the standard practice, who pays the first asking price without negotiation: I think this is not a moral failure — perhaps naive, but not wrong.

Fifth, a visitor in Italy who insists on maintaining a rigid, single-file queue at a café counter where locals rely on an informal cluster-and-acknowledgement system: I think this is wrong, but the reason feels different from the Tokyo case.

Sixth, a tourist in Japan who leaves a cash tip at a restaurant, knowing that in this context it can be interpreted as insulting or as implying charity: I think this is wrong.

Seventh, a visitor in London who jumps a queue at a bus stop: wrong.

Eighth, I keep returning to the case of a visitor in a culture where sustained direct eye contact during conversation is avoided as disrespectful, yet who deliberately maintains steady eye contact because they believe it signals honesty: is this wrong in the same way as the queue-jumping, or closer to the Berlin jaywalk?

The tension is that both the bargaining case and the queuing cases involve violating a local convention, yet I judge only some of them morally defective. Meanwhile, the Berlin jaywalk and the Tokyo queue-break both involve breaking rules, but I treat them differently. I have been tempted to distinguish between "coordination conventions", where the point is to solve a collective action problem, and "recognition conventions", where the point is to acknowledge another person's standing, but I am unsure whether this tracks my verdicts or whether I need something like a principle of "normative hospitality" that binds visitors differently depending on whether the convention structures distributive expectations.

Please state the principle — or a small family of precise candidates — that best captures this pattern; say which of my verdicts it forces me to overturn if simplicity is to be preserved; apply it to the eye-contact case; and predict its verdict for a case I have not listed: a visitor who, in a country where sales tax is added at the point of sale rather than included in the displayed price, expresses outrage and refuses to pay the tax portion, claiming they were deceived.

Please write your answer in British English, and do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citations.
