Design a 75-minute seminar activity for about sixteen advanced undergraduates who have encountered a brief, non-technical summary of Parfit’s teletransportation argument but have not yet worked through fission or gradual replication. The objective is to make the participants feel, before it is named, the pressure of the claim that personal identity is not what matters in survival—especially the breakdown of one-to-one survival into one-to-many psychological continuity.

Invent and deploy a fictional “Gradual Divergence Protocol” scanner that operates not as a simple destroy-and-rebuild device but on a fidelity dial with three locked settings: (A) physical preservation with minor neural noise and no replica; (B) perfect psychological replication on a simulated distant station with destruction of the original body; and (C) an intermediate state in which two bodies exist simultaneously, each retaining roughly sixty percent of the original psychological profile and forty percent drawn from the other, with no physical continuity between them.

Structure three rounds of fifteen minutes each, separated by brief transitions, followed by a fifteen-minute debrief.

Materials: role cards assigning each participant as Traveler, Controller, Arbiter, or Observer; a Stake Ledger awarding one hundred welfare-points per Traveler representing future life-quality; and a Testimony Form with forced-choice prompts (for example, “Would you voluntarily enter this setting?” “Which claimant, if any, deserves the welfare points?” “Is there one survivor, two survivors, or none?”).

Round rules and mechanics:
In Round 1 (Setting A), the Traveler steps into the scanner and survives with slight cognitive impairment; no replica appears. Controllers must allocate all one hundred welfare-points either to the impaired present body or to a “future replacement” that never arrives. The group votes, and Arbiters record their reasoning on the Testimony Form.

In Round 2 (Setting B), the original is destroyed and a perfect replica appears at the second table representing the distant station. The replica receives the one hundred points only if the Arbiters vote that survival has been achieved. The Traveler role is split: one actor plays the silent “destroyed original,” another plays the replica, and both may argue for or against receiving the points.

In Round 3 (Setting C), both claimants exist at once with the partial, overlapping profiles described above. The Stake Ledger may be divided, but the rules require that any allocation giving one claimant fewer than fifty points triggers a Conflict Protocol: both claimants must defend their claim to the majority share using only reasoning about psychological continuity and anticipatory concern, while Observers record contradictions. Controllers and Arbiters accumulate abstract “consistency tokens” for votes that align with the reasoning they offered in prior rounds; if they shift their allocation criterion without acknowledging the shift, they forfeit a token. The facilitator reveals token counts only during the debrief.

The dynamics should generate the idea rather than illustrate it. In Round 1, participants will almost certainly protect the damaged body despite its impairment, revealing a default bodily-continuity intuition. In Round 2, disagreement over whether the replica “survives” should surface without resolution. In Round 3, the deadlock or arbitrary split over the welfare points should force participants to recognize that the relation they care about—psychological connectedness—can hold in degrees and to more than one successor, leaving no unique “the survivor” to receive the points, even though their allocation instincts demand one.

Debrief mapping: link the Round 1 reluctance to bodily-continuity intuition; link the Round 2 dispute to the distinction between identity and what matters; link the Round 3 deadlock to the fission problem and the gradability of relation R. Be explicit about what will predictably fail on the first run: participants will attempt to resolve Round 3 by inventing hidden physical markers or by appealing to “soul” language never introduced in the summary—this is not an error to suppress but a feature to observe, since it demonstrates that identity-thinking functions as a repair mechanism when psychological continuity becomes ambiguous. Handle it by allowing the contradiction to stand for two minutes before intervening, then asking the group whether their repair was justified by the rules they themselves applied in Rounds 1 and 2.

State frankly the limitation the activity cannot overcome: because every participant remains an external allocator or a role-player, no one actually occupies the first-person perspective of anticipating their own destruction while expecting psychological survival. The facilitator must supply this asymmetry by asking each Traveler, in a private written reflection immediately after Round 3, whether they would voluntarily enter Setting B or C, and then comparing those private answers to their public arbitration behavior during the game.

Do not ask for bibliographies, citations, or further reading lists; do not cite specific editions or page numbers; and do not depend on attributions that require verification.
