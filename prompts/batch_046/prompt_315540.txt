I am a retired molecular biologist who has only recently begun to read philosophy with any seriousness. I am preparing an interview for a small podcast series in which I talk with philosophers about arguments that strike me after a lifetime in the laboratory. Next week’s guest is an unnamed philosopher who defends Albert Camus’s anti-death-penalty position — not as biography, but as living moral argument.

Please draft eight to ten interview questions that do not ask him merely to summarise the view. I want at least one question on Camus’s comparison between state execution and the murders committed during the Occupation, together with a follow-up that anticipates the reply that democratic procedure distinguishes the two; at least one on Camus’s claim that bureaucratic ritual conceals the physical reality of killing, together with a follow-up keyed to the objection that all institutional punishment involves administrative distance; and at least one on Camus’s empirical argument that capital punishment fails as a deterrent, together with a follow-up that addresses the retributivist’s dismissal of deterrence as irrelevant.

Because my own thinking has been formed by experimental science, I also want one question that presses whether Camus’s insistence on the irreversibility of execution gains or loses force when compared to irreversible interventions in natural systems — for instance, the extinction of a species or the irreversible release of a gene drive — and whether that analogy exposes a hidden premise about human moral corrigibility. The questions should avoid generic biographical openings, must not ask for bibliographies or reading lists, and should be pitched so that both philosophers and non-specialist listeners can follow. Please write in a formal register suitable for a prepared interview, using British English.
