I'm a novelist whose craft turns on distributed focalization and polyphonic interiority — the diffusion of consciousness across characters through free indirect discourse, shared mental spaces, and narrative voices that lack a single embodied anchor. Restate Douglas Hofstadter's argument that an "I" is not confined to one brain but can persist as a replicable pattern in another mind, particularly his claim that after his wife's death her self-pattern continued within his own cognition, using only the vocabulary of narrative theory: focalizer, diegetic level, implied author, narrative contract, and the ontology of fictional minds. Take each structural element of his view — the strange loop of self-reference, the copying of a mental pattern across substrates, the persistence of one consciousness inside another — and translate it precisely. For each, say what maps cleanly onto narrative form, what requires distortion to fit, and what has no analog at all. Then deliver the residue report: do the failures of translation indicate that fiction theory lacks the resources to represent intersubjective identity, or do they reveal that Hofstadter's position depends on metaphysical commitments — such as the reducibility of the self to transferable information — that narrative practice implicitly denies?
