I've been trying to get my head round the causal exclusion argument since a seminar last term where everyone just kept repeating Kim's dilemma and then shrugging. The set-up's familiar enough: mental properties supervene on physical ones, physics looks causally closed, so mental causes either overdetermine effects or they're epiphenomenal. Non-reductive physicalists reply with the usual moves — supervenience doesn't compete, mental properties are realised by physical ones so there's no real exclusion, or we should apply some proportionality principle about relevant causes. But it's been going on for decades and it feels like the same chess game replayed every year; nobody concedes anything. My working theory is that it's not reely about mental causation at all, but that both sides are operating with different metaphysics of causation — one thinking in terms of productive push-pull, the other in terms of difference-making or control — and they keep using the word 'exclusion' to talk past each other. Is that the right diagnosis, or is there actually a hidden empirical question about whether causal closure holds at the level of complex biological systems, or maybe a bedrock intuition about whether mental properties do any 'causal work' that neither side can argue for?

If it is mostly verbal or about different questions being asked — ontology versus explanatory practice — then what would progress even look like? Would it take a formal result about overdetermination in mechanistic models, a clear conceptual distinction that dissolves the problem, or should we just accept that the impasse is permanent because the underlying commitments are just incompatible? I'd like an honest answer about whether anything could move it, and please say how confident you are in whatever diagnosis you give, singling out the point you feel least sure about.
