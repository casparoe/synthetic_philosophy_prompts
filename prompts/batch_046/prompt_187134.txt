I am running a 90-minute philosophy-of-decision-theory seminar with 10 graduate students. Design a single in-class activity (about 40 minutes of play, then 20 minutes of debrief) that makes participants feel the force of superrational coordination and its failure modes, combining a Platonia-style dilemma with a modified Luring Lottery. Give explicit rules, roles, and small real stakes—points exchangeable for a coffee voucher—so the tension emerges from play rather than from any lecture up front.

Structure it in two rounds. Round 1 (Platonia structure): pair students anonymously; each independently decides whether to send a cooperative signal to a counterpart they know is reasoning identically, with payoffs arranged so that mutual cooperation is best for both but symmetric reasoning about defection creates instability. Round 2 (Luring Lottery): each student secretly picks an integer from 1 to 100; selecting a number that no one else selects wins a large point bonus, but number 99 is a designated “lure” with a slightly higher payout designed to attract clustering. Superrational reasoning should push identical agents away from the lure and away from obvious patterns toward symmetric randomization, yet without direct communication this is hard to execute.

Specify exactly how the facilitator runs each round, what instructions are given (without naming “superrationality” or “Platonia” in advance), and what will predictably go wrong—for example, clustering on the lure, or students treating Round 1 as a standard Prisoner’s Dilemma rather than as a symmetry problem. In the debrief, map each round onto the formal idea: identify the moment participants appealed to “they will do what I do,” show where the physical game breaks the analogy to pure reasoning—probably the opacity of others’ reasoning processes—and state how the facilitator must supply that missing element verbally. Be frank about which feature of superrationality the setup cannot reproduce. Finally, state clearly what students should come away seeing: not a settled doctrine, but a felt tension between individual rationality and symmetric reasoning in games with identically situated agents.
