I work as the lead curator for a small regional museum, and every five years we rebuild our permanent local-history gallery. Right now we're designing a plaque about why the steel plant here shut down in 1987, and I keep getting into fights with our board about how many "reasons" to list. I want a decision procdure my interns can run — grounded in whatever philosophy actually says about overdetermination and multi-causal explanation — for picking which causes belong on the plaque and which ones just clutter it.

The procdure should be a checklist in order: first question, second question, etc., with what counts as a yes/no at each step, where it stops with "leave it off," and where it has to hand the call up to me because it's values, not facts. I need it to cover the hard cases: a cause that was technically sufficient but invisible to anyone living through it; two causes that are each necessary only together; and what I've started calling "shadow causation," where one cause is real but every outcome it explains is already covered by a more visible cause, so adding it doesn't change the explanation but does change the politics. Apply the procdure to three proposed causes for our closure: the 1986 river flood, the federal tariff repeal that winter, and the union's strike decision. One of these should come out "exclude" in a way that will piss off our local labor supporters, and I want the procdure to flag that this is a judgment call, not a mistake in the logic.

Write it for people who aren't philosophers — no jargon unless you define it — and be honest about where it will misfire or need a human sign-off. Don't ask for a bibliography or cite specific texts; just build it from the idea itself. Keep the whole thing under 500 words, with the worked examples in short paragraphs not bullet lists, and flag the one case where I'm hoping nobody asks but I need to know what the procdure can't do.
