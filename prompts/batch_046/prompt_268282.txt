I am a junior at my high school and I sit on the student welfare review board, a group that meets every other Thursday for about fifteen minutes to evaluate proposals affecting the student body—last week we argued for nearly forty minutes about a cafeteria menu change, which made me realize we have no consistent framework for comparing harms of different scales. I need a reusable decision procedure, grounded in the ethical puzzle about whether countless barely-noticeable burdens spread across many people can ever outweigh one catastrophic harm to a single individual, that my board could actually run under time pressure without anyone reading formal philosophy. The procedure should list questions in order, specify what counts as an adequate answer for each, mark the exact points where a judgment call replaces calculation, distinguish whether small harms accumulate on the same people or are distributed across different ones, and be explicit about where it is likely to misfire or recommend an answer that feels wrong.

Please apply it to three proposals. First, installing a morning health screening that adds ten minutes every day for all eight hundred students but prevents one student with severe asthma from being hospitalized. Second, sending a mildly embarrassing automated reminder about overdue library books to every student, avoiding a severe funding penalty that would damage the whole school but causing a brief moment of shame for each recipient. Third, canceling the winter dance to spare six hundred attendees from a minor cold, even though about fifty students have planned for months and would suffer deep disappointment; I include this specifically because I suspect the procedure might advise cancellation based on aggregation, and I think that result would be wrong, so I want to see precisely where the reasoning diverges from my intuition. The procedure should also flag cases involving intentional versus accidental harm as outside its scope, state clearly what it cannot settle, and avoid requiring me to look up any sources or cite specific theories.
