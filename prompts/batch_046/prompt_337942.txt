i run a small grad workshop in phil of social science, about 10 people, 90 minutes. i want an in-class activity that makes them actually feel the wall you hit trying to say whether institutions, geography, or culture makes nations rich - not a lecture on correlation vs causation, but them running into it themselves.

design a structured deliberation/game. split them into three teams (Institutions, Geography, Cultural Norms). each gets a dossier on four made-up countries with realistic but fictional profiles - say one with strong courts but landlocked and poor, one with weak property rights but huge coastal trade access and rich, etc. every dossier highlights different patterns but they all share a hidden backstory revealed only after initial presentations: they were all shaped by the same colonial extraction logic, but extraction intensity was set by geographic accessibility, which then influenced both institutional design and cultural attitudes toward risk. so the "causes" are all tangled in the same loop.

rules: each team gets 20 min to build a causal claim and present it. then they must vote on which factor is "primary" for the group - but they can only cast a vote if they can point to a counterfactual country inside the dataset that proves separation. they can't, because all four are confounded. then open debate for 25 min where they can propose "natural experiments" (like a country split in two), but you veto any proposal unless they name a real-world analog that isn't also confounded - which forces them to stay in the room and face the absence of clean cases.

debrief: map the veto moment onto the actual methodological crisis in comparative history - specifically the lack of exogenous variation and the synthetic-control problem for whole nations. inject what i call the "parallel-paths problem": institutions and culture co-evolve with geography so tightly that isolating one requires a counterfactual history that never existed. be frank about what the game can't reproduce: it doesnt let them run actual IV regressions or statistical synthetic controls, so the faciliator has to supply that verbally. also tell me what predictably goes wrong - probably someone brings up Korea or East/West Germany - and give me a scripted response that shows why even split-country cases remain contested without leaving the framework. include an honest note about which feature of causal skepticism the activity misses entirely (maybe the gradual Bayesian lowering of confidence rather than a sudden wall?).

make sure its runnable, include timelines and roles, and keep it casual - a typo or two is fine.
