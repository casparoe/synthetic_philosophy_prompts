I am writing a feature on whether W. E. B. Du Bois’s claim that all art is propaganda can survive the shift from print and performance to algorithmic, collectively authored media, and in preparing the piece I spoke with a curator who reconstructed what she takes to be Du Bois’s implicit verdicts across four artistic cases. She says he would have embraced the Negro spirituals as propaganda because they marry beauty with a collective message of endurance; he would have dismissed a technically brilliant but apolitical portrait of a wealthy patron as failing art’s racial purpose; he would have accepted an abstract geometric mural reserved for Black church ritual even though it carries no literal narrative, because its form and placement serve communal identity; and he would have rejected an explicitly political protest flyer distributed solely to white donors, not for lacking a message but because its audience and purpose betray collective advancement. She insists these four rulings hang together, yet I can see at least two—perhaps three—different principles that license them all. Now I am trying to apply her reconstruction to a new case I am investigating: a real-time, biometrically responsive sound environment called Resonance, generated anonymously through an open-source algorithm and installed in a museum space for Black audiences. It has no fixed score, no representational imagery, and no single author; it adapts to listeners’ emotional states to produce emergent harmonic structures intended solely to foster collective mourning and affirmation. Would Du Bois, on her reconstruction, count Resonance as propaganda? I am less interested in a bare yes or no than in the competing rules her four verdicts actually license—for instance, one that demands a consciously authored racial-political purpose manifest in the work’s form, one that requires only embedded communal function regardless of authorship or explicit content, and one that insists on both aesthetic distinction and an intentionally designed collective purpose. For each rule, what does it predict about Resonance? Which prediction best coheres with the closest analog among her four cases, and what principle would she be adopting either way? And finally, what single further case—perhaps in a different medium or with a different audience—would most sharply distinguish the rule she is actually running from the others?
