I have noticed a recurring reasoning pattern in thought experiments about awakening and duplication, and I am trying to determine whether it has an established name or whether it needs one.

Consider two structurally parallel scenarios involving a fair coin. In Scenario A, if the coin lands heads, a single subject is put to sleep and then awakened in Room 1 the next day; if it lands tails, that same subject is duplicated, with both copies placed in identical Room 1 and Room 2 environments and both awakened with no memory of the duplication event. In Scenario B, the setup is identical except that on tails the experimenters do not duplicate the original subject; instead, they place a different, psychologically similar but numerically distinct individual in Room 2, so that heads yields one occupant in Room 1 and tails yields two occupants in identical rooms, but never two copies of the same person.

In both scenarios, an outside observer knows the coin is fair and knows that heads produces one awakening and tails produces two. Now consider the first-person reasoning of an occupant who finds themselves awake in one of the rooms with no further information. In Scenario A, many reasoners hold that the occupant should assign credence 1/2 to heads, on the grounds that both outcomes produce an awakening experience indistinguishable from this one, so the observation does not discriminate. In Scenario B, by contrast, many of the same reasoners hold that the occupant should assign credence 1/3 to heads, on the grounds that there are twice as many individuals who could be having this experience under tails, and one should reason as if selected randomly from all actual awakenings.

What strikes me is not the specific credence values themselves, but the fact that the shift between the 1/2 judgment and the 1/3 judgment tracks nothing about the occupant's available evidence, the physics of the rooms, or the prior probabilities of the coin; it tracks only whether the other occupant, should there be one, is conceived as a continuation of the self (a "me" in another room) or as a separate person altogether. The first-person phenomenology—waking in an indistinguishable room, lacking any distinguishing information—is identical in both cases.

Is there a specific established concept for this framing-dependent divergence in evidential reasoning, where the assignment of probability to an objective event shifts solely according to whether alternative instances of the experience are indexed to the same continuing subject? How does it differ, precisely, from the standard Sleeping Beauty problem, from the non-identity problem, and from generic applications of the principle of indifference to self-locating belief? If no established term isolates exactly this shift, I would welcome a proposed name together with a precise definition and clear boundary conditions—particularly conditions that distinguish it from cases where the difference in reasoning is genuinely driven by empirical differences in available evidence rather than by the metaphysics of personal identity across the scenarios.
