I'm running a tiny workshop for amateur poets who think translation is mostly about picking the right synonym. I want to teach them why Marot's rondeaux break that idea — specifically how the formal cage (refrain lines, rhyme scheme, syllable count) doesn't just dress up meaning but actually reshuffles it. The audience is six people, none read French, one of them thinks rhyme is dead in modern English. I need the teaching arc, not the lecture itself.

Here's the twist I want injected: don't treat preservation of form as fidelity to Marot's intent. Instead, build the session around the claim that when you translate a rondeau into English and keep the formal constraints, you end up generating an ambiguity or irony in the target text that wasn't present in the original. So the carrying example should be one specific rondeau — not the usual anthology piece — and the point is that the constraint creates something new rather than saving something old.

Plan the dependency order: what concept has to be in place before the example makes sense? Then the example itself — what it needs to have (probably the refrain structure visible even in translation) and what it must lack (maybe a clean modern English rhyme partner for one of the French sounds). Then the misconceptions they'll bring, in the order they'll raise them: 'translation is just a thesaurus job', 'English syllabics are impossible now', 'the form is decorative', and probaly 'if the meaning shifts then the translation failed'. Tell me exactly where in the sequence to head each one off, not after.

Also: the check at the end — one question or task that shows they've understood form as a generator rather than a box. And be explicit about what I'm leaving out — maybe the Pléiade editorial history or biographical gossip — and say I should refuse to explain it at this level.

Be committed: don't lay out three equally valid routes and leave me to choose. Pick the order that actually works, defend why the example carries the idea, and tell me which misconception is the dangerous one. Write in British English, and don't correct thier spelling if I slip up.
