I am a software engineer without formal training in philosophy, and I have agreed to lead a single ninety-minute session for a small reading group composed mostly of developers and one physicist. Our subject is the problem of universals, but I want to treat it as a live metaphysical puzzle rather than a museum of historical positions. Before I design the session, I need a teaching plan that respects conceptual dependency: what idea has to be secured first—perhaps the distinction between a particular and a property—so that asking whether redness exists apart from red things actually sounds puzzling rather than merely grammatical? Next, I need the one carrying example that should run through the entire session. I am inclined to avoid the usual philosophical stock examples—triangularity, humanity—because to programmers they sound like abstract class names, which invites a specific misconception. Instead, I want an example drawn from physical objects or perceptual experience that cannot be cleanly mapped onto inheritance or interface implementation, and I need you to explain why it must have that resistance.

Given the audience's professional reflex to translate metaphysical talk into software architecture, I expect they will form misconceptions in a predictable order: first, that a universal is just a type signature; second, that realism is merely the claim that abstract classes exist in the compiler; third, that nominalism is just duck typing. I want to know exactly where in the explanatory sequence each of these mappings should be anticipated and headed off—before it is introduced, during, or after—so that the analogy serves as a ladder rather than a trap. I also want guidance on what must be left out at this level. I am thinking of the more technical variants of nominalism and the historical debate over universals in mathematics, but I want you to confirm whether omitting them weakens the core argument, and how to state the omission without sounding evasive. Finally, propose one precise check-for-understanding question for the end of the session—something that would reveal whether they understand why shared properties pose a metaphysical problem rather than whether they can label the three standard positions.
