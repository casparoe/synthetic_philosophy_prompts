Write a dialogue between two philosophers of history—call them Mara and Elias—who disagree about whether non-human entities such as pathogenic germs, long-term climate shifts, and volcanic eruptions can legitimately be described as historical actors without collapsing into environmental determinism. Mara defends a view she calls relational protagonism: an entity becomes an actor not through intention, but through its capacity to restructure the field of human possibility in ways that are irreversible and non-substitutable. She argues that the Laki fissure eruption in Iceland, by transforming European grain economies and political discourse in the late eighteenth century, did not merely cause scarcity but reshaped the very options available to states and peasants in a manner no other contemporaneous event could have replicated, thereby earning a form of agency that is structural rather than mental. Elias counters with plural causation without attribution: he grants that such events are indispensable nodes in causal chains, but insists that labeling them actors obscures the distributive responsibility of human institutions and risks reifying geology or microbiology as autonomous forces. He presses Mara on whether her non-substitutable restructuring criterion can genuinely distinguish actors from background conditions—why is a volcanic winter an actor while a predictable seasonal drought remains only context?

The dialogue must contain real back-and-forth: each speaker should respond directly to the specific argument just offered, not deliver independent monologues. Both should be equally competent; neither should be a strawman. They should address the perils of environmental determinism explicitly—Mara must explain how her view avoids reducing human politics to a reflex of temperature or microbial load, and Elias must explain how his refusal of agency-language still allows historians to take non-human forces seriously without treating them as passive scenery.

To keep the exchange from being generic, inject the following conceptual constraint into their debate: introduce the idea of thermodynamic asymmetry—the claim that an event’s actor-status depends on whether human responses to it are asymmetrical, meaning societies can adapt to its presence but cannot retroactively neutralize its restructuring effects. Mara should defend this asymmetry as the boundary between genuine actors and mere conditions; Elias should attack it by showing that human technological or institutional adaptation can sometimes restore symmetry, thereby dissolving the boundary. Do not ask for citations, bibliographies, or references to named theorists beyond what is necessary for the argument itself. The dialogue should read as an original conceptual investigation rather than a summary of existing literature, and it should run to roughly 800–1000 words.
