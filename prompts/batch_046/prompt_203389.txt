As a journalist writing on the conceptual foundations of global history, I want to commission a structured adversarial-collaboration document addressing whether the contrastive framing “why Britain and not China?”—the Needham question, central to Great Divergence debates—is methodologically well-posed or a teleological misstep. Please draft the joint statement that two opposing camps could both sign: on one side, the Comparative Institutionalists, who defend the framing by arguing that eighteenth-century Britain and Qing China can be treated as comparable units facing analogous resource-allocation challenges, and that divergence in fiscal-military capacity and property-rights regimes explains the outcome without presupposing inevitability; on the other side, the Co-Constitutive Globalists, who reject the framing on the grounds that it treats “Britain” and “China” as conceptually isolated containers, takes industrial modernity as an unexamined endpoint, and ignores the world-systemic interdependence—silver flows, colonial extraction, ecological exchange—that made both polities outcomes of a single global process rather than independent cases. The document should begin with a numbered section of agreed claims—covering, at minimum, the empirical record of eighteenth-century agricultural productivity, the formal logic of comparative explanation, and the failure of single-factor monocausal accounts—followed by a disagreements table in which each camp’s position is rendered in wording its own members would accept. In particular, each side should state precisely what it takes “state capacity” to mean, whether that concept can be operationalized through an outcome-independent metric (for instance, a fiscal-stress index comparing Qing Yellow River conservancy expenditure with British parliamentary debt management that does not smuggle industrialization into the benchmark), and whether global interdependence renders isolationist comparison incoherent or merely incomplete. Identify the cruxes: the central ones are whether comparative macro-history requires metrics that do not presuppose the outcome being explained, and whether co-constitutive global processes make the Britain-China unit comparison conceptually invalid or only empirically harder. For each crux, specify the concrete historical probe or conceptual test both sides agree would bear on it, record in advance each side’s prediction about what that probe would reveal—particularly whether applying a common fiscal-stress metric would show structurally comparable state capacities or incommensurable institutional logics—and state whether both sides agree that any result from this probe would actually move the debate or whether they each regard it as inconclusive by design. Each camp should also state what specific argument or evidence would cause it to revise its position, and you should flag any portion of the dispute that is merely verbal—say, if “divergence” is being used by one camp as an empirical event and by the other as a historiographical construction—explaining exactly why resolving that ambiguity does not dissolve the substantive disagreement. The document must not request bibliographies, reading lists, or citations, and should remain fully intelligible without web access. Finally, please include a brief meta-commentary indicating where your own reasoning in constructing this collaboration is most vulnerable: what finding about institutional comparability or global economic integration would lead you to conclude that one camp’s formulation is empty rather than substantive, and what would change your mind about whether the Needham question is well-posed at all.
