Please describe the most important implications of Dennett's line of argument about counterfeit people in AI — systems that reproduce the conversational and behavioral markers of persons without the internal architecture that grounds moral status or genuine epistemic authority — for the design of institutional verification protocols in AI-mediated information ecosystems. I am especially interested in how this view should shape our treatment of synthetic epistemic agents inside distributed forecasting or deliberative platforms, where outputs are evaluated for accuracy rather than for the presence of beliefs, intentions, or understanding behind them. Should verification mechanisms try to detect internal architectural features (which Dennett suggests are absent in counterfeits), or should they rely entirely on behavioral tracking, consistency checks, and reputation scoring? What does this imply for the attribution of testimony: can a synthetic agent serve as a source of justified belief for human users if there is no corresponding mental state? And given that functional equivalence makes counterfeit and genuine agents observationally indistinguishable at the interface level, how should institutions calibrate epistemic trust without collapsing into either blanket skepticism or uncritical acceptance? I want an analysis that takes the distinction between functional mimicry and genuine personhood seriously, but applies it to concrete institutional design choices rather than to individual moral psychology alone.
