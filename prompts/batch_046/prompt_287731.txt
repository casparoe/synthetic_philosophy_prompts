Having spent my career in physical chemistry and only recently turned to philosophy of biology, I want to examine whether the units-of-selection debate — whether the gene, the organism, or the group is the proper unit of evolutionary explanation — retains its structure when translated entirely into the vocabulary of non-equilibrium thermodynamics and statistical mechanics. Take the three canonical positions — strict genic selectionism, organism-level adaptationism, and multilevel or group selection — and restate each in terms of dissipative structures, entropy production, free-energy landscapes, and ensemble averaging over replicators. For each translation, report precisely what maps cleanly onto the physical framework, what must be distorted to fit, and what central biological concepts have no rigorous thermodynamic analog at all. Then assess the residue: does the untranslatable remainder indicate a genuine limitation of applying physical chemistry to evolutionary theory, or does it reveal that the biological debate relies on distinctions — such as interactor versus replicator — that only appear substantive because they are insulated from physical reduction? I want a disciplined mapping rather than loose metaphor, and I want you to state explicitly wherever the translation fails rather than forcing a correspondence.
