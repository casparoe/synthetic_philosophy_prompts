As a retired experimental physicist who has recently turned to philosophy of mind, I am drafting a proposal for an interdisciplinary workshop on personal identity under gradual technological replacement. I want to avoid the well-worn teletransportation and Ship-of-Theseus scenarios and instead examine a precisely defined clinical trajectory: over one year, each functional column of a patient's prefrontal cortex is replaced one at a time by a neuromorphic analog that replicates both the column's feedforward computation and its local predictive-error dynamics, with biological tissue removed only after a thirty-day period of bidirectional integration with adjacent columns. The central philosophical issue is whether the patient survives as one continuous subject throughout this sequence, and whether survival depends on treating the year-long process as a single extended replacement event rather than as a chain of discrete micro-substitutions.

Write the proposal—stating the precise question, explaining why standard psychological-continuity, animalist, and narrative-identity accounts fail to resolve it given this specific integration constraint, outlining an approach based on conceptual analysis of functional-continuity thresholds rather than empirical neuroscience, specifying what a practical deliverable for clinical ethics boards would look like, and giving an honest assessment of failure conditions and scope—presented as exactly five numbered items, each consisting of one or two sentences, with no introductory or concluding remarks.
