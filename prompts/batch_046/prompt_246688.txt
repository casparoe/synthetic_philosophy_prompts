As a novelist constructing a historical fiction, I regularly adopt convictions about my characters' private motives—convictions that outstrip the documentary record—simply so that the narrative can cohere. This practice feels structurally akin to William James's defense of adopting beliefs through an act of will when evidence is insufficient yet the option is living, forced, and momentous, yet it also invites the evidentialist objection, associated with W.K. Clifford, that holding any belief without adequate evidence is ethically impermissible regardless of context. Write an essay that weighs whether the novelist's constructive belief is ethically distinguishable from James's religious or existential belief, and whether Clifford's evidentialism can accommodate the special epistemic obligations of fiction-making. Reach and justify a definite conclusion—whether the novelist's practice represents a legitimate exception, a disguised intellectual vice, or a case that dissolves the opposition between James and Clifford altogether. Please respond in American English.
