As a novelist constructing a multi-perspective narrative about the collapse of a family enterprise, I have been studying Jain anekāntavāda not as an exotic ornament but as a potential logic for holding contradictory descriptions together without collapsing them into synthesis. Before I commit to the architecture of the novel, I want to know whether the doctrine of many-sided truth (anekāntavāda) and the framework of conditional predication (syādvāda) can deliver consistent verdicts across the following cases, or whether I must abandon one of my intuitions.

First, two witnesses describe the origin of a destructive fire. One reports grease in the kitchen; the other reports smoke rising from the attic. In fact, an electrical fault ignited both locations simultaneously. Verdict: both accounts are true in a non-contradictory sense; they capture different aspects (syāt) of one complex event.

Second, a historian attributes the collapse of the enterprise to structural economic inequality; a character within the narrative attributes it to a specific act of personal betrayal by the founder. Verdict: both explanations are partially true and genuinely in tension—causation is not merely perspectival—yet neither should be dismissed as false. They must be held together under some form of conditional or qualified predication.

Third, a child describes her parents' separation as occurring because her father was "mean"; a clinical observer describes it as arising from irreconcilable attachment patterns. Verdict: neither account is false, but neither is complete. The child's moral vocabulary and the observer's structural vocabulary are not reducible to one another, yet they do not contradict in the way that a false date contradicts a true one.

Fourth, one narrator dates the bankruptcy to 1987; documentary evidence fixes it in 1989. Verdict: this is straightforward falsehood. Many-sidedness does not license empirical error.

Fifth, two narrators describe the emotional valence of the collapse. One calls it "tragic but necessary"; the other calls it "catastrophic and senseless." Verdict: both judgments stand, and I resist synthesizing them into a single emotional verdict. Yet I feel differently about a hypothetical pair who call the same event "morally good" and "morally evil": there, I think one must be wrong. So there is an asymmetry between emotional complexity and moral contradiction that I cannot articulate.

Sixth, a minor character insists that a specific cousin embezzled the inheritance; forensic records exclude this entirely. Verdict: false, not a partial side.

The tension among these judgments is this: I want to preserve contradiction in Case Two and Case Five as complementary, exclude contradiction entirely in Case Four and Case Six, and allow radical incompleteness without falsehood in Case Three. Meanwhile, I want to distinguish the permissible emotional opposition of Case Five from the impermissible moral opposition I imagine in its hypothetical variant.

Is there a coherent principle—perhaps a corrected or applied version of the seven-fold predication (saptabhaṅgī)—that distinguishes permissible partial truths from impermissible falsehoods, explains why some contradictions are complementary while others collapse, and accounts for the boundary between emotional complexity and moral exclusivity? If such a principle exists, state it precisely, indicate which clause is motivated by which case, and say what it predicts for a scenario I have not listed: a narrator who describes the collapse using only geometric metaphors—fractures, angles, voids—refusing all causal, emotional, or moral language. Does this constitute an additional side of the event, or does it represent an evasion of truth-claims altogether? Finally, be explicit about which of my six verdicts must be overturned if we prefer the simplest principle that saves the others.
