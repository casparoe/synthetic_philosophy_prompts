In philosophy of medicine I keep noticing a particular defensive pattern, and I want to know whether it has an established name. Someone begins with a naturalistic definition of disease — for example, that it is a statistically abnormal reduction in the functional efficiency of a biological part or process. A counterexample is then raised: a condition that is routinely treated clinically and causes serious suffering, yet shows no detectable functional reduction (say, certain chronic idiopathic pain syndromes, or asymptomatic pathogen-carriage states that only become harmful in specific social environments). Rather than revise the core definition, the defender adds a supplementary clause — the condition must also involve harm, disability, or social disadvantage. A second counterexample follows: a state that involves clear harm and sometimes clinical intervention, but is intuitively not a disease (for instance, the physiological adaptations to long-term incarceration, or the distress of profound grief). So a further clause is added, usually restricting the harm to being internal to the organism or not externally imposed. Each clause is individually reasonable, but together they transform the definition into a reactive list of exclusions that protects the original naturalistic intuition rather than refining it. Is there a specific term for this kind of progressive, clause-by-clause fortification of a concept? How does it differ from ordinary conceptual refinement, from ad hoc auxiliary hypotheses in science, and from a Wittgensteinian family-resemblance account? If no precise term exists, propose one with a brief definition and state its boundary conditions — specifically, how to tell it apart from legitimate pluralism about disease. Finish with a line that reads exactly 'Verdict: X', where X gives the nearest established concept or 'no established name — [proposed term]' together with the single feature that separates it from ordinary refinement.
