I hold a structural interpretation of the absent-minded driver problem: the driver's failure is not a miscounting of expected utility, but an unavoidable feature of sequential choice under self-locating uncertainty, in which any policy that conditions on the belief 'I am at the exit' necessarily alters the belief state that made reaching the exit possible. Consequently, the only consistently rational strategies are either unconditional commitment or a probabilistic stopping rule that treats prior decision nodes as observationally equivalent; objections that invoke straightforward utility maximization therefore miss the point, since the paradox arises from the interaction between epistemic access and action rather than from miscounted payoffs. Tell me what would genuinely count against this position. Rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward: which formal results in decision theory—concerning dynamic consistency, belief revision under amnesia, or representations of self-locating belief—would damage it; which intuitive counter-cases or competing models of agency would challenge it; and which philosophical commitments about practical reasoning it risks. Indicate which of these a committed defender of the structural view would concede as damaging, and which they would dismiss. Include an honest assessment of whether the view has been qualified into unfalsifiability—say, by restricting the term 'rational' to policies rather than to particular actions—and whether such insulation is a virtue or a vice for a view of this kind.
