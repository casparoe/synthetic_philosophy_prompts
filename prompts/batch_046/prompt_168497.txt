In Adam Smith's Theory of Moral Sentiments, sympathy seems to operate through direct affective replication: I judge another's sentiment appropriate only when my own emotional state mirrors theirs with sufficient fidelity, and the impartial spectator functions as an internalized statistical mean of these replicated sentiments across society. Given that recent work in affective neuroscience emphasizes individual differences in interoceptive precision and emotional prediction error, this model appears to presuppose a relatively homogeneous emotional population in order for the spectator to achieve normative authority. How does Smith's framework accommodate deep moral disagreement between groups whose interoceptive profiles and affective norms diverge—say, cultures that institutionalize radically different expressions of grief or honor—without collapsing into a form of moral relativism? Or is the spectator in fact not an aggregator of shared feelings at all, but a mechanism whose normative force does not depend on direct affective matching?
