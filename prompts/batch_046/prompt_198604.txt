I keep encountering a particular structural pattern in strategic reasoning with mixed strategies, and I am unsure whether it carries an established name. The situation arises as follows. One begins with a finite game, eliminates all strictly dominated pure strategies, and takes the convex hull of what remains. Inside that convex set, one examines mixtures rather than pure actions alone. A distribution placing, say, two-thirds weight on action A and one-third on action B may not be dominated by any other mixture within the current hull, yet it can be strictly dominated by a mixture that lies just outside — one that assigns positive weight to a pure strategy previously eliminated. Once that exterior mixture is admitted, further interior mixtures become dominated in turn, and the admissible region contracts inward in a second round. I have observed this in three-action classroom examples where dominance relations emerge only after probabilities are introduced, producing an iterative boundary shift that does not settle after a single pass. The pattern seems adjacent to rationalizability, but rationalizability is typically framed in terms of best-response sets over pure strategies or over the full simplex, not as a sequential contraction of a convex region driven by mixtures referencing eliminated actions. Does this specific loop — convex-hull contraction triggered by domination across the boundary of the current rationalizable set — have its own concept or label? If the literature does not isolate it, should it be classified as a variant of iterated dominance applied to distributions, or as a distinct failure mode? I am not seeking a bibliography; I want to know whether the carving itself is recognised, how it differs from ordinary rationalizability and from weak-dominance elimination in pure form, and where its precise edges lie.
