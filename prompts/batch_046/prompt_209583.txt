I have come to hold a qualified teleological view of history: that over long durations, institutional arrangements tend to correct toward justice not because of any metaphysical purpose, but because of an asymmetry in memorial transmission. Injustices generate articulate grievances, documentary traces, and reactive institutional innovations that accumulate in public memory, whereas stable just arrangements are typically silent, self-effacing, and leave thinner archival footprints. Consequently, the historical record functions like a selective filter that enriches for correctives over time, giving the appearance—perhaps the reality—of an arc bending toward justice. What would genuinely count against this view? I want the defeaters ranked from fatal to merely awkward: which historical patterns, which conceptual distinctions (say, between justice and mere stability, or between grievance articulation and actual improvement), and which results from other domains (perhaps evolutionary biology or epistemology) it stands or falls with. Which of these would a defender of this memorial-filter thesis concede as damaging, and which could be dismissed by refining the claim? I also want an honest assessment of whether the view has been qualified into unfalsifiability—if, for example, any persistent injustice can be explained away as not yet having reached the archival threshold—and whether that insulation is a defect for a claim of this kind. Please state how confident you are in your assessment and single out the specific point about which you are least certain.
