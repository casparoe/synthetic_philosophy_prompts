I have been working through the Meno problem in a graduate seminar and find myself with a cluster of verdicts about when knowledge is genuinely more valuable than mere true belief, but I cannot tell whether they hang together. My working thought is that knowledge derives its surplus value from what I will call counterfactual epistemic resilience: a known belief remains correct and action-guiding across nearby perturbations of evidence, social challenge, or transmission to others, whereas a merely true belief is brittle and collapses under such perturbations. This seems to capture several of my judgments. In the thermometer case, a person who glances at a broken instrument that happens to read correctly forms a true belief about the temperature, but if the weather had been slightly different the instrument would have misled her; a person who knows via calibrated instruments tracks the temperature across those nearby worlds, and that resilience is why her state is better. In the juror case, two jurors reach the correct acquittal, but one relies on an inarticulate intuition while the other grasps the logical structure of the alibi; only the latter survives adversarial cross-examination, which suggests knowledge is superior precisely because it withstands social-epistemic perturbation. In the delegation case, I truly believe a climate projection because I trust an expert, while she knows it; for my solitary decision about travel, our beliefs are equally good, but if I must teach the result or revise it in light of anomalous data, only her knowledge survives the transmission and revision perturbations. So far the resilience principle looks promising.

But I have two verdicts that pull in the opposite direction. First, the placebo case: a patient forms, by pure coincidence, the true belief that a particular sugar pill will cure her headache, and because she lacks knowledge that the pill is inert, the placebo effect succeeds; a physician who knows the pill is inert cannot induce that cure. Here knowledge seems not merely less valuable but actively worse for the desired outcome, which is hard to square with resilience being a uniform good. Second, I think that in purely solitary, non-adversarial, non-transmissive contexts—say, a hiker who correctly guesses that a particular mushroom is edible and eats it—the true belief and the knowledge (say, from botanical training) yield exactly the same survival value, and I do not feel there is any hidden surplus value in the knower's state. These cases suggest that resilience is either irrelevant or harmful in isolated first-person action.

Is there a coherent principle that saves all five verdicts—thermometer, juror, delegation, placebo, and solitary hiker—or must I drop one? If the resilience principle is right, I need to explain why it fails to apply, or applies negatively, in the placebo case, and why it does not generate surplus value in the solitary hiker case. Alternatively, if I should abandon resilience in favor of a different account—perhaps one that distinguishes agential robustness from doxastic robustness, or that treats knowledge as valuable only within specific functional roles—say so clearly, state the repaired principle with each clause tied to the case that motivates it, and tell me exactly which of my verdicts it cannot accommodate. I want a committed answer: take a definite stand on whether knowledge has a uniform surplus value or a context-dependent one, defend your choice with explicit reasons, and do not merely list the options for me to decide.
