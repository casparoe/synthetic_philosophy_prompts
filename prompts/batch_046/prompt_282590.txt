In recent discussions of George Berkeley’s economic writings—especially The Querist (1735–37)—commentators often draw an analogy between his defense of paper currency and modern decentralized digital ledgers. The comparison is usually deployed to license a specific conclusion: just as blockchain tokens have no intrinsic value and function only through distributed collective verification of a transaction history, Berkeley’s proposed paper notes are pure “signs” of labor whose value is sustained not by gold or silver reserves but by a shared social agreement to honor them as claims on the community’s productive capacity. From this, it is claimed that Berkeley was essentially an early theorist of fiat-by-consensus, and that his attack on commodity-money critics anticipates modern arguments against gold-backed currencies.

Audit this analogy systematically. First, provide an explicit correspondence table: in Berkeley’s scheme, what plays the role of the distributed ledger, the verification mechanism, the token, the consensus protocol, and the “intrinsic value” that both systems reject? Second, identify where the mapping breaks: Berkeley’s notes were issued by a sovereign-authorized national bank with redemption obligations tied to specific national land and labor, not by a permissionless peer-to-peer network; the social trust he appealed to was hierarchical, clerical, and tied to state authority, not algorithmic; and his central distinction between money (a counter or ticket) and wealth (the labor and goods signified) does not map cleanly onto the token/utility distinction in digital currencies, since for Berkeley the reliability of the sign depended on moral and political authority rather than on cryptographic proof. Third, ask which features the analogy quietly imports—especially decentralization, algorithmic neutrality, and the idea that value can emerge without sovereign decree—and whether Berkeley’s actual position possesses any of them. Fourth, evaluate whether the conclusion that he “anticipated modern fiat-by-consensus” survives once these disanalogies are on the table, or whether the analogy was carrying that conclusion all along.

Finally, if the blockchain comparison collapses under scrutiny, propose a closer historical analogy—perhaps to medieval tally sticks, bills of exchange, or theater tickets—and explain why that replacement better captures Berkeley’s specific claim that money is a counter of labor rather than labor itself, without smuggling in anachronistic notions of decentralized verification.
