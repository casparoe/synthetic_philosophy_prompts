(1) For Lewis, a convention is a regularity in behavior that solves a coordination problem, sustained by common knowledge that everyone preffered to conform given that others do.
(2) But evolutionary dynamics can stabilize behavioral regularities through noisy imitation and payoff-biased learning, without any agent representing the regularity or having beliefs about others' preferences.
(3) Call these shadow conventions: they solve coordination problems funcitonally but lack the common-knowledge mental-state component.
(4) Linguistic norms like using 'bank' for the financial institution seem like clear conventions, yet they also spread through evolutionary dynamics (meme transmission, usage frequency) rather than explicit agreement.
(5) So either linguistic norms are shadow conventions, which means they lack the mental-state condition Lewis requires, or they are full Lewisian conventions, which means every speaker must have common knowledge about every other speaker's preferences for ambiguity-avoidance.
(6) The second option is obviously false for large speech communities where nobody knows anyone.
(7) So linguistic norms must be shadow conventions.
(8) But if shadow conventions are real conventions, then Lewis's mental-state condition is redundant.
(9) And if it's redundant, then any accidental correlation that solves a coordination problem counts—like everyone in a town wearing blue on Tuesdays because of a past factory shift—making convention ubiquitous and useless for explanation.
(10) So Lewis's account either misses real linguistic norms or collapses into triviality.
