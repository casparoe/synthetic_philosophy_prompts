I have been asked to help a small mutual-aid collective decide how to divide an unexpected grant among members who all lost income when a local employer collapsed. The disagreement centres on three members. Leo turned down several safer external offers to stay with the failing firm out of loyalty, though some now suggest he underestimated his own mobility and therefore bears partial responsibility for his current need; Priya was actively seeking other work but was repeatedly rejected because a chronic health condition made the available shifts physically inaccessible to her; and Sam left a stable position to pursue a risky venture that failed, arguing that since he knew the gamble and made it freely, he should not be treated more generously than those whose misfortune was clearly unchosen. Some in the group want to distribute strictly by present need, while others think Sam should receive less because his hardship follows from his own decision, with Leo falling in an ambiguous middle where loyalty might itself be shaped by conditions he did not fully control.

I am trying to understand whether there is a principled way to separate what people genuinely choose from what is forced upon them, especially when the line seems to blur in practice: Leo's sense of obligation might reflect a social environment that discouraged leaving, and Sam's willingness to take risks might have been cultivated by prior advantages that made failure feel survivable rather than catastrophic. Given that any attempt to allocate according to responsibility seems to depend on judgments about psychological and social background conditions that are themselves contested, does the distinction between choice and circumstance remain useful for actual distribution? Or does its instability suggest that an approach which tries to reward or penalise people for their choices collapses into either an unworkable inquiry into personal histories or a retreat to equal division regardless of cause? I would value guidance on how to navigate this without reducing the problem to generic fairness.
