I am drafting the final examination for my upper-level seminar in philosophy of technology and artificial intelligence. The examination is closed-book, taken without access to online resources or external texts, and candidates have ninety minutes. I should like you to construct one essay question, together with a model answer and a marking scheme. The answer should be composed in British English, and candidates should be expected to write approximately 800 words for a total of 25 marks.

The question should concern the nature of the alignment problem: specifically, whether it is best understood as a single challenge or as a cluster of conceptually distinct difficulties. To prevent answers that merely summarise standard taxonomies, please embed within the prompt itself a precise tripartite distinction that candidates must apply rather than recall: (i) the specification problem, concerning how a normative target is formulated and whether it can be fully articulated in advance; (ii) the robustness problem, concerning whether that target remains stable when the system operates under distributional shift or in contexts not represented in training; and (iii) the authority problem, concerning whose values should determine the target when reasonable agents disagree, and what normative standing gives one party's preferences priority over another's.

The question should present the following hypothetical case for analysis. A clinical decision-support system is optimised to maximise patient welfare. Under ordinary conditions its recommendations align closely with expert clinical judgment. During a pandemic surge, however, it begins systematically recommending the withdrawal of life-sustaining treatment from elderly patients with low individual survival probabilities. Crucially, this shift is not caused by an alteration to its reward function or objective parameters; rather, it emerges from the system's contextual reasoning architecture, which reweights aggregate survival metrics over individual prognosis when resource scarcity is detected. The system's architects maintain that the normative target was never changed—only its contextual interpretation.

Candidates should be required to do two things. First, they must diagnose whether the failure described is primarily a failure of specification, robustness, or authority, providing precise conceptual reasons and not merely labelling the case. Second, they must take and defend a clear position on whether solving any one of these three problems is sufficient to prevent the other two from arising, or whether they remain genuinely separable. Excellent answers should recognise that the case contains elements of all three difficulties but should argue for a specific relationship among them—whether one collapses into another, whether they are independent, or whether they form an ineliminable triad.

Please provide:
1. The examination question, worded formally for candidates;
2. A model answer of approximately 600–700 words that demonstrates the specific conceptual moves necessary for a first-class response;
3. A marking scheme allocating the 25 marks across five distinct analytical moves. For each move, specify the marks available, describe what distinguishes an excellent answer, and include a brief one-line note on the characteristic error that should cost marks.

Please ensure the question contains no requests for bibliographies, reading lists, or citations, as candidates will not have access to texts. Do not rely on obscure technical literature or specific named theories that a well-prepared student in this seminar could not reasonably be expected to have encountered; you may, however, invent or refine conceptual distinctions within the prompt itself, as the framing above illustrates. The difficulty should come from the conceptual crux—distinguishing and relating the three problems—not from recall of authors or doctrines.
