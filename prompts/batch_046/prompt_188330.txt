I write novels in the mornings—long, slow books about people who systematically misunderstand their own motives—and in the afternoons I serve as a senior acquisitions editor for a small independent press that publishes narrative non-fiction. The rhythm is steady: every few weeks a proposal arrives that does not merely report events but advances an interpretive programme, a unified framework with a hard core of claims protected by auxiliary hypotheses that can shift to absorb anomalies. I have come to think that the philosopher Imre Lakatos’s distinction between progressive and degenerating research programmes—the hard core, the protective belt, the negative heuristic that shields the core, the positive heuristic that should guide new predictions, and the danger of ad hoc adjustments that reduce predictive content—might be exactly the lens I need, but only if it can be turned into an instrument I can actually use. I evaluate perhaps six to eight of these proposals each quarter, must render a preliminary sign-off within forty-eight hours, and must do so without being able to master every primary archive or disciplinary literature involved; my publisher expects consistency, the authors expect fairness, and my contract requires that I flag precisely where method ends and judgment begins.

Please construct a reusable editorial procedure—a flowchart rendered in words, an ordered sequence of tests with stopping conditions and explicit hand-off points—that I can run on any proposal manuscript. It should include: first, a step-by-step method for isolating the proposal’s hard core and distinguishing it from its protective belt, together with specific linguistic markers I can scan for in the text itself; second, criteria for deciding whether a belt adjustment is anticipatory, generated by the programme’s own positive heuristic, or reactive and ad hoc, along with a small set of sentence-level diagnostics I can apply directly to detect embroidery that saves the core only by shrinking predictive surplus; third, clear verdict points where the procedure yields progressive, degenerating, or stalled, and clear points where judgment is ineliminable—for instance, when a reactive adjustment is independently corroborated by evidence outside the programme, or when a degenerating framework might still merit publication for literary, ethical, or historical reasons despite its method; fourth, an honest accounting of what the procedure cannot settle, including cases where rival programmes explain the same anomalies with equal economy or where the core itself is too vaguely formulated to protect. Then apply the procedure to three hypothetical proposals. Case one: an argument that the rapid diffusion of a particular Bronze Age metallurgical technique across Eurasia is driven not by trade networks but by a hidden circuit of itinerant ritual specialists, predicting that previously ignored iconographic motifs should cluster in specific river valleys—show where the tests land. Case two: a proposal by an author I deeply admire that the collapse of a well-known twentieth-century political alliance was caused by a single covert psychological operation, where every contradictory archival document is dismissed as a deliberate disinformation plant and no independent predictions are advanced—show that the procedure returns a degenerating verdict I will dislike, and identify exactly what I must still decide by values rather than method. Case three: an ambiguous framework with a sharply defined hard core and minimal belt adjustments, whose positive heuristic has been silent for over a decade—the author explains past anomalies with elegance but offers no new directions for research—show where the procedure stops and demands human judgment. Do not provide bibliographies, reading lists, or citation references; do not ask me to consult texts I do not have; and build the procedure so that it requires only the proposal manuscript, forty-eight hours, and an editor’s ordinary attention.
