I have no formal training in philosophy or history of science and work entirely outside academia, but I have been thinking independently about simultaneous discovery—particularly Newton and Leibniz with calculus, and Darwin and Wallace with natural selection. I want a short research proposal of about four to five pages, suitable for an independent study grant or workshop pitch, that addresses a question I think the standard debate misses. The usual framing asks whether discoveries are inevitable and how credit should be divided; I want to ask whether, when two researchers reach mathematically or empirically equivalent results, the particular formulation—the notation, the explanatory narrative, the range of problems immediately addressed—makes one version more scientifically fruitful than the other, and whether that should change how we value the second discovery. For instance, Leibniz’s differential notation spread more widely than Newton’s fluxions, and Darwin’s long, evidence-laden argument may have had different persuasive effects than Wallace’s shorter, more abstract presentation. The proposal should state the precise research question; explain why answers focused on inevitability or priority fail to settle it; propose a comparative method that examines formulation differences without requiring access to rare archives or specialized citation databases; describe what a useful result would look like and what would count as failure (for example, if formulation differences turn out to have no measurable effect on adoption or extension); and assess honestly whether the scope is realistic for a short independent project. Please do not include a bibliography, reading list, or citations—I will supply those myself. The tone should be formal, self-contained, and appropriate for an educated but non-specialist review panel.
