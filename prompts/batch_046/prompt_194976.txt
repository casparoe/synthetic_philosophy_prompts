As a novelist drafting a scene in which a post-revolutionary tribunal debates whether contracts executed under the deposed regime remain enforceable, I have found myself returning to the Hart-Fuller dispute over legal positivism and natural law. After studying both sides, I am struck by how the debate appears to circle back upon itself for decades without resolution, despite shared premises about what legal systems do. My working theory is that the disagreement is not genuinely about the moral content of law but about what conditions must be satisfied for an institutional practice to count as a legal system at all—specifically, whether internal coherence among officials can substitute for minimal moral decency. Is this diagnosis correct, or is there a substantive conceptual or normative disagreement hiding beneath the terminology? I ask not for a survey of positions but for an analysis of the disagreement's structure: do the sides answer different questions under the single name "valid law"; is one side offering conditions for the existence of a system while the other offers standards for the appraisal of officials within it; and could any formal distinction—say, between the existence conditions of a legal system and the moral obligations of its participants—actually dissolve the impasse or merely relocate it? Finally, what would genuinely count as progress here: a clarified conceptual distinction, a disciplined historical case study, or should we conclude that the question is ill-posed?
