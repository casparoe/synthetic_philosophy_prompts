Write a poem of about fourteen lines in blank verse — unrhymed lines with roughly ten syllables and a steady, walking rhythm — with a clear turn near line eight or nine. Take up the familiar puzzle about doing harm versus allowing it, but ground it in a single intimate image: the decision to switch off a machine that is sustaining someone’s breath, compared with the earlier decision never to have turned it on at all. Give the poem two voices, one that acts and one that waits, and let the turn be the moment when the listener realizes both voices describe the same empty room, only with different verbs. I want the grammatical shift from active language — pulling, flipping, stopping — to passive language — waiting, watching, letting — to do real structural work, so that the form itself shows how the moral grammar changes while the outcome does not. Keep the tone reflective, like someone talking a hard thought through aloud rather than delivering a lecture. No philosopher names, no citations, no reading lists — just the idea moving through the rhythm and the turn.
