I've been in this weird study-group loop about Neurath's picture-language project — ISOTYPE, with the little standarized icons meant to show factories, populations, trade flows, that kind of thing. One friend keeps saying it was conceptually doomed from the start: every pictogram needs a verbal legend or a conventional rule to fix whether it means "one factory" or "many," or whether the arrow means causation or just sequence, so the pictures are always parasitic on some natural-language syntax. The other friend says that's wrong — ISOTYPE failed for boring institutional reasons, funding and politics and people not learning the system — and points to airport signage that works across languages without anyone thinking in words. We've been stuck for months, same examples, same replies. My working guess is that we're not actually fighting about history or psychology, but about what "understanding" a representation requires: does grasping an image have to involve some propositional, language-like structure, or can recognition alone do the job? But I don't know if that's right. So I'm asking: is this disagreement verbal — are we just using "language" differently? Is it about hidden empirical claims about cognition that neither side can cite? Or is it a clash of bedrock intuitions about representation that no experiment could settle? And given your diagnosis, is there any move — a formal analysis of pictorial syntax, a conceptual distinction about kinds of ambiguity, even just a sharper framing of the question — that could break the stall, or are we basically talking past each other?
