I am a retired experimental physicist who has begun studying philosophy of mind only in the last two years, and I have been invited to contribute to a philosophy-of-science seminar. I intend to defend the following interpretation: in Turing’s well-known 1950 essay on machine intelligence, his brief engagement with extrasensory perception is not merely a dismissive concession to popular objections, but reveals an implicit commitment to testing mental capacities through probabilistic comparison rather than through a binary, threshold-based criterion; consequently, the imitation game should be read as an open-ended comparative measure rather than as a decisive test for consciousness. The seminar audience includes two philosophers of mind who regard the canonical reading of Turing as settled, a historian of science familiar with mid-century British intellectual culture, a cognitive scientist who considers parapsychological references professionally disreputable, and several graduate students whose exposure to Turing is largely through secondary summaries. I need a preparation sheet, not a defense of the thesis itself: list the objections in the approximate order in which they will be raised, attributing each to the kind of interlocutor who will press it and phrasing it as they would; indicate for each whether it carries real weight, what I should concede immediately, and how I should reply in a brief seminar exchange; highlight the weakest point in my own position that I should prepare to defend rather than hope is overlooked; and conclude by stating how confident you are in this preparatory analysis, singling out specifically the point about which you are least sure—namely, whether the probabilistic reading of Turing’s remarks can be sustained without anachronistically importing conceptual apparatus he did not possess.
