Consider a proposed institutional design—call it “revocable expert delegation”—in which legislative seats are filled by stratified random sampling, but seated citizens may, on any given vote, delegate their decision authority to a randomly drawn expert from a certified technical pool; the delegation is binding for that vote only, is publicly recorded, and can be revoked instantly by the citizen. Evaluate how well the standard philosophical justifications for pure sortition apply to this modified system.

Rank the following five arguments according to how intact they remain under this design, assigning each a score from 1 (severely undermined) to 10 (fully intact): (A) the epistemic argument that random selection yields cognitively diverse deliberation superior to self-selected elites; (B) the democratic equality argument that every citizen possesses an equal claim to direct rule; (C) the anti-corruption argument that removing electoral campaigns eliminates patronage and financial capture; (D) the descriptive representation argument that a random sample mirrors the polity's demographic and experiential composition; (E) the civic transformation argument that serving in government educates citizens and builds democratic character.

For each score, provide a one-sentence rationale. Then state which of the five, if adopted as the sole foundational justification for the institution, would be most susceptible to the charge that revocable expert delegation reintroduces precisely the kind of hierarchical distinction between rulers and ruled that sortition was meant to dissolve.
