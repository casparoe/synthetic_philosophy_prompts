I am preparing a session for my philosophy of science graduate seminar on Kurt Gödel's argument that the objective passage of time is illusory, an argument he developed in connection with rotating-universe solutions to Einstein's field equations in which closed timelike curves appear. The students will have only an intuitive, non-mathematical grasp of special relativity and no training in differential geometry; my aim is not to turn them into physicists but to make the metaphysical conclusion depend with full transparency on the physical premises. I need a teaching plan rather than the explanation itself: the exact order in which the conceptual components must be introduced—beginning with the distinction between local time-order and global temporal orientation, moving through the idea that rotational symmetry can destroy a universal time-slicing, and only then arriving at the claim that if a global time coordinate fails in principle then temporal becoming lacks an objective substrate—so that each layer supports the next. I also need guidance on the single carrying example or analogical model that should bear the weight of the argument, together with an explicit account of which familiar pictures—such as the river-flow metaphor or the static block-universe visualization—must be excluded because they either presuppose what is at issue or misrepresent the rotational structure.

Specifically, I want to know the predictable misconceptions these students will bring or form in the course of the session—above all, the tendency to confuse Gödel's closed timelike curves with ordinary circular causation of the kind found in deterministic loops, and the further tendency to read his claim as a mere modal possibility about distant worlds rather than as an argument about the ontological structure of our actual universe given that rotating solutions are not empirically excluded. For each misconception, I need to know precisely at which point in the dependency sequence it should be intercepted, and what brief corrective move will dislodge it without derailing the flow. I also want one focused check-for-understanding question I can pose near the end that reveals whether a student sees why the failure of global hyperbolicity in the metric undermines the reality of temporal passage, rather than merely recalling that Gödel believed time was unreal. Finally, please specify what technical distinctions—such as local versus global causality, or the explicit form of the Gödel metric—should be named and briefly characterized but never derived, and what must be left out entirely so that the session remains philosophical rather than collapsing into a physics tutorial.
