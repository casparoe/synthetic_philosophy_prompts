Having spent my career in experimental physics and only recently turned to philosophy, I find that debates about moral luck map surprisingly well onto problems of measurement error and confounding variables in natural science. I would like you to design a decision-tree taxonomy that classifies philosophical positions on moral luck according to the answers they give to a sequence of concrete, case-based questions—never according to the labels philosophers prefer. Begin with a single diagnostic scenario: two agents perform the identical negligent act (driving while distracted), but only one causes harm because an unpredictable event—say, a child stepping into the road at precisely the wrong instant—intervenes for one and not the other. From this root, construct branch nodes that ask: first, whether this outcome difference should alter our moral assessment at all; second, if so, whether the relevant luck is circumstantial, constitutive (shaping the agent’s character over time), causal (stemming from deterministic background conditions outside agency), or resultant (affecting post-action consequences that feed back into blame); third, whether the proper philosophical response is to correct our evaluative practices analogously to calibrating an instrument for noise, to reject moral responsibility altogether as a spurious measurement, or to accept luck as an irreducible feature of moral life comparable to ecological variability in field data. Each terminal leaf should identify an actual named position where one is recognized—for instance, a strict Kantian immunity-to-luck view, the paradoxical acceptance associated with Nagel, a Williams-style resistance to corrective purity, a hard determinist elimination of responsibility, or a revisionist “calibrationist” position that treats moral evaluation as correctable for stochastic interference—while marking any logical combination that appears unoccupied and briefly diagnosing why it might be empty (incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored). Every leaf must carry a one-sentence statement of that position’s most awkward commitment. The tree must be answerable by someone who does not already know the philosophical nomenclature: each branch should be phrased as a claim about the case rather than as a doctrine name. Explicitly flag any pair of named positions that your tree cannot genuinely separate. Please do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citations, and do not invoke highly specialized theories outside standard philosophical discourse; the goal is a classification instrument that sorts people by what they actually believe about luck and blame, not by what they call themselves.
