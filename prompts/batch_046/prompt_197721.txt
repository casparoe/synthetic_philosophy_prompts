Here is an argument that one of my students constructed, which I find suspicious because it seems to prove far too much, yet each step looks defensible in isolation. The student is especially interested in what she calls a normative proxy belief — a belief about what is morally required whose justification is indexed not to the believer's own grasp of the supporting reasons but to the epistemic authority of some other source, whether another person or one's own past self. The argument runs as follows. (1) If moral testimony is a genuine source of moral knowledge, then a listener L can come to know that action φ is required solely on speaker S's say-so, without L herself being able to cite the reasons that support φ-ing. (2) But moral knowledge, unlike empirical knowledge, contains an essential first-personal condition: to know that I ought to φ, I must be able to provide the supporting reasons in my own voice; lacking this, I do not have moral knowledge but only a normative proxy belief directed at S's authority. (3) Therefore moral testimony never yields moral knowledge; at best it produces normative proxy beliefs. (4) Now apply the same reasoning intrapersonally: if I form an intention today solely on the basis of a resolution I made yesterday, without presently rehearsing or grasping the underlying reasons, then my present practical commitment is also a normative proxy belief — this time directed at my past self. (5) But rational agency requires that I be able to rely on prior resolutions without perpetual re-derivation; otherwise practical deliberation never terminates and action becomes impossible. (6) So normative proxy beliefs must be permissible in the intrapersonal case. (7) If they are permissible when indexed to one's own past authority, there is no principled barrier to permitting them when indexed to another person's authority. (8) Hence moral testimony is fully legitimate, and there is no special epistemic problem about acquiring moral beliefs from others. (9) Yet if there is no special problem, then there is also no competence filter: I could come to know highly complex moral obligations — say, obligations in bioethical contexts far outside my expertise — simply by treating a casual acquaintance's word as a normative proxy. (10) That result is clearly absurd, so something in the chain must fail.
