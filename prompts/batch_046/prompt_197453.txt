Here is the speaker and her thesis.

The speaker is defending a view she calls domain-weighted critical-range totalism. On this view, well-being is not a single scalar but comprises at least three irreducible dimensions: autonomy, hedonic quality, and relational meaning. A life contributes positively to the value of a population only if it meets a domain-specific critical threshold in at least two of these dimensions. Adding a very large number of lives that barely clear a single-dimensional pleasure threshold—but remain below the autonomy and meaning thresholds—therefore does not increase overall value. This blocks the repugnant conclusion without abandoning totalist aggregation, without introducing sharp discontinuities in value, and without collapsing into an average-utilitarian framework.

She will present this at an interdisciplinary workshop on population ethics hosted by a philosophy department with close ties to development economics. The audience is precisely composed of:

- Three analytic moral philosophers who defend either average-utilitarian or standard critical-level-utilitarian positions. They are skeptical of multidimensional well-being metrics and worry that dimensional weighting is ad hoc.
- Two welfare economists who apply population ethics to climate and development policy. They care about operationalization: can the view be measured, compared across contexts, and embedded in cost-benefit analysis?
- One decision theorist who is skeptical of interpersonal utility comparison and will press on whether the domain thresholds disguise ordinal rankings as cardinal ones.
- Several graduate students well-versed in the canonical literature on the repugnant conclusion and person-affecting restrictions, but unfamiliar with recent formal work on multidimensional welfare.

The speaker needs a prep sheet, not a full defense. Order the objections she will actually face—by how likely they are and how damaging—phrased in the vocabulary each audience faction would use. For each objection, provide:
