In my graduate seminar on Maimonides' Guide for the Perplexed, the same deadlock has persisted for years over animal sacrifice in Part III, particularly chapter 32 and its relation to the replacement of sacrifice by prayer. One camp, the concessionists, reads Maimonides as treating sacrifice as a purely pedagogical accommodation: the Israelites were immersed in Egyptian and surrounding animal cults, so God permitted a redirected, restricted form to wean them away, but the practice has no intrinsic metaphysical or religious value. The other camp, the integrationists, argues that this misses the cosmological architecture of the Guide. On their view, sacrifice is the ritual subordination of animal vitality—especially the nutritive soul associated with blood—to human intellectual worship, and it therefore enacts the hierarchical ascent from matter to form that structures Maimonides' entire metaphysics. Both sides cite the same passages, invoke the same hermeneutic principle that Maimonides writes for the perplexed with deliberately hidden depths, and appeal to the same distinction between the general purpose of the Law and its particular commandments. No one has moved.

My working hypothesis is that the stall conceals a normative-theological disagreement about whether divine wisdom can be purely instrumental. If sacrifice is only a historical weaning device, then it is in principle revocable once the idolatrous context disappears—which seems consistent with Maimonides' claim that prayer replaced sacrifice in exile—whereas if it enacts a timeless metaphysical structure, its binding force is permanent regardless of historical change. But I want you to examine a more specific structural possibility: that the two sides are answering different questions under the single heading Why did God command sacrifice?—one asking for historical-genetic explanation, another for normative justification, and a hidden third asking for metaphysical symbolism—without recognizing that these can come apart. In particular, consider whether the hidden disagreement concerns the modal status of sacrificial law: is the commandment revocable in principle because its justification is contingent on a past human condition, or is it permanently binding because the metaphysical relation it enacts between animal life and human intellect is timeless?
