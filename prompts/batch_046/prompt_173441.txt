A colleague in my philosophy of physics reading group described an extended disagreement between two other members about Gödel’s rotating universe and its bearing on the reality of time. The first participant argued that the model demonstrates objective time is not real: because the metric contains closed timelike curves, an observer could in principle travel into her own past, which means there is no consistent global ordering of events and therefore no genuine “flow” of time to be real. The second participant objected that this misreads Gödel’s philosophical purpose. She noted that Gödel was a mathematical Platonist who believed in an objective passage of time, and that he constructed the rotating solution not to eliminate time but to show that Einstein’s field equations permit geometries lacking a global time coordinate—thereby proving that general relativity does not by itself entail either the reality or the unreality of time. The first replied that regardless of Gödel’s personal beliefs, the logical structure of the model undermines objective time: if events can stand in both “before” and “after” relations to themselves, temporal ordering becomes contradictory and must be abandoned. The second countered that this confuses global coordinate time with local experienced time; in the rotating universe every observer still ages along her worldline with a well-defined proper time, and the closed curves arise only at cosmological scales that may be physically unattainable. The first then raised an empirical objection: modern cosmological observations—particularly the isotropy of the cosmic microwave background and the observed expansion of the universe—rule out the rigid rotation and non-expanding conditions required by Gödel’s metric, so the model is physically irrelevant and cannot support any metaphysical conclusion about time in our world. The second responded that Gödel’s argument was always conceptual rather than empirical; the point was that if the best physical theory of gravitation allows a universe without objective global time, then one cannot claim physics has vindicated the reality of time. She added that some interpreters take Gödel to hold that the absence of a global time slice in his model actually supports his view that physical time is an illusion and that true time is non-physical, though she admitted this reading is contested. The first concluded that both sides were treating a physically impossible toy model as if it had metaphysical authority, and that the real lesson is that philosophical conclusions should not be drawn from exact solutions that violate observed boundary conditions.

Please adjudicate this exchange point by point. For each round, say which party is right about the specific claim made, what both are missing, and whether any move depends on misrepresenting Gödel’s actual position or the structure of his solution. In particular: does the existence of closed timelike curves entail that objective temporal ordering is contradictory? Does Gödel’s personal Platonism about time conflict with or support his cosmological argument? Is the empirical irrelevance of the rotating universe a fatal objection to its philosophical use, or does conceptual possibility remain significant? And do either of them correctly capture what Gödel intended to show about Einstein’s theory and the nature of time? Finally, identify anything both parties overlook—such as the distinction between local proper time and global simultaneity, the specific conditions on the cosmological constant and rotation rate in the metric, or the difference between showing that a theory permits X and showing that X is actual.
