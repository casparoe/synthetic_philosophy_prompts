Consider the family of problems raised by Galen Strawson’s Basic Argument: the claim that true moral responsibility is impossible because any agent’s mental nature—character, values, cognitive dispositions—is the product of heredity, environment, and prior causes outside her control, so that being responsible for an action requires being responsible for the self that performed it, generating either an infinite regress or an impossible demand for self-creation (causa sui). Some philosophers respond by trying to halt this regress analogously to how epistemic foundationalists halt the regress of justification with basic beliefs: they propose basic actions, sourcehood conditions, or non-derivative ownership of mental states that need no prior responsibility to ground them. Meanwhile, others argue that Strawson’s conclusion, if sound, threatens only retributive desert and leaves intact forward-looking practices of accountability, moral education, and social coordination.

1. The structure of the regress itself: is Strawson’s infinite regress genuinely vicious, or can it be halted by a coherent notion of non-derivative sourcehood or basic mental ownership without collapsing into the very self-creation Strawson denies is possible?

2. The scope of the damage: does the argument undermine all forms of moral accountability, or only a specific kind of basic-desert retributivism?
