For a philosophy seminar I am preparing a brief adjudication of a dispute that arose in my study group, and I want to treat both sides fairly. Two of my classmates—call them A and B—were discussing Nelson Goodman’s problem of induction after we read about the predicate grue. A proposed a digital-age variant: call an image-file blure just in case it renders blue before 1 January 2030 and green thereafter, regardless of its actual chromatic content. A argued that, prior to the cutoff, observing thousands of blure files gives us exactly the same inductive support for expecting future blure files as observing blue files gives for expecting future blue files; therefore, if we reject blure as non-projectible, we must be smuggling in an arbitrary temporal bias. B responded that blure is syntactically and conceptually parasitic on blue and green in a way that makes it disqualified, but when A pressed her to name the precise feature—simplicity, causal connection, or entrenchment—she conceded that each criterion either applies circularly or fails to distinguish blure from legitimate compound predicates. B then shifted ground: she said that projectibility is not a purely descriptive feature of predicates but is partly constituted by our successful inductive practices, so the fact that we have entrenched blue and not blure is not an arbitrary convention but a reflection of which distinctions have actually guided us successfully. A countered that this makes induction a matter of linguistic sociology rather than a rational guide to the future; if projectibility depends on what we happen to have practiced, then there is no objective fact about whether tomorrow’s files will be blure or blue, only a fact about our habits. B replied that this is not sociological reductionism but a recognition that the norms of induction and the structure of the world are not separable in the way A assumes. They have asked me to adjudicate. Please go through the exchange move by move: rate A’s initial analogy, B’s parasitism objection, B’s shift to practice-constitution, and A’s charge of sociological reductionism, from clearly mistaken to clearly right, with brief justification for each rating. Then say whether B’s final reply actually answers A’s charge or changes the subject, and identify anything both are missing—particularly regarding whether Goodman’s original puzzle was meant to show that induction needs a non-arbitrary criterion of projectibility, or rather that no such criterion can be purely formal.
