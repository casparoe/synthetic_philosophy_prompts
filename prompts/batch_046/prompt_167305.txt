Consider this argument I constructed for a seminar on intertemporal rationality. (1) Pure time preference is rational only if the mere future tense of an experience is, by itself, a reason to assign it lower weight. (2) Temporal location, like spatial location, is not an intrinsic feature of the state of affairs valued. (3) So if temporal separation justifies reducing the weight of one's own future welfare, spatial separation should justify reducing the weight of another person's present welfare by an analogous factor. (4) But applying distance-based discounting across persons yields an indefensible moral solipsism in which distant suffering counts for nothing. (5) Therefore pure time preference must be rejected as irrational. (6) The same analogical move applies diachronically: the gap between my present self and my future self is a temporal separation like any other. (7) Consequently I have no special reason to privilege my future interests over those of a contemporaneous stranger. (8) Yet if present and future interests are weighted equally with strangers' interests, long-term planning, promise-keeping, and saving collapse, since present costs are never offset by uniquely privileged future benefits. (9) Thus abandoning (1) seems to undermine practical rationality itself, not merely intertemporal bias. Which step in this chain should be rejected, and what does giving it up cost? I would prefer to keep (5) intact if possible, but I want to see whether the damage can be localized. Please respond as a numbered list of exactly five items, each one or two sentences long, with no introduction or conclusion.
