The examination question read: "The demarcation problem asks how science differs from pseudoscience. Karl Popper proposed falsifiability as the criterion, yet critics invoke the Duhem-Quine problem to argue that any theory can be shielded from refutation by revising auxiliary assumptions. Assess whether this shielding renders falsificationism indistinguishable from conventionalism, and explain whether Popper's own methodological rules prevent such shielding without abandoning empirical rigor."

The student submitted the following response:

"Popper's solution to the demarcation problem is falsifiability: a statement is scientific only if it can be contradicted by some conceivable observation. This replaced the logical empiricist program of verificationism, which Popper showed to be self-undermining because universal claims can never be conclusively verified. Nevertheless, the Duhem-Quine thesis demonstrates that theories never face the tribunal of experience alone; they are always accompanied by auxiliary hypotheses about instrumentation, initial conditions, and background assumptions. When an anomaly arises, scientists may preserve the core theory by altering one of these auxiliaries. Critics contend that this maneuver makes falsificationism collapse into conventionalism, because conventionalists also protect central beliefs by adjusting peripheral ones. Popper anticipated this objection by allowing modifications to the protective belt of auxiliary hypotheses, provided those modifications themselves yield new, risky predictions. In this respect, Popper's framework incorporates conventionalist flexibility within an empiricist structure. Therefore, falsificationism avoids collapsing into conventionalism: science is defined not by immunity from revision, but by the willingness to expose the revised system to further severe tests. Pseudoscience, by contrast, alters its protective belt in an ad hoc manner that reduces rather than increases empirical content."
