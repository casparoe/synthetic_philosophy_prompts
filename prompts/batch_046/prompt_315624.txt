I am a software engineer with no formal background in philosophy, urban planning law, or economics, but I have been contributing to an open-source platform that models zoning variance applications and aggregates neighborhood feedback for planning boards. The more I work on the data pipeline, the more I realize the system sits on top of conceptual tensions that documentation does not resolve. There is a persistent conflict between local democratic control over land use—often characterized as NIMBY resistance to new development—and broader moral claims about a right to the city or regional obligations to accommodate housing growth. I am less interested in the politics themselves than in the conceptual architecture: which open questions in this domain actually determine how a decision-support system ought to weigh objections, and which are philosophical dead ends that sound important but would not change any design choice?

To make this concrete, I am trying to prioritize among several lines of conceptual work that arise repeatedly in informal discussions with urbanists and policy staff. The first concerns the nature of zoning authority itself: is it best understood as a property right held by existing residents, a delegated public power subject to distributive fairness constraints, or a form of collective self-determination that can be exercised legitimately even when it produces exclusionary outcomes? The second concerns whether any restructuring of zoning rules can constitute a safe Pareto improvement—one that increases regional housing supply without making any existing homeowner strictly worse off in terms of neighborhood character or property value—and whether the apparent absence of such improvements undermines the moral urgency of reform or merely reflects a failure of imagination. The third concerns the boundary between legitimate democratic persuasion—such as neighborhood associations articulating genuine local knowledge about infrastructure limits—and manipulation through strategic framing of risk, particularly as algorithmic mediation begins to filter which objections reach a planning board and in what form.

I am not asking for a bibliography, a reading list, or citations to specific scholars; I need a defended agenda. If you were advising a small interdisciplinary team—engineers, planners, and perhaps one ethicist—on which two or three conceptual problems to settle first, how would you rank them? For each, defend the ordering by explaining why it is upstream of the others, what kind of tractability it offers for people without deep backgrounds in political philosophy, and what conceptual or practical evidence over the next several years would cause you to reorder the list. Additionally, which common framings—such as treating exclusionary zoning as unjust enrichment, treating NIMBYism as a collective-action failure, or treating local control as an inalienable democratic right—do you regard as overrated in the sense that resolving them would not materially alter how a zoning-decision support system ought to be designed?
