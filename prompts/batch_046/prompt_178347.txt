I'm trying to get my philosophical commitments about reduction, emergence, and explanation sorted before I draft a longer paper, and I want an audit of whether this package hangs together or whether I'm smuggling in incompatible motivations. Here is my current position:

(1) Ontologically, I think chemistry and biology are just complicated physics: every causal interaction in a cell, reaction, or organism is ultimately an interaction among fundamental constituents, and there is no distinctively "biological" causation that isn't also physical causation.

(2) At the same time, I think strong emergence is real but narrowly confined to collective quantum phenomena—certain superconducting states, highly entangled configurations—where the system has properties that literally none of its constituent parts possess, even counterfactually.

(3) I accept downward causation in evolutionary biology: selection operating at the organism level genuinely constrains which molecular mutations are preserved. I think this is compatible with the causal closure of physics because I hold an interventionist account of causation, not an energy-transfer account.

(4) I think consciousness is weakly emergent: it arises necessarily from certain informational architectures, but it adds no new causal powers to the physical dynamics. Phenomenal properties are real, but they are causally inert with respect to the system's behavior—they "ride along," so to speak.

(5) I believe reduction is purely an explanatory achievement, not an ontological claim. To reduce X to Y is just to show that Y's theoretical and causal resources can explain X; it doesn't entail that X is identical to, or nothing over and above, some arrangement of Ys.

(6) I also hold a scale-relative ontology: whether something counts as a part or as a whole depends on the explanatory scale adopted, so "reduction to parts" isn't a scale-independent relation.
