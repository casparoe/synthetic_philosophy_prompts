Over the past year I have watched my sister move through a prolonged crisis of religious belief that does not fit the usual narratives of rebellion or disappointment; she describes it instead as a kind of absence—sustained seeking, sustained openness, and no response. That experience has made the philosophical problem of divine hiddenness feel less like an abstract puzzle about theism and more like a phenomenological report that demands explanation. In the literature, however, the debate appears to have reached a peculiar standstill: defenders of hiddenness appeal to goods like human freedom, soul-making, or the value of epistemic distance, while critics—following Schellenberg’s basic structure—insist that a maximally loving being would not permit widespread, non-culpable nonbelief, and both sides have spent decades refining counterexamples without ever conceding the core intuition. Everyone seems to agree on the descriptive fact that many people sincerely seek and do not find, yet the normative inference from that fact to a conclusion about God’s existence or nature remains exactly where it was.

What frustrates me is that the arguments are well mapped—culpable versus inculpable ignorance, the distinction between belief and relational presence, the claim that not all goods are epistemic goods—yet the disagreement does not behave like a standard empirical dispute that could be settled by better data, nor like a purely formal disagreement that a theorem might dissolve. I find myself wondering whether the stall is really about theology at all, or whether it hides a deeper divergence about evidential standards: is the absence of widespread theistic belief a datum that requires explanation, or is religious cognition so cognitively heterogeneous that no uniform distribution of belief should be expected in the first place? I also suspect that the two sides may be answering different questions under a single heading—one about generic philosophical theism, another about specific revealed traditions that claim universal accessibility—while a further conceptual ambiguity lurks in whether “hiddenness” denotes an epistemic failure or a relational mode closer to apophatic theology than to missing evidence. Could the impasse be bedrock, a clash of intuitions about what love demands that no argument can bridge?

Given that framing, I want a diagnosis rather than a verdict. Is this disagreement substantive, verbal, or structurally misaligned—perhaps mixing semantics with cognitive science—such that the sides talk past one another? If it is substantive, is there a hidden empirical or formal question doing the work, maybe about the modal probability of belief under conditions of epistemic distance? And what, precisely, would count as progress: a conceptual distinction that separates accessibility from intimacy, a result about the distribution of religious experience across cultures, or an honest admission that the question is ill-posed because it treats divine love as if it were an epistemic service rather than a personal presence? I am less interested in another survey of positions than in understanding why intelligent people with the same premises remain immobile, and whether anything—an experiment, a distinction, or a change of question—could actually move them.
