Consider the following line of reasoning, which I have tried to formalise but now suspect proves far more than it should.

(1) Panpsychism holds that every fundamental physical entity bears some minimal phenomenal property.
(2) Where entities stand in the right causal-structural relations, there is no metaphysical obstacle to phenomenal bonding: their micro-experiences can integrate into a unified macro-experience.
(3) Phenomenal bonding, if it occurs, is transitive across any chain of physical interaction.
(4) Since physical interaction is globally connected—directly or through intermediaries—all fundamental entities belong to one continuous bonding chain.
(5) Consequently, panpsychism entails a single cosmic subject whose experience encompasses all others.
(6) Yet first-person evidence reveals sharp epistemic boundaries: I do not experience distant stellar processes, let alone another person's toothache.
(7) To preserve these boundaries, the panpsychist must deny that bonding ever occurs, but then there is no mechanism by which macro-consciousness could emerge from micro-consciousness.
(8) Hence panpsychism collapses either into universal cosmic consciousness, which contradicts observed phenomenal fragmentation, or into an inability to account for macro-subjects.

I am inclined to resist (5) or (6), yet I cannot locate the precise misstep—perhaps a hidden modal slide between "can bond" and "must bond" across arbitrary distances, or a confusion between causal connection and structural integration. Which premise should I reject, and what does rejecting it commit me to—say, to a form of phenomenal cancellation, or to mereological essentialism about conscious subjects?
