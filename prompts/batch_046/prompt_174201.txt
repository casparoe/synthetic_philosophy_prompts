As a novelist drafting a character who is both a psychiatrist and a reluctant patient, I have been trying to map a pattern I keep encountering in the literature and in clinical anecdotes, and I want to know whether it is already named. Here are two concrete instances.

First, a diagnostic category—call it a particular dissociative subtype—is introduced to capture a cluster of trauma-related symptoms. Patients begin to recognize themselves in the description, and their self-narratives gradually align with the criteria; this is the familiar dynamic Ian Hacking describes as a looping effect, where classification interacts with the classified. But then clinicians notice that the most extreme presentations occur among patients who have read the diagnostic literature and are either deliberately enacting or actively resisting the criteria. The manual is revised so that "awareness of one's own diagnostic profile" becomes a formal feature of the condition. Once that revision is in place, individuals who wish to avoid the label begin to conceal their knowledge of the criteria; clinicians then interpret that concealment as a more refined symptom—something like "meta-dissociation"—in which denial of awareness is itself pathognomonic. The category no longer tracks a pre-existing mental state so much as it tracks an adversarial game between classifier and classified, and the people caught in it are partly constituted by their attempts not to be constituted that way.

Second, in addiction studies, a new behavioral syndrome is defined around compulsive self-monitoring of consumption. Early adopters of the diagnosis start tracking their behavior with clinical precision. Researchers then observe that the tracking itself intensifies the behavior, so they add "hyper-reflexive monitoring" to the syndrome's definition. At that point, some subjects stop tracking in order to escape diagnosis, and their cessation is read as "covert monitoring"—a hidden form of the same syndrome. Again, the classification seems to have moved one loop past Hacking's model: it is not merely that people change to fit or resist the label, but that the label is rewritten to absorb both the fitting and the resistance, so that there is no outside to the category.

I am not asking for a bibliography or a reading list. I want to know: does this specific second-order, adversarial configuration—where a looping effect is recursively incorporated into the taxonomy so that escape attempts become diagnostic—have an established name of its own? How exactly does it differ from standard looping effects, from iatrogenesis, and from reactive measurement in the social sciences? Where are its edges: at what point does strategic resistance become indistinguishable from genuine symptomatology, and does that boundary collapse matter for the ontology of the category?

Please say what would change your mind—what specific argument or empirical finding would convince you that this is merely an unremarkable extension of Hacking's looping effects rather than a distinct phenomenon requiring its own conceptual carving?
