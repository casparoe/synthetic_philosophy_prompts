I am working through a conceptual problem at the intersection of evolutionary psychology and Daniel Dennett’s taxonomy of minds—specifically the Darwinian, Skinnerian, Popperian, and Gregorian categories introduced in Kinds of Minds. In an 1871 passage from The Descent of Man, Darwin observes that caution in animals is partly an inherited instinct and partly the result of individual experience, and he suggests that in human beings the awareness of a persistent instinct—whether innate or partly acquired—gives rise to the sense that one ought to obey it, even though disobedience remains possible. I am paraphrasing this rather than quoting it verbatim. What puzzles me is whether Darwin’s account of an inherited mental habit that can be consciously represented and either followed or overridden describes a creature that has entered Dennett’s Gregorian level—because it apparently possesses an internal representational tool (the normative framing of its own dispositions) that permits offline evaluation—or whether it remains, at best, Popperian, since the evaluation is still anchored to a single biologically fixed drive rather than to an open-ended, culturally transmitted system of reasons.

To sharpen the difficulty, I want to introduce a distinction not standard in the literature: call it the recursive inheritance constraint, by which I mean the demand that any adequate account of Gregorian cognition must explain how mental tools are transmitted in a way that is neither reducible to genetic selection (Darwinian) nor to individual operant conditioning (Skinnerian). Darwin’s text seems to straddle precisely this boundary, treating the instinct as both biologically inherited and experientially modified, yet leaving unclear whether the conscious representation of that instinct functions as a genuine cultural artifact or merely as an internalized biological signal. So I ask two things. First, help me parse the logic of the passage: is Darwin claiming that consciousness transforms an instinct into a reason, or only that it makes us aware of a reason we already possess biologically? Second, adjudicate whether Dennett’s taxonomy can accommodate this hybrid case without collapsing the distinction between Popperian and Gregorian minds.

Please separate, in your response, what is relatively settled in the interpretation of Dennett’s categories and in Darwin’s evolutionary psychology from what remains genuinely contested—especially regarding whether representational tools must be culturally transmitted to count as Gregorian, and whether biological inheritance can ever constitute a tool in Dennett’s sense. Do not present any one reading of the taxonomy as the consensus view if it is in fact disputed among philosophers of mind and evolutionary theorists.
