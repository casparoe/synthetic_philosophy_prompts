Consider an exam in political philosophy and welfare economics that asks students to compare two frameworks for interpersonal evaluation: (1) a cardinal interpersonally comparable utility index based on self-reported life satisfaction, and (2) a capability-based partial ordering that tracks achieved functionings rather than mental states.

The exam presents the following case. Citizen A lives in severe material deprivation but reports life satisfaction of 8/10; researchers attribute this to adaptive preference formation, in which expectations contract to match available options. Citizen B lives in abundant resource conditions but reports satisfaction of 6/10; researchers attribute this not to deprivation but to aspirational asymmetry, in which an individual's internal standards and reference group expand faster than achievements, producing dissatisfaction despite high objective functioning. The exam asks the student to address three points: (a) explain precisely why adaptive preference formation undermines the cardinal scale's validity for evaluating A; (b) explain why aspirational asymmetry does not similarly undermine the capability framework's assessment of B; and (c) identify one structural limitation of the partial ordering that prevents it from yielding a complete social ranking between A and B when they differ along a single functioning dimension.

Below is one student's answer.

"The cardinal index should be rejected for A because adaptive preference formation suppresses true welfare: A has lowered expectations, so the 8 overstates A's deprivation rather than concealing it. In other words, the mental-state metric is corrupted in the direction of false satisfaction. For B, aspirational asymmetry corrupts mental states in the opposite direction—B's 6 understates welfare because B expects too much—so the cardinal scale is unreliable for B as well. The capability framework escapes both problems by measuring achieved functionings directly, ignoring subjective reports entirely, which is why B's high functioning justifies prioritizing B despite the lower number. Turning to the structural limitation, Sen's partial ordering requires Nussbaum's fixed list of ten central capabilities to determine which dimensions matter; without adopting that list, the framework has no content. Therefore, if A and B differ only in a functioning outside that list—say, aesthetic appreciation—the ordering provides no guidance. This shows that the capability approach is incomplete without an objective list."
