After a long argument last night with a close friend about climate-policy risk assessment, I have been trying to isolate a reasoning pattern I kept encountering, and I am uncertain whether philosophy has already carved it out with the precision I need. The pattern works as follows. When evaluating a proposed regulation, an interlocutor examines ten distinct failure modes—supply-chain disruption, regulatory capture, technological lock-in, unintended substitution effects, and others of that kind. For each mode taken individually, they grant that its probability is low, perhaps three to five percent, and conclude that this particular mode is not a serious objection to the policy. They do this for all ten. Then, when I observe that the disjunction of these failures is quite likely—perhaps sixty percent or more—they agree, but they do not treat that collective likelihood as undermining any of the individual dismissals. They do not say that one of the specific probabilities must therefore be higher, nor do they identify which components should be prioritized for mitigation. Instead, they maintain both judgments simultaneously: each individual risk remains negligible, the aggregate risk is substantial, and yet the substantial aggregate provides no reason to act on any particular component. It is not quite the lottery paradox, because in that case the contradiction is explicit—believing that each ticket will lose and also that some ticket will win—whereas here the person endorses both the conjunction of low individual probabilities and the high disjunctive probability without experiencing any tension. It is also not quite the preface paradox, because there is no meta-belief about error in one's own reasoning; the aggregation is statistical rather than epistemic. Is there an established concept for this exact configuration—this non-feedthrough aggregation in which collective significance fails to redistribute back to the parts? If there is, how does it differ from the fallacy of division, from probability neglect, and from ordinary risk pooling? If there is not, can it be defined precisely, with boundary conditions that separate it from those neighboring errors, and what would be an appropriate term for it?
