After a long and somewhat heated argument last night with my closest friend—who is not an academic philosopher but has an unusually rigorous moral imagination—I realized I still do not understand the exact rule she runs when she judges whether a moral compromise is a legitimate “gain from trade” or merely corruption of principle. She thinks that value systems, like economies, can sometimes exchange goods they value differently: one side relaxes a constraint, the other secures a larger protection, and both are better off without either principle being hollowed out. She describes this descriptively as “moral arbitrage,” though she does not mean it cynically. I pressed her on five concrete scenarios, and she gave firm verdicts, which I have reconstructed below as faithfully as I can.

She approved the following. First, a strict Kantian joins a hospital ethics board that uses consequentialist reasoning for resource allocation, but only on the contractual condition that she retains unilateral veto over any decision that treats a person merely as a means. Second, an environmental activist accepts a research grant funded by a fossil-fuel corporation, with the explicit terms that every dollar is used to litigate against that same corporation’s other extraction projects and that she retains full publication rights. Third, a physician with conscientious objections to abortion works in a clinic that provides them, provided she never performs the procedure herself and uses her presence to expand non-directive counseling resources. She rejected these two. A free-speech absolutist takes a job inside a platform-moderation division that applies proportionality tests rather than categorical rules, even though her specific role is to maximize protected expressions; she said this was trading the principle for its shadow. And a pacifist researcher accepts a government grant for conflict-resolution studies housed administratively within the defense ministry, with her salary drawn directly from that budget, even though her publications are independent and her work is entirely non-violent; she called this accepting the poison with the medicine.

We never resolved the case that actually prompted our disagreement. A privacy advocate holds an absolute, identity-constituting commitment to data minimalism—she regards any non-consensual aggregation of personal information as a categorical wrong, not a negotiable preference. She has been offered the position of chief ethics officer at a large language-model company that trains on user data, with a contract granting her unilateral authority to halt any training run involving non-public personal data, and she has negotiated that thirty percent of her salary will be donated automatically to digital-rights litigation. The company’s core business continues regardless. Would my friend treat this as analogous to the Kantian board member or the environmental litigator—an internal position that redirects resources and blocks specific violations—or as closer to the pacifist with defense funding or the moderation engineer, where presence itself legitimizes the violating institution?
