I'm helping a regional conservation board revise its funding criteria, and we've noticed an asymmetry in how protection statutes operate. Under the framework they administer, recovery funding and legal safeguards are tied to formal taxonomic ranks—species, subspecies, or distinct population segments—listed by an authority that treats those ranks as discrete gates. Taxonomists regularly split one listed entity into several, or merge previously separate ones, and legal protection shifts with the revision, sometimes leaving a population unprotected overnight. Meanwhile, hybrid populations and so-called evolutionary significant units—genetically distinct lineages that fail the reproductive-isolation criterion—receive no standing unless they happen to fall inside an already-listed taxon. The board wants to understand whether this design is philosophically neutral or whether it quietly commits them to a specific view of biological reality.

What ontology of species, and what theory of conservation value, does this institutional practice presuppose? Reconstruct the implicit theory: does treating taxonomic rank as the gatekeeper for protection require that species are real, discrete natural kinds with objective boundaries, or is the practice equally compatible with pluralism, nominalism, or a purely pragmatic scheme? Distinguish what the practice strictly requires from what merely fits it well—for instance, does the exclusion of hybrids and evolutionary significant units reveal a commitment to reproductive isolation as the essential feature of a protected unit, or could a nominalist defend the same exclusions on tractability or administrative grounds alone? Identify where the design is philosophically underdetermined, so that rival theories of species might license roughly the same lists and exclusions. Finally, describe what a funding framework built on a phylogenetic species concept—splitting populations by genetic divergence rather than breeding barriers—or built solely on evolutionary-significant-unit status would have to change in terms of listing procedures, hybrid-zone management, and the allocation of recovery resources.
