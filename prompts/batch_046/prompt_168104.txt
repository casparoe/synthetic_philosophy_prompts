I am writing up my position for a philosophy of institutions seminar and want a consistency check before I present. My commitments are these: (1) Marriage is an institutional kind constituted primarily by social recognition practices and by a normative function—stabilizing long-term intimate care, economic interdependence, and household vulnerability—not by any biological or natural feature. (2) Still, marriage carries a diachronic identity condition: if recognition practices and legal structures were revised so radically that generative potential and lineage transmission became entirely external to its definition, the resulting institution would be a different kind—closer to a registered domestic partnership—whatever label was retained. (3) The state should recognize institutional forms only when they serve a distinctive public good that lighter contractual arrangements cannot replicate; for marriage, this good is the stabilization of care across life-course vulnerability and the reduction of uninsurable social risk. (4) Same-sex couples fully realize this care-stabilization and risk-reduction function, and excluding them protects no distinct public good. (5) At the same time, same-sex couples do not realize the generative-potential aspect in the direct, biologically integrated form that has historically anchored the diachronic identity mentioned in (2). (6) Therefore, including same-sex couples in marriage should not be read as altering the institution's core function, since the core was always the care-stabilization in (3)—even though I also want to maintain that the generative aspect was genuinely part of the historical identity condition in (2).

Audit this package for joint tenability. Which pairs are in genuine tension, which only appear to conflict, and what is the least costly revision for each real conflict? Rank the tensions by severity rather than treating them as equal. Specifically, I need to know: does (2) smuggle an essentialist constraint incompatible with the social-constitution claim in (1)? Does the combination of (5) and (6) commit me to saying that same-sex marriage transforms the institution after all, undermining (4)'s claim that exclusion serves no purpose? And is there a tension between (3) and (6) that dissolves once we separate the state's justification for recognition from the metaphysics of institutional identity? I am not signaling which commitment I am willing to drop; map out the costs of each revision. This should be a conceptual audit, not a literature survey—no citations or reading lists needed.
