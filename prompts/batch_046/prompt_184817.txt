I'm a journalist covering philosophy of mind and cognitive science, and I'm preparing a feature on substrate-independent consciousness that grew out of a symposium I attended last month. During a panel on whether mental properties depend on biological matter, two philosophers—I'll call them Dr. A and Dr. B—fell into a sustained argument about a deliberately modified version of the familiar "Einstein's brain copied into a book" scenario, the kind discussed in works like The Mind's I. In their version, Einstein's complete neural architecture is not digitized but printed: every synaptic weight and firing rule is encoded as cross-referenced text on pages of a vast, non-linear volume. An automated, sensor-coupled mechanism turns pages in response to environmental stimuli, so the causal sequence of brain-states unfolds discontinuously, in discrete jumps, across paper rather than neurons. Dr. A maintained that the system achieves genuine phenomenal consciousness because the functional and causal architecture is preserved exactly; she insisted that substrate—ink, paper, and mechanical page-turning versus carbon and ion channels—is conceptually irrelevant if the integrated information dynamics are isomorphic. Dr. B countered that consciousness requires continuous temporal binding within a unified substrate, and that the discrete gaps between page-turns break the phenomenal unity necessary for experience; he added that if Dr. A were correct, then any sufficiently complex library catalog with automated retrieval could also be conscious, which he treated as a reductio. Dr. A replied that temporal continuity is an empirical prejudice rather than a conceptual necessity, and that the catalog objection fails because catalogs lack the specific causal density and integrated structure of the brain-copy, but she conceded that random page-turning—breaking the causal sequence—would destroy consciousness. Dr. B then pressed her: if breaking the sequence destroys consciousness, then the consciousness must belong to the external page-turning mechanism controlling the book, not to the book itself. Neither yielded ground.

Please respond in four sections, in this order, with the approximate lengths indicated. First, "The dispute reconstructed" (about 100 words): briefly restate Dr. A's and Dr. B's core positions and the specific variant of the thought experiment they are debating. Second, "Adjudication point by point" (about 350 words): work through the exchange step by step—the claim that substrate is irrelevant, the temporal-binding objection, the library-catalog reductio and Dr. A's reply about causal density, and finally the random-page-turning counter together with Dr. B's claim that consciousness then resides in the mechanism. For each move, say who is right about the specific point, where the reasoning holds, and where it slips or assumes what it needs to prove. Third, "What both miss" (about 150 words): identify a shared assumption or conceptual gap that neither interlocutor addresses—particularly whether consciousness can be distributed across discontinuous media if the coupling is causally dense, or whether there is a hidden commitment to spatial contiguity that neither defends. Fourth, "Bottom line" (about 75 words): give a concise overall verdict on whether the sensor-coupled book could be conscious, with the single reason that does the most work. Keep the focus on the arguments as actually made rather than on a general survey of theories of consciousness.
