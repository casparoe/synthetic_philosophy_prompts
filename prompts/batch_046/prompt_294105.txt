I am preparing questions for a podcast interview with a scholar of early modern philosophy who has recently advanced a revisionist interpretation of Émilie du Châtelet's Discours sur le bonheur. Instead of reading the text as a conventional blend of Epicurean moderation and Stoic self-cultivation, she argues that du Châtelet offers an energetic theory of happiness: bonheur is not a stable mental state but a dynamic equilibrium of the soul's forces, modeled directly on the physics of vis viva that du Châtelet developed in her Institutions de physique. On this reading, happiness is maintained only through continuous intellectual expenditure—study and a form of love reconceived as a shared cognitive project—while withdrawal from public ambition is not a retreat into passivity but a strategic redirection of vital energy away from the dissipating forces of social competition. The scholar further claims that this interpretation resolves a tension in du Châtelet's biography: her own public scientific career is compatible with her doctrine because the energy she expends in physics is precisely the kind of structured, self-directed activity that produces bonheur, whereas the ambition she warns against is the unregulated pursuit of external validation.

Write me a sequence of eight to ten interview questions that press on the substance of this thesis rather than on biography or general philosophy of happiness. I want at least two questions that challenge the textual license for importing the concept of vis viva into moral psychology, anticipating her likely appeal to analogies between the soul's passions and physical forces in the Institutions; at least one that asks how this energetic model accommodates du Châtelet's explicit insistence that happiness requires liberating oneself from the domination of external opinion; at least one that probes whether the love-as-shared-cognitive-project reading can survive du Châtelet's warning that romantic attachment frequently destroys happiness; and at least one that asks what this interpretation implies for comparing du Châtelet with her contemporaries, such as Voltaire's pragmatic hedonism or the Marquise de Lambert's stoic rigor. For each major question, include a sharp follow-up keyed to the answer she is most likely to give—especially where she might retreat to the claim that du Châtelet uses physical metaphors without ontological commitment. End with a question asking what kind of evidence—textual, biographical, or conceptual—would actually count against her energetic reading, rather than merely showing that alternative interpretations remain available.
