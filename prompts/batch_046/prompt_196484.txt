I've been chewing over the discursive dilemma ever since I read about that jury example—where everyone agrees on the premises but splits on the conclusion, or vice-versa, and suddenly you've got a collective that contradicts itself. I'm putting together a weekend ideas piece, and I want to push a line that hasn't really been aired properly: that premise-based aggregation should be ditched not just because it can spit out logical inconsistencies, but because it smuggles in a false kind of epistemic authority. The group never actually believed the premises in any unified sense; treating them as binding commitments is a bit like saying a crowd has a single mind because they shouted similar words.

So here's the ask. Can you make the argument—properly, with the reasoning laid out—that we should move to a modified conclusion-based rule? Specifically, one where the collective adopts the majority conclusion only if it's backed by at least one internally consistent bundle of premises that gets a qualified supermajority—say, two-thirds—while treating those premises as heuristic scaffolding rather than binding votes. I think this sidesteps the List-Pettit impossibility without pretending the group is some kind of super-agent with direct acess to facts. It'd be brilliant if you could also tackle the obvious objection that this just hides the dilemma under a threshold, rather than actually solving it.

Oh, and I realise I'm probably muddling the formal details—I'm a journalist, not a logician—so don't worry about citing specific papers or giving me a bibliography. Just build the case in plain language, though I'd like it sharp enough that someone who knows social choice theory wouldn't roll their eyes. Cheers.
