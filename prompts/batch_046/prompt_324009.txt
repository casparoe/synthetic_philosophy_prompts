You are an expert in formal epistemology with experience translating Bayesian confirmation theory into operational guidance for working scientists and peer reviewers. Before answering, please take a moment to think carefully about where procedural rules must yield to irreducible judgment, and about the subtle difference between logical availability and practical accessibility of empirical findings.

I serve on an interdisciplinary review board, and I need a reusable, step-by-step decision procedure—something like a flowchart in words with explicit tests I can run in about fifteen minutes—to determine whether a well-known empirical result should be treated as genuine Bayesian confirmation for a new theoretical framework, or whether it falls under the problem of old evidence and therefore carries negligible weight. The procedure must be grounded in the actual logic of Bayesian updating: it should require me to articulate the prior plausibility of the new framework, to distinguish between a result being improbable under the null and the framework being probable given the result, and to separate evidence that was technically available during theory construction but never actually used in that construction from evidence that genuinely constrains the framework’s prior. It must also flag, at each stage, which steps are unavoidable judgment calls dressed up as procedure—such as estimating how surprising the result really is for competing rivals, or deciding whether an explanation is genuinely predictive rather than retrofitted. Please apply the procedure to two hypothetical review scenarios: first, a new cognitive architecture that precisely fits a classic, decades-old memory-decay curve that prior models failed to capture, where the authors claim strong confirmatory support; second, a recently proposed evolutionary framework that absorbs long-cited sexual-dimorphism measurements in primates but offers no novel quantitative prediction beyond redescribing them. For each case, walk through the steps, identify the unavoidable judgment call, and state clearly whether the procedure yields an answer the authors will dislike. Finally, include a brief note on a genuinely ambiguous boundary case—perhaps digitized archival measurements that were technically published but practically inaccessible to the theorists—where the old-evidence distinction breaks down and the procedure must explicitly defer to the reviewer’s discretion rather than forcing a binary verdict.
