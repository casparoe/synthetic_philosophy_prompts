I'm designing a session for a philosophy-of-science reading group of second-year graduate students who have completed coursework in metaphysics but have no training in physics beyond an introductory level. The session must cover the argument—associated with Rietdijk and Putnam—that the relativity of simultaneity in special relativity supports a block-universe ontology. Rather than giving me the explanation itself, lay out the teaching architecture. First, state the dependency chain: which conceptual commitments must be secured before the argument can proceed, and why acceptance of the absence of a privileged reference frame for temporal ordering is a load-bearing step rather than an incidental premise. Second, propose the one carrying example or analogy that should do the explanatory work for the session, specify what properties it must possess and what it must lack, and explain why the canonical Einstein train-with-lightning scenario should be set aside at this level. Third, map the predictable misconceptions in the order they will arise—especially the conflation of frame-relative simultaneity with psychological subjectivity, and the inference that presentism is empirically disconfirmed rather than conceptually undermined—indicating the precise point in the sequence where each should be intercepted. Fourth, design a single check or exercise that would distinguish genuine comprehension of the argument's logical structure from mere acquisition of the slogan that time is like space. Fifth, identify the physical derivation that should be openly refused at this level, state exactly what should be said about taking it on authority, and note any related idea that should be deliberately left out because it depends on that missing derivation. The audience will have no access to external resources during our discussion.
