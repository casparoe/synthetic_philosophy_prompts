I'm taking an upper-level seminar on free will and moral responsibility, and during a discussion section two of my classmates had a sustained disagreement over Galen Strawson's Basic Argument. I am reporting it as a bystander and don't want to tip my own view, but I would like a point-by-point adjudication.

(1) The first classmate said that Strawson's Basic Argument is decisive: to be truly morally responsible for what one does, one must be responsible for the mental state—character, desire, or belief—that produces the action. But to be responsible for that mental state, one must have produced it through an earlier mental state for which one was responsible, and so on. Because an infinite regress of self-creation is impossible, no one ever satisfies the condition for true moral responsibility, and desert-based blame or praise is never genuinely warranted.

(2) The second classmate replied that this only works if one accepts an implausible "origination" condition. Source compatibilism, she said, does not require being a causa sui; it requires only that an action flow from one's own reason-responsive, value-endorsing psychological mechanisms. Those mechanisms may be shaped by genetics and environment, but if they are genuinely integrated and rationally revisable, then the actions that issue from them are properly attributable to the agent. The regress is therefore a red herring.

(3) The first came back that the second is conflating practical accountability with basic desert. Even if a mechanism is "yours" in some psychological sense, if you did not choose to possess that reflective capacity, then praising or blaming you for its outputs is analogous to praising a well-calibrated thermostat for maintaining temperature—both are functioning well according to a design they did not author. True desert requires that the ultimate source of the action lie in the agent in a way that is not itself the product of prior external causation.

(4) The second said that the thermostat analogy fails because reflective agents can revise their own mechanisms through reason-giving and narrative integration. But she added a stronger point: if the first classmate's standard of ultimate origination is applied consistently, it undermines not only moral agency but rational belief-formation itself, since every belief-forming mechanism also has a causal history outside the agent's control. The first is implicitly demanding a "God's-eye" standard that no domain of human evaluation could meet. What both are missing, she suggested, is that autonomy might be better understood not as a point of origin but as an ongoing, structurally embedded capacity for self-modification—what she called a "diachronic structural autonomy"—which allows responsibility to be scalar rather than all-or-nothing.

Please adjudicate move by move: rate each of the four moves from clearly mistaken to clearly right and briefly justify each rating. Say specifically who is right about which point—the nature of the regress, the distinction between desert and accountability, the thermostat analogy, or the consistency challenge regarding rational belief-formation—and identify what, if anything, both parties are missing. In particular, address whether a middle position involving narrative or structural self-formation actually resolves the tension, or whether it simply relocates the problem that Strawson raises. Do not provide a bibliography or reading list; I want an assessment of the arguments as actually made.
