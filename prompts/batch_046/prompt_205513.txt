In my advanced ethics seminar this term, we have been working through Kant's treatment of veracity, particularly his response to the well-known case in which a murderer asks where an innocent person is hiding. I have come to defend a somewhat specific interpretation of his position, and I want to understand its vulnerabilities rather than its strengths. The view I hold is that Kant's absolute prohibition on lying remains fully intact even in the murderer-at-the-door scenario, not because consequences are irrelevant in some crude formalist sense, but because the moral agent retains options—strategic silence, misleading emphasis, or physical obstruction—that avoid false assertion entirely. On this reading, the categorical imperative never requires one to utter a falsehood to save a life, because the perfect duty of veracity governs only what one asserts, not what one withholds or how one arranges one's body in space. I take this to be more philosophically robust than the common caricature of Kant as indifferent to human life, but I recognize that it depends heavily on the tenability of a sharp distinction between commission and omission in communicative acts.

What I want from you is an honest map of this position's exposure. Which arguments, conceptual distinctions, or empirical observations about human communication would genuinely threaten it? I am not asking for the standard criticism that Kant is implausibly rigid—rather, I want to know whether speech-act theory undermines the claim that silence can be genuinely non-assertive, whether Kant's own taxonomy of perfect and imperfect duties supports or complicates this reading, and whether there exists a constructed case—perhaps involving compelled testimony, institutional roles, or digital communication—where the omission-commission boundary dissolves. For each potential defeater, please indicate whether a committed defender of this view would be forced to concede damage, or whether they could absorb it through further qualification, and rank these from fatal to merely awkward. I am especially interested in whether this interpretation risks collapsing into a rival position, such as a form of rule-utilitarianism disguised in Kantian language, and what evidence would show that collapse has occurred.
