After a three-hour argument last night with a friend over whether William James's defense of belief-formation under uncertainty can ever be reconciled with W.K. Clifford's evidentialist ethics, we realized we keep talking past each other because we never pinned down exactly what we agree on, where our formulations genuinely diverge, and what evidence would actually change either of our minds. Rather than revisiting James's familiar religious cases or Clifford's shipowner parable, we agreed to apply the dispute to a concrete, constructed scenario: a multidisciplinary scientific council must take a public stance—either endorsing an accelerated pilot program or a precautionary moratorium—on the release of gene-edited organisms designed to suppress an invasive species, in a situation where long-term ecological interactions remain unmodeled, the political decision window is narrow, and both action and inaction entail irreversible moral and environmental risks. One of us takes a Jamesian position adapted to institutional belief: when an option is genuinely forced, living, and momentous, and when evidence is structurally underdetermined rather than merely temporarily incomplete, the council may legitimately adopt the belief (and the corresponding policy posture) that best coheres with its broader practical and moral commitments, provided the commitment remains revisable in principle. The other takes a Cliffordian position: the council violates an epistemic duty owed to all affected parties—present and future—if it endorses either pathway on less than proportionate evidence; practical urgency may justify provisional action taken under explicit uncertainty, but it does not license the formation of belief that exceeds what the data warrant.

We want you to draft the joint adversarial-collaboration statement that both of us could sign. Structure it as follows.

First, a numbered section of agreed claims covering: (i) the nature of the council's decision situation—specifically that it is a forced option with living, momentous stakes, an irreducible evidential gap, and irreversible consequences for both paths; (ii) the distinction, if any, between forming a belief and undertaking provisional action; (iii) what James's and Clifford's core positions actually commit them to when applied to collective institutional agents rather than individual believers; and (iv) any shared normative premises about epistemic responsibility, transparency, and revisability.

Second, a disagreements table that states each side's position in wording that its own proponent would accept—precisely the conditions under which belief-formation is permissible beyond strict evidential proportion, versus the precise conditions under which only provisional, non-belief action is permitted, with each formulation phrased so that the opposing side can recognize it as the view they reject rather than a caricature.

Third, identify the cruxes—the sub-questions such that resolving them would move at least one side. We suspect at least two: whether practical urgency can ever alter the evidential threshold for belief as distinct from action, and whether institutional belief-commitments are genuinely analogous to individual doxastic states. For each crux, specify a concrete hypothetical case or empirical finding that both sides agree would bear on it, and record in advance what each side would predict about that case or finding.

Finally, indicate whether any part of our disagreement is merely verbal—about how to use terms like belief, evidence, forced option, or provisional action—and if so, which part and why.

The document should be formal, precise, and balanced: neither side should be rendered as a strawman, the agreed claims should not be so vague that they hide the dispute, and no bibliographies or external citations should be included, since the point is to see whether the positions can be made exact without appeals to authority.
