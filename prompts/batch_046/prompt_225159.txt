I have been teaching Aquinas's account of tyrannicide for some years—specifically his distinction between a ruler who seizes power without right and one who governs unjustly despite a legitimate title, together with the cluster of conditions he requires before removal can be permissible, among them that the act proceed from public authority and not generate greater disorder than the tyranny itself. In both seminar discussions and in recent commentary I keep noticing the same argumentative pattern. A discussant isolates one condition—typically the usurpation criterion—and treats its satisfaction as virtually decisive for licensing resistance, even when the remaining conditions are plainly unfulfilled. When I observe that no legitimate public authority has sanctioned the act, the response is that private citizens may intervene in extremis; when I point out that the likely aftermath is widespread chaos, the reply is that the present chaos already exceeds what the tyranny produces. Each missing condition is defended separately as an individually reasonable exception, so that the cluster, which Aquinas presents as jointly restrictive, operates in practice like an à la carte menu. The result is that the theory is never decisively falsified, yet its practical constraints erode one by one. Is there an established term—perhaps in moral theology, philosophy of law, or the study of argumentation—for this specific pattern: the gradual decomposition of a conjunctive normative license into independently defensible, individually reasonable exemptions? How precisely does it differ from an ordinary auxiliary hypothesis or from ad hoc rescue? And if no precise name exists, what should we call it, and what are its boundaries—does it occur only with explicitly enumerated necessary conditions, or also when the conditions are embedded implicitly within a single concept?
