Please evaluate the fairness of the following summary, which I reconstructed from a recent popular essay in the philosophy of biology. The essay asks whether DNA should be understood as a literal code that carries semantic information about organismal form.

The author begins by noting that the genetic code maps sixty-four codons to twenty amino acids in a manner that is largely chemically arbitrary: there is no law of physics that forces UUU to specify phenylalanine rather than leucine. From this arbitrariness, she infers that DNA operates as a genuine symbol system with representational content—specifically, that nucleotide sequences carry instructions that are about future phenotypic states, not merely causal templates that produce them. She further claims that this representational status explains why mutations can properly be characterized as errors in a message, rather than as neutral physical alterations, and she suggests that evolutionary biology ought therefore to be reconceived as the study of information transmission, editing, and fidelity maintenance across generations. Finally, she contends that critics who reject the code metaphor do so mainly out of a reductionist impulse: they identify DNA with its chemical substrate and deny that any biological process involves representation, thereby failing to see that representational properties can be physically instantiated without being reducible to chemistry.

I have two specific reservations. First, I wonder whether the inference from chemical arbitrariness to full semantic representation is valid, or whether it conflates the syntactic structure of a mapping rule with genuine aboutness or intentional content. Second, the characterization of critics seems to treat all opposition as substrate-reductionism, which may overlook positions that accept physical instantiation of information but deny that DNA itself—rather than the broader cellular or developmental context—bears the representational burden.
