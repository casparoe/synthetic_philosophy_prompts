Design a 90-minute seminar activity for sixteen upper-level undergraduates in a philosophy of rationality course. The session should make the backward induction paradox in the centipede game feel like a lived strategic tension rather than an abstract argument, using a modified structure that introduces a second-order move not found in standard presentations.

The core game uses a visible cooperative payoff ladder: each round both players choose to continue or stop, with mutual continuation increasing a shared point pool according to a simple linear schedule; if one stops, that player takes the larger share and the other takes a smaller share, with the exact split worsening for the cooperator as the ladder climbs. Crucially, the total number of rounds is hidden: the facilitator holds a deck of cards marking rounds 1 through N (with N unknown to players), turning over one card after each mutual-continuation decision to reveal whether the ladder has reached its hidden terminus. Each player also holds a single private token. At any point, a player may play their token and declare that induction demands defection now. Doing so immediately ends cooperative play and triggers a justification vote: the other player, without seeing the hidden deck state, must vote whether the token-play was rationally compelled or strategically premature. The vote determines the token-player's final bonus or penalty, applied to their accumulated points.

Use only symbolic points—printed cards or a whiteboard tally—no real money. Begin with a silent two-minute rules discovery: distribute the payoff cards and token descriptions but do not explain backward induction or name the paradox. Let participants play for approximately thirty minutes, then pause for a brief reflection writing (three minutes, silent) before the debrief.

The debrief should do four things. First, map the pull of the cooperative ladder onto the intuitive case for continuing. Second, map the token-play onto the backward-induction argument—especially the moment when invoking the reasoning feels like a strategic move within the game rather than an external observation about it. Third, map the hidden deck and the vote onto the common-knowledge conditions that make induction valid: show that the activity deliberately withholds perfect transparency, which is precisely what allows cooperation to survive in play but also what prevents the logical inevitability of round-one defection from being fully experienced. Fourth, be explicit about the feature the activity cannot reproduce: with perfect common knowledge of rationality and a fully transparent game tree, defection at round one is logically unavoidable, and no hidden-state mechanism can stage that inevitability without destroying the game. Note that the facilitator must supply this final logical closure verbally. Also predict two failure modes: (a) anxiety-driven token play in round one, which collapses the ladder before tension builds—counter this by making round-one token play yield the worst possible vote outcome; and (b) token hoarding until the hidden deck is accidentally revealed—counter this by allowing the facilitator to secretly reshuffle the deck if no token has been played by round four.

Do not open with a lecture, do not name the paradox until the debrief, and do not include bibliographies, reading lists, citations, or attributions to specific authors.
