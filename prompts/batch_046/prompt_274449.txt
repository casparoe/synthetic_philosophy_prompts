I've been digging into intentionality after a long thread on LessWrong about whether LLMs have beliefs, and I'm definately not an expert but I keep coming back to Dennett's two-bitser idea versus Searle's original-vs-derived distinction. From what I gather, Dennett thinks even a minimal system can have derived intentionality if we take the intentional stance toward it—like assigning beliefs to a thermostat—while Searle insists only biological brains have original intentionality and everything else is just observer-relative as-if aboutness that we project onto it.

But then I cooked up this wierd hypothetical with a friend: imagine a minimal physical system, not a brain and not a standard computer, more like a self-organizing chemical network that maintains internal states tracking external variables only when they disturb its equilibrium. It has no survival drive, no consciousness, but its states are functionally coupled to the world. Now here's the twist—imagine it can also enter a decoupled mode where it runs internal dynamics representing variables that aren't currently affecting it, kind of like counterfactual tracking, but without any goal or use for the information. Would Dennett's two-bitser framework classify this coupled-then-decoupled tracker as derived intentionality, or does the decoupled mode push it into something closer to original? And for Searle, is this still purely observer-relative, or does the physical coupling plus internal decoupling create a problem for his sharp biological cutoff? I'm less interested in who 'wins' than in exactly where the two views diverge on this synthetic equilibrium-tracker, especially whether Dennett's stance-based approach can distinguish coupled tracking from decoupled simulation without smuggling in biological criteria.
