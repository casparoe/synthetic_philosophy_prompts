I'm currently taking a graduate seminar on philosophy of artificial intelligence and serve on my university's Committee on Algorithmic Governance. For Thursday's meeting I must circulate a formal written opinion on a proposal to deploy an inverse-reinforcement-learning system that infers a reward function—described by the researchers as representing students' latent academic preferences—from anonymized behavioral traces: library entry logs, learning-management-system clickstreams, and course-registration histories. The inferred preferences would then drive automated personalized advising recommendations. The research team argues that explicit surveys suffer from social-desirability bias and that revealed preference through behavior is therefore more honest; the opposition replies that within preregistration constraints, seat caps, tuition incentives, and prerequisite chains, observed behavior largely tracks constraint accommodation rather than authentic preference, and that treating these traces as ground-truth risks what our seminar discussion called institutional preference circularity—naturalizing patterns that past institutional nudges helped create as if they were student desire independent of the bureaucracy. Please draft the committee's memo: recommend approval with strict conditions, rejection, or approval with conditions, specifying concrete criteria the researchers could actually respond to and a clear time frame for compliance review. The reasoning should treat the strongest version of the opposing view fairly—especially the claim that behavioral inference in heavily structured choice architectures can lock contingent institutional limits into an apparently objective welfare function—without relying on bibliographies, named theoretical authorities, or citations that a model without web access could not verify.
