In Avicenna’s “flying man” (al-insan al-ta’ir) thought experiment, a fully formed human subject is suspended in air, blindfolded, with limbs spread apart, having never used the senses or received any bodily impressions. In your answer, distinguish (i) the precise content of the suspended subject’s self-affirmation, (ii) whether the argument demonstrates only an epistemic independence of self-knowledge from sensory mediation or also an ontological separation of substances, and (iii) whether Avicenna treats this self-knowledge as presentational—direct, non-representational presence to oneself (huduri)—rather than as representational—mediated by an internal mental image or concept (husuli). Student answer:
The flying man argument asks us to imagine a person suspended in air with absolutely no sensory contact, not even with his own body. Even in this isolated state, he affirms that he exists. This is essentially the same philosophical move as Descartes’s cogito ergo sum: both begin from immediate, indubitable self-certainty and use it to establish the existence of a thinking substance. For Avicenna, this proves that the rational soul is a res cogitans whose essence is pure thought, entirely ontologically separate from bodily extension (res extensa). Because the suspended man lacks all sensation of his limbs and torso, he correctly concludes that he possesses no body at all; the absence of perceptual evidence for the body demonstrates its absence from the soul’s essence. The argument therefore establishes full Cartesian dualism well before Descartes. Furthermore, the flying man shows that self-knowledge operates representationally: the soul forms an internal mental representation of itself, analogous to how the senses represent external objects. By isolating the soul from all bodily mediation, Avicenna demonstrates that the potential intellect can function as a purely immaterial substance, confirming that the soul’s independence is not merely epistemic but a complete substantial separation.
