You serve on the advisory board of a university interdisciplinary initiative in planetary history. The program director has proposed a framing document for a required graduate module and a public lecture series, both titled “Non-Human Actors: Germs, Climate, and Geological Force.” The proposal argues that treating pathogens, climate anomalies, and volcanic events as “historical actors” corrects anthropocentric bias, but it also warns against “environmental determinism”—the error of explaining outcomes such as famine, migration, or state collapse solely through natural forces, thereby obscuring colonial extraction, labor regimes, and political choice. A minority of board members objects that attributing “agency” to viruses or eruptions is conceptually incoherent: agency requires intentionality or at least goal-directedness that these entities lack, so the framing collapses causation into agency and invites precisely the determinism it claims to avoid. The director responds by distinguishing “agential protagonism” (treating non-human forces as narrative protagonists without mental states) from “causal conditioning” (treating them as background constraints), and asks the board to endorse this distinction as the module’s organizing principle.

Draft the board’s decision memo—approve the framing, reject it, or approve it with conditions—containing a concrete recommendation, a timeline for implementation or revision, and reasoning that takes both the director’s distinction and the minority’s conceptual objection seriously. The memo should not request bibliographies, citations, or external reading; it should stand on conceptual argument alone.
