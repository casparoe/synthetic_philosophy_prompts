Write a short research proposal—approximately one page—for a three-month independent fellowship that addresses the following conceptual problem. Take seriously Fernand Braudel’s tripartite schema of historical time: the slow rhythms of geography and climate (the longue durée), the cyclical or structural rhythms of societies and economies, and the rapid, event-driven time of individuals and politics. Apply this schema as a philosophical test case to the emergence of the mechanical clock in fourteenth-century Europe. The precise tension to investigate is whether the clock belongs to the shallowest temporal layer—as a punctual, biography-driven invention attributable to particular artisans—or whether its appearance was structurally enabled, and perhaps structurally overdetermined, by deeper long-term shifts in monastic labor discipline, urban market coordination, and the climatic stability of the late medieval period that made precise, standardized time-measurement socially necessary long before it was technically achievable.

Your proposal should include: (1) a sharply stated research question that asks not for historical data but for a conceptual distinction—specifically, how to differentiate “structurally enabled invention” (where deep rhythms open a possibility space that individuals then navigate) from “structurally determined invention” (where the surface event is essentially an epiphenomenon of deeper forces); (2) an argument for why existing Braudelian scholarship and philosophy of technology have not settled this distinction, particularly in cases where an artifact seems simultaneously to depend on long-term institutional needs and on specific technical ingenuity; (3) a methodological justification for treating the clock as a philosophical object rather than as an empirical historical case study—explain why conceptual clarification of causation across temporal scales can make progress where additional archival research on clockmakers cannot; (4) a concrete deliverable, such as a framework specifying the conditions under which an invention should be assigned to one temporal layer versus distributed across them, together with clear criteria for failure (for example, if the framework collapses into pure technological determinism on one side or pure heroic individualism on the other); and (5) a brief justification of why this framing matters for current work in the history of science, where the clock is often treated either as a pure engineering problem or as a pure social construct, and why a philosophical intervention is warranted despite the absence of new empirical discoveries.

The proposal should not include a bibliography, a literature review organized by citation, or requests for specific archival sources. You may refer to Braudel’s framework and to the general historical context of European clockmaking in broad terms. The scope should be realistic for three months of conceptual work, and the tone should be appropriate for a foundation that funds philosophy of science and historical ontology but does not fund empirical historiography.
