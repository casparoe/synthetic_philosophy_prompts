I have been working out my own view on whether mental episodes are genuinely experienced at the moment they seem to occur, or are instead constructed retrospectively when they are reported or recalled. The question seems especially sharp for sleep, but I think the same structure applies elsewhere. Here are my verdicts on concrete cases.

1. A sleeper in REM reports a complex narrative upon waking. Verdict: there was no experience of that narrative during sleep; the narrative was assembled in the transition to wakefulness from fragmentary cues.

2. A person under a psychedelic reports seeing geometric patterns. Verdict: they genuinely experienced those patterns at the time.

3. A patient who is blind due to cortical damage denies blindness and offers elaborate descriptions of the room. Verdict: they are not visually experiencing anything; the descriptions are confabulated without experiential substrate.

4. A trained lucid dreamer sends pre-arranged eye signals from REM indicating awareness of flying. Verdict: there is a minimal concurrent conscious state, but it is not an experience of flying comparable to waking flight; the specific narrative is still largely reconstructed upon awakening.

5. A hypothetical neuro-implant can induce a specific false report upon waking—say, of a red circle—with no REM activity, no subjective transition, and no prior mental content. The subject insists they dreamed it. Verdict: there was never any experience, not even a retrospectively constructed one in good faith; only an implanted belief.

6. A waking subject in prolonged sensory deprivation reports vivid, involuntary imagery. Verdict: these images were genuinely experienced during the deprivation, despite lacking an external cause and being involuntary.

7. A person wakes from non-REM sleep reporting only a diffuse emotional residue—I felt anxious—with no narrative or imagery. Verdict: I am unsure; it might be a genuine affective experience that carried over, or it might be a waking mood misattributed backward.

The tensions I feel: Cases 1 and 4 both occur in REM and produce reports, yet I grant Case 4 a minimal concurrent awareness that I deny to Case 1. Cases 1 and 6 both involve involuntary mental content without external cause, yet I treat Case 6 as genuinely experienced and Case 1 as not. Cases 3 and 5 both involve reports detached from experience, but in Case 3 I treat it as deficit-driven confabulation, while in Case 5 I treat it as pure implantation—though both seem to lack any underlying experiential event.

Is there a single principle—perhaps involving the presence of a concurrent global workspace, or the possibility of first-person authority at the time, or the distinction between narrative and non-narrative content—that captures all seven verdicts? If so, what does it predict for a case I have not listed: a subject who, immediately upon being startled awake from REM, reports only a single static image (a blue triangle) with high confidence, but whose brain recordings show no corresponding activation pattern in visual cortex? And if no single principle works, which of my verdicts—especially the split between Case 1 and Case 4, or between Case 1 and Case 6—should be abandoned for the sake of coherence, and what does the resulting simpler principle say about Case 7?
