I've been turning over in my mind the old problem about whether there's a real moral difference between killing someone and letting them die, especially after caring for a relative where doctors kept talking about "withdrawing treatment" versus "not initiating it" as if the physical motions involved were completely different worlds. I'm not trained in philosophy — I work in retail management and I run a casual discussion circle at my local library, about a dozen people, mostly neighbors, who meet for seventy-five minutes on Thursday evenings. I'd like you to design a structured in-room simulation, almost like a board game with human stakes, that makes us feel the force of that distinction before anyone puts a name to it.

The setting is a single session, no technology, just paper cards, a kitchen timer, and tokens we can trade for tea and cookies afterward. I want the rules arranged so that participants physically perform actions — sliding cards, releasing levers, passing tokens — where the exact same bodily motion is framed in one round as an active intervention that causes death and in another round as a passive failure to prevent it, with only the description of the "prior state" changed. The payoff should be social or token-based, not real money, but designed so that people genuinely want to avoid being the one who "does" the harm, and then genuinely feel conflicted when they realize they've already done the equivalent motion in the other framing. By the end, the group should have experienced that our intuition tracks something like whether we see ourselves as initiating a causal chain or withdrawing from an existing one, rather than any observable difference in movement. To keep this from feeling like a replay of the standard trolley-problem setup everyone has seen, please build in an unconventional twist: frame the "life" in the game as something that requires continuous hidden labor to maintain, so that "doing nothing" is actually the active cessation of support, and let the physical mechanics make that hidden labor visible — for instance, through a relay of hands holding up a card that must stay suspended.

Please lay out the materials, the rules round by round, the token economy, and a debrief that maps each moment onto the philosophical idea. Also tell me what will predictably fail the first time — for example, someone refusing to play the second framing, or the group arguing that the framing is cheating — and be frank about which feature of the doing/allowing distinction the game simply cannot capture without me explaining it afterward, such as its legal status or its application over long time scales. Do not ask for citations, bibliographies, or further reading; I need something I can print and run next Thursday without anyone leaving the room to look something up.
