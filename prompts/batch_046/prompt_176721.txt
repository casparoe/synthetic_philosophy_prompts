I'm drafting an essay for a philosophy of value workshop, and I want structural and conceptual feedback—not references or a bibliography—on the following argument.

The essay examines lexical-threshold negative utilitarianism (the view that suffering has lexical priority over pleasure up to some welfare baseline, but not infinite weight) and asks what unstated meta-ethical commitments about the commensurability of pleasure and pain it requires. I propose that this view implicitly relies on what I call "threshold pluralism about affective value": below the lexical boundary, pleasure and pain are incommensurable in the sense that no quantity of pleasure can offset severe suffering, whereas above the boundary they become interpersonally comparable on a cardinal scale, allowing standard aggregation and trade-offs.

The essay proceeds in three stages. First, I distinguish lexical-threshold NU from both absolute negative utilitarianism and from weighted NU that uses a steep but continuous discount factor. I argue that the lexical version requires a genuine discontinuity in the value scale: if the metric were fully continuous, the boundary would be an arbitrary cutoff rather than a normative hinge. Second, I examine whether this discontinuity is defensible on independent grounds. One possible defense is that the boundary marks a qualitative shift in the kind of value at stake—from basic welfare or dignity to preference satisfaction—rather than a quantitative jump in a single homogeneous currency. I consider whether this defense saves the view from the charge of ad hoc stipulation, or whether it merely renames the discontinuity. Third, I apply the framework to the well-known "small risk of catastrophic suffering" problem. If suffering is lexically dominant only when it crosses the threshold, then a policy carrying a tiny probability of catastrophic harm might still be justified by near-certain moderate benefits, provided the catastrophic harm is not guaranteed to push every affected individual past the boundary. But if the threshold is set low enough to catch catastrophic suffering reliably for all affected parties, the view collapses back toward absolute NU; if set high, it loses its distinctive protective force against severe harm.

Please comment specifically on: (1) whether threshold pluralism is internally coherent, or whether it inevitably collapses into either full incommensurability (which would block aggregation entirely) or full cardinal commensurability (which would erase the lexical priority); (2) whether the appeal to a qualitative shift—from basic welfare to preference satisfaction—can genuinely ground a discontinuity in the value scale without begging the question against continuous weighted NU; (3) whether the analysis of the "small risk" problem correctly captures the dynamics of lexical priority, and whether relocating the arbitrariness to the boundary itself is a theoretical cost or an acceptable design feature. I am particularly interested in whether hidden tensions arise when aggregating across individuals who sit on different sides of the lexical boundary—say, when a policy produces moderate gains for many people well above the boundary while imposing severe but sub-catastrophic suffering on a few people just below it.
