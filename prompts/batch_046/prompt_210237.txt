I am a high school student preparing a fifteen-minute presentation for an interscholastic philosophy symposium. My thesis is that Daniel Dennett's notion of counterfeit people—entities that exhibit the behavioral and rational marks of personhood without the underlying intentional architecture—applies to contemporary large language models, but only if we revise the framework to accommodate what I call distributed emergent counterfeiting. On Dennett's classic account, a counterfeit person results from an intentional agent who presents something as possessing personhood when it lacks the requisite internal structure. I argue that LLMs are counterfeits not because any individual engineer designed them to deceive observers about personhood, but because their person-like outputs emerge statistically from the compressed aggregation of human linguistic behavior in training data. Consequently, they lack original intentionality and phenomenal consciousness, so they are not persons in Dennett's full sense; yet they also fail to fit the individual-deception model that makes counterfeiting morally uncomplicated. I conclude that treating them as mere tools ignores a distinctive structural feature of their creation, and that any moral evaluation should turn on the social and institutional roles they come to occupy rather than on a simple counterfeit-versus-genuine binary.
