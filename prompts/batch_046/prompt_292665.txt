I am taking a course on early modern political philosophy and have been working through Pascal’s discussion of justice and coercion. I am trying to determine whether my own judgments about legitimate force form a coherent pattern, or whether they collapse into contradiction.

My verdicts are these. A state that uses physical punishment to enforce genuinely just laws—forbidding murder, protecting property—is acting legitimately. A state that uses identical punishment to enforce arbitrary laws—banning harmless religious practice—is tyrannical. A revolutionary movement may justly employ lethal force to overthrow a regime that systematically violates rights, even if innocent lives are lost. But the same movement may not justly employ lethal force to overthrow a regime that is merely corrupt or inefficient. A community that relies solely on non-coercive social sanctions to maintain order is admirable; yet if it fails to stop a serious wrongdoer, it has failed in justice. At the same time, a state that preserves order through constant surveillance and the perpetual threat of punishment, even with just laws, seems corrupted in its very method.

These judgments pull in opposing directions. Cases three and four suggest that the justice of the cause determines whether force is permitted, whereas cases one and two suggest that the justice of the specific law is what matters. Case five demands that justice possess coercive teeth, while case six implies that excessive coercion undermines justice itself.

Is there a single principle—perhaps one that corrects or sharpens Pascal’s own view—that accommodates all six verdicts? If coherence requires abandoning one, which should go, and why? Finally, what does the best principle predict for this unlisted case: a democratic majority votes to impose coercive taxation and redistribution to remedy historical injustice, but the affected minority had no meaningful voice in the decision?
