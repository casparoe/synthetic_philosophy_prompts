I am a retired experimental chemist who, since leaving the laboratory, has turned to the philosophy of science and have been working carefully through Goodman's problem of projectibility. Next month I am moderating a colloquium interview with Dr. Elena Voss, a philosopher of science who has recently defended a modified Goodmanian position applied specifically to chemical taxonomy. On her view, the predicate "element" is historically entrenched in a way that blocks standard grue-type reversals for naturally occurring substances, but synthetic transuranic isotopes and artificially stabilized nuclear configurations open a vulnerability: she introduces the predicate "stabium"—defined as "stable with respect to decay until the year 2030, and decaying thereafter"—to show that taxonomic classifications in nuclear chemistry can be subjected to exactly the temporal asymmetry Goodman identified. Voss argues that the only adequate defense is a strict entrenchment criterion, though she modifies Goodman by allowing that entrenchment can be overturned when a new predicate participates in successful law-like predictions that unify previously disconnected domains of chemical behavior.

Please draft for me a sequence of eight to ten interview questions suitable for this setting. The questions should turn on the actual content of her position, not on biographical matters, and should press where the literature on induction and projectibility genuinely presses. I want at least one question that challenges whether her modified entrenchment criterion collapses into a pragmatic conventionalism about chemical taxonomy rather than solving the paradox; one that asks whether her criterion merely postpones the grue problem, since all scientific predicates appear eventually revisable in light of new instrumentation or synthesis techniques; and one that asks what specific empirical discovery—perhaps the synthesis of an element whose properties systematically violate the periodic trends used to entrench "element"—would actually count as evidence against her view rather than being accommodated by a predicate revision. For each of these three, include a follow-up keyed to the answer she is most likely to give. Write in a formal, polished register appropriate to a moderated philosophical colloquium, and avoid generic or introductory questions.
