I'm trying to identify a recurring pattern in philosophical debates about vagueness—especially those involving the sorites paradox—and I want to know whether it already carries an established name. Here is the structure as I keep seeing it. First, a speaker deploys a sorites sequence—adding one hair, one neuron, or one day of development—to argue that there is no sharp metaphysical boundary between being a person and not being a person, or between being conscious and not being conscious. They use this to reject any precise cutoff as arbitrary. Then, in the very same argumentative framework, they defend a practical, legal, or normative rule that relies on exactly such a cutoff—treating viability at a specific gestational week, or membership in a collective at a specific population threshold, as the point where full moral protections apply. They defend this rule not merely as a useful convention, but as tracking something genuinely real about the cases, without reconciling that stance with their earlier claim that no such point exists. The move is not simple hypocrisy, because they may concede the cutoff is conventional when pressed directly; yet they continue to reason with it as if it bore metaphysical weight. I have noticed versions of this in discussions of personhood, consciousness, and even heap-like properties applied to collective entities. Is there an established concept for this specific asymmetry—using sorites-style reasoning to dissolve boundaries in the abstract, while preserving and reifying an unstated or conventional boundary in practical judgment? If there is no standard label, please coin one, define its precise boundary conditions, and distinguish it carefully from ordinary contextualism, pragmatic tolerance, and straightforward equivocation. In particular, explain what makes it a distinctive failure mode in reasoning about vagueness rather than just a practical compromise with theoretical skepticism. Please write your answer in American English.
