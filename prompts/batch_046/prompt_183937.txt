Hey, I retired from a biophysics lab last year and have been trying to read philosophy for the first time, mostly Hofstadter and Dennett's The Mind's I. That dialogue about Einstein's brain in a jar and whether a book could be conscious really stuck with me, so I made up some variations and tried to decide which ones feel conscious and which don't, but my answers are all over the place.

Here are my gut calls. Einstein's actual brain kept alive in a vat with artificial input: yes, still conscious. Replace every neuron one by one with silicon chips that do the same job: yes, I think so, though it feels a bit spooky. Now write every synaptic weight and firing rule into a massive printed book and put it on a shelf: definatly not conscious, it's just ink. But build a machine that reads that book and updates valves and lights in real time to reproduce the brain's dynamics: yes, that feels conscious, even though the "brain" is a book being read. Then there's the weird middle case: a grad student in a room with the same book, manually updating a whiteboard every millisecond according to the rules. The dynamics are reproduced in real time, but there's a person in the loop doing the work. I want to say it's still conscious, but that feels wrong because the student is the one actually thinking. Finally, take a perfect computer simulation of the brain, save the code to a hard drive, and turn the server off: not conscious.

So I have yes for the vat, yes for silicon, no for static book, yes for book-plus-machine, unsure/no for student-in-loop, no for dead code. Is there a principle that captures all of these? Or am I just being inconsistent? I keep thinking it's about "real-time causal integration without an outside mind mediating it," but I'm not sure if that saves the student case or kills it. Can someone lay out a precise principle — maybe more than one candidate — that explains each clause, says what it predicts for cases I haven't mentioned, and is honest about which of my verdicts has to go? I'd like to know if I'm closer to being right about the machine case or the student case.
