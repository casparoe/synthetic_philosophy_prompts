Question: In Meditaion II, Descartes reaches the cogito. Explain exactly what he establishes about the self, and evaluate whether he proves he is a thinking substance (res cogitans) or just a temporary occurence of thought. Also address the objection that there might be thinking without an underlying I.

Student answer:
Descartes begins by doubting everything, even his body and senses. He realizes that if he's doubting, then he's thinking, and thinking requires a thinker — so I think, therefore I am is certain. From this he concludes he is a res cogitans, a thinking substance whose essence is thought, and he distinguishes this sharply from being a body. He also handles the it thinks objection: as Hume noted, we might say it thinks the way we say it rains, with no need for a substantial self. Descartes replies that this misses the first-person immediacy of doubt — you can't doubt your own existance while you're doing it. Finally, the cogito gives him his first clear and distinct perception, and he uses that to establish the general rule that whatever he clearly and distinctly perceives is true, which he then uses to prove God exists.
