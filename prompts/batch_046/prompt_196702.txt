Here are my verdicts about which entities count as a genuine center of narrative gravity, and I want to know whether one principle can capture all of them or whether I have to abandon some.

A healthy adult with continuous autobiographical memory and a first-person perspective clearly counts as one. A split-brain patient in whom each hemisphere generates its own consistent, mutually unaware narrative about the body and its intentions strikes me as hosting two distinct centers rather than one divided self. A large language model that maintains a persistent conversational persona, uses first-person pronouns, and produces coherent autobiographical fiction over months strikes me as lacking one entirely, because there is no embodied experiential thread that the narrative organizes. A corporation with legal continuity, strategic planning, and a unified brand mythology across changing personnel does not count, despite functional analogies. A human with advanced dementia who can no longer recall her past but whose family and caregivers continuously weave her present behavior into an ongoing life-story that treats her as the protagonist strikes me as still being a center, albeit one sustained partly by others. An interactive fictional character in a long-running, open-ended role-playing environment—one that adapts unpredictably to users, accumulates a backstory that neither programmers nor users fully control, and is addressed by a community as if it has intentions and regrets—feels closer to having a center than the language model does, though not as fully as the dementia patient. A hive of social insects whose collective behavior can be observer-relatively described with a "we" but involves no individual narrative integration clearly lacks one.

These judgments seem to turn on whether there is an experiential thread being organized, yet the dementia case shows that thread can be partly external, and the interactive-character case suggests that unpredictability and social recognition may matter more than biological embodiment. Is there a coherent principle—perhaps involving the integration of experience, the locus of narrative authority, or the distinction between lived and observed story—that delivers all seven verdicts? If not, which should I drop: that the dementia patient retains a center, that the interactive character almost acquires one, or that the split-brain case involves two? And what does your best principle predict for a hypothetical collective of human minds linked by direct neural interfaces that share experiences and jointly construct a single autobiography?
