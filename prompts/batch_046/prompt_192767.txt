I'm working on a feature about whether a nation can be guilty across generations — you know, Jaspers' four categories, criminal, political, moral and metaphyiscal — and I want to close with a poem that actually does some thinking rather than just decorating the page. Could you write a sestina for me? Thirty-nine lines, six stanzas of six lines each plus a three-line envoi. The six end-words need to be: nation, archive, judge, silence, inherit, other. Here's the concept I'm stuck on: political and moral guilt can be shared and inherited, but metaphysical guilt — the kind Jaspers tied to an individual's relation to transcendence — is indexical, it needs a singular 'I'. When people try to pluralise it, it doesn't become collective guilt; it turns into a kind of distributed shame that nobody actually holds. I want the form to track that breakdown. In the first three stanzas the grammar should stay comfortable with collective subjects, but by the fourth stanza the end-words should start feeling like they're in the wrong sentences — 'nation' trying to 'judge' itself, 'inherit' clashing with 'other' — and the envoi should land on the idea that inheritance stops being moral the moment it stops being personal. Make it accurate enough that someone who's read Jaspers' post-war writing on guilt wouldn't wince, but don't quote him directly; my editor won't run block quotes in a poem. No bibliography or reading list, please — just the poem. Oh, and write it like you're talking to a colleague over coffee, not like an academic programme note.
