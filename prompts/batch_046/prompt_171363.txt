A colleague serving with me on a philosophy-of-mind review board has been ruling, case by case, on whether an explanatory gap between a complete lower-level description and a mental phenomenon marks a genuine metaphysical shortfall or only an epistemic limit. Her verdicts to date:

— Between neurophysiology and phenomenal colour experience: metaphysical.
— Between neurophysiology and the acquisition of belief: epistemic.
— Between distributed cortical dynamics and the unity of a single conscious field: metaphysical.
— Between algorithmic computation and strategic understanding: epistemic.
— Between neurochemical mechanisms and the functional role of pain-avoidance: epistemic.
— Between synaptic transmission and the phenomenal feeling of fear: metaphysical.

She has not yet addressed the meta-problem itself. The new case is as follows: suppose we possess a comprehensive predictive-processing model that captures every reportable feature of introspection and self-reference, leaving only the bare judgment that 'there is something it is like' to be the subject of this model — a judgment we experience not as a missing detail but as a persistent sense that the account is incomplete. Would she treat the gap between this full structural-functional description and our residual intuition of an unexplained remainder as metaphysical or as epistemic?

Please predict her verdict, articulate the two principal rules that could consistently generate her earlier rulings, indicate what each rule predicts for this new case, state which rule you take her to be employing and why, and identify one further case that would conclusively distinguish the two rules. Reply in British English.
