I'm a senior serving as chair of my high school's Student Technology and Ethics Committee, which meets twice a month to review proposals involving automation, data use, or AI in school life—such as generative tools for official documents, algorithmic auditing of club budgets, or confidential reporting systems—and I need a reusable decision procedure I can run in about twenty minutes per case with other student members, none of whom have studied philosophy beyond an introductory course. The stakes are moderate but real: our verdicts go to the faculty advisor for sign-off, they affect whether a proposal moves forward, and we are expected to justify any rejection in writing, so the procedure must be rigorous enough to defend but simple enough that we do not misapply it under time pressure.

I want the procedure grounded in Kant's universalizability tests—specifically the Formula of Universal Law, with its distinction between contradiction in conception and contradiction in will, and the Formula of Humanity—but adapted for our setting because every proposal arrives not as a private action but as a collective authorization rule. To handle this, the procedure must include a preliminary translation step that formalizes the proposal as a "framework maxim" stating exactly what the rule permits, for whom, and under what conditions; then it must apply the universalizability test to that framework maxim by asking whether a rational agent could consistently will that every comparable institution adopted it; and finally it must run a "structural dignity check" that asks not merely whether one individual is used as a means, but whether the rule's design makes it systematically impossible for any affected party to pursue their own rational ends regardless of how they choose to respond. The deliverable should be an ordered checklist of specific questions, each with a criterion for a sufficient answer, explicit stop points where the answer is "reject" or "approve only with conditions," and clearly marked judgment nodes—especially where universalizability is satisfied but the dignity check raises a concern, or where the contradiction is only in will rather than conception—that must be handed to our faculty advisor rather than resolved by students. It should also be honest about where it will misfire, such as with proposals that are technically universalizable but impose severe burdens on a small minority, or with rules that depend on contingent technological capacities that might change.

Please construct this procedure with its steps and decision logic clearly labeled, then apply it to three proposals: first, allowing the yearbook staff to generate background images with an AI tool without labeling them, which I personally believe should be permitted and I want to see whether the procedure forces a rejection I will disagree with; second, requiring all student clubs to submit budgets to an automated auditor that flags "inefficient" line items for immediate faculty review without prior student explanation, which I suspect will be rejected but I want the procedure's explicit reasoning; and third, a peer-support program that promises confidentiality except when a student mentions self-harm, which sits in the hard middle where universalizability and dignity might pull in different directions. For each case, show the step-by-step run, note exactly where judgment is unavoidable, include the result that I will not like, and conclude by stating what the procedure definitively cannot do—for instance, resolving conflicts between universalizability and consequentialist concerns, or substituting for legal compliance review—so my committee understands its limits before we adopt it.
