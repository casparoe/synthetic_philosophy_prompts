The debate over whether biological functions are essentially etiological—fixed by a trait's evolutionary history of being selected for producing a particular effect—or causal-role—fixed by a trait's present contribution to the capacities of the containing system—has persisted for decades with remarkably little structural change. Each side rehearses the same counterexamples: hearts that pump blood but arose through genetic drift rather than selection for pumping; exapted structures that now serve one systemic capacity but were originally selected for another; or vestigial traits that retain causal efficacy but lack any selectional pedigree. Etiological accounts treat causal contribution without appropriate history as mere accident or malfunction, while causal-role accounts treat evolutionary history without present contribution as biologically irrelevant to current explanation. Yet the exchange rarely moves beyond these classifications, as if both sides agree on the cases but disagree on which label applies.

My working hypothesis is that this is not primarily a substantive disagreement about biological ontology but a masked disagreement about explanatory standards—specifically, whether the concept of function is meant to serve evolutionary-historical explanation or mechanistic-systemic explanation—compounded by the fact that both camps implicitly restrict the domain to naturally evolved organisms. The stall may therefore be verbal or, worse, ill-posed: the question "What is the function of X?" might be answered differently depending on whether one is asking why X is present in a lineage or what X currently does in an organism's physiology, with neither answer invalidating the other.

Diagnose the impasse using the actual commitments of each side: do they differ about facts (e.g., whether selection histories are generally knowable), about what counts as data (e.g., whether synthetic biological parts are legitimate test cases), about normative standards for explanation, or about bedrock intuitions regarding "proper" versus "accidental" contribution? In particular, consider whether the rise of synthetic biology—where engineered metabolic components perform unambiguous causal roles within pathways that have no evolutionary history whatsoever—exposes a hidden normative commitment in the etiological view, or whether it merely lies outside the intended scope of both theories. Is there a hidden empirical question about the limits of selectionist explanation that could resolve the dispute, or is the conflict dissolved once we distinguish function-ascription from functional explanation?

Finally, be honest about progress: would a formal distinction between explanatory kinds (historical versus mechanistic) end the debate or simply rename it? Could an empirical finding—say, about the prevalence of drift-derived traits with stable causal roles—force one side to concede? Or is the disagreement fundamentally about starting intuitions that neither side can argue for, meaning that no experiment or theorem should be expected to move it? Give reasons for your diagnosis, not a survey of the literature.
