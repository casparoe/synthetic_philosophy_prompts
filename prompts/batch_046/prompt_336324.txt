I'm a senior analyst at a regional environmental permitting office. Every quarter I review roughly twenty proposals for industrial expansions adjacent to protected wetlands. Before signing off, I can require an expensive hydrological survey—costing about $80,000 and delaying the decision by three weeks—or I can issue or deny the permit based on existing data. I need a reusable decision procedure, grounded in Good's theorem on the value of information, that tells me within a 48-hour window whether requiring that survey is rational. The procedure must be runnable repeatedly without a decision-theory background, but it must preserve the distinctions that actually matter: when information is genuinely free versus costly, when my prior beliefs and the survey's error structure allow a proper Bayesian update, and when the survey result could actually change my permitted action.

Please construct the procedure as an ordered sequence of questions or tests. For each step, specify what counts as a sufficient answer, where the procedure stops with a recommendation not to require the survey, and where it must hand the decision to human judgment because the issue involves institutional values, public accountability, or risk tolerance rather than information value. Include explicit checks for cases where the measurement model is too unreliable to support updating, where the regulatory action is identical across all possible survey outcomes, and where the cost is procedural—such as a statutory deadline—rather than monetary, breaking the standard value calculation.

Then apply the procedure step-by-step to three concrete proposals:

1. A factory expansion where the survey could reveal groundwater contamination risk that shifts the permit from approval to conditional denial.
2. A warehouse build where stakeholders demand transparency and the survey would provide publicly valuable data, but the regulatory action—approval with standard buffers—remains identical regardless of the result; the procedure should recommend against ordering the survey, and you should flag why that answer will be unwelcome.
3. A pipeline repair where the survey is technically feasible but the statutory decision clock expires before results could arrive, rendering the information useless for this decision.

Finally, state clearly what the procedure cannot do: which judgment calls it still smuggles in despite appearing mechanical, what errors it will systematically make, and how two analysts running identical steps might reach opposite conclusions. Identify the boundary conditions—such as commitment to future actions or non-Bayesian belief revision—where Good's conditions fail and the procedure should declare itself inapplicable rather than force an answer.
