I am working through a hypothetical casebook for a seminar on urban justice, and I have been tracking the rulings of the Metro Regional Land-Use Ethics Panel, a body that reviews major development proposals for consistency with what it calls the "right to the city"—though the panel has never defined that phrase precisely. Its track record is as follows. First, it rejected a luxury waterfront tower that would have displaced several hundred long-term renters through eminent domain, holding that physical removal from one's established neighborhood violates a protected interest in remaining, regardless of compensation. Second, it approved a subsidized housing cooperative on public land, but only on the condition that existing adjacent residents be given a binding vote on design elements and that all units be subject to a permanent resale restriction preventing market-rate transfer; it emphasized that procedural autonomy and anti-commodification must operate together. Third, it rejected a tech-campus expansion that promised thousands of local jobs but included no permanently affordable housing, reasoning that expanded economic access without residential security does not satisfy the right in question. Fourth, it approved a transit-adjacent "missing-middle" upzoning that granted current tenants a right of first refusal at below-market rates, explicitly framing this as protecting occupancy continuity while allowing density. Fifth, and somewhat surprisingly, it rejected a well-funded proposal to build public housing on a vacant industrial lot because the local neighborhood association had been excluded from the planning process, stating that procedural exclusion undermines the legitimacy of even redistributive outcomes.

The panel now faces a new proposal: a "vertical commons" high-rise on a former industrial waterfront site, where units would be allocated by a regional lottery weighted by length of residence anywhere in the metro area—not merely in the adjacent district—under a covenant that permanently bans market-rate resale and requires collective governance of shared spaces. The project requires demolishing an informal settlement of long-term squatters who hold no legal title but have maintained and improved the site for over two decades; the proposal offers immediate relocation assistance and temporary housing, but no guaranteed right to return, and the local neighborhood council was consulted only after the lottery mechanism had already been fixed. If the panel wants to remain consistent with itself, what should it decide—approval, rejection, or conditional approval—and which of the five precedents is the closest analog? I am less interested in the bare verdict than in the underlying rules that best fit its past judgments: for instance, whether it runs an anti-displacement principle that protects de facto occupancy regardless of legal title, a procedural-consent principle that requires meaningful local democratic input, an anti-commodification principle that privileges non-market tenure, or some hybrid. If the precedents genuinely underdetermine the outcome, what principle would the panel be adopting with each possible choice? Finally, what single further case—perhaps involving a climate-refugee settlement on privately owned greenbelt land with no prior residents—would best distinguish between the competing interpretations?
