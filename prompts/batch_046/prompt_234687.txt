As a journalist covering the philosophy of technology and moral psychology, I have been drafting a feature on whether reinforcement-learning systems can ever be proper targets of blame, and in working through the logic I keep returning to a version of the basic argument against moral responsibility—associated with Galen Strawson—that seems to swallow not merely individual human agency but every form of collective and artificial decision-making I can imagine. I want to check whether the chain is actually valid or whether I am smuggling in a false premise. Here is the argument exactly as I have mapped it.

(1) True moral responsibility for an action requires that the agent is the ultimate source of the psychological states—beliefs, values, decision weights—that produced it. (2) To be the ultimate source of those psychological states, the agent must have deliberately authored them through prior exercises of agency. (3) Any prior exercise of agency itself depends on psychological states that the agent must have authored, generating an infinite regress that no finite being—biological or synthetic—can complete. (4) Therefore, no finite being with a temporal psychological history can ever be the ultimate source of its actions. (5) Now extend this to synthetic agents: a large model's decision weights are authored not by the model but by its training process, which is itself a causal chain extending back to human designers, data curators, and environmental feedback loops, none of which are ultimate sources. (6) So synthetic agents are doubly excluded—they lack both self-authorship and a terminating source in their causal history. (7) The same applies to hybrid bodies, such as an AI-assisted judicial panel: every contributing psychological state traces back either to a human caught in the regress or to a synthetic agent caught in the causal chain of (5). (8) The conclusion is that responsibility is conceptually impossible for individuals, artificial systems, and every hybrid institution in between—which seems to prove far too much, since it would render incoherent not only criminal law but also the basic practice of holding anyone or anything accountable.

My instinct is that the flaw lies in (2)'s demand that authorship must take the form of deliberate choice, but I am unsure whether relaxing that condition saves human responsibility without also opening the door to attributing responsibility to purely automated systems. Could you identify the weakest step in this chain, explain why it initially looks unavoidable, and then price the alternatives—particularly what we must pay if we reject (2) versus if we reject (1)?
