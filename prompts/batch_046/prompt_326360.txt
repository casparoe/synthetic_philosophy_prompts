In my graduate seminar on the history of ethics and epistemology, we have been examining Henry Sidgwick's largely neglected involvement with the Society for Psychical Research, particularly his attempt to apply rigorous evidential standards to claims of telepathy and post-mortem survival. A recurring analogy in our discussions — one I have seen echoed in recent philosophy of science literature — compares Sidgwick's committee-based evaluation of mediumistic phenomena to modern institutional peer review: both assemble panels with opposing biases, enforce blinding protocols, and treat statistical rarity as a filter rather than an immediate disqualifier.

I would like you to audit this analogy systematically. First, provide an explicit correspondence table: what in Sidgwick's practice plays the role of the editor, the referee, the replication study, the publication threshold, and the retracted paper? Then identify the points where the mapping collapses, especially regarding the ontological commitments each institution is designed to protect and the kind of error — false positive versus false negative — each is calibrated to avoid. Next, inject a distinction that these comparisons usually suppress: Sidgwick's explicit separation of the evidential question (whether a phenomenon occurs) from the metaphysical question (whether it violates natural law), a separation modern peer review rarely makes because its mandate already presupposes a background ontology. Does the analogy's failure on this point undermine its use in defending Sidgwick's openness to anomalous evidence, or does it instead clarify why his method was conceptually distinct from scientific gatekeeping?

Please separate what is settled about Sidgwick's procedural commitments from what remains genuinely contested among historians of psychology and philosophers of science, and do not present any disputed interpretation of his motives or results as the consensus view.
