I've been thinking about a stricter version of the honor-status story for moral revolutions -- the idea that norms like dueling or chattel slavery don't collapse because people suddenly see they're wrong, but because the secondary elites who enforce the norm stop getting enough status payoff to justify the coordination costs. Appiah's dueling case and Williams's general skepticism about moral progress fit here, but I'm pushing it further: moral argument is purely post-hoc renaming, not even an accelerant. So what would count against this? I don't want generic 'people made arguments' objections. I want the specific historical pattern, psychological finding, or cross-case comparison -- maybe with foot-binding bans or blood-sport abolition -- that would show argument or empathy actually caused the cascade, not just rode it. Rank the defeaters: fatal, awkward, or easily absorbed by the theory. And say which ones a defender of this view would concede actually hurt, versus what critics claim that just misses the mechanism.
