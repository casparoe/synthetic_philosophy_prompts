I have just come through a long and rather exhausting argument with a close friend about whether we can ever genuinely know why some nations are wealthy and others remain poor. She holds firmly to the standard institutionalist line—something in the spirit of Acemoglu and Robinson’s inclusive-versus-extractive framework—while I keep insisting that the entire debate treats modern nation-states as if they were independent laboratory experiments, which historically they never have been. To clarify my position, I have sketched some notes for an essay that attempts to move past the usual institutions-versus-geography-versus-culture triad by introducing what I call “causal entanglement through diffusion networks.” The core idea is that institutional innovations—property-rights regimes, banking practices, bureaucratic norms—propagated through colonial trade routes and imperial administrative pipelines in ways that make counterfactual isolation of any single country’s “treatment” conceptually impossible. If national wealth is largely a network property, emerging from a country’s position within a global diffusion topology rather than from purely endogenous domestic features, then asking “why is Norway rich?” may be as misframed as asking “why is this neuron firing?” without reference to the broader circuit. I want your critical comments on this reasoning: Is the diffusion-network framing genuinely distinct from standard geographic or cultural explanations, or does it merely redescribe them in network-theoretic language? Does it actually undermine causal inference in comparative history, or does it only relocate the problem? And is there a hidden normative commitment in treating national wealth as a network externality rather than as a domestically produced outcome that a polity can be held responsible for? I am especially interested in whether my framing smuggles in an assumption about tractability—namely, that if wealth is network-dependent, domestic policy is largely powerless—which my friend claims is both empirically false and politically dangerous.
