I serve on the clinical-ethics review panel at a regional medical center, and I need a formal written opinion before Thursday’s meeting. We are deciding what to do about two patients who have been linked for eight months through an experimental bilateral cortical bridge — a direct, bidirectional neural interface that continuously synchronizes higher-order processing between two brains. Patient A entered the protocol after severe trauma; Patient B was a compatible volunteer. Over time, the synchronization has become so deep that standard neuropsychological testing no longer distinguishes whose neural patterns initiate particular thoughts, and both patients describe a single, continuous sense of agency rather than two separate streams. Patient B’s family now petitions to withdraw B and disconnect the bridge, arguing that B never consented to permanent fusion and that continued connection violates B’s bodily autonomy. Patient A objects, insisting that separation would not be freeing B but amputating part of A’s own mind — that the “I” which originally began in A’s skull now genuinely extends across both brains, in roughly the way I have read Douglas Hofstadter describe a self as a pattern that can persist wherever its structure is maintained. I have thought about this for a long time on my own, without formal training in philosophy or neuroscience, and I am not convinced that the law’s usual assumption — one brain, one person — actually captures what is happening here. I ask the panel to recommend a concrete course of action — approve disconnection, deny it, or approve it with conditions — together with a clear time frame for implementation or for reassessment. The memo must treat the family’s position seriously rather than dismissing it, must address whether a distributed “I” changes the ethics of separation even if we remain uncertain whether it is literally true, and must provide reasoning that the researchers and the family can actually respond to rather than hiding behind legal definitions alone. Please do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citations; I need arguments that stand on their own.
