As a recently retired experimental physicist now turning to philosophy of mind, I have been studying Frank Jackson’s thought experiment about Mary, the neuroscientist who knows every physical fact about color vision while confined to a monochrome environment. The argument is usually taken to show that her first experience of red reveals a non-physical fact. Yet it occurs to me that Mary’s case might be structurally parallel to a familiar feature of physical theory: many descriptions are gauge-invariant in form, but particular manifestations depend on the observer’s reference frame or interaction context. One could therefore propose that Mary’s prior knowledge captures all gauge-invariant physical information about color vision, while her release supplies only the observer-relative, gauge-dependent coordinate—the specific phenomenological frame—without introducing any ontologically novel property. If phenomenal qualities are physically grounded but frame-relative in this way, her epistemic advance looks like a change in perspectival access rather than the discovery of something outside physics. Could you explain why philosophers generally reject this gauge-relational interpretation of Mary’s learning? In particular, does the analogy fail because phenomenal properties lack the transformation laws that govern genuinely gauge-dependent quantities, or does the knowledge argument rest on a premise about phenomenal concepts that survives regardless of how one models observer-dependence in physical theory?
