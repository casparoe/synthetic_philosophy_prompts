I am writing this in connection with a seminar I am taking on epistemic rationality and higher-order evidence. We have been working through cases in which an agent receives evidence that her own reasoning process has been unreliable—say, she learns that she formed belief B while fatigued, emotionally invested, or operating under a known bias—yet she continues to hold B. That structure is familiar enough as epistemic akrasia, but I keep noticing a more specific variant that the standard discussions do not seem to isolate.

The pattern I have in mind works like this. The agent explicitly acknowledges, at the second-order level, that her first-order evidence for B is weak or that her cognitive process was flawed. Rather than suspending judgment or downgrading her confidence, she treats this very acknowledgment as a redeeming feature: she claims that because she can now recognize her own fallibility, she is exercising a distinctive form of rational self-management, and that this meta-awareness somehow licenses her to retain B despite the first-order deficit. In concrete arguments I have encountered—both in philosophical examples and in informal debate—someone will concede, “I know I am probably biased on this point,” but then immediately add, “and precisely because I can admit that, I am not merely a victim of the bias; therefore I am entitled to stick with my judgment.” Here the higher-order evidence of unreliability is not used to correct the belief. Instead, it is reframed as evidence of the agent’s epistemic virtue, which then retroactively justifies maintaining the very belief the flawed process produced.

Is there an established name for this particular move—where second-order recognition of one’s own epistemic failure is converted into a first-order defense of the belief that failure generated? If the literature treats it only as a subtype of ordinary epistemic akrasia, I would like to understand exactly how it differs from the standard cases in which an agent simply believes against her better judgment without this reflexive, self-exonerating structure. And if there is no distinct concept, could you help me delineate its boundaries: how it differs from ordinary rationalization, from a motte-and-bailey defense of the belief, and from legitimate cases in which higher-order awareness of error genuinely supports maintaining a belief because one’s correction mechanisms are now actively engaged? I am not looking for a bare label; I want to know what the phenomenon is, what it is not, and where its edges lie.
