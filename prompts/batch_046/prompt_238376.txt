Following a long and somewhat heated discussion with a friend last night about whether David Lewis’s analysis of convention can guide anyone deliberately constructing a shared regularity — or whether it only explains norms that have already survived evolutionary selection by accident — I need an operational framework I can actually use. I work as a facilitation lead for intentional communities and worker-owned cooperatives, and every quarter I am asked to draft proposed usage protocols for shared resources — tool libraries, kitchen rotations, parking allocations, quiet-hour agreements — in settings where legal enforcement is essentially impossible and compliance must be self-sustaining through peer expectation alone. I need a reusable decision procedure my team can run on any draft proposal before it goes to a member vote. The procedure should translate Lewis’s conditions — a coordination problem, a regularity that solves it, common knowledge of that regularity among participants, a preference for conformity conditional on others conforming, and the arbitrariness of the solution relative to alternatives — into a sequence of ordered questions with clear stopping rules: what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure returns a hard “no, this will not stabilize as a convention,” and where it must hand the decision to human judgment because the issue is one of values rather than structural fit. Because designed conventions lack the evolutionary history that makes competing alternatives invisible, the procedure should include a specific “shadow-alternative visibility audit” — a check for whether the design process itself has made a technically available substitute salient enough to destabilize the proposed regularity once stress occurs. It should also distinguish first-order conventions (behavioral regularities) from second-order conventions about how to revise them, flagging cases where a group is trying to solve a coordination problem with a rule that actually requires authority or moral agreement rather than mutual expectation. Apply the procedure to three cases: (A) a rotating maintenance schedule for a shared compost system, where assignment of days is arbitrary but interdependence is clear; (B) a proposed “no single-use packaging” rule for a community kitchen, which I personally support but which may fail the structural test; and (C) replacing an informal “take-what-you-need” tool-library norm with a digital reservation app, which changes the salience structure of alternatives. For case B, the procedure should reach a conclusion I will not like — specifically, that it is not a convention in the relevant sense and should be treated as a governance decision requiring explicit authority, not as a self-sustaining peer expectation. The deliverable should be honest about where the procedure will misfire — for instance, when newcomers lack the tacit background knowledge that makes common knowledge possible — and should state explicitly what it cannot do, such as predict the speed of adoption or resolve deep value conflicts masked as coordination failures. Constraints: I have about thirty minutes per proposal, my background is in organizational sociology rather than formal game theory, and the final sign-off is a democratic vote, so the procedure must be runnable without mathematical modeling and must flag the points where democratic judgment, not technical analysis, is doing the work.
