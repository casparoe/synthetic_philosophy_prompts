As a novelist preparing an essay for a literary-philosophy review, I want to read Philip K. Dick’s short story The Minority Report through the lens of Benjamin Libet’s experiments on voluntary action—the readiness-potential studies and the subsequent concept of conscious veto, by which a subject might suppress a motor tendency the brain has already begun to prepare. Dick’s Precog system seems to externalize exactly this kind of unconscious predictive machinery: the mutants foresee crimes before they occur, and the Precrime police arrest the future perpetrator in advance. I need an honest critical reading that asks where the parallel holds and where it collapses. Does Anderton’s decision to hunt down and ultimately defy his own predicted murder dramatize Libet’s veto—the conscious blocking of an initiated action—or does the story confuse an epistemic forecast with metaphysical determinism, treating foresight as if it were causal necessity? There is also a temporal discrepancy that worries me: Libet’s veto operates within a few hundred milliseconds and suppresses a tendency arising in the motor cortex, whereas Anderton’s reversal unfolds over days with full reflective deliberation. Does this mismatch mean the story is not really about neurobiological agency at all, but rather about the retrospective narrative construction of the self—the post-hoc authorship of one’s choices? If so, what does Dick’s fiction contribute to the free-will debate that laboratory reports on readiness potentials cannot? I want a reading faithful in both directions: one that does not reduce the story to an illustration of neuroscience, that does not distort Libet’s findings to fit the plot, and that says clearly where the analogy breaks and what philosophical work remains once it does.
