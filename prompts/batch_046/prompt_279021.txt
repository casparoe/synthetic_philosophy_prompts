In arguments against individual moral obligations regarding climate change, factory farming, or democratic participation, a recurring move is the bucket analogy: one agent’s carbon footprint, meat purchase, or ballot is likened to a single drop of water added to or withdrawn from a bucket that is already overflowing. The drop does not alter the water level, so, the argument runs, the action is causally inert and therefore carries no distinctive moral weight—this is the core of the so-called inefficacy objection.

Audit this analogy systematically. First, provide an explicit correspondence table: what in the target domain plays the structural role of the drop, the bucket, the overflow threshold, the measurement procedure that detects level changes, the mechanism by which drops are added or removed, and the observer who cares about the level? Second, identify the disanalogies that matter specifically for the inefficacy objection: does the target domain involve discrete thresholds (e.g., tied elections, supply-chain batch orders) that make the “no visible change” premise false? Does it involve nonlinear or stochastic dynamics where many imperceptible inputs jointly determine the outcome? Does the analogy smuggle in a linear, independent-causation model that misrepresents how collective harms actually accumulate?

Third, separate the disanalogies that undermine the conclusion from those that are merely decorative: for instance, if moral responsibility tracks expected contribution to risk rather than actual counterfactual difference to a specific outcome, does the analogy’s failure to map “counterfactual dependence” onto “drop absence” defeat the objection, or does the objection survive by shifting to an expectation-based framing? Fourth, evaluate whether the analogy quietly imports normative premises—such as that only actions with individually observable effects are morally assessable—and whether those premises are defensible in the domains of climate, consumption, and voting.

Finally, if the bucket analogy collapses or carries the conclusion, propose a more accurate analogical framework for reasoning about individual contribution to collective harm—one that can accommodate threshold effects, probabilistic causation, and distributed agency without presupposing that each contributor must be a necessary condition for the harm.
