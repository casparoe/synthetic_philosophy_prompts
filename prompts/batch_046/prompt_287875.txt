I am running a ninety-minute session for an upper-level seminar on philosophy of perception, fourteen graduate students, in a room with movable chairs and a projector, and I want an in-class simulation that makes them feel the pressure of the argument from illusion before they ever hear the name, then experience the genuine strain of resisting it with a disjunctivist reply. Before the session begins the facilitator secretly assigns each student one of two hidden roles using sealed envelopes opened only at the start: half become direct perceivers who view an actual slowly rotating geometric solid projected at the front, and half become matched non-perceivers who view an identically calibrated retinal image delivered through headsets that block all ambient light and remove any causal link to the real projection. Every student writes a private first-person phenomenological report describing shape, color, and apparent motion using only subjective language, then the reports are anonymized and read aloud. The group must reach a unanimous decision on whether the two sets of reports describe mental states of the same fundamental kind, using a structured sequence of open discussion, secret ballot with only two options—common kind or disjoint kinds—and a public justification round in which anyone who changed must explain why. The stakes should be social and epistemic rather than monetary: if they vote common kind, they collectively sign a statement granting both groups identical perceptual authority; if they vote disjoint kinds, they must agree that the non-perceivers possess no perceptual knowledge whatsoever, not even knowledge of appearance, and defend that conclusion in front of the other half. Only after the vote is locked are the envelopes opened and roles revealed. The debrief should map each stage—the writing of reports, the comparison of descriptions, the pull toward unification, and the cost of splitting—onto corresponding moves in the argument from illusion and in the disjunctivist denial of a shared mental factor, and it should include a precise timeline for the ninety minutes, a list of materials beyond envelopes and headsets, and a brief script for how the facilitator reveals the roles without letting the discussion collapse into mere psychology. Be frank about what will predictably go wrong the first time: for instance, participants may conflate phenomenal similarity with ontological identity almost instantly, or the headset group may feel unfairly stigmatized once revealed, and tell me how to handle both. Also be honest about which feature of disjunctivism the simulation cannot reproduce—most likely the metaphysical claim that veridical seeing is literally a relational state to an external object rather than an internal representational state—and explain how the facilitator must supply that missing piece verbally once the exercise ends. Finally, inject a specific constructed framing into the rules: call the voting threshold the indifference point of phenomenal licensing, defined as the moment when a group treats two phenomenally matching experiences as carrying identical epistemic entitlement regardless of causal origin, and ask me to explain in the debrief why that point is exactly where the argument from illusion acquires its greatest force. What participants should come away seeing is not a doctrine but a lived tension between the naturalness of grouping matching experiences together and the radical cost of refusing to do so.
