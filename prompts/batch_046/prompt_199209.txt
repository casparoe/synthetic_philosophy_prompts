I am in the middle of revising a long manuscript about a cartographer who becomes convinced that every territory she charts contains a region that cannot itself be charted, and I have been using this conceit to think about reflexivity in fiction. In my theoretical notes I have been working with Douglas Hofstadter’s discussion of tangled hierarchies and the idea he describes as an inviolate level—the claim that in loops of self-reference there remains a stratum untouched by the loop, analogous to the paper on which Escher’s two hands drawing each other are rendered, never drawn by the hands themselves. I want to understand whether this conceptual structure can be rigorously translated into the vocabulary of narrative poetics, with which I am professionally fluent but which I have never systematically applied to formal models of self-reference.

Specifically, I ask you to restate Hofstadter’s account of tangled hierarchies and the inviolate level using only the conceptual resources of embedded narration, diegetic levels, and what I call the silent register—the unrepresented causal substrate that makes narrative representation possible without itself being representable within the fiction. For each element of Hofstadter’s picture, indicate whether it translates cleanly, whether it survives only if distorted, or whether it finds no expression at all within this narrative framework. Then, treating the residue as the philosophically interesting part, tell me whether the failure of translation reveals a genuine limitation in narrative theory’s capacity to model reflexivity, or whether it suggests that Hofstadter’s notion of an inviolate level was less precisely demarcated than the analogy to Escher’s image implies.
