For a feature I am writing on digital ethics, I have sketched an argument about virtual harm that feels like a trap: every exit seems to deny something obvious. (1) If two experiences are phenomenologically identical in intensity and quality, then the harm they constitute is the same, regardless of cause. (2) Sustained virtual harassment can produce psychological distress that is indistinguishable from the distress caused by physical stalking. (3) So virtual harassment can cause real harm. (4) If harm is real, the victim must have real interests that can be violated; and if those interests are genuinely violated, the bearer of them must have a kind of persistence that generates obligations across interruptions. (5) Virtual entities lack what philosophers call modal persistence: they can be duplicated, suspended, or restored without contradiction, unlike biological persons. (6) Therefore either virtual entities lack real interests (which contradicts 3), or modal persistence is unnecessary for moral standing (which makes physical death far less significant than we ordinarily assume). (7) The second option dissolves the special wrongness of killing. (8) So (1) is false: substrate or persistence conditions do matter, and virtual distress, however real it feels, is not the same harm. I am reluctant to surrender (3) because the suffering is undeniable. Which step should I reject, and what does that rejection cost? Does it save the reality of virtual harm without trivialising physical death? Please reply in British English.
