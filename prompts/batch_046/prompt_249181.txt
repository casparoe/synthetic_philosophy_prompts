Give me your credence, on a scale from 0 to 1, that the classical Abhidharma doctrine of anatta—interpreted as the denial of svabhava (inherent existence) in all five aggregates—entails what I will call a radical replacement view of personal identity: that there is not merely a reducible or conventional self, but that the very framing of whether person A at t1 survives as person B at t2 is a category error produced by clinging to a non-existent substrate. Decompose your answer into four sub-estimates: (a) the likelihood that Abhidharma texts intend this metaphysical denial rather than a primarily phenomenological or soteriological one; (b) the likelihood that radical replacement is conceptually coherent without collapsing into an eliminativism that undermines agency; (c) the likelihood that this view genuinely converges with Western psychological-continuity reductionism rather than diverging from it in some hidden structural way; and (d) the likelihood that moral responsibility can be reconstructed on purely event-causal or karmic-continuity grounds if radical replacement is correct. Provide a rough number for each, indicate how they combine into your overall credence, name the term in which you are least confident and what kind of argument or textual consideration would move it most, and briefly note whether a moderate reductionist-but-continuous interpretation is more plausible and by roughly how much.
