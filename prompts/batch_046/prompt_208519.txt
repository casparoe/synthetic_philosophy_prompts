I'm working through different theories of moral desert for a seminar paper, and I recently came across a summary—perhaps in a popular philosophy of religion article or in a friend's notes—of karma as a theory of moral desert that I want to check for fairness. The summary runs like this: karma is essentially an impersonal, law-like mechanism of moral causation that requires no divine judge; every intentional action deposits a causal potential that must eventually mature into an experience specifically proportionate to its moral quality for the agent who performed it. Because this maturation can occur across successive lives, karma is said to solve the problem of moral luck in a way Western retributivism cannot: if a virtuous person suffers now, that suffering is the precise ripening of past misdeed, and if a wrongdoer thrives, that prosperity is only temporary credit that will eventually be debited with interest, so over the long arc every agent receives exactly the welfare they deserve. The summary concludes that karma is therefore a stricter and more complete form of moral desert than any theistic or secular Western counterpart, since it guarantees perfect correspondence between moral character and experienced well-being with no remainder and no need for social enforcement.

Something about this strikes me as too neat—perhaps it treats karma like a cosmic accounting system—but I am not sure which parts are standard interpretations and which are subtle distortions. I have a vague sense that Buddhist accounts emphasize the mental state behind action (what is sometimes called cetana) rather than treating karma as a mechanical scorekeeper for external behaviors, and that the conditions under which a karmic potential actually ripens depend on contingent circumstances rather than operating with automatic inevitability. I also wonder whether the claim that karma "solves" moral luck merely relocates the contingency to previous lives rather than eliminating it, and whether modern interpreters all accept literal rebirth as necessary for the theory to function. Could you audit this summary carefully? Please separate what is accurate to mainstream Buddhist and philosophical treatments of karma, what is a subtle misrepresentation or overstatement—particularly regarding the role of intention, the mechanics of ripening, and the relationship to moral luck—and whether the conclusion that karma is a uniquely complete form of retributive desert survives once those corrections are made. I am especially interested in whether there is anything in the summary that a defender of karma as moral desert would reject as a category mistake rather than a mere exaggeration.
