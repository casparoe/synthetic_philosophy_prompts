I'm preparing to moderate a Q&A session at a legal-history colloquium with an unnamed scholar who has recently defended a revisionist account of Raphael Lemkin's legacy. Her central claim is that Lemkin's original concept of genocide was not meant to be a narrowly juridical category but rather what she calls a threshold concept—a hybrid moral-legal device that converts historical description into binding juridical obligation the moment it is applied. She argues that the 1948 UN Convention's exclusion of political groups and cultural destruction was not a conceptual clarification, as is usually taught, but a geopolitical compromise that accidentally revealed the instability of Lemkin's hybrid: once you try to make the category precise for prosecution, its moral force either collapses into ordinary crimes-against-humanity charges or expands into an unmanageable moral taxonomy. She further holds that contemporary debates over whether to name specific mass atrocities genocide—for instance, campaigns of forced displacement that lack explicit intent to destroy a protected group in whole or in part—are therefore not merely political disputes disguised as legal ones, but structural demonstrations that the label itself performs unavoidable normative work that adjacent categories like ethnic cleansing or mass atrocity cannot replicate.

I want six to eight interview questions that press this position rather than letting her restate it. At least one question should target her historical claim about the drafting of the 1948 Convention, anticipating the standard reply that sovereign states simply needed a workable enforcement mechanism and including a sharp follow-up. At least one should interrogate the threshold-concept idea directly: ask what specific moral or legal obligation is triggered by applying the genocide label that is not already present under crimes-against-humanity frameworks, and include a follow-up keyed to her likely answer that the difference lies in the non-derogable duty of prevention rather than in punishment severity. I also want a question that makes her defend why cultural destruction should be read back into the concept despite its exclusion, anticipating her move to distinguish physical from biological destruction and pushing her on whether that distinction survives scrutiny in cases of forced assimilation. Finally, end with a question about the normative upshot: if the category is indeed structurally unstable and politically unavoidable, should we abandon the legal term in favor of a graded, non-binary taxonomy of mass violence, and what exactly would be lost in that transition?
