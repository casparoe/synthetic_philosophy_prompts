I've been working through the unilateralist's curse—particularly the variant that applies when actors can take irreversible steps not just physically but epistemically, by releasing models, publishing datasets, or setting de facto technical standards—after engaging with the standard rationalist literature on multipolar traps and races to the bottom. I'm presenting a reframing at a small interdisciplinary workshop on AI governance and collective action: the audience will be mostly institutional economists who think in mechanism design and credible-commitment terms, two decision theorists who resist any framing that sounds like normative "shoulds" rather than equilibrium analysis, and a philosopher of science focused on epistemic norms and peer-review structures. My core thesis is that the curse is misdiagnosed when treated purely as an action-threshold failure—"raise the bar for unilateral moves"—because the deeper problem is asymmetric private information about both downside risk and actor competence. The least cautious actor often has the worst signal, not merely the lowest risk aversion. So instead of universal threshold-raising, I propose a staged disclosure covenant: actors commit to revealing risk assessments in tiered stages—internal audit, restricted peer review, then conditional release—tied to verifiable milestones rather than to any single party's unilateral judgment. The idea is to correct the information asymmetry without requiring global agreement on risk tolerance.
