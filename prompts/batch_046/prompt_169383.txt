I serve on the charter revision committee of an interdisciplinary research institute currently governed by a hybrid model: a small executive board handles operational decisions, hiring, budget allocations, and disciplinary appeals, while a larger faculty assembly votes on strategic priorities and membership criteria. A proposal now before us would abolish the board entirely and transfer all authority—including tenure recommendations, budget line approvals, and disciplinary rulings—to direct majority vote of the full assembly, with no intermediate filtering body. The proponents cite Spinoza’s argument that democracy is the form of government closest to the state of nature, that sovereignty resides in the collective power of the multitude rather than in a separated council, and that representative intermediaries necessarily alienate democratic power from those who hold it. The opposition responds that Spinoza himself excluded those governed by passion rather than reason from democratic participation, that he envisioned democratic councils of sufficient rational competence to stabilize the commonwealth, and that he warned the multitude led by affect rather than reason tends toward faction and instability; they argue our board serves precisely this Spinozist function of filtering impulsive majorities and protecting specialized judgment in technical domains.

Please draft the committee’s written opinion, to be circulated before the membership vote scheduled for next month. Recommend a concrete structural outcome—retain the hybrid model, move to pure direct democracy, or adopt a modified intermediate structure—with an explicit implementation timeline and clear allocation of powers. The opinion should engage Spinoza’s democratic theory in detail: address his claim that democracy preserves natural equality and that separated councils diminish sovereign power; but also address his concern that democratic stability depends on whether the voting body is guided by reason, his corresponding restrictions on democratic citizenship, and his view that the size and composition of a council affect its rational capacity. Do not treat these as abstract slogans—show how they apply specifically to our institution’s scale, to the technical nature of tenure and budget decisions, and to the particular risk of majoritarian passion in disciplinary appeals. Give an honest, substantive account of the strongest consideration on the side you reject. The deliverable must be actionable: specify which bodies retain which powers, any conditions or phased transition for change, and how minority expertise is either protected or deliberately exposed to democratic decision under your recommendation. Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citations to secondary literature; the reasoning should stand on the conceptual arguments themselves.
