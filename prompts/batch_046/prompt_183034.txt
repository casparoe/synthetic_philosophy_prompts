She summarized the view for me — she called it the 'agentive environment' framework — and said it basically means that things like the plague bacterium or the 1815 Tambora eruption have genuine agency in history, not just mechanical causation. So according to her, Yersinia pestis didn't merely weaken feudal labor markets during the Black Death; it actively 'chose' to dismantle serfdom through selective mortality patterns, almost like a microbial reformer. And Tambora didn't just cause harvest failures that sparked unrest; it practiced what she called 'geological diplomacy,' reshaping the Congress of Vienna alliances through grain-price networks as if the volcano were negotiating treaties. From all this she concluded that human kings and revolutionaries are mostly side effects of weather and microbes, so we're really in a hard environmental determinism where political agency is an illusion.

I think there's a legitimate point about taking non-human forces seriously in history, but her summary feels like it jumps from 'causes matter' to 'people don't.' Is this a fair representation of what scholars in this area actually argue?
