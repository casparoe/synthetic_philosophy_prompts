I am a retired physicist who has only recently begun reading philosophy of space and time, and I find myself holding a cluster of intuitions about whether spacetime exists independently of matter that seem to pull in opposite directions. I would like to know whether there is a precise, unified principle—substantivalist, relationist, structuralist, or otherwise—that captures the pattern, or whether some of my verdicts must be surrendered for the sake of coherence. Here are my judgments.

First, in the rotating-bucket scenario, if water climbs the walls of a vessel even when we imagine away every distant star and galaxy, I take that as evidence for an absolute spatial structure: the rotation is with respect to something—spacetime itself—not merely to other bodies.

Second, consider an otherwise empty universe containing only a single rigid body spinning about its axis. I am inclined to say there is no fact of the matter about whether it rotates; without other bodies to define relative motion, attributing spin seems physically empty.

Third, take two identical particles orbiting one another in perfect isolation. I think there is a genuine rotational fact here—orbital dynamics and internal stresses depend on their mutual separation and angular velocity—yet I do not feel compelled to invoke an underlying manifold to ground it; the relations between the bodies seem sufficient.

Fourth, imagine a massive hollow shell rotating in an otherwise empty universe. Inside, a suspended pendulum slowly precesses relative to the shell. This makes me think that the presence and motion of enclosing matter genuinely alter local inertial properties, which sounds relationist. But I also want to say that what is being affected is a substantive metric field, not merely a summary of particle relations.

Fifth, in our actual universe, cosmic expansion is described not as galaxies moving through a pre-existing space, but as the scaling of distances encoded in the metric itself. That sounds to me like space has dynamical properties, hence substantival.

The tension should be plain: I invoke an independent spacetime for the bucket and for cosmic expansion, deny it for the solitary body and the orbiting pair, and split the difference for the rotating shell. Is there a coherent principle—perhaps one that distinguishes global boundary conditions from local dynamics, or treats the metric field as physically real without treating it as a container—that saves the majority? Or must I drop one of these verdicts?

Please state your principle precisely, show how it handles each case above, and be explicit about which verdict or verdicts it cannot accommodate and why. Then apply it to a case I have not listed: a spatially closed universe with periodic boundary conditions and no asymptotic region at infinity, in which a single galaxy rotates. In such a world there are no distant stars and no "edge" of space to serve as reference. Does your principle say that rotation is still absolute, that it is undefined, or that the global topology itself supplies the missing reference frame? Defend the answer.
