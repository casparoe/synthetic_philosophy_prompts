Design a 90-minute, in-person seminar activity for ten upper-level philosophy students—already familiar with Daoist themes but not specialists—called the Transformation Council. The goal is to make them feel the force of Zhuangzi’s view that death is a harmless transformation of qi rather than a loss of a fixed self, and that grief rests on a conventional attachment rather than on metaphysical harm. Give them this concrete scenario: four deceased individuals, each described on a confidential “Continuity Card” according to one of four metaphysical profiles drawn from Zhuangzian ideas—complete dissolution into undifferentiated qi; transformation into a non-conscious natural form; cyclical reconfiguration into a new living being with no psychological continuity; or persistent but radically altered existence in ongoing transformation. The group must collectively allocate a fixed pool of ritual resources—time, material goods, emotional acknowledgment—across these four cases, but they are permitted to allocate resources to a case only if they can publicly justify that the deceased still exists in some form that can be wronged by neglect. Assign specific roles: a Ritual Auditor who enforces consistency, a Continuity Skeptic who challenges every justification, and Kin Advocates assigned to argue for particular cases. Choose a specific resource pool and a specific decision rule—secret ballot or public deliberation—defend that choice, and do not present alternatives. The rules and social stakes should be arranged so that participants discover, before anyone names the philosophical point, that they apply stricter continuity conditions to strangers and looser ones to loved ones, thereby revealing the conventional basis of grief obligations. Include a precise timeline, a payoff structure that uses public justification and peer scrutiny rather than money, and a debrief that maps each moment of the activity onto a feature of Zhuangzi’s argument—especially his refusal to mourn his wife—while being frank about which feature of the view, specifically the phenomenological experience of non-attachment, the activity cannot reproduce and the facilitator must supply. Also predict exactly what will go wrong the first time: some students will conflate emotional distance with metaphysical commitment, and at least one will likely reject the allocation logic entirely; say how to handle both failures without shutting down the exercise. Finally, take a committed position on which of the four profiles best captures Zhuangzi’s actual challenge to conventional mourning, defend that interpretive choice, and include an honest note on where your design takes liberties with the text or relies on a contested framing of qi and personal identity. Make all design choices definite—specify the cards, the pool, the voting rule, and the debrief sequence—rather than listing options.
