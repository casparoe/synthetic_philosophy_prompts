A philosophy professor summarized Kripke's rule-following paradox and its skeptical solution for me as follows: "Kripke demonstrates that there is no fact of the matter about which rule I meant by 'plus' in the past, since any finite history of use is compatible with infinitely many rules—including quus, according to which 68 plus 57 equals 5. Because mental states and dispositions are equally compatible with all these rules, they cannot determine meaning. Wittgenstein's insight, on Kripke's reading, is therefore that meaning is constituted entirely by the linguistic community's collective acceptance: the community's agreement literally creates the normative fact that addition is correct. The skeptical solution avoids private-language skepticism not by recovering hidden mental facts, but by substituting social practice for them. From this it follows that any system lacking membership in a human linguistic community—such as an isolated artificial neural network—cannot genuinely follow rules, because its internal weights are just as underdetermined with respect to every possible rule as my past mental states are. Hence Kripke's argument ultimately shows that rule-following is an irreducibly social achievement, and that the objectivity of mathematics rests on collective convention rather than on independent mental or physical facts." I am inclined to accept the inference about neural networks, but I worry the summary may conflate Kripke's skeptical problem with a stronger social-constructivist claim he never endorsed.
