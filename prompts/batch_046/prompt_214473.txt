For a framework paper I'm developing at the intersection of moral psychology and philosophy of action, I want an internal consistency audit of the following five commitments before I finalize the architecture.

(1) The Knobe asymmetry—where harmful side-effects are judged intentional more often than beneficial ones—is real, but it is overdiagnosed as a direct moral evaluation of the agent. Instead, it arises from a differential salience of normative expectation violations: harming activates a prohibition-frame that integrates the outcome into the agent's profile, while helping activates a permission-frame that leaves it structurally external.

(2) Conceptually, intentional action requires that the agent represent the outcome as a constituent of their means-end plan, not merely foresee it. By this standard, side-effects in the classic vignettes fail the condition in both the harm and help conditions; the folk judgment that they are intentional in the harm case is therefore a psychologically real but conceptually mistaken attribution.

(3) Experimental philosophy should rely primarily on within-subjects comparative designs—presenting both harm and help conditions to the same respondent—because the intuitive architecture is revealed in the comparative judgment, not in absolute attribution rates, and between-subjects designs risk obscuring the structural relation.

(4) Folk-psychological concepts such as "intentional" are cluster concepts without strict necessary and sufficient conditions. They should be experimentally mapped, but armchair conceptual analysis that legislates definitional boundaries—such as the means-end condition in (2)—should be treated as a normative proposal rather than a discovery of folk content.

(5) The mechanism driving the asymmetry, which I call "normative-boundary integration," operates below the level of full-blown moral judgment: it is an agent-centered monitoring system that binds outcomes to agents when a normative boundary is breached, without requiring evaluation of the agent's character, desert, or overall moral status.

Please assess which of these hang together and which pull apart. I am especially concerned about three potential fault lines: whether (1) and (5) are in tension because (1) appeals to normative frames while (5) claims independence from moral judgment; whether (2) undermines (1) by rendering the asymmetry conceptually impossible rather than psychologically explicable; and whether (3) sits awkwardly with (4), since comparative mapping might seem to presuppose a stable concept being mapped across conditions. Rank the tensions by severity, identify which commitments must be qualified or abandoned to restore coherence, and flag any apparent conflicts that dissolve on inspection. Do not treat all five as equally problematic; discriminate sharply and say what the least costly revision would be.
