Consider the following argument, which I think captures a tension running through macro-historical explanation but which I also find suspicious because its conclusion seems to dissolve the very field it targets.

(1) Any explanation adequate to the temporal and spatial scale of big history—say, the adoption of agriculture, the consolidation of states, or the integration of continents—must treat structural conditions (climate, geography, demographic density, disease ecology) as the primary explanatory variables.

(2) If structural conditions are primary, then the occurrence of the event is counterfactually robust with respect to the beliefs and intentions of the individuals involved: had particular agents believed or chosen differently, the structural conditions would still have produced the outcome.

(3) Therefore, under a grand-narrative framework, macro-historical outcomes are modally necessary relative to the structural baseline—individual agency is causally redundant.

(4) But moral evaluation—calling the agricultural revolution a fraud or trap, judging colonial expansion as unjust, or condemning the emergence of hierarchy—presupposes that agents could have acted otherwise; ought implies can, and responsibility requires causal efficacy.

(5) Harari’s account of the cognitive and agricultural revolutions, Diamond’s geographic determinism, and even Graeber and Wengrow’s critique of evolutionary stage-theory all deploy precisely such moral and counterfactual language: they speak of choices that were hidden, paths not taken, and injustices committed.

(6) So grand narratives simultaneously depend on the causal redundancy of individual agency (to maintain their explanatory scale) and on its efficacy (to sustain their moral and critical vocabulary).

(7) Hence the practice of big history is conceptually incoherent: it must deny the very agency it invokes to evaluate its objects.

(8) The only consistent responses are either to abandon macro-scale structural explanation or to purge historical writing of all moral judgment.

Where does this argument slip? Identify the weakest step, explain why it looks compelling, and price what each of the three frameworks—Harari’s narrative-cognitive model, Diamond’s geographic determinism, and Graeber and Wengrow’s contingency thesis—would have to surrender to reject it. If the argument is valid and the real choice is which plausible premise to abandon, say so explicitly, and defend which one you think is least costly. I would prefer to keep (4) and (5) intact if possible, but I want to know exactly what preserving them costs the other premises.
