Ever since people started arguing about Kierkegaard's Abraham in Fear and Trembling — especially since the 80s and 90s when every other philosopher wrote about the 'teleological suspension of the ethical' — the debate feels completely stuck. Side A says Abraham is the knight of faith, putting his absolute duty to God above the universal ethical command not to kill his son; Side B says that's just romantic language for a child-killer, and if the ethical is truly universal then suspension is either impossible or monstrous. Same arguments, same counter-arguments, nobody budges. Is this a real substantive disagreement about moral ontology, or mostly verbal — do they mean different things by 'ethical' (say, social convention vs rational law)? Or are they answering different questions, with one side describing an existantial leap and the other doing normative justification? Diagnose it using what each side actually says, and say whether there's a hidden formal problem — like whether universalizability can tolerate exceptions without collapsing. I also want to float a hypothesis: the stall might come from a false binary, that either the ethical is totally suspended or not at all. Some people have tried analogies to legal 'necessity defenses' or sociological 'role exit', where you shift normative frameworks rather than blow them up, but those analogouges never quite stick in the literature. Could progress come from a graded or layered model of ethical commitment instead? Or is the question just ill-posed? Tell me what would actually count as progress here — a distinction, a formal result, changing the question — or argue that we should just drop it. No need for reading lists, just your take.
