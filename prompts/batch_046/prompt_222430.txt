In a debate about replacing unwritten scientific peer-review norms with fully explicit algorithmic scoring rubrics, an opponent of the change appealed to Chesterton's fence — the maxim that one should not tear down a fence without knowing why it was erected. Can you lay out the different precise principles this slogan could express? Consider, for instance: an empirical claim about hidden institutional functions; an epistemic claim about the limits of explicit articulation; a normative burden-of-proof principle placing risk on reformers; and a conservative bias toward existing arrangements. For each reading, say whether it is trivial, substantive and defensible, or false, and indicate which ones would actually justify resisting the rubric replacement rather than applying symmetrically to the status quo itself.
