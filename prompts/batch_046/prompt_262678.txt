In anthropic reasoning, the Self-Sampling Assumption directs an agent to treat herself as randomly drawn from a specified reference class of observers, so that evidence about her location updates beliefs about the class only through ordinary conditionalization; the Self-Indication Assumption, by contrast, treats her very existence as favoring worlds that contain more observers, because larger populations make her presence more likely. These two principles yield opposite verdicts in the “presumptuous philosopher” scenario, where a cosmological theory predicting vastly more observers is either strongly confirmed or left untouched depending on which assumption is adopted. Despite decades of formal discussion—Bayesian derivations, reference-class puzzles, and attempts to derive one principle from the other—the dispute has not advanced toward resolution; practitioners often simply declare an intuition about whether existence should count as data. My working hypothesis is that the impasse is not a hidden empirical disagreement about cosmology or a technical error in the probability calculus, but a deeper divergence over what “evidence” means when the sampling procedure necessarily includes the sampler: one side treats self-location as a selection effect to be corrected away, the other as an indexical observation with direct evidential weight, and neither side has established why its framing of the reference problem should be mandatory. Is that diagnosis correct, or is there a substantive formal or conceptual disagreement in disguise—perhaps about the legitimacy of conditioning on one’s own existence, about the boundaries of a reference class, or about whether the two assumptions are actually answering different questions under one name?
