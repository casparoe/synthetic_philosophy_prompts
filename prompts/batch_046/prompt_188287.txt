As a novelist who recently began co-hosting a literary-philosophy podcast with a philosopher colleague, I am drafting the interview script for next month's episode, in which our guest will be an unnamed philosopher of cognitive science who defends what she calls a layered consolidation model of the color phi phenomenon. Rather than treating the intermediate chromatic trajectory as either a pure Orwellian memory revision—in which no continuous experience ever occurs and the narrative is rewritten only after the second dot arrives—or a pure Stalinesque perceptual revision—in which a fully formed continuous experience is generated in real time and then immediately airbrushed from the record—she maintains that early sensory traces are initially encoded with explicit temporal ambiguity, then selectively consolidated into a continuous trajectory only if post-stimulus attentional resources remain available within a narrow consolidation window. She is particularly adept at deflecting the standard binding objection by insisting that the binding occurs in the consolidation layer rather than in the perceptual present, and she tends to dismiss appeals to subjective report by observing that introspective access is itself a late-arriving reconstruction subject to the same layered dynamics. I need a sequence of eight to ten interview questions that turn on the actual content of this hybrid position: each should anticipate her standard reply and include the follow-up that makes the deflection harder to redeploy, with particular attention to the timing of experience rather than to reportability alone. At least one question should probe whether her consolidation-window hypothesis can be genuinely threatened by findings from masked priming or rapid serial visual presentation—specifically, what result would force her to abandon the window rather than reinterpret it as yet another layer of revision? Another should ask whether her view commits her to an ontologically thicker present moment than rival accounts allow, given that ambiguity must be preserved long enough for selective consolidation to occur. The sequence should conclude with a question about what empirical or conceptual evidence could count decisively against her view altogether, framed in terms of the temporal architecture of consciousness rather than in terms of introspective error. The tone should remain rigorous but accessible to listeners familiar with Dennett's original distinction yet unacquainted with the recent psychophysical literature.
