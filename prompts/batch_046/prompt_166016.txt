I serve as senior legislative counsel for a mid-sized municipality, and over the past year my office has been asked to clear an increasing number of administrative rules that govern automated eligibility and allocation systems—benefit formulas executed by software with minimal human review. The work has become personally urgent for me because last month I had to advise on a draft ordinance that was unambiguously authorized by our charter and state enabling statute, yet structured the algorithm so opaquely and with so little general applicability that it seemed to violate every intuitive standard of legitimate governance, even though no court would likely invalidate it on formal grounds alone. I want a reusable decision procedure—something my small staff can run in under an hour per draft—that translates the core disagreement between Hart’s separation thesis and Fuller’s internal morality of law into concrete steps for evaluating these rules, with explicit nodes where judgment cannot be eliminated and where the file must be handed to an elected official because the issue is one of values rather than legal craft.

The procedure should be ordered as follows. First, apply the Hartian gate: determine whether the draft is formally authorized by our charter and enabling law, with clear criteria for what counts as authorization and a stop point if it fails. Second, apply Fuller’s conditions of legality—generality, promulgation, non-retroactivity, clarity, non-contradictoriness, possibility of compliance, stability through time, and congruence between the rule as declared and the rule as actually administered by the system—with specific tests for each, marking which failures are remediable by redrafting and which are structural. Third, determine the status of any failure at step two: either it is a legal defect that blocks enactment, or it is an external moral objection that should be recorded and communicated but does not invalidate the rule. At every stage, identify the judgment calls that are dressed up as procedure, specify what evidence is required and what is sufficient, and state where the procedure halts with a recommendation to reject, where it halts with approval plus an advisory flag, and where it must defer to the council because the conflict is irreducibly normative. Apply the procedure to three proposals: (i) an algorithmic housing-voucher rule that is charter-authorized but so opaque that affected residents cannot understand the decision criteria applied to them; (ii) an emergency winter-shelter allocation rule that targets a specific neighborhood in clear violation of generality but responds to an imminent lethal cold snap; and (iii) a technically sound, clear, and stable rule that delegates all discretionary interpretation to a proprietary vendor with no public audit mechanism. One of these should yield a recommendation that I will find morally repellent.

In constructing this, take a definite position: either treat Fuller’s conditions as internal to legal validity—so that failure at step two is a legal defect that blocks enactment regardless of moral urgency—or treat them as external moral checks that can recommend rejection but never invalidate a formally authorized rule. Defend that architectural choice within the procedure itself, explain what it implies for my duty as counsel (whether I am strictly a guardian of legal validity or also a guardian of minimal moral decency in governance), and be explicit about what your procedure cannot settle—particularly where algorithmic opacity makes congruence between declared rule and actual administration impossible to verify. I want a committed answer, not a menu of options, and I want you to flag precisely which of the three cases forces me, under your procedure, to approve or reject something I will strongly resist.
