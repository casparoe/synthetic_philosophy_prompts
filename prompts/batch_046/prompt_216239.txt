You are serving as the senior philosophy-of-science and clinical-methodology advisor to a multi-site research consortium studying disorders of consciousness, and you have been asked to operationalize Dennett’s heterophenomenological framework for daily use. Our research associates—who hold bachelor’s-level qualifications and have no access to external databases—must evaluate first-person reports from patients every day, some delivered through conventional aided-communication boards and others mediated by brain-computer interface decoders, without committing to the metaphysical reality of whatever experiences are reported. Before drafting anything, please think carefully about the core distinction between treating a report as a heterophenomenological text (that is, as part of the subject’s phenomenological world) versus treating it as direct neurophysiological evidence, and briefly brainstorm the specific failure modes—decoder artifacts, caregiver prompting, confabulation, motor reflex misread as intentional signal—that could corrupt either interpretation. Then write a reusable, step-by-step decision procedure in prose: a flowchart of ordered tests with explicit pass, fail, flag, and “judgment-required” conditions; specify the documentation that must be recorded on a standardized form within a fifteen-minute review window; mark precisely where an associate may not decide alone and the principal investigator must sign off; and include three worked applications: (1) a consistent, richly detailed visual narrative produced via an aided-spelling board; (2) a BCI-decoded statement that flatly contradicts concurrent eye-tracking behavior and the team’s prior clinical assessment; and (3) a brief, ambiguous vocalization that might indicate pain or might be a reflexive sound, where your procedure must yield an answer the clinical team will dislike—namely, exclusion or indefinite suspension despite pressure for rapid determination—together with the reasoning for that outcome. Finally, conclude with an explicit statement of what the procedure cannot do: for example, establish ontological status, replace clinical judgment about level of consciousness, resolve calibration disputes about the decoder, or substitute for informed-consent review. Do not provide bibliographies, reading lists, or citation lists, and do not quote any source verbatim; ground the procedure only in the conceptual structure of heterophenomenology itself.
