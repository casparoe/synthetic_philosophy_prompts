I'm a retired physicist who has recently begun working through problems in ontology on my own, and I'm trying to determine what I actually think about identity when critical individuating information is epistemically inaccessible. Here are my intuitive verdicts on several cases, which I hope can be unified.

Consider a real-time digital twin of a nuclear reactor that continues to run on predictive algorithms after the physical plant is dismantled; I am inclined to say it remains "that reactor" in a meaningful sense, because its causal-functional profile is preserved. By contrast, if a mammalian cell line is cloned and the original culture is destroyed by contamination, I want to say the clone is not "the same line" but a genetic successor. In the black hole information paradox, if evaporation leaves only thermal radiation with no record of the initial state, I judge the black hole is simply gone—not that the radiation is it—despite energy conservation. With quantum entanglement, two particles are prepared in a singlet state and separated; once one is measured and destroyed, I feel there is no fact of the matter whether the surviving particle is "the partner," since indistinguishability makes individual identity inapplicable. Yet with an artwork that is ninety-five percent destroyed by fire and reconstructed from preparatory sketches with chemically matching pigments—so that no original material remains in the rebuilt sections—I still tend to call it "the same painting," sustained by institutional and art-historical continuity rather than material persistence.

The case I keep revisiting involves a legal treaty: the sole paper record is digitized and then shredded. Years later, a backup is discovered to contain a single corrupted character in a critical clause, but without the original there is no way to detect the error. Part of me says the file is still "the treaty" because the chain of custody is intact; part of me says it is not, because its individuating information is now both inaccessible and possibly altered.

Is there a precise principle, or a small family of competing principles, that captures these reactions without forcing me to abandon one? What does each candidate say about the archive case? And which of my verdicts must be revised if we demand consistency—particularly the tension between functional continuity saving the reactor, institutional continuity saving the painting, and informational loss dissolving both the black hole and, perhaps, the treaty?
