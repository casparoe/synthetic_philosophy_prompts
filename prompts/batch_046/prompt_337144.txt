I'm giving the opening remarks at a workshop titled "Intuitions Across Boundaries: Philosophy, Anthropology, and Cognitive Science." The audience is evenly divided between philosophers of culture and experimental philosophers. The former tend to treat reports of cross-cultural divergence in moral judgment—for instance, differences in how participants in broadly collectivist versus individualist societies respond to sacrificial-dilemma scenarios—not as evidence about universal normative intuitions, but as symptoms of deeper incommensurability: the very framing of the problem as an individual choice among fixed options may itself be culturally specific. The latter treat such divergence as precisely the kind of data that can test, and sometimes undermine, the universality of philosophical theories. I want a formal, polished ten-minute address that does not paper over this tension with platitudes about interdisciplinarity. Instead, it should turn on a specific conceptual distinction—between what I will call "surface disagreement" (different answers within a shared conceptual framework) and "architectural disagreement" (disagreement about which framework governs the domain in question)—and show why both sides have a genuine stake in keeping the distinction sharp rather than collapsing one into the other. The speech must be accessible to the anthropologists without being condescending to the philosophers, and it should close by posing a concrete shared question that both groups can recognize as genuinely open, not merely rhetorical. Please write the speech.
