In service economies where tipping is customary but not legally required, a visitor’s failure to tip is routinely framed as a direct moral wrong to the worker, whereas a local who undertips faces only mild social disapproval; similarly, queuing norms and jaywalking rules are enforced against tourists through explicit moral appeals—“please respect our customs”—even though locals violate them with impunity. What theory of moral obligation and normative authority does this differential framing presuppose? Must it hold that visitors acquire associative duties upon entry, that local conventions constitute a bounded moral community with membership conditions, or that the wrongness of the violation is purely expressive and therefore applies only to those presumed to know the code? Reconstruct the implicit view, distinguish what the practice strictly requires from what it merely accommodates, and say what would have to change in host-guest relations if this were replaced by a uniform, context-independent theory treating convention-adherence as purely prudential or contractual.
