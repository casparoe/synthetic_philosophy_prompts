I'm taking this course on consciousness studies and we hit Dennett's heterophenomenology, and my study group is kinda divided on something that feels like a hidden tension in the method itself, not just the usual eliminativism worry. The basic move is treating first-person reports as texts describing a heterophenomenal world — like, the subject's world-as-they-believe-it-to-be — rather than direct readouts of consciousness. So far so good. But then we started arguing about negative reports, like when someone says there was no visual experience at all during that blackout, or my awareness was empty of content. With a positive report you can just file it under that's how their heterophenomenal world is structured without committing to what's really in the brain. But absence reports seem different, because saying there was no X only makes sense against some background expectation that X might have been there.

So is there a built-in asymmetry where heterophenomenology can comfortably absorb presence-reports but has to smuggle in theoretical assumptions to interpret absence-reports? And does this matter for debates where meditators claim to experience pure awareness with no object while other researchers treat that as conceptually incoherrent? I feel like there's a verificationist bias lurking here but I can't quite map it out. Can you walk through the conceptual problem of how heterophenomenology should treat null or negative phenomenological reports, and whether that undermines its claim to treat all first-person data symetrically?
