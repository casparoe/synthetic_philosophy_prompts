(1) If an action can produce an outcome of genuinely infinite value, and that outcome is not literally impossible, then its expected value is infinite regardless of how small the assigned probability is.

(2) A rational agent ought to choose the action with the highest expected value.

(3) There are hypothetical future scenarios—say, a system that, if developed, could eventually sustain an unbounded number of conscious lives—whose existence I cannot assign exactly zero credence without claiming a kind of perfect knowledge I do not actually possess.

(4) So I ought to be willing to pay any finite cost, including extreme personal sacrifice, to take any action that might bring such a system about.

(5) But this reasoning applies not just once; every similar claim I could encounter assigns me another infinite obligation, and together they demand more resources and suffering than any single life can contain.

(6) Therefore either expected-value reasoning collapses whenever unbounded outcomes are in play, or I must assign exactly zero probability to all such scenarios, or I am permanently required to do the impossible.

I want to keep the intuition that I should not dismiss genuinely possible enormous goods merely because they seem remote, but I also do not want to conclude that rationality demands I bankrupt myself for every speculative promise. Which step should I reject—(1), (2), (3), or the move from (4) to (5)—and what does giving it up cost? In particular, if I reject (1), do I have to abandon expected-value reasoning in ordinary life as well, or can I restrict it without an arbitrary stipulation?
