You are a voting member of the Exhibition Ethics and Design Board of the National Museum of Social and Natural History. The curatorial division has submitted a proposal for a permanent gallery, tentatively titled "Distributed Agency: Pathogens, Climate Regimes, and Volcanic Winters in Human History," which treats microbial outbreaks, long-duration climate oscillations, and large-scale volcanic events not as passive background conditions but as causally efficacious entities that reshape political possibilities—what the proposal calls "non-human co-agency." The gallery would feature three core installations: one on the 1815 Tambora eruption's climatic aftermath and its cascading effects on European grain markets and state fiscal policy; one on the microbiological dynamics of the 1918 influenza pandemic and their interaction with wartime mobilization logistics; and one on multi-decadal El Niño–Southern Oscillation cycles and their correlation with patterns of civil conflict in pre-modern agrarian societies.

The board must decide whether to approve the gallery, reject it, or approve it subject to mandatory revisions and a six-month review cycle. A minority of board members—led by a senior historian of modern political economy—argues that framing climate, pathogens, or volcanic aerosols as "agents" collapses into a form of environmental determinism that obscures human intentionality, moral responsibility, and the structural violence of political decisions. They point specifically to an installation comparing labor-market contractions after the Black Death with concurrent peasant revolts, warning that attributing causal weight to the pathogen risks implying that the revolts were inevitable natural outcomes rather than acts of collective political will. The curators reply that their framework distinguishes "causal efficacy" from "intentional action" and includes a "responsibility firewall" methodology, but the critics maintain that this distinction is unstable in visitor-facing text and that the gallery's narrative architecture will inevitably privilege deterministic explanation over human accountability.

Your task is to draft the board's formal decision memo—addressed to the board chair, the director of curatorial affairs, and the museum's public-affairs office—recommending one of the three options. The memo should:

- State the precise philosophical issue at stake: whether non-human forces can be narrated as historical agents without committing to environmental determinism, and what conceptual distinctions (e.g., between causation and agency, or between distributed causation and deterministic necessity) are required to maintain that boundary.
- Offer a clear, actionable recommendation with specific conditions or timelines if you choose approval with revisions.
- Propose at least one concrete, non-standard curatorial mechanism—such as an "intentionality gradient" taxonomy for each display, a "counterfactual responsibility panel" that isolates human decision-points within causal chains, or a "distributed causation visitor protocol" requiring active visitor comparison of human and non-human causal weights—that addresses the determinism objection in a way museum visitors could actually navigate.
- Include a serious, non-dismissive treatment of the determinism objection, identifying the strongest version of that concern and explaining why it either defeats or fails to defeat the proposal.
- Conclude with an honest assessment of the best argument for the option you did not recommend.

Write in formal institutional prose. Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, citation lists, or requests for further reading. Do not invoke highly specialized theoretical vocabularies that depend on knowledge of obscure primary texts; instead, build the conceptual distinctions into the memo's own reasoning. The memo should be self-contained and actionable.
