I am serving as a student referee for a philosophy journal that receives submissions attempting to resolve Benacerraf's dilemma—the conflict between a causal condition on knowledge and the apparently non-causal nature of abstract mathematical objects—after taking a course on epistemology that left me unsure how to apply the argument consistently across papers. To evaluate these submissions without reconstructing each author's formal apparatus from scratch, I need a reusable decision procedure I can run in roughly thirty minutes per paper, with clear outputs that I can defend to the editor. The procedure should translate Benacerraf's core challenge into an ordered sequence of practical tests: first, does the proposal identify a specific causal pathway—direct or mediated—linking the knower to the mathematical fact in question; second, if it denies that causation is necessary, does it provide a principled reason why empirical knowledge is not thereby undermined rather than merely asserting a special exemption for mathematics; third, does it avoid relocating the problem into an unexplained representational proxy, such as a layered mediating system that itself requires epistemic justification without having causal contact with the abstract object; and fourth, does it preserve a meaningful distinction between mere reliability and genuine knowledge, or does it collapse the two? At each stage, specify what counts as a sufficient answer, where the procedure halts with a "framework fails" verdict, and where it must hand the decision back to human judgment because the issue is one of values—specifically, whether mathematical domains ought to be held to stricter causal standards than applied sciences. The procedure should be explicit about where it will misfire: for instance, it may reject appeals to "structural causation" via physical computation even when those appeals are philosophically defensible, and it cannot resolve deep metaphysical disputes about the ontology of numbers.

Apply this checklist to three hypothetical submissions: (a) a neural-symbolic AI paper claiming that arithmetic knowledge arises because silicon-based computation tracks causal regularities in physical counting devices; (b) a pluralist argument that Benacerraf's uniform causal demand is mistaken because pure mathematics and applied mathematics operate under genuinely different epistemic norms; and (c) a "proxy-chain" theory holding that mathematicians access abstract objects through layered representational mediators that form a transitive causal bridge without direct contact. For each case, show precisely where the procedure stops, where it invokes judgment rather than fact, and what recommendation it yields—including one case where the procedure issues an unfavorable verdict despite the view's intuitive plausibility. Finally, please state how confident you are in the procedure's outputs overall, and single out the specific step—particularly the evaluation of mediated or proxy-based causal pathways—that you are least sure survives the translation from Benacerraf's original argument into this practical form.
