I was recently discussing Stoicism with a colleague in clinical psychology who mentioned that several of her patients now use Stoic journaling applications to manage anxiety, and our conversation turned to whether the modern revival has turned the ancient dichotomy of control into something almost unrecognizable. I am preparing notes for a graduate seminar in philosophy of psychology, and I want to work through the tension using a concrete historical case rather than abstract exegesis. Take Admiral James Stockdale’s seven-year imprisonment at the Hanoi Hilton from 1965 to 1973 and analyze it as a test case for the Stoic dichotomy of control—specifically Epictetus’s original formulation, in which the division is not a simple internal-versus-external binary but a tripartite structure: what is fully up to us (judgments, impulses, desires, and moral character, or prohairesis), what is not at all up to us (body, property, reputation, and external outcomes), and an intermediate domain of actions that depend partly on conditions outside our control. I know the standard narrative that Stockdale survived torture by concentrating on what he could control, and I am familiar with the so-called Stockdale paradox—the simultaneous maintenance of absolute faith in eventual release and the unflinching confrontation with brutal facts. What I need is a disciplined mapping: assign each salient feature of Stockdale’s reported experience—his refusal to cooperate with propaganda, his efforts to sustain fellow prisoners, his management of despair, and the eventual political circumstances of his release—to the correct Stoic category, and identify precisely where the modern revival’s tendency to psychologize the dichotomy as a cognitive coping mechanism (akin to locus-of-control exercises in therapy) either captures or obscures the normative, character-building dimension that Epictetus emphasized. Then run the analysis in the other direction: examine whether Stockdale’s survival depended on factors that Stoicism places outside our control, such as his prior military training, the solidarity of other prisoners, or the specific political timing of the Paris Peace Accords, and determine whether those dependencies reveal a blind spot in the ancient theory or instead demonstrate that the modern revival was never genuinely engaged with the original doctrine. Please be explicit about where the theory is silent, avoid forcing the framework to speak where it does not apply, and conclude with what this episode ultimately shows about whether the contemporary Stoic revival has preserved, distorted, or entirely replaced the classical account of human agency.
