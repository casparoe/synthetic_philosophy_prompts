Exam question: Max Weber's thesis on the Protestant ethic holds that Calvinist inner-worldly asceticism contributed to the emergence of modern capitalism not through deterministic causation but through elective affinity with the capitalist spirit. A common philosophical objection is that culture belongs to the realm of meaning and interpretation, whereas causation belongs to the realm of explanation, so that explaining capitalism by appeal to religious values commits a category mistake. Evaluate whether Weber's framework genuinely resolves this tension, or whether his appeal to elective affinity merely relocates the problem. Student answer: Weber's Protestant Ethic thesis argues that Calvinist inner-worldly asceticism stood in an elective affinity with the capitalist spirit—not as a deterministic cause, but as a historical congruence that made capitalist practices meaningful and sustainable. This resolves the tension between culture and causation because Weber assigns values to the interpretive sphere of Verstehen, where they function exclusively as constitutive conditions rather than efficient causes. The mistake critics make—following the category-error objection that Weber himself raises—is to demand that culture operate like a natural-law variable. Instead, Weber shows that salvation anxiety created a psychological premium for systematic accumulation at the societal level, but this premium only worked because the religious value-system had already constituted worldly success as a sign of election. Therefore, culture is not an external constraint like Durkheim's social facts, but an internal orientation that acquires causal force through the motives it supplies. Elective affinity thus occupies a precise methodological middle ground: it explains without reducing meaning to mechanism, and it allows values to be causally relevant without being mechanically deterministic. The thesis succeeds precisely because it refuses to treat values as either pure causes or pure meanings, even though elective affinity remains only a heuristic metaphor.
