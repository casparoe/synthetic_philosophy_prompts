I am a retired physicist who, since leaving the laboratory, has taken up philosophy with the same methodical patience I once applied to experimental design. Recently, in discussions with a reading group composed largely of fellow scientists, I have become absorbed by Augustine's hierarchical classification of falsehood—specifically, his gradation from malicious deception through jocular exaggeration to the corruption of sacred teaching—and I have attempted to apply this framework to a problem that strikes me as structurally analogous but conceptually under-examined. In scientific communication, particularly when translating probabilistic or statistically nuanced findings for a general audience, a researcher often faces a tension between literal precision and communicative efficacy: one might present a correlation as more deterministic than the data warrant, not from malice or self-interest, but from a conviction that the audience's cognitive limitations make full disclosure more misleading than a carefully calibrated simplification.

In my notes, I have tentatively mapped this practice onto what I understand to be Augustine's intermediate grades—those falsehoods told without intent to harm but which nevertheless depart from literal truth—while wondering whether the researcher's appeal to epistemic charity, perhaps a kind of qualified disclosure intended to preserve the listener's understanding rather than deceive, could function as a moral justification analogous to the distinctions Augustine draws between deception that corrupts faith and falsehood arising from other motives. I am concerned, however, that I may be conflating Augustine's theological commitment to absolute veracity with a pragmatic ethics of information design, and I may be inventing a category—this notion of pedagogical qualified disclosure in empirical pedagogy—that has no genuine parallel in his moral psychology.

Would you be willing to comment on these notes? I am especially interested in whether the analogy between scientific simplification and Augustine's graded taxonomy holds up conceptually, whether there is a hidden paradox in treating communicative efficacy as a justification for literal falsehood within his framework, and whether my introduction of an epistemic-charity principle distorts the rigor of his account. I do not need references or a bibliography—merely your assessment of whether the argument, as sketched, is philosophically coherent or whether it collapses into an anachronistic projection of modern information ethics onto patristic theology.
