My thesis is that Leibnizian morality is best reconstructed not as a theological deontology nor as a proto-Kantian rationalism, but as a global variational principle: moral laws function analogously to the Euler-Lagrange conditions of a constrained optimization, in which God selects the actual world by minimizing metaphysical evil (disorder, suffering, moral failure) subject to the hard constraint of maximal compossible being, and in which human freedom operates not as an exemption from natural law but as a boundary condition within the same calculus. I will present this using only the conceptual apparatus of Leibniz’s own texts—compossibility, the principle of sufficient reason, the hierarchy of perfections, and the distinction between hypothetical and absolute necessity—though I will frame them with the vocabulary of constrained optimization familiar from my former field.

The audience will be eight to ten specialists: three Leibniz scholars deeply familiar with the Theodicy and the correspondence with Clarke, two Kant scholars suspicious of any reduction of moral law to cosmic optimization, one historian of science who knows variational mechanics but little philosophy, and two graduate students in philosophy of religion. They care about anachronism, textual fidelity, and whether I am smuggling modern physics into seventeenth-century theology. They will not be impressed by analogies unless those analogies earn their explanatory keep.

Please draft my Q&A prep sheet. Order the objections by the damage they would do to my position if left unanswered, from most lethal to least. Indicate which objections I should concede partially up front and which I must hold without concession. Include the single objection I am hoping nobody asks—the one that exposes the deepest structural weakness in my variational framing—and advise me whether to defend it head-on or to deflect.
