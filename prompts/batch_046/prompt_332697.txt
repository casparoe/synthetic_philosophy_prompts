The exam prompt, which I have reproduced below, introduces a hypothetical technology—call it neuro-aesthetic replication—that can induce in a viewer the precise neural activation pattern produced by viewing an original Vermeer, without any canvas, pigment, or causal connection to Vermeer’s hand. The question asks whether the resulting experience counts as an encounter with the work, a forgery, or something else entirely, and it requires the student to explain whether Nelson Goodman’s distinction between autographic and allographic arts supports the claim that the original painting retains value the replication lacks, and whether Arthur Danto’s institutional or “artworld” theory undermines that conclusion, with precision about what each philosopher actually claims.

Question: Goodman divides arts into autographic categories, in which the specific history of production is constitutive of the work’s identity, and allographic categories, in which a notational scheme permits multiple genuine instances. Danto, for his part, maintains that perceptual indiscernibility between objects does not guarantee artistic identity, since interpretation within an appropriate theoretical framework is decisive. Apply these positions to the neuro-aesthetic replication case described above. Is the induced experience an encounter with the artwork, a forgery, or a distinct category? Does Goodman’s framework imply that the original retains a value—ontological or otherwise—that the replication cannot claim? And does Danto’s emphasis on institutional interpretation actually undermine Goodman’s conclusion about causal history, or does it address a different problem altogether? Be explicit about the steps in your reasoning.

Student answer: Goodman separates autographic arts, like painting, from allographic arts, like music or literature. In autographic cases, the work just is that particular object with that particular causal history, so even a perfect visual copy is not the original but a forgery. In allographic cases, the work is defined by a notational scheme, so any score or performance that conforms to the notation is a genuine instance rather than a fake. This means that for painting, the original retains a special status no duplicate can share. Turning to Danto, his theory says that artistic status depends on interpretation within an artworld, not on perceptible properties alone. His well-known examples of indiscernible objects—such as an ordinary Brillo box versus Warhol’s version—show that two items can look identical yet differ in artistic value. Applying this to neuro-aesthetic replication, one might think that if the replication is presented within the right institutional context and interpreted as a Vermeer, it acquires the same artistic status as the original canvas. Therefore, Danto undermines Goodman’s insistence on causal history: what matters is not how the experience was produced, but how it is received by an audience with the correct theoretical background. Goodman might reply that in autographic arts the work is that specific physical object, so replicating a viewer’s brain state does not replicate the work itself but only an effect of it. I think this shows that while Goodman protects the uniqueness of originals through ontology, Danto makes originality depend on interpretation. Still, both philosophers agree that a perfect fake lacks something important that the original possesses, even if they disagree about whether that missing element is metaphysical or institutional.
