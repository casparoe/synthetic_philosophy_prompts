At a recent roundtable on comparative economic history, I witnessed two scholars — one an historian of British industrialisation, the other of Qing economic institutions — become locked in a disagreement over whether the canonical framing 'Why Britain and not China?' is genuinely well posed. I am preparing a long-form feature on how conceptual framing shapes historical explanation, and I should like an adjudication of their exchange. Please reply in British English, and do not provide bibliographies, reading lists, or citations.

The first scholar (A) opened by defending the contrastive question. She argued that around 1750 Britain and Qing China were the two largest, most technologically advanced agrarian economies, with comparable levels of commercialisation, proto-industrial output, and state capacity to mobilise resources. Given that similarity, she said, asking why one underwent sustained mechanised industrial growth while the other remained within what economic historians term a 'high-level equilibrium' is not only legitimate but necessary: without the contrast, we are left with parochial narratives of British exceptionalism rather than causal explanations. She insisted that comparative history necessarily selects on outcomes to some degree, and that the Needham question — why China, despite earlier technological leadership, did not generate an indigenous industrial revolution — supplies precisely the counterfactual leverage required to isolate factors such as coal geography, property rights, and the organisation of scientific knowledge.

The second scholar (B) pushed back. He said the framing commits what he called 'modal selection bias': it picks Britain and China because we know the outcome, not because they were ex ante the most plausible candidates for industrial take-off. He noted that the Indian subcontinent, the Ottoman Empire, and parts of Southeast Asia also possessed large markets and sophisticated craft production, yet they rarely feature in the same comparative breath because hindsight makes China's pre-eminence around 1400 more salient. More fundamentally, he argued that 'industrialisation' is not a single binary outcome but a heterogeneous cluster — mechanised textile production, fossil-fuel energy transitions, chemical manufacturing, and financial innovation — each with different preconditions. Comparing Britain and China as if they were running the same race, he said, treats institutional paths as interchangeable modules. He suggested that the Qing economy was not a 'failed' industrialiser but a successful adaptation to a different equilibrium: one optimised for labour-absorbing agrarian productivity and state-mediated resource allocation rather than coal-intensive, capital-deep mechanisation. Consequently, he concluded, the explanatory work should ask what regional conditions made fossil-fuel mechanisation profitable in north-western Europe, rather than treating China's trajectory as a deviation from a British norm.

A replied that B was overstating the difficulty. She granted that industrialisation bundles several processes, but said that causal inference in history necessarily requires holding background conditions constant and varying institutional or geographic factors. The Britain–China pair does exactly that: both were large, unified empires with long-distance trade, yet they diverged sharply after 1800. To abandon the contrast, she argued, is to abandon comparative method altogether and retreat into descriptive particularism. She also observed that B's 'successful adaptation' framing risks its own teleology — it implies that every path was optimally suited to its environment, which makes it impossible to explain why some regions achieved sustained per-capita growth while others did not.

B countered that he was not rejecting comparison per se, but insisting on 'explanatory parity' — the principle that comparators should share the same possibility frontier with respect to the outcome being explained. He claimed that Britain's access to Atlantic trade networks and New World resources, combined with specific coal deposits, created a contingency space that Qing China, with its different ecological and geopolitical constraints, simply did not inhabit. Thus, asking 'why not China' presupposes a counterfactual stability that historical processes do not provide. He proposed reframing the inquiry around 'institutional branching points' — moments where similar initial conditions led to divergent organisational forms — rather than around a binary outcome comparison.

A concluded by saying that even if B's branching-points framework is valuable, it still needs to explain why the branch leading to fossil-fuel industrialisation occurred in Britain and not elsewhere. Without naming the path that did not occur, she said, the framework loses its critical edge.

Please adjudicate this exchange move by move: rate each of the five arguments (A's opening defence, B's modal-selection-bias objection, A's reply on causal inference and particularism, B's explanatory-parity counter, and A's concluding point about critical edge) on a scale from clearly mistaken to clearly right, with brief justification for each rating. Then state what, if anything, both parties are missing — in particular, whether their disagreement is primarily empirical, conceptual, or both, and whether there is a framing that could accommodate both of their concerns without collapsing into either exceptionalism or teleology. Please write entirely in British English.
