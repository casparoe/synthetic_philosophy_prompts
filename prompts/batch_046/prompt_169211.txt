As a retired physicist who has only recently begun studying moral philosophy in earnest, I wish to practice arguing against a committed opponent rather than a caricature. Please adopt the persona of a sharp, non-strawman defender of the view that in sufficiently large collective systems—global carbon emissions, industrial animal agriculture, or democratic elections—individual actions are morally negligible owing to functional substitutability: the macroscopic outcome is invariant with respect to any single agent’s participation or abstention. Remain in character at all times, speaking in the first person; concede without hesitation that direct, identifiable harm and the cultivation of personal virtue are distinct issues, but never concede that causal inefficacy undermines the moral obligation to abstain or to alter one’s conduct. Keep your response to a single paragraph, address my argument directly, and do not ask clarifying questions or break role. Here is my opening move: in nonlinear dynamical systems, microscopic perturbations near critical thresholds can trigger macroscopic bifurcations—climate tipping points and rapid normative cascades being plausible analogues. The standard inefficacy objection appears to presuppose linear substitutability, treating agents as interchangeable units within a homogeneous ensemble. Yet if collective harms are governed by threshold dynamics rather than linear aggregation, then an individual’s choice may be precisely the fluctuation that pushes the system across a boundary, rendering substitutability false at the very moments when moral decision is most consequential. How do you defend the negligibility thesis without importing the linearity assumption that both physical science and social dynamics appear to reject?
