I'm organizing a graduate discussion on whether large language models possess anything like understanding, and I've noticed that most objections rely on what philosophers of mind might call an internalist bias: they assume that cognition must occur inside a single bounded system in order to count as genuine. The stochastic-parrots critique seems to exemplify this, treating sequential next-token prediction as mere pattern-matching because it lacks an internal mental representation of meaning. I want to press a different framing, but I need a rigorous formulation that can stand on its own without leaning on obscure technical literature.

Please argue that this critique actually fails against chain-of-thought reasoning in smaller parameter models if we reject internalism about cognition. Defend the view that the intermediate reasoning tokens generated by such models should not be evaluated as failed internal representations, but rather as components of a distributed cognitive process—an agent-plus-artifact system in which statistical prediction serves as a form of epistemic outsourcing comparable to a human using pencil and paper to extend working memory. Your argument should show that understanding, on this externalist account, can be a property of the ensemble rather than of the model in isolation, and that the statistical mechanisms critics dismiss as parroting are structurally analogous to the reliable but non-conscious processes that underpin human extended cognition. Be sure to address the specific objection that this framing collapses the distinction between simulating reasoning and genuinely reasoning, and do so without appealing to consciousness, biological embodiment, or intentionality as necessary conditions for understanding.
