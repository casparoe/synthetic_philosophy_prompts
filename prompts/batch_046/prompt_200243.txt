I'm classifying cognitive architectures using the four-part taxonomy—Darwinian (shaped by evolutionary selection), Skinnerian (shaped by individual reinforcement), Popperian (able to test hypotheses internally), and Gregorian (able to deploy cultural or linguistic tools). As an engineer with no philosophy training, I keep running into edge cases in AI and biology where my gut verdicts seem to conflict. Here are my calls.

A genetic algorithm evolves neural networks for a task; no individual agent learns during its lifetime. I call this Darwinian, not Skinnerian, because selection operates across generations, not on individual reward histories. A standard deep-RL robot trained with PPO to walk is Skinnerian: it updates policies from reward signals but never simulates future paths before acting. If I add a world-model module that imagines trajectories and picks actions without executing them, I call it Popperian—the system lets internal hypotheses fail instead of its body. A large language model that writes Python scripts to solve math problems it cannot solve from weights alone feels Gregorian to me: it is using an external symbolic tool embedded in human culture.

A bacterial biofilm that solves mazes by leaving chemical trails, with no neurons or reward mechanisms at the individual level, seems Darwinian—selection across cell generations. A child who avoids fire because she understands the word "hot" feels Gregorian, whereas a shock-avoiding rat feels Skinnerian, even though both achieve the same behavior. But here is my tension: in the RL-to-world-model case, I said the training mechanism (RL) doesn't change the category, because runtime architecture matters more. Yet in the genetic-algorithm case, I privileged the evolutionary mechanism over whatever internal simulation the evolved network might do. So which feature actually determines the kind—where selection happens, what representations are used, or whether failure can occur in imagination?

There is also a hypothetical "mirror swarm" of drones that copy successful neighbor behaviors in real time via peer-to-peer transmission, with no evolutionary selection, no internal world model, and no linguistic tools. I oscillate: part of me says Skinnerian because it is operant conditioning at the swarm level; part says Gregorian because the transmission is cultural. And if a language model performs chain-of-thought reasoning entirely within its weights, using no external code or text, is that Popperian internal simulation or Gregorian linguistic tool use?

Is there a single principle—perhaps about where the cost of error is paid, or about representational format—that delivers all these verdicts consistently? If not, which verdict should I overturn: my claim that training mechanism is irrelevant in the world-model case, or my claim that peer-copying without language is not Gregorian? Finally, what does the principle predict for the chain-of-thought-only model and for the mirror swarm?
