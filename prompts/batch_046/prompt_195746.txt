I have been trying to understand Bradley's regress and its modern resolution. As I see it, Bradley argued that if we relate two terms—say, a and b—by a relation R, then we must relate R to a and R to b, which requires further relations, generating an infinite regress. Russell is usually said to have dissolved the problem by treating relations as terms that do not themselves need relating; they simply hold between relata. But this strikes me as leaving a deeper difficulty untouched. Even if we avoid the infinite hierarchy, we still need an account of what makes 'a R b' a single unified proposition rather than a mere aggregate of a, R, and b. Russell's logical atomism seems to offer only a kind of structural silence here: the list is present, but the binding is unexplained. Some philosophers now suggest that logical form supplies the missing unity, yet logical form itself appears to be just another relational item in the inventory. If that is right, then have we not merely relocated Bradley's regress rather than resolved it? So my question is this: does the contemporary rejection of Bradley's regress depend on conflating the elimination of an infinite hierarchy with an explanation of propositional unity? And if the unity problem is genuinely distinct, what would an adequate account of it look like without reintroducing the very regress that Russell sought to avoid?
