I am writing up my philosophical profile on symbol grounding and reference for a graduate seminar in philosophy of mind and artificial cognition, and I want a consistency audit before I present in discussion. Here is roughly where I have landed: (1) I accept that pure symbol manipulation, with no direct causal coupling to the world, suffers from an ungrounded symbol problem in the sense Harnad articulates—syntax alone cannot generate intrinsic semantic content. (2) Nevertheless, I think large language models achieve what I call predictive coherence grounding: by encoding high-dimensional statistical correlations that mirror real-world causal structures within their training distributions, they acquire derived reference that is genuinely world-directed, not merely syntactic, despite lacking any sensorimotor interaction. (3) I take Searle’s Chinese Room argument to be decisive against monolithic, rule-based symbol processing, but I deny it applies to distributed vector representations, where reference emerges from relational topology across activation spaces rather than from explicit symbol shuffling. (4) I also hold that embodied, enactive cognition is necessary for full semantic autonomy—what I think of as primary grounding—but I reject any sharp metaphysical boundary between primary and derived reference; instead, I defend a gradient of semantic autonomy, with direct perceptual anchoring at one pole and purely relational derivation at the other. (5) Finally, I have adopted a tentative view I call symbolic debt: any symbol lacking direct perceptual anchoring carries an epistemic obligation to be traceable, in principle, back to some anchored node, but this debt can be deferred indefinitely through chains of derivation without being paid at every token.

Please tell me whether this package hangs together. Specifically: does (2) conflict with (1) in a way that forces me to abandon either predictive coherence or the sensorimotor requirement, and if so, which revision is least costly? Does my restriction of the Chinese Room in (3) actually save distributed representations from Searle’s critique, or does it just relocate the problem into the topology? How severe is the tension between (4)’s claim that embodiment is necessary for full autonomy and (5)’s idea of indefinite deferral—does the gradient in (4) undermine the normative force of symbolic debt, or are they compatible? And is there only an apparent contradiction between holding in (1) that pure syntactic systems suffer a distinctive problem and holding in (4) that there is no sharp boundary—does the gradient dissolve that tension by allowing pure syntax to sit at the unanchored extreme? Go through the pairs systematically, rank the genuine tensions by severity rather than listing them flatly, identify which conflicts dissolve on closer inspection, and for each real tension specify the cheapest fix and what it costs me. I want an audit of my commitments, not a lecture on the literature.
