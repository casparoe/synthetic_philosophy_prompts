I have been working through the literature on virtue ethics and the situationist critique—particularly the body of empirical findings suggesting that moral behavior correlates more strongly with situational variables than with stable, cross-situational character traits—and I have come to defend a modified position I am calling modular virtue ethics. On this view, virtues are not globally uniform dispositions but are anchored to specific institutional or relational modules (for instance, professional roles, kinship structures, or civic contexts), so that a person may genuinely possess the virtue of honesty within one module while failing to display it in another without this being reducible to hypocrisy, weakness of will, or mere situational noise. The central claim is that once virtues are reconceived as locally stable but globally fragmented dispositions—each sustained by the norms, incentives, and feedback mechanisms particular to its module—the situationist data cease to be a straightforward refutation and instead become a map of where different modules begin and end.

What would genuinely count against this position? I am not asking for a restatement of the standard situationist objections, but for the specific observations, conceptual arguments, or empirical patterns that should weaken or defeat modular virtue ethics in particular, with a clear distinction between fatal defeaters and merely awkward ones. For example, if studies showed that people lack even module-specific consistency—say, that a clinician’s honesty in direct patient care fails to predict her honesty in administrative tasks within the same hospital—would that be decisive, or could the modular view absorb it by subdividing modules further? Would a demonstration that all apparently local virtues reduce to context-sensitive habits lacking any normative structure be fatal, or would it simply offer a rival framing? I also want an honest assessment of whether the position risks being qualified into unfalsifiability: if every counterexample can be handled by carving out a new sub-module, is that a theoretical vice for this kind of view, or is it a legitimate feature of any genuinely context-sensitive ethics? Finally, please indicate what defenders of modular virtue ethics themselves would concede as counting against them, as opposed to objections they would dismiss, and rank the potential defeaters by how decisive they would be. Be explicit about where the view makes risky predictions that distinguish it from both traditional global-trait virtue ethics and pure situationism.
