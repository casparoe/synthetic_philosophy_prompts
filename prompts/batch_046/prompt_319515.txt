In our philosophy-of-mind seminar on moral status as a matter of degree, my professor has endorsed the following judgments: adult humans possess full direct moral status; pigs, cows, and octopuses possess substantial status, making unnecessary suffering seriously wrong; fish possess minimal but nonzero status, so that quick killing for food is permissible but gratuitous harm remains wrong; individual insects lack direct status, though hive destruction can be wrong for indirect ecological reasons; and both simple thermostats and current large language models possess exactly zero direct status, being purely instrumental. Before she turns to the remaining syllabus cases, I am trying to reconstruct her implicit principle. How would she likely rule on a lobster with a distributed nervous system, on a Portuguese man o' war that functions as a unified agent but is biologically a colony of zooids, and on a human-derived brain organoid displaying sustained neural activity but no behavioral output? I am less interested in the predictions themselves than in identifying the competing frameworks—such as neural integration, substrate-independent phenomenal capacity, behavioral flexibility as proxy, or biological embodiment—that are equally consistent with her prior verdicts, in saying which of these three cases best discriminates among them, and in assessing whether her denial of status to language models but acceptance for octopuses genuinely follows from any of those frameworks or instead reflects an unstated prejudice.
