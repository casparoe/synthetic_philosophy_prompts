As a journalist covering decision theory and its public reception, I keep encountering the maxim “if the stakes are high enough, any nonzero probability dominates,” usually invoked to defend longshot bets on transformative technology or, more provocatively, to dismiss objections to Pascal-style mugging. In a recent discussion, an interlocutor used it to shut down skepticism about yielding resources to an arbitrary agent who promises an astronomical payoff: since expected-value arithmetic allegedly requires compliance whenever utility is unbounded, refusal is supposedly irrational. I want to dissect the language rather than accept the move. Can you lay out, as a precise menu, the distinct theses this one-liner could actually be expressing—from the tautological observation that larger products outweigh smaller ones, to the strong claim that rational agents must maximize expected utility across all conceivable outcomes regardless of plausibility? For each reading, indicate whether it is a platitude, a defensible but substantive principle, or false; specify whether it depends on controversial premises about unbounded utility, subjective probability calibration, or aggregation across persons; and identify which of these readings, if any, genuinely supports the mugger’s conclusion and which are merely being smuggled in by rhetorical talk of “dominance.” Present the disambiguation so that I can see exactly which version is being appealed to when the slogan is used to end an argument, and flag any readings that collapse into incoherence once separated from the others.
