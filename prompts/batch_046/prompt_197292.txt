Plan a fifty-minute discussion section for first-year philosophy students who have just encountered Ryle's distinction between knowing how and knowing that. The students are convinced that knowing how is merely a subspecies of propositional knowledge directed at practical contents, and they repeatedly invoke bicycle-riding as an example of "knowing that one should lean left." Construct the explanatory arc: identify the prior conceptual distinction that must be secured before Ryle's anti-intellectualist argument can take hold; select one carrying example to replace the bicycle case, explaining precisely why the standard example collapses with this particular misconception; map the misconceptions students will form in sequence — particularly the "unconscious propositional knowledge" response and the reduction of ability to "disposition conjoined with belief" — and indicate the exact point in the arc where each must be intercepted; design one closing diagnostic question that separates genuine comprehension of the conceptual independence of knowing how from mere repetition of Ryle's slogans; and state explicitly which element of contemporary cognitive-science discussions of procedural memory should be omitted at this level, with a brief justification of why its inclusion would derail the philosophical point rather than clarify it.
