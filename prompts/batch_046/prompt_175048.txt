I sit on our university's human-subjects review board and we've got this weird proposal sitting in front of us. It's a cognitive-psych study that wants to test whether people can have what's called luminous access to their own confidence states — basically, whether if you're 70% sure of something you automatically know you're 70% sure, with no margin for error. The PI is borrowing from that epistemology literature about the KK principle (you know that you know) and wants to use a deception protocol where subjects rate their certainty on visual tasks but are actually being tested on whether they can report second-order knowledge about that certainty. Half the board thinks this is just routine methodological deception with debriefng, but the other half — including me — are worried that if luminosity fails then the study is built on a false model of self-knowledge and might mislead participants about what they're being asked to do epistemically. One reviewer even nicknamed it a "luminosity audit" but that's just informal.

We need to vote Thursday and I have to circulate a short written opinion before the meeting. Can you draft the board's decision memo — approve, reject, or approve with conditions — with reasons the researchers could actually respond to? I want the memo to take seriously the idea that if the KK principle doesn't hold for mental states then requiring subjects to have transparent second-order access might be asking them to do something conceptually impossible, but also address the counter that even if luminosity is false the study can still measure first-order accuracy. Don't ask for bibliographies or citations; just write it like a working document with a concrete recommendation and a time frame for any conditions. Also please engage the strongest consideration on the losing side honestly rather than just restating policy language. Maybe mention that the PI offered to add an explicit "no second-order claim" disclaimer in the consent form, which some of us think solves the problem and others think misses the point entirely.
