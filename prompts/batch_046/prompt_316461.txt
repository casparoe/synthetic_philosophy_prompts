Exam question: Sartre claims we are condemned to be free even in situations of extreme coercion. Explain what he means by radical freedom and bad faith, being careful about the distinction between facticity and transcendence. Then apply both concepts to this case: A concert pianist, after a disastrous recital, tells herself "My hands betrayed me — I am not responsible, since the technical failure was in my fingers, not in me." Analyze whether this attitude counts as bad faith on Sartre's view, and whether her claim that she can genuinely split her real self off from her bodily facticity holds up. Be precise about which pole of the dyad she is actually denying.

Student answer:

Sartre's idea of radical freedom means we're always free to choose the meaning of our situation, even when we can't change the external facts. Being condemned to be free just means we can't escape making choices about how we relate to our facticity — our body, past, and situation. Bad faith is when you lie to yourself about this freedom, usually by treating yourself as pure facticity (like an object with no choice) or as pure transcendence (like a god with no body or past).

In the pianist case, she is clearly treating herself as pure facticity. She identifies her real self with her consciousness and pushes all blame onto her hands, which she sees as just part of her bodily facticity. So she's denying her transcendence — she's acting like her consciousness had nothing to do with the failure, as if her hands acted on their own. This is bad faith because she's fleeing from the fact that she chose her practice regimen, her interpretation of the music, and her emotional attitude toward performing.

But Sartre also says we never actually lose our freedom. Even in prison you're free to choose resistance or despair. So since the pianist can still choose how to respond to the failure — practice more, change careers, etc. — she hasn't actually lost her freedom. That means she's not really in bad faith; she's just made a causal mistake about where the failure came from. Bad faith requires actually suspending your freedom, which never happens. So her statement is wrong about responsibility, but it's not the specific self-deception Sartre calls bad faith.
