I've been working through Quine's gavagai thought experiment, in which a field linguist hears a speaker shout gavagai whenever a rabbit runs past, yet the behavioral evidence—patterns of assent and dissent—never fixes whether the term refers to rabbits, to undetached rabbit parts, or to temporal rabbit-stages. In my philosophy class, the instructor drew an analogy to scientific theory choice: just as two physicists might hold empirically equivalent theories that postulate different unobservable entities, two translators can build empirically equivalent manuals that assign different references to gavagai, with the observable data leaving the rest free. She concluded that translation is therefore underdetermined in exactly the same way science is, and that we should treat reference as a conventional choice rather than a discovery. Another student pushed back, arguing that in science we at least hope for a future decisive experiment—say, measuring a particle's spin—whereas in Quine's framework the analytical hypotheses, the part of translation not fixed by stimulus meaning, are permanently insulated from observation, so the analogy misleads us into thinking the indeterminacy is temporary confusion rather than permanent.
