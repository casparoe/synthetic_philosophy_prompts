As a retired experimental physicist who has spent the last few years working through contemporary philosophy of science and technology, I find the discourse on artificial intelligence alignment conceptually muddled in a way that reminds me of early debates about quantum measurement: everyone uses the same vocabulary, but they are describing different phenomena. I am trying to understand whether the alignment problem denotes a single coherent research question or an umbrella for several distinct conceptual difficulties—perhaps involving value specification, behavioral robustness under distributional shift, and the ontological status of preferences in systems that do not possess biological drives. To help me orient my own study and possibly advise a small interdisciplinary reading group, I would like a dependency-aware prioritization.

Specifically, I see at least four candidate lines of conceptual work. First, clarifying whether alignment is fundamentally a problem of preference aggregation across human stakeholders, which carries a social-choice dimension. Second, determining whether it requires solving the specification problem for values that resist explicit formalization, which touches philosophy of language and ethics. Third, assessing whether robustness failures are best understood as epistemic limitations of the system or as structural misalignments between its objective function and its operational environment, a philosophy-of-science dimension. Fourth, investigating whether the framing of alignment presupposes a particular metaphysics of agency that may not apply to artificial architectures, a philosophy-of-mind dimension. Among these, which questions are genuinely upstream—such that settling them would restructure the others—versus which are largely independent or derivative? I am particularly interested in which lines are tractable for philosophers without direct access to large-scale model training, and which depend so heavily on empirical machine-learning progress that philosophical analysis is currently premature.

Furthermore, I have been struck by an observation from risk theory: just as undefined expected values in decision problems, such as those arising in certain non-ergodic or unbounded-utility frameworks, can make rational choice theory indeterminate, alignment failures might involve situations where expected harm is formally undefined rather than merely high. This suggests an additional, perhaps neglected, conceptual task: determining whether alignment should be modeled within standard expected-utility frameworks at all, or whether it requires a different normative architecture—perhaps one that treats extreme tail risks as categorically different from quantifiable probabilities. Given limited intellectual resources—say, a philosopher's career span or a small grant—how should effort be distributed among these conceptual divisions? Which two or three open questions deserve priority, not merely because they are unsolved, but because resolving them would change how the remaining work is structured?
