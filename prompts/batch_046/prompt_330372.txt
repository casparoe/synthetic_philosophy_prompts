I am a journalist who covers the philosophy and practice of science, and next month I am presenting the central thesis of a long-form feature at an interdisciplinary science-studies seminar. The argument I am defending is that in climate attribution modeling, the canonical philosophical framing of underdetermination—where observational evidence is logically compatible with multiple competing theories—fails to capture the actual epistemic blockage. I contend that what operates in practice is a structural phenomenon I call "reflexive validation lock-in": the benchmark datasets and cross-validation protocols used to test model architectures are themselves produced by parameterization schemes embedded within those same architectures, so that evidence can reject an outlier parameter choice but cannot adjudicate between competing structural frameworks, since every available validation benchmark carries the assumptions of at least one framework being evaluated. The result is not symmetric logical underdetermination but an asymmetric crowding of the parameter space that renders structural falsification computationally intractable and institutionally invisible, without being formally impossible.

The audience will be divided precisely. About half are philosophers of science with backgrounds in scientific realism, the Duhem-Quine problem, and debates over non-empirical virtues; they tend to treat underdetermination as either overstated or resolvable through theoretical coherence and predictive success. The other half are practicing climate modelers and science-policy researchers—several of whom have worked on IPCC assessment cycles—who are skeptical of philosophical abstraction but fluent in ensemble forecasting, out-of-sample testing, and structural uncertainty quantification. There are also two science journalists in attendance who report on climate but have no formal training in philosophy of science.

Prepare me for the objections they will actually raise, ordered by likelihood and potential damage. For each objection, phrase it in the vocabulary of the specific interlocutor who will deliver it—philosophers invoking theory-ladenness and virtue-based selection, modelers invoking independent validation streams and structural error budgets, journalists asking whether this claim undermines public confidence in attribution statements. For each, provide a concise reply I could deliver in roughly two minutes, and flag explicitly where the reply is soft or concedes ground I do not want to lose. Identify what I should concede up front about the practical limits of philosophical framing for working scientists, and distinguish objections that have genuine force from those that are largely rhetorical defenses of the modeling status quo. Finally, include the objection I am hoping nobody asks—the one that exposes the weakest point in my own position, specifically whether reflexive validation lock-in differs in any operationally meaningful way from ordinary inter-model dependence or shared auxiliary assumptions in standard Duhem-Quine cases—and tell me how to defend that point rather than hoping the audience misses it.
