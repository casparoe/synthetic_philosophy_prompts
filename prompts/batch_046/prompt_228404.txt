I'm currently preparing a seminar contribution on whether we can speak coherently of long-term moral progress with respect to collective violence, and I've found that most debates collapse into disputes over measurement rather than substance. To clarify my own position, I want to evaluate how defensible different quantification strategies are as objective, intertemporally comparable benchmarks. Please rank the following five conceptualizations of violence-trend assessment into tiers—say, from highly defensible to seriously flawed—according to how well each survives scrutiny as a neutral metric rather than a normative framing in descriptive clothing. For each tier, provide a brief justification in a sentence or two.

The candidates are: (1) age-adjusted per-capita fatalities in organized interstate and civil warfare, normalized against global population; (2) absolute annual death tolls from all forms of collective physical violence, including state and non-state actors; (3) a structural-violence index tracking excess mortality attributable to institutional deprivation—preventable disease, malnutrition under coercive regimes—relative to a democratic counterfactual baseline; (4) population-weighted aggregates of subjective security and fear-of-violence surveys; and (5) a synthetic construct I'm tentatively calling distributed harm density, defined as total direct and indirect physical and psychological harm divided by the square root of the affected geographic area, meant to penalize metrics that ignore spatial diffusion of trauma. In your ranking, indicate which of these is most susceptible to the charge that it covertly embeds moral judgments about what counts as harm, and whether that susceptibility undermines its use in empirical arguments about moral progress altogether. Finally, note whether any pairing among the five strikes you as genuinely incommensurable, or whether a single composite index could in principle integrate them.
