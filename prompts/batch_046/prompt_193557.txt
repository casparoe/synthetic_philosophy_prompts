Design a 90-minute classroom simulation for a graduate seminar of 14 students who have read Keynes's 1930 essay on long-run economic possibilities but have not studied his Bloomsbury-era writings on ethics. The goal is to make participants feel, through structured choice rather than lecture, the force of Keynes's transition problem: once satiable absolute needs are met, insatiable relative needs—those whose satisfaction depends on feeling superior to others—do not disappear, and the very habits of economic rationality become obstacles to recognizing leisure, art, and contemplation as non-instrumental goods. Embed Keynes's distinction between absolute and relative needs directly in the mechanics: give each participant a hidden profile showing which goods they treat as comparative status markers versus intrinsic satisfactions, then ask the group to negotiate a collective post-abundance charter that caps labor and redistributes time. Use a payoff scheme with small, tangible stakes—points that translate into a minor collective reward—so that participants genuinely experience the pull of working more to maintain relative position even after everyone has cleared the absolute-need threshold. The rules should be arranged so that students discover for themselves that artistic or leisure goods get converted into status signals and that unanimous agreement on reduced work hours collapses once hidden comparative preferences are revealed.

The deliverable should include: a precise timeline broken into allocation, negotiation, reveal, and vote phases; the role or card structure that hides information; the payoff formula; materials needed (paper and tokens only); and explicit rules that prevent the facilitator from explaining the concept before the failure occurs. Follow with a debrief that maps each phase onto Keynes's claim that solving the economic problem requires not merely abundance but a moral revolution away from the love of money; identify what will predictably go wrong the first time—likely that students will try to solve the coordination failure by adding more information rather than changing values, or that they will treat the artistic goods as productivity investments; and state honestly which feature of Keynes's view the simulation cannot reproduce: specifically, his idea—drawn from the ethical framework he shared with Moore—that the good life consists in cultivating non-comparative good states of mind (love, beauty, knowledge) in a manner that is implicitly elitist and incompatible with democratic equality, which the facilitator must supply in the closing discussion. Conclude with a brief note on how to adapt the hidden-card distribution if the group is smaller or larger, and be frank about which aspect of Keynes's prediction the activity deliberately exaggerates to make the dynamic visible.
