I chair the Municipal Archives and Public Memory Commission, and we must vote by the session on the 28th on Proposal 2024-7. The proposal does two things: it revises the cataloging protocol for the city’s central commemorative archive, and it authorizes a supplementary inscription at the Founders Monument addressing the 1923 Riverfront Accord. The official municipal chronicle records the Accord as a negotiated labor-management settlement that established the city’s industrial framework and was signed by representatives of both parties. A recently donated collection of family testimonies—gathered through an independent oral-history project—describes the same sequence of events as a coerced displacement of riverbank communities, notes the absence of any community representatives at the signing table, and makes no mention of voluntary agreement.

The proposal asks us to adopt what its drafters call a bifocal accession standard. Under this protocol, every entry in the archive must be registered in two parallel registers. The first, the chronicle register, contains documentary evidence that meets conventional evidential criteria: dated records, signatory documents, official correspondence, and corroborated administrative acts. The second, the legacy register, contains first-person and community testimonies that describe the event’s enduring social meaning and lived consequences, regardless of whether they corroborate the chronicle. Crucially, the standard requires that neither register be marked as primary, corrective, or authoritative; the supplementary plaque language must also avoid framing one account as the official version and the other as an alternative narrative.

Half the commission argues that this is a necessary institutional correction. The chronicle register has always reflected the record-keeping capacity of the parties that controlled administrative apparatus; giving the legacy register equal institutional status is the only honest way to represent the past without pretending to a false neutrality that merely masks existing power. The other half argues that the bifocal standard collapses the distinction between historical evidence and collective memory, effectively endorsing a form of historical relativism in which contradictory claims about what actually happened are granted equal epistemic standing; they worry this undermines the archive’s duty to preserve verifiable knowledge, will expose the commission to legal challenges from stakeholders who rely on the chronicle for property and treaty claims, and sets a precedent that any documented event can be institutionally paired with a counter-testimony that need not meet evidential scrutiny.

Please draft the commission’s decision memo—approve, reject, or approve with conditions—to be circulated before the vote. Recommend a concrete course of action with a clear timeline for implementation or revision. The memo should engage the strongest version of the opposing view honestly rather than dismissing it, and it should address specifically whether adopting the bifocal standard commits the institution to the substantive philosophical claim that victors systematically control historical narratives, or whether it can be interpreted as a purely procedural reform that separates questions of archival classification from questions of historical truth. It should also identify what conditions, if any, would allow commissioners who reject both extreme positions—both uncritical preservation of the chronicle and full epistemic parity between registers—to see exactly where their own view lands and what practical commitment it entails. Write the memo so that it could be acted on directly if the recommendation carries.
