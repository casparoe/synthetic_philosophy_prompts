In an online discussion about a controversial museum acquisition, a commentator dismissed negative critical reviews with the phrase "beauty is in the eye of the beholder," using it to imply that any further argument about whether the work succeeds on its own aesthetic terms was illegitimate. I want to understand what precise theses this proverb could actually be advancing, particularly in relation to Hume's essay Of the Standard of Taste, where aesthetic judgment is rooted in sentiment yet apparently answerable to something like an intersubjective standard. Separate the distinct claims hiding in the slogan: a trivial psychological claim that people differ in their preferences; a stronger non-cognitivist claim that aesthetic disagreement is always a bare conflict of feeling with no possibility of error; a Humean claim that judgments express sentiment but remain correctable by an ideal critic possessed of delicacy, practice, comparison, and freedom from prejudice; and a more radical claim that there is no shared normative framework at all. For each reading, say whether it is a platitude, a substantive and defensible position, or false. Then identify which reading the commentator was likely invoking, and whether that reading actually licenses using the phrase to end an argument about the acquisition's merit. In particular, address whether Hume's framework allows sentiment to ground norms without collapsing into the crude relativism the slogan usually expresses, and whether the "eye of the beholder" framing wrongly treats aesthetic judgment as analogous to raw sensory perception rather than to a reflective sentiment subject to correction. Present the readings as a menu with verdicts rather than selecting a single interpretation.
