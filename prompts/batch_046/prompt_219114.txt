I'm noticing a recurring asymmetry in moral reasoning that I don't have a precise word for. In one kind of case, people identify a psychological tendency—say, judging harms to distant strangers as less urgent—and treat it as a bias that rational reflection should correct, because it tracks an arbitrary feature like geography. In another kind of case, people encounter a tendency of structurally identical form—say, judging harms to artificial minds, to members of a rival cultural group, or to future generations as less urgent—but protect it as a legitimate preference or scope of concern, even though it is equally widespread, equally resistant to revision, and equally traceable to contingent origins. The deciding factor seems not to be the psychological mechanism but whether the group being favored already lies inside the circle of entities whose moral standing the framework takes for granted; if it does, the deviation is a value, and if it does not, it is a bias. I have seen this replayed across debates about distant strangers, non-human animals, future people, and machine consciousness. Is there an established concept for this specific asymmetry—where the classification of a tendency as either bias or preference depends circularly on whether its object is already endorsed as a proper subject of concern? If there is no precise term, can you propose one and define its boundaries, distinguishing it from ordinary partiality, from parochial loyalty, and from straightforward disagreement about who counts?
