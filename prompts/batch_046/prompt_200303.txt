I'm working on an independent study project about decision theory and rational agency, and I recently read Douglas Hofstadter's account of the Platonia dilemma together with the Scientific American Luring Lottery. I tried to formalize the reasoning behind superrationality into a principle I'm calling Epistemic Symmetry in Rational Agency, or ESRA. Here is the argument I derived, and it now strikes me as suspicious because the conclusion is far too sweeping.

(1) In the Platonia dilemma, twenty identical agents face identical payoff structures: exactly one must send a telegram to win one billion dollars, but if more than one or none do, everyone gets nothing. The superrational strategy is randomization with probability 1/20, because any deterministic choice made by one rational agent must be made by all, yielding either universal sending or universal abstention. (2) The Luring Lottery applies the same logic: contestants maximize expected winnings by matching others' expected entries, but if all apply identical deterministic reasoning, the result is either zero entries or an unbounded runaway; so superrationality requires each to roll a simulated die with sides equal to the expected number of respondents. (3) The underlying principle is ESRA: whenever agents share identical information, reasoning capacity, and payoff structures, rationality forbids deterministic divergence, and the only permissible strategy is a shared randomization protocol weighted by collective optimality. (4) But ESRA applies well beyond artificial games. Consider career selection: thousands of students with identical aptitude and information face the same choice, and by ESRA each should randomize their career path according to social need rather than committing deterministically. (5) Even private belief formation—whether to accept a particular philosophical thesis or adopt a specific ethical framework—occurs under conditions of peer epistemic symmetry, since other rational agents have access to the same arguments and evidence. (6) Therefore, ESRA entails that rational agents must randomize over beliefs and commitments whenever peers face the same question, which implies that deterministic conviction—whether in medicine, ethics, or metaphysics—is irrational. (7) This conclusion is absurd; people clearly form stable, deterministic commitments without randomizing. (8) So ESRA must be false, which means the superrational solution to Platonia is also unfounded.

I'd prefer to keep (1) and (2) intact, since they describe the actual games accurately, and I'd also like to preserve the intuition that symmetry genuinely matters for rational choice. But I need to know where the error enters. Does the flaw lie in extending ESRA from strategic interaction to belief and career choice in steps (4) through (6), or is there a deeper problem in how (1) and (2) justify randomization in the first place? If I have to choose, I'd rather reject (3) or (6) than abandon the symmetry intuition, but I want to understand exactly what rejecting each step costs.
