I have noticed a recurring argumentative structure in discussions of communicative ethics—particularly when Dutch directness is contrasted with Japanese indirectness, and when the morality of white lies is debated—that I do not know how to classify. In each case, both parties accept that honesty and social consideration are genuine goods. Their disagreement is not about weighing these goods, but about which one functions as the governing constraint on speech acts in the relevant domain. For example, a defender of direct refusal treats softening language as a breach of honesty that imposes hidden costs on the listener, while a defender of indirect refusal treats unsoftened refusal as a breach of consideration that imposes a social burden. With white lies, one party frames kindness as an exception to the norm of honesty, while the other frames honesty as subordinate to kindness in low-stakes contexts. The pattern is not ordinary value pluralism, because each side treats its preferred good as lexically prior within pragmatics rather than as one factor to be balanced. Is there an established term for this kind of hierarchical framing conflict—perhaps in virtue ethics applied to language, or in cross-cultural pragmatics? If so, how does it differ from Gricean maxim tension or from a simple disagreement about weights? If not, I would like a precise coinage, together with boundary conditions that distinguish it from cases where one party simply denies that the other good applies at all.
