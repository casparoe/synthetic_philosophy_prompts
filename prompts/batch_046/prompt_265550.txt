As a retired experimental physicist who has turned to philosophy of science and metaphysics in retirement, I am serving as an external examiner for a graduate seminar on naturalism. I have been given the following examination question together with a single student response, and I should like you to grade the answer as an impartial assessor—without signaling your own philosophical commitments—assigning a specific mark (say, on a scale of 0 to 10, or with a letter grade) and then supplying point-by-point comments that justify the mark. I am especially concerned with whether the student correctly distinguishes Quine's logical criterion of ontological commitment from causal or explanatory conditions that belong to other naturalistic programs, whether the student handles the regimentation of normative discourse with appropriate logical care, and whether the student misreads Quine's scattered remarks on ethics by importing doctrines foreign to his view.

The question posed to the students was this:

Quine's criterion of ontological commitment holds that we are committed to the existence of just those entities over which our best scientific theories quantify, once those theories are regimented in first-order logic with explicit quantifiers. In Word and Object (1960) and Pursuit of Truth (1990) he applies this criterion to abstract objects such as sets and numbers, treating them as indispensable theoretical posits rather than as Platonic forms. How, if at all, does this framework extend to moral values? In particular, address (a) whether normative sentences can be regimented so as to involve objectual quantification over moral properties, as opposed to mere predicative attribution; (b) whether any such properties are indispensable to our best overall theory of the world, understood as the unified web of belief that includes psychology and sociology but is anchored in the physical sciences; and (c) whether Quine's brief comparison of moral judgment to aesthetic taste undermines any attempt to grant moral values ontological parity with scientific entities.

The student's answer is as follows:

Quine's naturalism requires that every entity we genuinely accept must be quantified over in the canonical formulation of our best scientific theory. Moral sentences, such as 'torture is wrong,' can indeed be regimented using a predicate W, but doing so binds no individual variables ranging over moral properties themselves; the predicate applies to actions, not to freestanding values. Because we can describe human behavior, including moral discourse, entirely within psychology, sociology, and evolutionary biology without quantifying over wrongness as a theoretical entity, moral properties fail the indispensability condition. They are therefore not part of Quine's ontology.

Moreover, Quine's criterion demands more than logical quantification: an entity must also be causally efficacious within the physical world in order to earn ontological commitment. Moral values exert no causal influence on particles or neural firings; they are epiphenomenal at best. This reinforces the conclusion that they are excluded.

Quine does compare moral judgment to aesthetic taste, noting in Pursuit of Truth that both involve a large element of cultural conditioning and personal sentiment. From this he infers that ethical claims are non-cognitive expressions of feeling, analogous to Ayer's emotivist account, and therefore not candidates for truth or falsity. Nevertheless, they retain pragmatic value as instruments of social coordination. Thus, while moral values are indispensable for human flourishing, they lack both the logical and the causal credentials required for entry into our ontological inventory, and Quine's framework rightly excludes them.

Please assess this response. Give your grade and then, in separate points, indicate where the reasoning is sound, where it conflates or misattributes a view, and where an inferential step fails—particularly regarding the logical structure of ontological commitment, the role of causation in Quine's account, and the interpretation of Quine's remarks on ethics.
