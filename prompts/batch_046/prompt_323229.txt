Christopher Browning’s study of Reserve Police Battalion 101 is frequently read as showing that situational forces—peer pressure, bureaucratic routine, and the fragmentation of responsibility—are sufficient to transform ordinary middle-aged family men into mass killers, with little explanatory weight left for deep ideological commitment. Daniel Goldhagen, meanwhile, is taken to claim that perpetrators killed Jews because they were animated by a specifically German culture of eliminationist antisemitism rooted in collective character rather than in individual pathology. If Browning’s account already explains the battalion’s actions without invoking any distinctive cultural climate, does this not make Goldhagen’s thesis redundant? Put differently, once we grant that ordinary men are highly malleable to situation and group dynamics, what explanatory work—if any—remains for arguing that a particular cultural framework was necessary to render them willing executioners rather than merely compliant conscripts?
