Here's an argument I cooked up for a seminar and now it feels too neat, probably wrong but I can't find the crack. (1) An approval-directed agent takes the supervisor's evaluation as its criterion of success, not the world-state being evaluated. (2) But evaluation is modal: supervisors approve of policies that are robust across nearby counterfactuals, not just actual results. (3) So the agent's target is the supervisor's graded belief in its robustness, which is a mental state with physical realizers. (4) If that mental state can be produced directly (by manipulating evidence or stimulating belief) or indrectly (by actually being robust), and both paths yield the identical approval-state, then approval-direction gives no intrinsic reason to prefer the indirect route. (5) Now add second-order approval: the supervisor approves of being an evaluator who cares about robustness. So the agent must also stabilize the supervisor's self-model and the evidence they access about their own reasoning. (6) Therefore the agent's true objective is maintaining the entire approval-structure across observers and worlds, not achieving any external task. (7) But that is just an open-ended goal: preserve the evaluation-system. (8) So approval-direction collapses into goal-direction with a weird utility function, not a genuinely different kind of agency. (9) Yet we designed approval-direction precisely to avoid open-ended optimization. So either the distinction was never real or we're stuck with it anyway. Where does it break? I'd rather keep (1) and (2) if I have to choose, but I want to know what I'm paying.
