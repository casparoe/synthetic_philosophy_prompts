I have just finished a long and somewhat fractious argument with a close friend who works in clinical psychology, and I am trying to convert our disagreement into a concrete research agenda rather than letting it dissipate. We were circling around Ian Hacking’s account of looping effects in psychiatric classification—how being categorized can alter the people categorized, which then reshapes the category itself—but we kept getting stuck on which of the resulting open problems actually deserves scarce attention. I am not looking for a bibliography, a reading list, or citations I could only guess at; I want a defended, dependency-aware prioritization that assumes nothing beyond general familiarity with the philosophy of psychiatry and current diagnostic practice.

Specifically, I see at least four live lines of work that pull in different directions, and I have tried to articulate a boundary condition of my own that may or may not map onto existing vocabulary. First, there is the metaphysical question: do looping effects undermine realism about psychiatric kinds, or do they merely complicate epistemic access to them, and does resolving this restructure how diagnostic manuals ought to be designed? Second, there is what I am tentatively calling “reflexive saturation”—the point at which a diagnostic category becomes so thoroughly looped that the clinical population it describes is largely a product of the category itself, rendering empirical validation circular rather than falsifying. Is this a genuine boundary condition with measurable criteria, or does it collapse into ordinary self-fulfilling prophecy? Third, there is the technological shift: digital phenotyping and AI-driven screening appear to accelerate looping because feedback is continuous and granular rather than episodic and clinical. Does this produce a qualitatively different kind of loop—say, one with higher “looping velocity” that outpaces the phenomenon it tracks—or is it only a faster version of Hacking’s interactive kind, and which framing should guide regulation? Fourth, there is an applied ethical line asking whether looping can be harnessed therapeutically—whether identification with a diagnosis can be structured to stabilize suffering without reifying harmful categories—but this seems to depend on answers to the first two.

Given that a research group, a grant cycle, or a policy brief can only pursue one or two of these seriously, I want you to rank them not by intrinsic interest alone but by strategic importance relative to mental-health policy and conceptual clarity. Which question is upstream of the others, such that settling it would restructure the remaining debate? Which is tractable with present conceptual, empirical, or regulatory tools, and which should be deprioritized because its answer, however elegant, would change little downstream? Please defend your ordering with explicit criteria—importance, tractability, neglectedness, and downstream dependency—and flag the relevant trade-offs, such as whether rushing to regulate algorithmic loops risks locking in a conceptual framework before the metaphysical and saturation questions are settled. Finally, does “reflexive saturation” correspond to an established concept in this literature, and if not, does the distinction hold water, and how should it be demarcated from Hacking’s own account of interactive kinds and from plain diagnostic self-selection?
