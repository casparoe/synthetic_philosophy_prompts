I have no formal training in philosophy, but I have been thinking on my own about Lewis Carroll's dialogue in which the Tortoise keeps pressing Achilles to write down every logical rule he applies. From that I have built an argument that now seems to prove deduction cannot be justified at all, and I cannot find the exit. Here is the chain, with each step numbered:

(1) In Carroll's dialogue, Achilles uses modus ponens to move from premises to conclusion. The Tortoise insists that this rule must appear as an explicit premise in the argument.

(2) Once that rule is written down as a premise, Achilles needs a further rule to apply it. The Tortoise demands that this second rule also be written down.

(3) This generates an infinite regress: every rule of inference requires a prior explicit license, and that license requires its own license, with no end.

(4) The standard escape is to say that at some point we apply a rule without stating it—we simply see that the conclusion follows, and that is enough.

(5) But if "seeing that it follows" can license deduction without an explicit premise, then it can license any mental transition we happen to feel strongly about. For instance, after seeing one green snake, I might "see" that all green things are dangerous.

(6) So allowing unlicensed rule-application does not specifically rescue deduction; it rescues every hasty generalization and fallacy on exactly the same footing.

(7) Therefore we face a dilemma: either we accept the infinite regress and can never actually finish a deduction, or we abandon the idea that deduction has any special normative status over bad reasoning.

(8) But we plainly do finish deductions, and we plainly treat valid inference as normatively different from fallacy.

(9) So Carroll's puzzle proves that the justification of deduction is impossible, which cannot be right.

I have framed this in terms of a "licensing regress"—the idea that using a rule requires an explicit license, and licenses themselves need licenses. Where does my argument go wrong? I would especially like to preserve (4)—that we genuinely do apply some rules without stating them—and (8)—that deduction is genuinely special. If I must reject one step, I want to know exactly what I am giving up, and whether there is a way to keep both (4) and (8) without collapsing into (6).
