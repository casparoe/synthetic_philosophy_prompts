Analyze the 2015 Paris Agreement negotiations—specifically the dispute over whether to include binding compensation or liability language for climate-induced loss and damage in the agreement's operative architecture—as a case study in intergenerational justice. Approach the episode through two conceptual lenses: first, the standard distributive framing, which asks what obligations present emitters owe future populations for climate harms measured in welfare or resources; second, a structural framing, which treats the central injustice not as mere deprivation but as the preemptive constriction of future democratic agency—locking in carbon-intensive infrastructure, foreclosing adaptation pathways, and thereby altering the conditions under which future citizens can exercise collective self-determination.

Map the actual negotiating dynamics onto these lenses. Identify the positions of the Alliance of Small Island States, which sought justiciable liability-based mechanisms; developed nations, which resisted any language implying legal responsibility or binding compensation; and the eventual compromise, which placed loss and damage in a dedicated provision outside the agreement's core liability framework. Where does the distributive lens best explain the stalemate and the resistance of wealthy states? Where does it fail to capture why small island delegations treated adaptation funding as insufficient and insisted on recognition of harm? Does the structural framing illuminate why the distinction between voluntary cooperation and admitted liability mattered so intensely, and does it explain the shape of the final compromise?

Then reverse the analysis: if intergenerational climate justice is fundamentally about preserving the conditions for democratic self-determination across generations rather than optimizing welfare across time, does the Paris outcome represent a partial acknowledgment of that obligation or its systematic denial? Reach and defend a conclusion about whether the episode supports a welfare-based or an agency-based account of intergenerational obligations, and say what the structural framing reveals that the distributive framing obscures.
