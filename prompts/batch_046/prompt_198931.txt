I'm a doctoral student in philosophy of religion, currently working on the ethics of exception and the structure of moral justification under institutional pressure. In a seminar last month on existentialist ethics, I found myself tracking a recurring argumentative pattern that I don't have a precise name for, and I'm hoping to determine whether the literature already distinguishes it sharply from the concept it superficially resembles. The pattern arises when an agent treats an ordinary ethical obligation—truthfulness, procedural fairness, or respect for autonomy—as temporarily inoperative because a higher-order end (institutional survival, collective welfare, long-term flourishing) demands it. What distinguishes the cases I have in mind is that the agent does not experience the suspension as a private, anguished rupture with the universal, as in Kierkegaard's account of Abraham's relation to the ethical; rather, the suspension is presented as administratively rational, publicly justifiable, and reversible. I have observed it in three rather different contexts: a senior administrator who overrides standard hiring protocols to secure funding she believes will save the institution; an activist who systematically misrepresents scientific data to accelerate a regulatory change she considers existentially urgent; and a physician who withholds diagnostic information from a patient, not out of classical paternalism, but because disclosure would, she claims, undermine the therapeutic trajectory she has designed for him. In each case, the agent invokes a teleological override, yet there is no appeal to the absurd, no isolation of faith, and—crucially—no clear criterion for when the ethical norm is reinstated. The suspension tends to become habitual rather than singular.

Does this pattern have an established name in moral philosophy or political ethics? I am not asking for a broad label like "moral exceptionalism" or "the end justifies the means"; I want to know whether there is a concept that captures specifically this instrumental, repeatable, and administratively sanitized form of ethical suspension—one that mimics the structure of Kierkegaard's teleological suspension but lacks its religious interiority and its singular, anxiety-ridden boundary conditions. If the literature treats this only as a corruption or misapplication of Kierkegaard's idea, I would like to understand exactly where the boundary lies: is the absence of the knight of faith structure sufficient to disqualify it, or is there an independent conceptual category—perhaps in discussions of moral licensing, role morality, or bureaucratic rationality—that maps this more precisely? And if nothing established fits, I would welcome a proposed term with explicit boundary conditions that distinguish it both from ordinary auxiliary hypotheses in practical reasoning and from the genuine teleological suspension that Kierkegaard describes. I am not looking for a bibliography or a reading list, but for the conceptual carving itself: what this phenomenon is, what it is not, and where its edges sit.
