Last week, after watching a large language model construct a coherent evolutionary argument with no biological understanding, I realized I want to teach the parallel inversion that both Darwin and Turing represent: competence arising without comprehension, reversing the usual order in which understanding is taken to cause successful performance. I am planning a single ninety-minute session for a mixed group of literature graduates and first-year computer-science students. The core idea is that Darwinian selection produces adaptive design without a designer who grasps the problem, while universal computation executes any calculable function without the machine representing what its symbols mean; in both cases the mechanism succeeds precisely because it does not need to understand.

Plan the explanatory arc rather than delivering the explanation. Which distinction—between procedural success and representational understanding, or another—must be secured first so that the inversion strikes them as philosophically strange rather than merely obvious? Select one carrying example for the biological side and one for the computational side (or a tightly paired case) that deliberately avoid the overused eye-evolution and chess-program illustrations; specify what properties the example must display and what it must lack so that the absence of comprehension is visible rather than hidden. Map the misconceptions this audience will form: I expect they will interpret 'no comprehension' biologically as 'pure randomness' and computationally as 'mere statistical correlation', and that they will try to relocate hidden understanding inside the genome or inside the model weights. Indicate the precise point in the sequence where each misconception should be intercepted and dissolved. Provide one final check-for-understanding question that distinguishes having grasped the structural inversion from having adopted the slogan. Finally, state clearly which piece of philosophical machinery—perhaps Dennett's tower of generate-and-test or the formal proof of universality—should be named as being deliberately omitted at this level, and why admitting that omission matters.
