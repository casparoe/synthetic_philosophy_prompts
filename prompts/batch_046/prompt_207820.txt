Question: Adaptive preferences are often used to attack interpersonal utility comparison—if people in deprivation report high satisfaction, direct comparison seems broken. One proposed fix is counter-adaptive indexing: instead of comparing satisfaction scores, we compare how stable each person's preference ordering stays when we run counterfactual perturbations on their resources and opportunities. Does this actually solve the comparison problem, or does it just relocate the difficulty?

Student answer:
Counter-adaptive indexing is an interesting structural move but it doesnt really solve the problem, it just shifts it. The motivation is to avoid the classic adaptive preference issue Sen talked about—like the deprived housewife who reports being content—by focusing on preference dynamics rather than static satisfaction. If someones ordering flips right away when we imagine them with more education or income, that shows their current utility is artificially compressed, not genuinely high. This keeps us in a subjective framework without importing an objective list of goods. But theres still a comparison problem hiding in the baseline: to call one persons preferences pathologically unstable and anothers robustly stable, we need a cross-person standard for what counts as normal resistance. That standard itself requires comparing people, so weve relocated the issue to the meta-level of stability coefficients. Sens capability approach is actually closer to solving this because it compares achieved functionings directly. So counter-adaptive indexing works as a diagnostic but cant stand alone as a comparison method.
