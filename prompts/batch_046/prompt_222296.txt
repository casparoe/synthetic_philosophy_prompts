I am preparing a graduate tutorial in philosophy of history and social ontology on whether societal "collapse" names a genuinely discontinuous ontological event, or whether it is only a retrospective framing of continuous institutional transformation seen through a normative lens of expected complexity. I want students to treat the Late Bronze Age disruptions, the Classic Maya lowland transition, and the Easter Island ecological trajectory not as archaeological narratives to be mastered, but as conceptual stress tests for this distinction. I need a pedagogical architecture with the following components, in this order of concern. First, the dependency structure: which conceptual distinction—say, between material decomplexification and cultural extinction, or between demographic contraction and institutional dissolution—must be in place before the students can even formulate the question, and in what sequence must the remaining distinctions be layered? Second, the carrying example: identify one synthesized or selected case that exhibits both pronounced material simplification and unambiguous institutional continuity, and specify precisely what feature it must possess and what feature it must lack in order to serve as the central analog for the session. Third, the misconception map: the students will likely arrive with teleological assumptions about societal lifespans, ecological determinism that separates climate from social choice, and the category error of equating population decline with civilizational death. Specify the order in which these misconceptions will typically arise, and indicate exactly where in the explanatory sequence each should be intercepted and dissolved. Fourth, a single diagnostic question I can pose at the end that distinguishes genuine comprehension of the collapse-as-interpretation thesis from mere repetition or discomfort with ambiguity. Fifth, an explicit boundary: what specific archaeological detail—chronological precision, site-level data, quantitative demographic modeling—should be openly acknowledged as outside the philosophical scope of the session and deliberately set aside? Present this as a formal teaching plan rather than as the explanation itself.
