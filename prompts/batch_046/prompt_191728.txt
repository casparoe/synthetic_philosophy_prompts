There's this analogy people always reach for when they talk about self-deception — that it's like a computer running two virtual machines, where VM1 holds all the evidence for p being false but VM2, the one you actually log into every morning, is firewalled off from VM1. Some philosophers use this to say self-deception isn't paradoxical at all, it's just bad internal netowrk architechture: you don't simultaneously beleive p and ~p, you just have compartmentalized storage. I want you to audit how far that actually holds. Map out what corresponds to what — the firewall rules, the shared hard drive, the user account — and then tell me where it breaks. Does it smuggle in the idea that there's a real 'you' sitting in VM2 who gets deceived by VM1, and if so doesn't that just push the problem back one level? There's also this niche idea about 'epistemic escrow' — treating a belief like cash you deposit with a third party so you can't spend it or deny you own it — does the VM analogy accidentally depend on something like that? And if the firewall breaks, does the original paradox come back, or can we just say the belief was never really held in VM2 to begin with? Work through it without worrying about citations.
