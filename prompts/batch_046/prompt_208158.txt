The philosophical literature on the boundary between legitimate historical revisionism and denialism—especially regarding the Holocaust, the Armenian genocide, and the Nanjing Massacre—has remained structurally static for decades: historians charge epistemologists with archival naivety, while philosophers charge historians with letting moral outrage substitute for evidentiary standards. I want a diagnosis of this stall rather than a verdict. Is the disagreement substantive—about whether extreme documentary density justifies a non-standard shift in burden of proof—or verbal, with "denialism" operating as a normative stigma rather than an epistemic category? Or do the sides answer different questions—one about the sociology of knowledge production, one about rational belief revision—under one heading? My working hypothesis is that a hidden disagreement about evidentiary asymmetry is doing the work: the claim that well-documented catastrophic events require evidential rules that standard philosophy of science cannot formalize. Diagnose the stall by engaging what each side actually says about archival gaps, testimony weighting, and whether establishing genocide requires a distinct historiographical kind. Then specify what would count as progress: a conceptual distinction, a formal model of burden-shifting, or an argument that the dispute is irreducibly normative and should be relocated? If this framing rests on a confused premise—if there is no unified debate, only scattered disciplinary hostility—say so plainly and correct it.
