Exam question:
In The Ethics of Memory, Avishai Margalit distinguishes ethical remembrance—rooted in what he calls thick relations of care and loyalty—from broader moral demands that apply more thinly across strangers. David Rieff, in In Praise of Forgetting, argues that collective forgetting can be a moral achievement rather than a failure when public remembrance fuels cyclical conflict. Consider this case: a post-conflict municipality maintains a digital archive of atrocity records. The archive redacts the names of low-level perpetrators from public view but preserves them in an encrypted memory vault accessible only to verified descendants of victims. Drawing specifically on Margalit's account of shared memory and Rieff's critique of institutionalized remembrance, evaluate whether this policy represents a defensible form of remembrance, a dereliction of the duty to remember, or an unjust form of forgetting.

Student answer:
Margalit argues that remembrance is governed by ethics rather than universal morality, since the duty to remember arises within thick relations—those bonds of loyalty and care that define family, friendship, or national community—rather than extending to every stranger who suffered. In this framework, shared memory is not passive archival storage but an active, communal practice of integrating the past into present identity. The municipality's vault therefore misses Margalit's point: by encrypting the records and allowing only descendants access, the policy transforms shared memory into private data retrieval, stripping it of the communal ritual that gives remembrance its ethical force. Still, the redaction from public view respects the boundary of thick relations by shielding victims' families from the spectacle of outsiders consuming their grief. On Rieff's side, his praise of forgetting is directed at the institutionalization of grievance in public monuments and archives that keep conflict alive. The redaction of perpetrator names from public view therefore realizes Rieff's recommendation that societies practice strategic forgetting at the collective level. Rieff also emphasizes that forgetting must be mutual and reciprocal among former adversaries to be just, which the vault achieves by removing the names from public circulation altogether. One concern remains: Margalit distinguishes shared memory from common memory, where common memory requires adversaries to construct a single narrative together; by sealing the records, the municipality prevents the formation of common memory and therefore blocks the path to reconciliation that Margalit thinks is ethically required. Consequently, the policy honors Rieff's call for collective amnesia but violates Margalit's deeper requirement that remembrance eventually become common rather than merely shared.
