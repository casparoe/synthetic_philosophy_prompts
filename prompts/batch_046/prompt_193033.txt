For a graduate seminar in historical sociology and the philosophy of history, construct a conceptual taxonomy—specifically a branching decision tree—of interpretations of “societal collapse” using three diagnostic cases: the Late Bronze Age crisis in the Eastern Mediterranean (c. 1200 BCE), the Classic Maya lowland transformation (c. 800–900 CE), and Rapa Nui / Easter Island’s long-term demographic and ecological trajectory (from the end of intensive moai construction through European contact). The tree must be built from the logical space rather than from a list of school-names: each internal node should be a binary or trinary claim, case-judgment, or conceptual split phrased so that a respondent can navigate it without already knowing which interpretation it selects.

Start at the root with the distinction between collapse-as-rapid-multi-system-failure and collapse-as-gradual-restructuring-with-persistent-identity. From there branch on whether demographic decline is necessary, sufficient, or irrelevant; then on whether evaluation requires an external baseline (peak population, peak monument production, peak inter-regional network connectivity) or can proceed from internal institutional coherence alone. Add a cross-cutting split on whether systemic interdependence—such as the Bronze Age palace-economy network—is a defining feature of collapse or merely a contingent historical circumstance that makes one region’s restructuring visible elsewhere.

Introduce the three cases as tests that cut across every branch: for each terminal combination, specify how it classifies the Bronze Age palace disintegration, the Maya Classic center abandonment with continued occupation in highlands and peripheries, and Easter Island’s shift from moai construction to later ritual forms and reduced population—marking each as collapse, restructuring, observer-dependent labeling, or an unoccupied conceptual space.

Every leaf must carry: (1) a named position if one occupies it in the literature (e.g., complexity-collapse, ecological-determinist, continuity/restructuring, interdependence-systems, post-contact demographic revision), or “unnamed” if the combination is coherent but unrepresented; (2) one sentence stating its most awkward commitment (for example, having to treat a drastic population contraction as non-collapse because ritual continuity survives); (3) a brief diagnosis of any empty branches—whether incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored.

Explicitly flag any pair of leaves that your tree cannot cleanly separate, explaining whether the distinction collapses into rhetorical emphasis rather than differing case-judgments. Do not provide bibliographies, reading lists, or requests for citations; the instrument should be answerable from conceptual analysis of the cases themselves. Finally, inject at least one non-obvious conceptual distinction—such as whether “collapse” is an etic analytic category imposed by observers or an emic self-description available to the society in question—so that the taxonomy is not reducible to standard encyclopedia summaries. Keep the delivery formal, with nodes phrased as direct questions or claims a student could answer from reasoning about the cases alone.
