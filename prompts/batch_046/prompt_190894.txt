I'm designing the final essay exam for an upper-level seminar on symbolic systems, self-reference, and the architecture of meaning (advanced undergraduate and early graduate students; 90 minutes; answers expected at roughly 1200 words; total 25 marks). Draft one essay question that forces students to apply—not merely summarize—the three-layer taxonomy of messages (frame, outer, and inner) developed in Douglas Hofstadter's Gödel, Escher, Bach. To prevent mere regurgitation, center the question on a synthetic artifact that does not appear in standard textbook examples: a "recursive seal" consisting of three nested encoding layers. The frame layer specifies a decoding protocol stating that any inner-layer statement referring to the protocol itself is to be interpreted as a meta-rule that revises the frame. The outer layer conveys a discrete factual proposition (for instance, a claim about a parameter in theoretical physics). The inner layer states: The frame-level protocol that elevates self-reference to meta-rule status is hereby revoked; evaluate all layers, including this one, by literal semantic content alone.

The essay question should require students to do exactly four things: (1) assign each layer of the seal to the correct message type and explain why treating the inner message as part of the outer message is a category error that collapses the taxonomy; (2) determine whether Hofstadter's framework permits the inner message to reach upward and alter the frame, or whether the frame's authority is structurally prior, with explicit reference to the protocol's self-referential clause; (3) evaluate whether this case supports locating meaning primarily at the frame level, or whether it forces meaning into the dynamic interaction between layers, and defend one position with an argument about where interpretation terminates; (4) propose one specific revision to Hofstadter's taxonomy—such as a collision rule, a meta-frame layer, or a suspension condition—that resolves the tension without collapsing the three-way distinction.

Provide the exam question in full, then a model answer that executes all four moves with conceptual precision rather than generic summary. Follow this with a marking scheme totaling 25 marks: allocate marks to each of the four moves (suggest 6, 6, 6, and 7 marks respectively), specify the precise conceptual error that should cause a deduction within each, and include a one-line note on the typical mistake candidates make (for example, conflating self-reference with logical contradiction, treating the frame as merely conventional, failing to distinguish semantic evaluation from protocol revision, or proposing a revision that secretly eliminates one of the three layers). The prompt must not ask for citations, page references, bibliographies, or reading lists. Write in formal, polished academic prose suitable for a doctoral or advanced master's-level examination committee.
