I have spent the last year working on a distributed robotics project in which thousands of identical, simple units coordinate through purely local interaction rules, with no central controller, no biological substrate, and no representational architecture designed into them by engineers. Recently my colleagues and I disagreed about whether the emergent collective patterns—persistent adjustments to an obstacle environment that are best predicted by attributing to the group a belief about the maze layout—count as intentional in any meaningful sense, or whether they are merely complex causal mechanisms. This debate has sent me back to my philosophy-of-mind seminar from last term, where my professor delivered a consistent series of verdicts on the original-versus-derived distinction that now seems directly relevant, especially in light of Searle’s biological naturalism and Dennett’s two-bitser challenge, which distributes comprehension across sub-personal parts in order to undermine the idea that intentionality requires a single, unified biological subject.

Her rulings were as follows. Human beings engaged in perception and action possess original intentionality. Printed books and written maps possess only derived intentionality, inherited from their authors. Thermostats and simple feedback loops possess no intentionality whatsoever. Computers running conventional programs with explicitly encoded symbol representations possess derived intentionality, inherited from their programmers. Non-human mammals with complex nervous systems possess original intentionality, though of a less articulated kind. And in the Chinese Room scenario, the individual person retains original intentionality for the instructions they understand, but the system they constitute—processing symbols without comprehension—lacks original intentionality entirely. Given this track record, I want to know what she should say, for consistency, about our collective: thousands of non-biological units with only local rules, no evolutionary history, and no representational design by engineers, which nonetheless self-organize persistent tracking patterns that guide group navigation and fail predictably when the environment changes in ways that falsify those patterns.

Predict her verdict on this collective, identify which of her earlier cases is the closest analog, and say whether her rulings are best explained by a strict biological-naturalist rule—under which only biological substrates can bear original intentionality and artifacts are either derived or empty—or by a causal-representational rule—under which the crucial divide is whether representations are mediated by another intentional agent, so that a self-organizing, non-mediated system might qualify as original despite lacking biology. Give your best guess with reasons, and then specify the single further case—perhaps an artificially evolved silicon system with no designer-imposed representations—that would decisively settle which of these two rules she is running.
