Been arguing about UBI vs workfare on forums again and I keep running into this specific dance. Someone defends strict benefit conditionality by saying we need to stop fraud — they cite some stat about people gaming the system. You point out that in practice the screening catches mostly innocent people because actual fraud is tiny and false positives are high. Then they don't defend the accuracy; they switch to "well, the real point is deterrence, you want people to feel watched." Okay, but then you ask why deterrence justifies harming random innocent applicants, and they piviot again: "it's not about catching anyone, it's about sending the social signal that work is mandatory and benefits aren't free." So now we're not talking about fraud or deterrence, we're talking about symbolic norm-maintenance. And if you push on whether that symbolic goal is worth the real harm, they loop back to fraud being a serious problem.

It's not quite motte-and-bailey because the three claims — empirical fraud control, instrumental deterrence, symbolic norm enforcement — aren't nested versions of one idea; they're genuinely different mechanisms that happen to share the conclusion "keep conditions strict." And it's not just goalpost-moving because each claim comes with its own seperate moral framing. Is there an established concept for this kind of cyclical justification structure in welfare debates, where the rationale for conditionality rotates through empirical, instrumental, and symbolic registers whenever the prior one gets undermined? If there's no name, can someone carve out exactly what distinguishes it from ordinary policy pluralism or from the basic "undeserving poor" framing itself? I feel like I've seen this pattern in other domains too but I can't place it.
