I'm taking a graduate seminar on value theory and future generations, and after our session on why human extinction might be bad beyond the direct death toll, two classmates — call them A and B — got into an extended argument about Samuel Scheffler's claim that much of what we value depends on humanity continuing after our own deaths, an idea often referred to as the afterlife conjecture. They introduced a couple of distinctions not in the standard readings, and I couldn't tell who was right at each step. Here is exactly how the exchange went, reported without my own view.

Move 1 (A): Human extinction is primarily bad because of the deaths it involves. Scheffler's observation that we pursue long-term projects — science, art, justice — because we expect others to continue them actually weakens the case that extinction is uniquely catastrophic. If capable artificial successors could preserve and extend those same projects, then the loss of biological humanity is not specially bad beyond the death toll; the afterlife conjecture shows our values are portable, not tied to our species.

Move 2 (B): That misreads the structural point. Scheffler is not saying we instrumentally value projects; he is saying the goods themselves are constituted by a relation to future people. B introduces a distinction: recipient-dependence. The value of an afterlife requires not just that projects continue, but that there are human moral heirs — descendants, cultural recipients — who stand in the right inheriting relation to us. Substituting artificial successors fails because the dependency is on a community of human agents who reinterpret the goods, not on bare functional continuation.

Move 3 (A): Recipient-dependence begs the question. It assumes biological continuity is necessary without argument. If we genuinely care about knowledge, beauty, or justice, then any system capable of sustaining and appreciating them should count as a valid recipient. Without an independent reason to privilege carbon-based heirs, this collapses into an unargued species essentialism that Scheffler's own framework should reject.

Move 4 (B): It is not essentialism; it is about the specific structure of harm. B proposes a second distinction: the asymmetry of transmission. The wrong of extinction is not reducible to lost welfare or failed projects. It is analogous to destroying a living tradition — the harm is the breaking of the intergenerational chain through which meaning is transmitted. Even if artificial agents preserved every theorem and painting, the severing of the human-to-human link is a distinct collective loss, a kind of grief that death counts alone do not capture. Meanwhile, A never addresses goods — like intimate love or personal friendship — that seem to depend on an afterlife of other people but not on large-scale transmission, which suggests the framework may not cover all the values extinction threatens.
