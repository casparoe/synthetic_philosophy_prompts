As a journalist covering science and philosophy of mind, I am preparing a feature on whether Daniel Dennett's four-part taxonomy—Darwinian, Skinnerian, Popperian, and Gregorian creatures—can meaningfully accommodate today's large language models. The conventional view treats fluent generative output as evidence of Gregorian cognition: language used not merely for communication but as an internal tool for representing and reasoning about the world. I want you to argue against that classification. Make the conceptual case that these systems remain, at bottom, highly sophisticated Skinnerian creatures: they exhibit extraordinary behavioral flexibility shaped by predictive training, yet they lack the internal representational architecture and the Popperian capacity for hypothesis-testing against a stable world-model that Dennett treats as prerequisites for Gregorian status. Keep the argument strictly philosophical—no bibliographies, no appeals to specific technical papers, and no empirical benchmarking—focusing instead on what the taxonomy itself reveals about the gap between producing reasoning-like behavior and actually possessing the cognitive architecture that makes reasoning possible.
