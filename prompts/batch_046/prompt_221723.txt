I am constructing a historical novel centered on a physician in eighteenth-century Marseille who, during a plague outbreak, persuades the municipal council to seal infected quarters, fully aware that those inside will perish, yet convinced that the preservation of the civic body requires the sacrifice of a part. I have rendered his reasoning with historical fidelity: he draws on contemporary theories of corruption and communal health, not on any framework of individual rights foreign to his milieu. Now I face an editorial impasse. One advisor insists that a responsible narrative must deploy its modern moral authority to name the act as state-sanctioned killing; otherwise, she argues, the novel risks aestheticizing cruelty under the alibi of context. Another advisor replies that such explicit judgment collapses historical distance and transforms the characters into mere illustrations of our moral progress, effectively denying them the coherence of their own ethical world. I am not asking for writing craft; I am asking whether there exists a principled position—perhaps analogous to methodological debates among professional historians—by which a novelist can represent past moral reasoning without either endorsing it through narrative silence or annihilating it through presentist condemnation. I wonder, in particular, whether applying our ethical categories to condemn him is structurally analogous to applying our germ theory to criticize his medicine: both impose present knowledge on past agents, yet one feels obligatory and the other absurd. How should I navigate this tension?
