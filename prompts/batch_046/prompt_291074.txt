Consider the following argument about material constitution and artifactual identity.

(1) If two objects share every spatiotemporal part and every intrinsic physical property during an interval, and neither is a proper part of the other, then they are identical during that interval.
(2) The bronze statue Discobolus and the lump of bronze that composes it share all parts and intrinsic properties during every moment they coexist.
(3) Therefore, the statue and the bronze are identical.
(4) The bronze can survive being melted and recast as a gear; the statue, being essentially a representation of a discus-thrower, cannot survive that transformation.
(5) Identical objects have identical persistence conditions.
(6) So the statue can survive being melted into a gear, which contradicts (4).
(7) Hence (1) must be false: distinct objects can be exactly co-located.
(8) But if (1) fails, then whenever matter acquires a new functional form—when a tree trunk becomes a canoe, when clay is shaped into a bowl, or even when a chair is temporarily used as a step-ladder—a new object comes into being alongside the old matter, generating an extravagant ontology of co-located entities.
(9) We seem forced either to deny that artifacts exist as distinct from their material bases, or to accept rampant co-location.

This argument appears to trap us between collapsing artifactual identity into material identity and exploding our ontology with co-located entities. Which premise or inference step is the weakest? Explain why it initially seemed unavoidable. Then lay out what the constitution-without-identity view, four-dimensionalist stage theory, and mereological essentialism each have to pay to reject that step.
