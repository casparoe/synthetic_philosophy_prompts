I have been invited to deliver the opening remarks at a two-day workshop convening next month on historical methodology; the audience will be mixed, comprising professional historians, philosophers of history, and doctoral students from both fields. The announced theme is overdetermination and multi-causal explanation in history, a subject that has become unexpectedly personal over the past several months. After my father’s death, I undertook to organize his papers and draft a brief biographical sketch for our family archive, only to discover that nearly every major turning point in his life—his decision to emigrate in his twenties, his abrupt career change at midlife, even the precise year of his retirement—seems to have possessed multiple independently sufficient causes. Selecting one thread as the decisive reason feels like a narrative falsification, yet cataloguing every sufficient condition produces an unreadable list rather than an explanation. I want the remarks to use this tension as their structural backbone: they should welcome the participants, frame the workshop’s purpose, and acknowledge that responsible historical narrative cannot simply resolve overdetermination into clean linear causation. At the same time, they must not collapse into the claim that history is therefore inexplicable or that all causal accounts are mere fictions. The speech should be accessible to non-philosophers—perhaps by distinguishing between the kind of causal redundancy familiar in physical systems and what might be called an interfering multiplicity characteristic of human biography, in which sufficient causes modify one another’s effects—while still offering specialists a precise framing problem to work with over the next two days. Please write the remarks: approximately eight to ten minutes in delivery, formal in register but not lecturing in tone, shaped throughout by the recognition that how we handle multi-causal explanation determines what a historical narrative is permitted to claim.
