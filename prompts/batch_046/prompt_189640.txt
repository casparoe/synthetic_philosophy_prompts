I've been thinking about how a person can rationally hold degrees of belief in mathematical or logical claims they haven't actually proven—whether some enormous number is prime, or whether a particular program eventually halts. I understand there's an idea that one might treat these beliefs like prices in a kind of reasoning market that slowly converges toward the truth, often discussed under the name logical induction. But I'm caught on a conceptual distinction that feels more fundamental than the mechanics of that convergence. Is the uncertainty involved here genuinely the same kind as empirical uncertainty—just a temporary gap in information about a fixed reality—or is it a categorically different thing, since logical facts don't change and our only limitation is the reach of our own computation? To make this concrete: if such a system must assign probabilities not only to external logical statements but also to predictions about its own future beliefs—say, pricing in the claim that tomorrow it will regard a certain conjecture as seventy percent likely—does that generate a special reflexive loop that empirical uncertainty never encounters? I want to understand whether logical induction absorbs this self-reference by treating it as just another logical statement to be priced, or whether it forces us to recognize a separate conceptual category—uncertainty about one's own reasoning process—that empirical models don't require. Could you explain where that boundary is meant to lie, and whether shifting it would change what counts as rational belief in unproven mathematics?
