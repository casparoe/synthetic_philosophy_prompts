Having spent my career in condensed-matter physics and only recently turned to philosophy, I find myself puzzled by an emerging regulatory framework proposed for autonomous weapons systems. The framework distributes liability for an unintended lethal outcome across what it terms a "design consortium"—the hardware manufacturer, the training-data curator, and the deployment authority—rather than assigning it to a single human operator or to the system itself. Crucially, each party's share is calibrated not by direct causal contribution but by its control over the system's "moral salience parameters" at distinct lifecycle stages: the design-phase weighting of harm-avoidance heuristics, the data-phase encoding of protected categories, and the deployment-phase adjustment of contextual activation thresholds. This scheme treats autonomy not as an on-off switch but as a graded property mediated by these parameters, and it replaces the familiar "human-in-the-loop" fallback with a distributed, stage-sensitive model of accountability. I want you to reconstruct the implicit philosophical theory of responsibility and agency that makes this distribution coherent. Does it presuppose a scalar rather than binary conception of agency? Does it require a form of collective intentionality that extends beyond standard accounts of joint action? And if one replaced this distributed model with a strictly individualist liability view—where a single party bears full responsibility—what institutional practices would have to change, and which parts of the current conceptual architecture would become philosophically untenable? Please distinguish what the practice strictly presupposes from what merely fits it well, and indicate where it remains philosophically underdetermined. I am asking for conceptual reconstruction only, not for case law, statutes, or specific historical examples.
