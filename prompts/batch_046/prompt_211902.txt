Read Sophie's Choice — particularly the camp scene in which Sophie is compelled by a Nazi doctor to select which of her two children will be sent immediately to the gas chamber and which will survive in the camp — through the lens of Frankfurt cases and the principle of alternative possibilities. In standard Frankfurt counterexamples, a hidden controller ensures the agent will act one way while leaving an alternative technically open but psychologically or practically closed, and the agent is still held responsible. Sophie's coercion is overt and structural: she genuinely could refuse and see both children die, or choose one child over the other, so an unused path clearly exists, yet every path is catastrophic. Ask whether she retains alternative possibilities in the sense that matters for moral responsibility, or whether the extremity of the options collapses the distinction between having an option and having a viable one. Then push further by bringing in the distinction between sourcehood and leeway conditions on responsibility, together with the idea of moral remainder — that regardless of which child she saves, she incurs an irreparable moral stain. Does the scene suggest that Frankfurt's framework captures her responsibility correctly if it treats her choice as unproblematic because an unused path technically existed? Or does the work show that when every available path involves an unavoidable wrong, responsibility is not about the absence of alternatives but about being the source of an unavoidable moral loss? I want a reading that treats both the novel's dramatization and the philosophical debate with equal seriousness, that identifies where the work complicates the standard thought experiment — for instance, by making the coercion visible and the cost of refusal total rather than hidden — and that ends by saying what this particular scene contributes to the philosophy of moral responsibility that abstract Frankfurt cases tend to miss.
