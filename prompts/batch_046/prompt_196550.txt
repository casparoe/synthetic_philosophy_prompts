I've been working through the implications of pain asymbolia using a distinction I want to call affective nullity versus motivational nullity. If an asymbolic patient retains full sensory discrimination of nociceptive stimuli yet reports neither suffering nor any conative drive to withdraw—exhibiting, as it were, both nullities—doesn't this demonstrate that the badness of pain is not intrinsic to its sensory content but rather an optional psychological overlay mediated by intact limbic evaluation? And if unpleasantness can be selectively absent without altering the phenomenal core of the experience, wouldn't any hedonistic theory of harm have to concede that harm requires this contingent overlay rather than being constituted by the sensory state itself? I suspect I'm conflating first-order phenomenal badness with second-order evaluative absence, but I want to know precisely where the argument fails—particularly whether motivational nullity genuinely entails affective nullity in these cases.
