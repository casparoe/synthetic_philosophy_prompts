I chair the ethics review panel for a national professional association of civil engineers, and we are required to adjudicate formal complaints in which one member publicly censures another for an ethical breach—typically involving misrepresentation of risk assessments, undisclosed conflicts of interest, or selective data presentation. A recurring difficulty is that the complainant has themselves committed a comparable breach, sometimes in the past and sometimes concurrently, and the respondent invariably raises what the philosophical literature terms the hypocrisy objection: that the accuser lacks moral standing to blame. At present we have no reusable internal framework for deciding when hypocrisy actually vitiates standing, when it merely attenuates rhetorical force, and when institutional role preserves authority regardless of personal fault. I therefore need a practical decision procedure—ordered questions, explicit sufficiency conditions, clear stop-points yielding a definitive answer, and clearly marked zones where judgement is ineliminable—that three non-philosopher panellists can run in under thirty minutes per case.

The procedure should translate the conceptual distinction between forfeiting standing through an unacknowledged identical fault, retaining but weakening it through a remediated or structurally dissimilar fault, and holding institutional standing irrespective of personal moral status, into operational tests that do not require resolving metaphysical questions about moral authority. Specifically, it should include a taxonomy of hypocrisy that distinguishes active concealment of the same wrong, passive failure to remedy a structurally analogous wrong, and historical fault that has undergone acknowledged rectification; a remediation ladder tracking acknowledgement, restitution, and structural transformation; and a separation test for whether the blame concerns a discrete act or a systemic pattern. It must handle the whistle-blower with partial complicity, the decades-old reprimand in a different technical domain, and the formally unblemished panellist whose firm bears collective culpability. Please apply the procedure to three cases: (i) an engineer who concealed a conflict of interest and now blames a colleague for identical concealment, having never disclosed her own; (ii) a senior engineer reprimanded for data misrepresentation fifteen years ago in structural analysis who now blames a junior for similar misrepresentation in hydrological modelling; and (iii) a panellist with no personal fault who belongs to a firm with a documented history of the breach and issues a formal institutional censure. Include the case in which the procedure produces a result that feels intuitively wrong, and explain what that misfire reveals about the limits of proceduralising standing. The answer should be written in British English and should contain no bibliographic references, citations, or reading lists.
