Write a short research proposal (approximately 1,500 words, suitable for a one-semester independent study or a small interdisciplinary grant) on whether habitat destruction reduces net wild-animal suffering, framed specifically around the ecological replacement problem. State the precise question: if a habitat is destroyed and its resident populations decline or disappear, does the resulting change in total suffering depend only on the reduction in those individuals, or does the restructuring of the ecosystem—through niche collapse, range shifts, invasive replacement, or altered predator-prey dynamics—preserve or even amplify welfare burdens across the remaining and successor populations? Distinguish the empirical trajectory of populations from the normative aggregation of welfare, and specify whether your evaluation uses total suffering, average suffering per individual, or a structurally weighted measure that accounts for ecological role.

Explain why existing answers leave this open. Note that conservation literature typically treats ecosystem integrity and species persistence as primary goods without addressing individual welfare at all, while recent philosophical discussions of wild-animal suffering often treat population reduction as straightforward welfare reduction, implicitly assuming that destroyed niches are not replaced by welfare-equivalent life. Identify the hidden assumption: that suffering is fungible across individuals regardless of ecological context, and that the absence of a destroyed population is not offset by the suffering generated in competitive or successional dynamics elsewhere.

Propose an approach that avoids both pure empirical census (intractable in one semester) and pure armchair conceptual analysis. Construct a philosophical model that pairs a specific welfare-aggregation rule with three ecological replacement scenarios—complete niche collapse without biological replacement, partial replacement by generalist or invasive species under altered competitive pressure, and delayed replacement through ecological succession. For each pairing, derive whether destruction increases, decreases, or leaves indeterminate the net welfare burden. Argue why this modeling strategy is preferable to the obvious alternatives: a literature review alone cannot resolve the normative aggregation problem, while an empirical study alone cannot resolve the counterfactual welfare of non-existent replacements.

Say what a result would look like and what would count as failure. A successful result specifies, for each aggregation rule, the conditions under which habitat destruction reduces net suffering and the conditions under which it does not. Failure includes: (i) the model collapsing all aggregation rules into the same conclusion under ecological variability, rendering the normative choice moot; (ii) the answer proving fully indeterminate without empirical data that lie outside the project's scope; or (iii) the replacement scenarios being so underspecified that no determinate welfare comparison is possible.

Give an honest assessment of scope for one semester. Indicate which parts of the model can be fully developed (the formal pairing of aggregation rules with replacement conditions), which must remain a framework (the empirical parameterization of specific ecosystems), and which conceptual literature you will engage with positionally—such as the view that wild-animal welfare dominates ecosystem value, and the rival view that ecological integrity constrains welfare aggregation—without providing a bibliography or citation list. Note that specific references will be filled in during writing. Conclude by arguing that the project is tractable precisely because it substitutes formal modeling for empirical completeness, and that it is worth doing because current policy and philosophical debate operate with incompatible implicit assumptions about replacement and aggregation that this framework can expose. Do not request bibliographies, reading lists, or citations.
