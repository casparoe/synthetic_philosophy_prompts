Deliver the opening remarks — approximately ten minutes — for an interdisciplinary workshop titled "Enlightenment Protocols: Verification and Authorization." The room is divided: on one side, historians of early modern natural philosophy who study instrument calibration, correspondence networks, and the social mechanics of peer validation; on the other, philosophers of science and critical theorists who analyze how those same networks depended on colonial extraction, uncredited technical labor, and the exclusion of women from published authorship. The remarks must do real structural work around a specific conceptual framing — not decoration — namely the idea of "delegated witnessing": the Enlightenment practice of authorizing knowledge by appealing to absent or structurally erased observers (colonial subjects, laboratory technicians, salon correspondents whose letters were edited out of print) whose labor made reproducibility possible without being registered in the archive. Treat this framing seriously but do not resolve it. For the historians, acknowledge the archival reality of these erased figures without reducing their work to mere background; for the critical theorists, acknowledge the violence of authorization without collapsing into a total denunciation of reason that would end conversation. Explicitly state what the speech will refuse to claim — for example, that Enlightenment science was pure rationality later corrupted by politics, or that it was nothing but ideology — and explain why those omissions are necessary given this specific audience. The tone should be formal, invitational rather than accusatory, and should make both halves of the room feel addressed rather than performed for. Do not include a bibliography, reading list, citations, or named works in quotation marks.
