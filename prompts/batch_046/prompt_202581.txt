I'm trying to map my actual intuitions about democratic membership onto concrete cases and I keep hitting a wall. Here are my verdicts. Permanent residents who pay taxes but have no citizenship: should be in the demos, excluding them feels like taxaton without representation. Citizens who moved abroad thirty years ago: still belong, citizenship doesn't just evaporate. Fetuses: not members, they lack the kind of interests democratic representation is meant to protect. People with severe dementia: still members, membership isn't contingent on current rational agency. Non-human animals in the territory: out, wrong kind of moral status. Future generations who'll live with our climate decisions: in, but only via proxy, not literally sitting at the table. An AI that aggregates citizen feedback but has no welfare interests of its own: definitely out. Two-week tourists: out. Corporations: out, they're not people. But worker cooperatives where every decision-maker is an individual human: the collective should have representational weight, which makes me wonder if I'm smuggling in collective agency. The case I keep flipping on is "statistically inevitable climate migrants" -- people who will be displaced in 40 years but don't exist yet and have no present location. Do they belong now? There's a tension I can't shake: if future generations belong because they're affected, why don't the animals? If it's about being affected, the boundary gets messy fast. Is there one principle -- affected interests, stakeholder status, being a subject of law -- that captures all this without being ad hoc? Which verdict do I have to drop? Also predict for an unlisted case: a child born via surrogacy abroad to expat citizens. Please say how confident you are in whatever principle you propose and flag the point you're least sure about.
