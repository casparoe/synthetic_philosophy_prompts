I have been thinking on my own about how societies decide what is owed to a person who has been wronged, and I have sketched a short essay that asks whether honor cultures, dignity cultures, and what is sometimes called victimhood culture are best understood as three answers to the same underlying problem rather than as separate historical stages. My central idea is that each culture proposes a different moral arithmetic for damage to a person's standing. In an honor culture, standing is a shared resource that the group helps defend, so an offense creates a real debt that can be settled through retaliation or public apology; the community acts almost like a creditor, and balance is restored when the debt is paid. In a dignity culture, standing is treated as innate and inalienable, which means that an insult is not a real loss but a false claim about value; the only remedy is for the community to reaffirm equality, so no debt ever genuinely exists and nothing needs to be repaid. I then argue that victimhood culture arises precisely where dignity culture's promise of universal, equal worth meets the reality of unequal recognition; in that gap, documented injury becomes the only available currency, because claiming harm is the only way to make one's standing visible when it is not being acknowledged as innate. I am not formally trained in philosophy or sociology, and I am sure scholars have made finer distinctions than I have, but I would like your comments on whether this framing holds together, whether I have mischaracterized any of the three cultures, whether there is an obvious objection about the relationship between recognition and worth that I have missed, and whether you think any one of these three systems is morally superior or whether they are simply incommensurable responses to different social conditions.
