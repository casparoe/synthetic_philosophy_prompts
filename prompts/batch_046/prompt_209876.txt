I've been working through my own gut reactions to moral choice problems in which the beneficiary pool might be unbounded, and I'm struck by how uneven my verdicts are. Here are five cases, with my honest judgments. First, if I can pull one child from a well at moderate risk to myself, I clearly ought to do it; failing is seriously wrong. Second, if I can fund a one-time intervention that saves exactly ten lives with near-certainty, or fund a seed project with the same cost that has a one-in-a-thousand chance of initiating a self-sustaining system that, across an infinite future, saves an infinite number of lives, I feel the first is obligatory and the second is merely admirable—certainly not a comparable duty, despite the infinite expectation. Third, if I can prevent one definite torture now only by allowing a mechanism to persist that, in an infinite timeline or spatial expanse, generates an equivalent infinite total of distant suffering, I still think I must prevent the one; the distant infinite does not cancel it. Fourth, forced to choose between saving one thousand people now with complete certainty and saving nine hundred and ninety-nine people now with complete certainty but via a method that, if perpetuated, eventually saves infinitely many more, I think I should take the thousand—the infinite tail does not break the tie in its favor. Fifth, comparing a charity that saves one life per thousand dollars and is designed to operate forever (hence an infinite total) against one that saves two lives per thousand dollars but will certainly shut down within a century, I think I should give to the second; the unending stream does not outweigh the doubled present rate.

These five feel coherent to me, but they sit badly with any simple expected-value or aggregationist framework I can name. Is there a precise principle—perhaps anchored in the moral salience of definite, bounded interactions, or in some form of scale-sensitive deontology—that delivers all five? Or must one of my verdicts be overturned? State the principle with enough precision that I can see which clause handles which case, then apply it to one I haven't listed: suppose I know with certainty that my action will cause exactly one being to suffer now, but will also trigger an infinite cascade of well-being in distant regions or epochs. Does the principle condemn or permit the action? And be explicit about which of my five judgments it cannot accommodate if they cannot all be saved together.
