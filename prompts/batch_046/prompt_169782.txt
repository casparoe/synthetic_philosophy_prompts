The student wrote the following. The discursive dilemma arises when a group must collectively accept or reject interrelated propositions. In the classic court case, three judges vote on whether P is true, whether Q is true, and whether R follows, where R is entailed by P and Q. Judge 1 accepts P and Q and therefore R. Judge 2 accepts P but rejects Q, so rejects R. Judge 3 rejects P but accepts Q, so rejects R. A majority accepts P, specifically Judges 1 and 2, and a majority accepts Q, specifically Judges 1 and 3, yet a majority rejects R, specifically Judges 2 and 3. If the group uses premise-based aggregation, voting separately on P and Q and then deriving R, it accepts R despite only one judge supporting it directly. If it uses conclusion-based aggregation, voting directly on R, it rejects R, but then its accepted judgments are logically inconsistent with its acceptance of P and Q.

The premise-based procedure therefore violates the democratic intuition that the group's view on R should match the majority view on R; some critics call this the counter-majoritarian problem. The conclusion-based procedure, by contrast, violates logical closure, since the group believes P and Q but denies what they entail. Both procedures are defensible, but each sacrifices something.

List and Pettit analyze this in their work on group agency. They argue that the dilemma is structurally identical to Arrow's impossibility theorem applied to belief sets, and that it shows collective rationality is impossible unless we abandon anonymity. Specifically, they conclude that groups should always adopt premise-based aggregation, since only this respects the logical connection between premises and conclusion. By doing so, a group acts as a rational agent, even if its verdict on R conflicts with the direct majority preference. The cost is democratic legitimacy, but List and Pettit treat this as unavoidable for any group that wants to count as a genuine agent.
