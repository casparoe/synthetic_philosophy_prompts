I've come to think that "Western values" is not the incoherent grab-bag or rhetorical cudgel it's often taken to be, but a structurally unified normative cluster. On this view, its coherence doesn't depend on any particular item—democracy, secularism, individual rights, gender equality, free expression—but on a functional architecture: universalist moral egalitarianism coupled with institutionalized procedural legitimation and a built-in reflexive mechanism for self-revision. The claim is that these three features form an equilibrium in which each checks the others, producing a distinctive grammar of justification that is historically specific (emerging from post-Reformation rights discourse merged with administrative rationalism) yet not arbitrarily assembled. What would genuinely count against this? I want the defeaters sorted by severity: historical episodes where the cluster collapses into contradiction rather than manageable tension; conceptual arguments showing that universalism and proceduralism are not complementary but fundamentally at odds; comparative evidence that other civilizations have instantiated the same architecture without the "Western" genealogy; and rhetorical analyses demonstrating that in actual political discourse the phrase operates exclusively as an exclusionary boundary-marker with no descriptive content. Which of these would a defender of the coherence thesis be forced to concede, and which could be absorbed as internal tension? Please separate what is settled from what is genuinely contested among people who know the topic, and do not present a disputed interpretation—say, about whether the cluster is uniquely Western or genuinely universalizable—as the consensus view.
