I just spent the better part of an evening arguing with a close friend about whether Frankfurt-style counterexamples genuinely undermine the principle of alternative possibilities, and I realized we kept talking past each other because we were operating with two different conceptions of what it means for an agent to be able to do otherwise. I want to understand the conceptual terrain more precisely, without leaning on author names or texts I should be recalling.

Consider a modified Frankfurt case. There is an agent, Mira, who is deliberating about whether to sign a contract. Unbeknownst to her, an external intervener—the Monitor—has the capacity to force her hand if she shows any inclination not to sign. But the Monitor’s mechanism is not a simple blocker. Instead, it operates through what we might call a shadow pathway: if Mira were to hesitate, the Monitor would instantaneously suppress that hesitation and redirect her cognitive state into a parallel decision-track that still results in signing. This parallel track, however, is one that Mira’s actual reasoning architecture never accesses, never weighs, and never treats as live. In the actual world Mira deliberates freely, never hesitates, and signs on her own; the Monitor never activates.

My friend insists that because the shadow pathway exists in some modal sense—there is a physically possible sequence of events in which Mira’s behavior is rerouted—Mira still lacks the ability to do otherwise, and therefore the principle of alternative possibilities fails in her case. I countered that this conflates two readings of the principle. On one reading, it is a metaphysical claim about the causal structure: an alternative branch must remain unblocked in the world’s physics. On another, it is a claim about the agent’s practical reasoning space: an alternative must be genuinely available for the agent to recognize, weigh, and act upon. The shadow pathway is not an alternative possibility for Mira; it is a subpersonal rerouting mechanism that bypasses her agency entirely.

Please explain the conceptual issue at stake. Specifically, distinguish the metaphysical interpretation of the principle—concerning the existence of unblocked causal branches—from the deliberative interpretation—concerning the agent’s access to live options within her own reasoning framework. Then apply this distinction to the modified case: does the existence of the Monitor’s shadow pathway show that Mira lacks alternative possibilities in the relevant sense? Address whether the Frankfurt counterexample succeeds only by conflating these interpretations, and discuss what is conceptually lost if we reduce “could have done otherwise” to the mere non-activation of an external override mechanism rather than to the agent’s own capacity for divergent choice. I am not asking for a bibliography or specific citations; I want the structure of the argument mapped out clearly.
