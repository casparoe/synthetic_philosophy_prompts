I'm closing the final session of a graduate seminar in historiographical metaphysics, where we have spent the term comparing cyclical and linear models of historical change. Our readings have included Polybius's account of constitutional anacyclosis, Ibn Khaldun's theory that dynastic power erodes as asabiyyah dissipates across three generations, Spengler's morphological argument that each culture possesses an organic life-cycle predetermined in its inner form, and Toynbee's counter-proposal that civilizations develop linearly through successive challenges and creative responses rather than biological repetition. The discussion ended precisely where these models become incommensurable: whether the repetition of forms is determinative or merely descriptive, and whether human agency can break the pattern that Polybius and Ibn Khaldun treat as almost mechanical.

For the closing exercise, I want to present a poem that embodies this suspension structurally rather than resolving it thematically. Write a poem of exactly fourteen unrhymed lines that uses the word "generation" as a shifting refrain—appearing first in a biological sense, then dynastic, then cultural-morphological—so that the poem's formal repetition enacts the very ambiguity between repetition and development that divided our seminar. The architecture should be precise: lines 1-6 voice the cyclical position through Polybius and Ibn Khaldun; line 7 must serve as a volta introducing Toynbee's linear-progressive objection; lines 8-14 should interweave Spengler's determinism with Toynbee's residual agency without allowing either to dominate. The closing image should be structurally ambiguous—capable of being read either as a cycle closing upon itself or as a line extending forward—so that the poem ends in suspension. The tone should be contemplative rather than didactic, accurate enough that a specialist in philosophy of history recognizes the specific theories rather than generic references to "the wheel of history," and suitable for being read aloud to an audience that has just debated whether history possesses a morphology at all.
