I have been reading some translations of Jain philosophy on my own—nothing formal, just long discussions with friends who study religion and ethics—and I am trying to turn my confusion into a concrete six-month independent research plan that I could pitch to a small self-directed-study grant. The question I want answered is this: can Jain anekāntavāda—the view that reality is irreducibly many-sided and that every judgment captures only one partial aspect—function as a practical decision framework for moral conflict, or does its commitment to non-absolutism necessarily suspend action? More precisely, when two partial moral perspectives genuinely conflict over what to do—say, one framing an environmental duty through radical non-violence toward all sentient beings, another framing the same situation through obligations of human flourishing—does anekāntavāda provide a procedure for resolving the conflict that is structurally different from Western liberal “reasonable disagreement” or from simple relativism? Or does the refusal to absolutize any single naya (standpoint) mean the framework can only describe the conflict without guiding choice?

Existing answers leave this open because treatments of anekāntavāda in philosophy almost always present it as a metaphysical or epistemological thesis about the nature of truth, not as a logic of practical reasons. Meanwhile, Western discussions of moral pluralism and value incommensurability assume a single decision-maker who must rank competing values, whereas Jain logic allows contradictory predicates to hold conditionally through syādvāda and the seven-fold predication (saptabhaṅgī). I do not think anyone has pressed the framework hard enough on what happens when partial truths do not merely describe the world differently but demand incompatible actions. I want to reconstruct syādvāda and saptabhaṅgī not as propositional logics but as structures for weighing practical reasons, then test the reconstruction against two carefully constructed moral conflicts—one concerning intervention in wild animal suffering, one concerning distributive justice—to see whether the framework generates determinate guidance or systematically defers judgment.

My method would be conceptual rather than historical: I will map how each naya frames the reasons at stake, apply the conditional predication rules to see whether one standpoint can be provisionally privileged without being absolutized, and then compare the outcome to how liberal pluralists and relativists handle identical cases. A successful result would be either identifying a principled, non-hierarchical way for anekāntavāda to select among partial moral truths for action—showing the framework is practically richer than quietism—or demonstrating that it necessarily withholds action whenever standpoints conflict, which would relocate its value in tolerance rather than decision. The project fails if I merely redescribe liberal pluralism in Jain vocabulary without showing any structural difference in practical outcomes. The deliverable would be one long essay of about eight thousand words plus a short appendix formalizing my reconstructed practical-logic version of saptabhaṅgī; I will supply citations myself, but I need the conceptual architecture precise enough that someone familiar with the tradition can tell whether I have distorted it. I am aware that without direct access to Sanskrit commentaries I may miss nuances, so I will flag sections that depend on secondary translations and treat them as provisional. The scope is realistic for one person working alone over six months, but I want an honest assessment of whether this framing is genuinely tractable or whether it collapses into either unworkable abstraction or mere translation.
