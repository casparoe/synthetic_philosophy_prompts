For a philosophical working group I am preparing a consistency audit on intergenerational ethics, and I want to know whether the following commitments form a jointly tenable package or whether they pull apart in ways I have not noticed. Here is my current position:

(a) I accept the canonical non-identity argument in its strongest form: in different-number cases, bringing people into existence with lives that are, on balance, worth living does not harm them relative to the counterfactual of non-existence, because for each such person the relevant comparison is with never having existed at all, and therefore any wrongness in the choice must be evaluated impersonally.

(b) Nevertheless, I hold that there is a meaningful, person-affecting wrong in knowingly conceiving, selecting for, or sustaining an institutional pathway that produces a specific child or cohort with severe, lifelong suffering—what is sometimes called a wrongful-life-type judgment—even though, given the modal fragility of genetic and environmental identity, that particular individual could not have existed with a different, better life.

(c) I take personal identity across reproductive choices and long-range policy scenarios to be modally fragile in a robust sense: differences in conception timing, gamete selection, or the environmental conditions that shape development yield numerically distinct individuals, so counterfactual “same-person” comparisons across policy scenarios are generally metaphysically unavailable.

(d) I maintain a strict person-affecting restriction for judgments of serious wrongness and injustice: an act or policy is wrong, or unjust, only if there is an identifiable individual who is made worse off, or whose rights or legitimate interests are violated, in a manner that does not reduce to a comparison with non-existence or to a purely aggregate statistical change.

(e) I reject impersonal moral theories—such as total or average utilitarianism, or pure axiological aggregation—as appropriate foundations for rights, justice, or serious moral wrongness, though I permit them as heuristic decision-rules for resource allocation.

(f) For large-scale institutional evaluation—especially climate mitigation and long-term demographic policy—I think the correct method is to compare the total well-being (or the distribution of well-being) of the actual future population that will exist under one policy with that of the entirely counterfactual future population that would exist under an alternative, treating this population-level comparison as morally decisive for policy choice, even when no individual exists in both scenarios.

Please audit this set: (1) identify which pairs are in genuine tension and which pairs only appear to conflict but dissolve on closer inspection; (2) rank the genuine tensions by severity, and for each specify what the least costly revision would be—i.e., which commitment should weaken, and in what direction; (3) finally, identify the single revision—dropping or substantially modifying one commitment—that would resolve the largest number of tensions at once, and explain what philosophical cost that revision carries.
