Consider the position—let us call it generalized proceduralist deference—that the unilateralist's curse, originally diagnosed in strategic contexts such as nuclear brinkmanship and common-pool resource depletion, extends deep into ordinary moral reasoning. On this view, whenever an action produces diffuse collective effects and individual judgment is fallible, a rational agent should suppress her first-order moral conviction and defer instead to an institutional or democratic decision procedure, even when she judges that procedure to be mistaken. The argument is not merely pragmatic but derives from expected-value reasoning: the aggregate risk of independent errors scales with the number of agents acting separately, so the expected harm of one agent being correct but acting alone is systematically outweighed by the expected harm of that same agent being wrong multiplied across the group. In applying this to charitable allocation and collective protest, I have introduced a further qualification—what I term the reflexivity gap—according to which moral domains, unlike engineered coordination systems, typically lack common knowledge of others' reasoning processes. Consequently, recognizing that one is inside the curse and achieving coordinated deference both require a meta-procedure that is itself vulnerable to unilateral initiation, which either undermines the deference or reinstates the curse at a higher level.

Assess this position honestly: what would have to be true for generalized proceduralist deference, including its reflexivity-gap qualification, to be wrong? I want a map of its actual exposure, not a catalog of generic objections. Which formal or empirical assumptions could fail—about how error costs scale with group size, about the comparative epistemic reliability of institutions versus individuals, about whether cheap signaling can bridge the reflexivity gap? Describe what the world would need to look like for each failure mode to obtain: for example, if individual moral judgments turned out to be highly correlated and institutionally biased, or if the meta-procedure for coordination could be established without anyone unilaterally imposing it. Distinguish decisive defeaters—such as a formal result showing that deference collapses into an equilibrium worse than independent action—from merely awkward cases, like isolated successes of unilateral moral action. Say what defenders of this view would themselves concede as genuinely counting against it, rather than only what critics allege. Finally, be willing to conclude that the position is well insulated—perhaps by definitional moves about rationality that render any apparent counterexample a case of irrationality—and tell me whether that insulation is a philosophical virtue or a methodological defect.
