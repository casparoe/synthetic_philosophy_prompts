I'm planning a fifty-minute introductory session for first-year philosophy students on procreative beneficence and liberal eugenics. The students come in assuming that selecting embryos for non-medical traits is obviously wrong because it treats children as commodities, and they conflate any parental genetic selection with state-coerced eugenics. I need a teaching plan — not the explanation itself — for the conceptual dependency between the principle that parents should select for the child's expected well-being and the liberal framework that keeps such selection voluntary, pluralistic, and non-coercive. Start with what distinction must be secured before the principle sounds plausible rather than monstrous. Then specify the single carrying example: I am considering a couple using polygenic screening to select an embryo with a higher predisposition for sociability rather than for disease resistance; tell me whether this example carries the right weight and what it must include and exclude. Map the misconceptions in the order students will likely form them — I anticipate they will first confuse beneficence with perfectionism, then read liberal safeguards as mere euphemism for coercion, and finally invoke the non-identity problem to claim selection is morally empty — and for each, indicate the precise point in the sequence where it should be headed off. End with one diagnostic question that tests whether a student grasps the dependency between the principle and its liberal constraints, not just whether they approve of embryo screening. Finally, state what dimension of the debate — perhaps access and distributive justice — must be explicitly left out at this level and why.
