I'm on a review board for a regional fisheries agency and we keep getting grant proposals that defend wild simplifications — like treating every fisher as a perfectly rational actor, or assuming ocean temperature is uniform across a basin — by waving around Friedman's F-twist. The idea seems to be that unrealistic assumtions are automatically okay if the model predicts well, but nobody on my panel can tell me where that logic actually stops, and we have to sign off on funding in about twnty minutes with no philosophy background required. So I need a reusable decision procedre we can actually run: ordered questions, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, the exact point where we stop with a reject, and clear flags saying "this is a values call, not a science call." It needs to handle the distinction between instrumental idealizations and ones that leak into the intervention — there's something about predictive insulation versus intervention leakage? — and be honest about where it will misfire.

Please build this for a practitioner who hasn't read the original literature, then apply it to three proposals: (a) an agent-based quota model assuming fishers always maximize short-term profit with no social norms; (b) a climate-downscaling model replacing local cloud dynamics with a single global parameter to save compute; and (c) a public-health model predicting vaccine uptake that assumes people never update beliefs from peers. One result should come out in a way my board won't like — I want to see the procedre bite — and you should say what it can't settle that still needs human judgement.
