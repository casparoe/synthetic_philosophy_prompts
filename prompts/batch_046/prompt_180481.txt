Read Cormac McCarthy’s The Road through Bernard Williams’s integrity objection, centered on the idea that a moral agent can have one thought too many when justifying the rescue of a loved one. I am especially interested in the father’s repeated refusal to assist strangers—the old man they encounter on the road, the victim of the thieves, the woman with the child—and in the way he offers no moral reasoning, only the bare fact of the boy’s survival. Does McCarthy present this radical partiality as a vindication of Williams’s claim that moral reasoning can alienate us from our deepest attachments, or as something darker: a collapse of moral identity into a single biological bond that Williams’s framework cannot adequately evaluate? In particular, consider whether the father’s silence about reasons is best read as the absence of that extra, alienating thought, or as a refusal of moral discourse altogether that makes the concept of integrity inapplicable. Then say where the novel resists the philosophical reading: does the post-apocalyptic setting strip away the social conditions under which Williams’s objection to utilitarian calculation is meant to operate, and if so, does the work still add something to the debate—perhaps about the limits of partiality when the world that makes such partiality intelligible has vanished?
