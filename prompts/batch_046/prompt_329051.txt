I work in software engineering without formal training in philosophy or archaeology, but I have become convinced that the concept of societal "collapse"—as applied to the Late Bronze Age, the Maya lowland abandonment, and Easter Island's deforestation and depopulation—is fundamentally a category error imported from mechanical thinking into social analysis. In distributed systems, we distinguish catastrophic failure from graceful degradation: when a service sheds non-essential nodes, drops to lower capacity, and continues operating within tighter constraints, we do not call it broken; we call it successful adaptation. By that framing, what historians label the collapse of the Maya or Easter Island looks less like a society breaking and more like a complex system intentionally downscaling—abandoning monumental architecture and dense urban cores not because social logic failed, but because organizational and energetic overhead exceeded sustainable supply. The reason we treat some trajectories as collapse and others as reform seems to be an unexamined teleological assumption that larger, more hierarchical, monument-building societies represent the healthy default, so any departure reads as pathology rather than reconfiguration. What would actually count against this view? I am not looking for a list of counterexamples, but for which specific archaeological or anthropological findings—say, evidence of abrupt versus gradual material-culture rupture, or proof that post-abandonment populations maintained intentional political structures versus fragmented into non-coordinated groups—would be decisive rather than merely awkward. Which elements of my engineering analogy do real conceptual work, and which are just rhetorical dressing? Furthermore, has this kind of position been qualified into unfalsifiability, such that any social transformation can be redescribed as successful adaptation at lower complexity, and if so, is that insulation a genuine weakness for an argument of this kind? I would like the defeaters ranked by how much they should move me, including whether there are conceptual distinctions that break the analogy independently of any empirical discovery.
