I've been volunteering for about a year at a small community workshop that restores damaged artifacts—mostly mechanical, acoustic, and paper—under a grant that only funds projects the board classifies as genuine restoration rather than replacement wearing the original's face. Our coordinator, Mara, makes the final call, and I've kept informal notes on her verdicts because they feel consistent yet resist any single principle I can name. She approved replacing a snapped chair leg with wood from the same fallen tree, repairing a cracked violin with hidden internal splints of matching grain, and reconstructing a ceramic vase with visible gold seams. She rejected replacing that same chair leg with a resin replica painted to match, and she rejected filling missing pigment in an old painting with modern acrylic chemically identical to the original batch. She also rejected a proposal to restore a corrupted analog speech recording by using an AI model to synthesize the missing thirty seconds in the speaker's voice, describing it as copycat slippage—the concept of the recording shifts once it contains sounds that never actually passed through that microphone. At the same time, she approved sandblasting rust from a vintage bicycle frame and repainting it with the original enamel formula, and she approved scanning water-damaged manuscript pages and rebinding them with new archival support paper.

Given this track record, do you think Mara would approve repairing a dead 1970s computer by replacing its burned-out CPU with a modern FPGA programmed to emulate the original chip's instruction set exactly, leaving the dead chip mounted in a display window inside the case? And what about reconstructing missing sentences in a handwritten letter using an AI trained on the author's other writings, then printing them on period-matched paper and inserting them with a small marginal symbol? I'm less interested in the specific yes-or-no answers than in the competing rules that fit her earlier verdicts equally well, how each rule predicts a different outcome for at least one of these cases, which rule you think she's actually running and why, and what single further case would best distinguish whether she cares about material continuity, about blocking mimetic substitution, or about preserving some other boundary that keeps the concept from sliding.
