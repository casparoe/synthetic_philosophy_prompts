I have been trying to determine whether deterministic chaos undermines determinism in any deep sense, or whether the problem is purely epistemic, and I think I have reached an argument that troubles the standard reconciliation. In a deterministic system with positive Lyapunov exponents, arbitrarily close initial conditions diverge exponentially over time. Determinism is standardly defined as the claim that, given an exact initial state S and the governing laws L, the future state is uniquely fixed as F(S, L). But any physical system is only ever instantiated with some state S ± ε, where ε is nonzero, however small. Because chaos amplifies ε without bound, the trajectory actually realized in nature will eventually diverge radically from F(S, L). The shadowing lemma guarantees that some exact trajectory remains close to our approximate one, but that exact trajectory corresponds to a different initial state S′ ≠ S. Consequently, for any real-world system, the deterministically fixed future F(S, L) is never the one that actually unfolds; only F(S′, L) unfolds, and S′ is in principle unknowable with infinite precision. Does this show that chaos converts determinism into a purely modal thesis about counterfactual initial conditions, with no genuine purchase on actual physical histories?
