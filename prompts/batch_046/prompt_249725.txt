I am drafting remarks for a philosophy-of-value seminar and keep encountering a compressed version of Moore’s view that is now doing live argumentative work: the claim that personal affection and the enjoyment of beautiful objects are not merely good but jointly supreme, so that any ethical framework allowing aesthetic experience to be weighed against social connection is already mistaken. A colleague invoked this line last week to dismiss my tentative comparison between a solitary but profound encounter with art and a shallow but widespread friendship, insisting that Moore had settled the matter by placing these two goods at an incommensurable peak. Rather than accept the slogan as an authority, I want it disambiguated precisely. Please present exactly five distinct readings of that one-liner—each in a sentence or two, with no introduction and no conclusion—distinguishing the trivial restatement from the substantive thesis, the false misreading, and the genuinely radical interpretation. For each reading, indicate whether it relies on Moore’s doctrine of organic unities, whether it illegitimately derives a moral prohibition from an intrinsic-value ranking, and whether it actually supports the move to treat these goods as non-tradable against one another. Number them one through five.
