I'm drafting a short essay that applies the inefficacy objection to a domain it rarely occupies: collective harms governed by non-linear dynamics with opaque thresholds. Here are the core notes.

The standard inefficacy objection—whether about carbon emissions, meat consumption, or voting—holds that my individual action is causally redundant: the collective outcome is overdetermined, so my abstaining changes nothing perceptible. This reasoning tacitly assumes a linear model of moral aggregation, where total harm equals the continuous sum of independent individual inputs. I want to argue that this model fails for systems with tipping points or discontinuous state transitions. In climate dynamics, harm is not uniformly distributed across emissions; critical thresholds may exist beyond which feedback loops radically amplify damage. Near such a threshold, a single contribution could in principle be the one that pushes the system over the edge, even if in the vast majority of counterfactual worlds it makes no visible difference.

The deeper problem for the objector is epistemic. Because we do not know where these thresholds lie—whether we are already past them, approaching them, or safely distant—no agent can claim justified confidence that her specific emission or purchase is causally redundant. The linear arithmetic that makes the inefficacy objection intuitive requires precisely the kind of fine-grained causal knowledge that non-linear, threshold-governed systems deny us. So the objection does not merely underestimate individual impact; it applies a framework of continuous aggregation to a domain where the ontology is discontinuous and our epistemic access is structurally limited.

I extend this to voting by noting that electoral outcomes can have discontinuous institutional effects—crossing a majority threshold may trigger an entirely different policy regime—so the same epistemic opacity undermines the claim that one vote never matters. For meat consumption, the argument is weaker, since agricultural supply chains are more linearly responsive to aggregate demand, though local discontinuities in supply contracts may still exist.

My tentative conclusion is that the inefficacy objection is domain-sensitive: it succeeds against linear collective harms but collapses for non-linear harms with opaque thresholds, not because individual actions are large, but because the reasoning that renders them small assumes a false model of the harm's structure.

First, does this fairly represent the standard inefficacy objection as it appears in climate and voting debates, or have I subtly shifted its target from causal redundancy to epistemic confidence about redundancy? Second, is there a coherent response available to the objector—perhaps that rational decision-making should integrate over the unknown threshold distribution using expected values, which might still render the individual contribution negligible? Third, does the argument need to distinguish more carefully between metaphysical possibility (my action could be the decisive one) and the practical requirements of moral reasoning? Finally, I would welcome suggestions on whether the injection of non-linear dynamics genuinely advances the philosophical debate or merely reframes a familiar point in the language of complex systems.
