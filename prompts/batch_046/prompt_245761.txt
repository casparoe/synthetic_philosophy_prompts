Drawing on Cicero's reasoning in Cato Maior de Senectute — particularly his claim that old age frees the mind from bodily appetites and should be filled with study, counsel, and public engagement rather than withdrawn idleness — predict how he would evaluate two post-classical developments. First, consider the modern biomedical ambition to extend human lifespan through regenerative medicine and pharmacological maintenance of physical function, especially in cases where such interventions preserve bodily vitality well into the tenth or eleventh decade without a corresponding preservation of memory, judgment, or civic capacity. Would Cicero see this as an expansion of the wise elder's accumulation of experience, or as a prolongation of the very bodily servitude he argued old age ought to escape? Second, consider the institutional practice of mandatory retirement at a fixed chronological age, which removes experienced citizens from professional and political roles regardless of mental vigor. Would he defend it as a protection of dignity and generational succession, or condemn it as a wasteful exile of minds still capable of contributing to the commonwealth? In answering, adhere closely to his distinctions between corporeal decline and intellectual flourishing, his view of death as natural and not fearful if life has been well lived, and his praise for continued activity in affairs of state.
