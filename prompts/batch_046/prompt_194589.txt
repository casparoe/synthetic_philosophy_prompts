I recently inherited a box of my grandfather's papers, and among them I found a sealed envelope bearing a typed label: "Open only in case of family emergency — contains the true account of the 1987 dispute." Inside is a single sheet: a photocopy of a newspaper article about that dispute, covered in my grandfather's handwritten annotations correcting names and dates. Stapled to it is a handwritten note reading, "Everything in the official record is false; destroy this after reading so that no one can misquote it." The envelope frames the contents as an authoritative emergency disclosure; the note frames them as a dangerous secret to be erased; but the physical object itself — a carefully preserved, corrected photocopy kept for decades — seems to frame them as enduring evidence meant to correct the record. I feel torn between honoring the literal instruction to destroy it, respecting the envelope's framing as a legitimate disclosure, and treating the preservation and annotation as the real message that this was meant to survive. Where does the meaning actually reside: in the explicit text, in the contextual frame that surrounds it, or in the inner message conveyed by the very fact that it was kept and annotated? If these layers conflict, is there a principled way to determine which one governs, or does the choice reduce to an arbitrary preference?
