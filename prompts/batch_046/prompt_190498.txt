Consider the following asymmetry in contemporary liberal governance: public retirement-savings systems are routinely structured with automatic enrollment and opt-out provisions, on the explicit ground that inertia and present-biased cognition are correctable defects that license the state to reshape choice architecture without violating autonomy; yet those same jurisdictions generally reject mandatory vaccination for competent adults, treating refusal—even when it imposes severe external costs—as an exercise of bodily autonomy that retains normative weight irrespective of its rationality. What conception of autonomy, rational agency, and the temporal unity of the self does that pairing presuppose? In particular, does it depend on a distinction between diachronic autonomy (authority over the arc of one's life) and synchronic autonomy (authority in the immediate moment), or on a further notion—call it volitional continuity—according to which preferences formed under cognitive bias fail to carry the normative authority of reflectively endorsed choices? Reconstruct the implicit philosophical framework, determine whether both policies actually rest on a single consistent theory or are philosophically underdetermined, and indicate what a uniform paternalist architecture—whether libertarian-paternalist or hard-paternalist—would have to alter if it replaced the implicit view with a straightforward preference-satisfaction or rational-endorsement standard.
