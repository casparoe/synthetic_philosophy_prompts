I am a doctoral student in philosophy of science with a focus on decision theory and cosmological reasoning. I have been developing a position that tries to navigate between Bostrom's self-sampling assumption and the self-indication assumption by introducing a causal-accessibility constraint on reference-class selection. I want to defend the view that anthropic reasoning should employ a causally restricted self-sampling principle: we should condition our probabilities only on observers who stand in an appropriate causal relation to the evidential mechanism at hand, rather than on all observers that exist in some cosmological model. I think this avoids the presumptuous philosopher problem—the absurd result that we should be highly confident we live in a high-population universe—without embracing the self-indication assumption's claim that our own existence is direct evidence for larger populations. Please construct a rigorous philosophical argument for this causally restricted principle. Your argument should do four things. First, clarify why the unrestricted self-sampling assumption generates the presumptuous philosopher result and explain why that result is genuinely problematic rather than merely counterintuitive. Second, show that the self-indication assumption resolves the problem only at the cost of making existence itself evidentially loaded in ways that undermine standard statistical practice, such as drawing inferences from random sampling in finite populations. Third, defend the causal-accessibility restriction as principled rather than ad hoc: explain how it connects to established norms for selecting reference classes in statistical inference, and why observers who are merely possible but causally disconnected should be excluded. Fourth, respond to the objection that this restriction either collapses into evidential solipsism—allowing us to ignore all distant possibilities—or fails to apply consistently to cosmological reasoning about the total size or structure of the universe. I am not asking for a literature review or citations; I want a sustained conceptual argument that could stand on its own.
