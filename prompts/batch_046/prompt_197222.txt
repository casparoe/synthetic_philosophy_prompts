I am drafting a historical novel in which the protagonist, a former scholastic theologian imprisoned for heterodox writings, spends his confinement reading Descartes’s Meditations in a smuggled Latin edition. In the passage I have been studying—the second meditation—the narrator undertakes a systematic demolition of belief: he resolves to treat his senses as deceptive, to reject mathematical certainties as possibly corrupted by an omnipotent deceiver, and even to suspend judgment about the existence of bodies, of space, and of God. Yet at the nadir of this doubt he encounters a resistance he cannot overcome. Whenever he considers that he might be deceived about every particular content of thought, he notices that the very occurrence of that consideration—this present act of being deceived, of doubting, of thinking—requires that there be something performing it. From this he draws the first indubitable conclusion: that he exists, at least so long as he thinks.

What confounds my character, and what I need to understand more precisely in order to write his interior monologue with philosophical rigor rather than historical costume, is the exact logical architecture of that conclusion. The text seems to begin with something minimal—the bare occurrence of thinking, an event that might belong to no enduring owner—and then, almost imperceptibly, it transforms that event into proof of a substantial self, a res cogitans that is essentially a thinking thing. My theologian, trained in Aristotelian categories, suspects that this is not one inference but two: first, that there is present thinking; second, that there is a thinker who possesses that thinking as an attribute or essence. He wonders whether the move from the first to the second is genuinely secured by the argument, or whether it depends on the token-reflexive structure of the pronoun—I—such that saying “I think” is tautologically guaranteed to refer to the speaker without thereby proving that the speaker is a metaphysically substantial subject rather than a transient mode or occurrence of thought. At the same time, he considers the opposing possibility: that to deny one’s own existence while performing the act of denial is not merely a logical contradiction but a practical or performative one, which might legitimate the slide from bare occurrence to enduring substance after all.

Could you help me parse the passage’s reasoning with that level of granularity? I want to know precisely what the cogito establishes—whether it proves only a momentary, unowned occurrence of thought, or whether it genuinely warrants the attribution of substantial identity—and where, if anywhere, the argument relies on something like performative self-reference rather than on a transparent metaphysical inference. If there is a specific step in the text where the move from “there is thinking now” to “I am a thinking substance” is made, I need to understand whether that step is defensible or whether it smuggles in the very ontology Descartes claimed to have bracketed. I am not looking for a bibliography or a survey of commentators; I am trying to determine what my imprisoned theologian should actually conclude as he turns the page, and whether his suspicion of an illicit leap is the reaction of a trained philosopher or merely the anxiety of a man who has lost his faith.
