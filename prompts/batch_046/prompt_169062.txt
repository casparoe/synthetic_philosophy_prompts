I have been studying the recorded rulings from Professor Miriam Kessler’s long-running graduate seminar on Nozick’s tracking conditions and the principle of epistemic closure. Her students have documented her consistent judgments across the following four cases. First, when a subject sees a red cup on a desk in ordinary light and believes it is red, Kessler rules that the subject knows. Second, when a subject knows she has hands and competently deduces that she is therefore not a handless brain in a vat being fed corresponding experiences, Kessler rules that the subject does not know the entailment. Third, when a subject knows that all bachelors are unmarried and that John is a bachelor, and competently deduces that John is unmarried, Kessler rules that the subject knows the conclusion. Fourth, when a subject observes only white swans across many regions and concludes that all swans are white, Kessler rules that the subject does not know.

Now consider a new case presented for her judgment. A researcher knows, by direct physical measurement, that a particular triangle in her laboratory has side lengths of exactly 3, 4, and 5 units. She applies the Pythagorean theorem, which she fully grasps, to conclude that the triangle is right-angled. What she does not know is that, although the metric structure of space is Euclidean in the actual world, there are nearby possible worlds with an imperceptible non-Euclidean curvature in which side lengths of 3-4-5 do not entail a right angle; in those worlds she would apply the same theorem and form the same belief.

If Professor Kessler wishes to remain consistent with her own track record, what should she rule about whether the researcher knows the triangle is right-angled? Which of her four earlier verdicts is the closest analog? And if her precedents genuinely underdetermine the outcome, what principle would she be adopting by ruling that the researcher knows, and what principle by ruling that she does not?
