Here is roughly where I have landed on the identity of indiscernibles and Black's symmetric universe, and I want to check whether these commitments hang together:

(1) In Black's scenario, the two spheres are genuinely distinct objects despite sharing every qualitative and relational property.
(2) I reject primitive haecceities—bare thisnesses—because they do no explanatory work.
(3) Instead, I think their distinctness is grounded in diachronic world-line identity: even in an otherwise static universe, the passage of time assigns each sphere a unique spacetime trajectory, and trajectory-identity individuates without being qualitative or haecceitistic.
(4) For fundamental quantum particles, I deny individual identity altogether; they are genuinely indistinguishable in a way that macroscopic composites are not.
(5) So I hold that Leibniz's principle fails for composite objects but holds at the fundamental level.

Do these hang together? In particular, does (3) either collapse into the very haecceities I reject in (2), or depend on a global temporal orientation that Black's static scenario may not provide? And does the quantum distinction in (4) sit comfortably with (1), given that the spheres are composed of particles that themselves lack individual identity? If there is a tension, order them by severity, specify what would have to give in each case, and say which revision is cheapest and what it costs.
