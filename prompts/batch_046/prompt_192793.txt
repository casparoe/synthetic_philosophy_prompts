A philosophy-of-physics essay summarizes Kurt Gödel's argument about time and rotating universes in the following way: "Gödel showed that Einstein's field equations permit a homogeneous, rotating universe. In such a model, every world-line can be closed, allowing an observer to travel into their own past. Gödel took this not merely as a physical curiosity but as a direct metaphysical proof that objective time does not exist: if the past can be revisited, then the distinction between past, present, and future has no objective basis and is only a subjective illusion, analogous to the perspectival appearance of left and right. Consequently, Einstein's own theory undermines the reality of temporal flow, confirming the older philosophical suspicion—shared by thinkers like Kant and McTaggart—that time is not part of the furniture of the world. The essay then objects that modern astronomical observations place strong bounds on global cosmic rotation, so the physical premise is false; therefore, Gödel's philosophical conclusion collapses."

Assess this summary. Go through it point by point: identify what captures Gödel's actual reasoning in his cosmological work and in his philosophical remarks on time, what subtly overstates or conflates distinct claims (for instance, between the existence of closed timelike curves in a particular metric and a general metaphysical thesis about all temporal ordering), and what is simply mistaken. In particular, discuss whether Gödel intended his rotating-universe model to serve as an empirical proof about our actual universe or rather as a demonstration of a conceptual possibility within general relativity, and evaluate whether an observational disproof of large-scale rotation is the appropriate response to his philosophical argument about the unreality of time. Reach a clear verdict on how fair the summary is and whether its rebuttal succeeds.
