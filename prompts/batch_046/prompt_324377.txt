Compare Peter van Inwagen’s restricted-composition account—on which only living organisms and simples genuinely exist, and arbitrary aggregates are ontologically unreal—with an “indexical composition” framework in which whether a given plurality of parts constitutes a unified object is not an absolute fact but depends on an indexing scheme (for instance, a functional or descriptive parameter that sorts parts into a unified role). Focus your comparison on three specific problems: (1) how each view treats symbiotic or parasitic subcomponents that blur the boundary of an organism; (2) whether either can grant artifacts such as ships or statues an intermediate ontological status between full-blown substances and mere heaps; and (3) how each handles diachronic persistence when the internal part-composition of a system changes gradually over time. Do not merely catalog similarities and differences; argue which framework better balances ontological parsimony with explanatory adequacy across these cases, and identify the deepest metaphysical commitment—absolute versus scheme-relative composition—that drives your evaluation.
