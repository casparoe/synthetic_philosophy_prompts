Question: Consider the sorites argument applied to the predicate 'is a mountain' using precise elevation measurements. Williamson's epistemicist claims there's a sharp semantic cutoff—say exactly 600 metres—but we can't know it because vague knowledge carries a margin for error. The supervaluationist denies a unique cutoff and holds that 'mountain' is true on some admissible precisifications and false on others at the borderline, leaving no fact of the matter.

Now suppose geologists discover a literal tectonic gap: no landforms exist between 599.99 m and 600.01 m. Does this empirical discontinuity help the epistemicist? Does the supervaluationist have to concede there's now a correct precisification?

Student answer (around 350 words):

Williamson's epistemicism treats vagueness as epistemic rather than semantic. There is a precise height at which mountainhood ends, but our knowledge is inexact, so there's always a margin for error pushing the unknown boundary away from clear cases. The tectonic gap might look like empirical confirmation—nature has drawn a sharp line at 600 m. But that's a category mistake: the epistemicist boundary is fixed by the conditions of correct linguistic application, not by geology. So the gap tells us nothing about where the semantic cutoff lies.

Supervaluationism, by contrast, rejects any unique sharpening. Fine argues that there are many admissible precisifications—classical reinterpretations that preserve definite mountains and definite non-mountains. At the borderline, 'this is a mountain' lacks a definite truth-value because it's true on some precisifications and false on others; this is where the margin-for-error principle shows we can't locate a hidden boundary. Given the tectonic gap, one might think supervaluationists must now pick the precisification that places the cutoff inside the empty region, since sharpening through a physical void is arbitrary. But this gets things backwards. Admissibility is constrained by linguistic consistency and paradigmatic cases, not by empirical geography. Even with the gap, precisifications placing the boundary at 590 m or 610 m remain admissible. However, I think supervaluationists still face pressure: any sharpening that crosses the gap assigns status to non-existent terrain, so those precisifications should be ruled out, leaving only ones that align with the discontinuity.
