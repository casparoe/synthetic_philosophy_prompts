I'm writing the conceptual framework for my dissertation chapter on scientific instrumentation and historical contingency, and I'd like an audit of whether my commitments hang together before I defend them in a workshop.

Here is the package:

(1) Path dependence in science is ontologically real: once a particular instrument design—say, the standardized cyclotron architecture that crystallized in the 1930s—becomes embedded in a research community, it genuinely constrains which experimental questions become tractable, independent of our retrospective narration.

(2) Counterfactual questions of the form "would the same physical results have arrived via a different instrumental route?" are metaphysically legitimate and can be evaluated by examining near-miss episodes and parallel laboratory developments.

(3) I reject macro-historical determinism: I think institutional noise and structural contingency leave room for genuine branching, so identical macro-outcomes are not causally guaranteed.

(4) I propose that we should measure path dependence by the density of causal connectivity in the local institutional graph, not by narrative coherence: a trajectory is locked in when deviation would require simultaneous disruption of multiple interdependent nodes.

(5) Nevertheless, I maintain that every ascription of path dependence is ultimately an epistemic heuristic—a tool for managing our ignorance about complex causal networks—without independent ontological weight.

The tension that worries me is between (1) and (5): (1) sounds like a claim about objective structural constraint, while (5) sounds like an anti-realist collapse of that constraint into cognitive limitation. There also seems to be friction between (2) and (5), since if counterfactual evaluation is just heuristic modeling, it is unclear why we should treat near-miss evidence as tracking a real modal fact rather than as a convenience. Meanwhile, (3) and (1) might look incompatible—if history is genuinely branching, how can a path be locked in?—but I suspect that appearance dissolves.

Please go through these commitments systematically: identify the genuine tensions, rank them by severity, say what each tension costs to resolve, and flag which pairs are only apparently in conflict. In particular, is (5) fatal, or can it be read as a claim about our epistemic access to the objective metric in (4) without undermining (1)? And which single revision—dropping or modifying one commitment—would clean up the most inconsistencies?
