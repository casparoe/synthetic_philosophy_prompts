After a long argument with a friend about Sartre’s existentialism, I am trying to determine whether her summary of his view on radical freedom and bad faith is essentially faithful or quietly distorts it in ways that make the position sound more extreme than it is. She puts it like this: Sartre holds that human consciousness possesses radical freedom, meaning we are never causally determined by our past, our biology, or our social situation; at every instant we could choose to be anyone or do anything, since consciousness is pure spontaneity that transcends all facticity. From this she derives that bad faith is specifically the refusal to acknowledge this absolute openness—typically by treating oneself as a fixed essence, such as identifying completely with one’s job, gender, or psychological history, and thereby pretending that one’s actions are dictated by those roles rather than chosen. She then claims that, for Sartre, this freedom makes us entirely responsible not just for our present choices but for the very meaning of our past and our circumstances, since we always choose how to interpret and live with them; consequently, she concludes, Sartre’s position rules out any genuine influence of social structure or psychological conditioning on human action, reducing sociology and psychoanalysis to descriptions of self-deception.

Something about this sounds too extreme—especially the suggestion that Sartre thinks we can literally transcend physical or historical constraints at will, or that he denies conditioning altogether—but I cannot tell whether I am reacting to a real feature of his view or to a misreading she has introduced. Could you audit this summary? Please separate: (a) what is accurate to Sartre’s account of radical freedom and bad faith, including the proper relation between transcendence and facticity; (b) what is a defensible popular simplification versus a subtle misrepresentation—particularly regarding whether freedom means physical omnipotence or the choice of one’s fundamental project, and whether bad faith operates only in the direction of hiding behind social roles; and (c) what Sartre would explicitly deny, especially about our relation to the body, the past, and social conditions. Finally, does her conclusion—that Sartre’s existentialism is incompatible with any meaningful social or psychological determinism—actually follow from the corrected view, or does it depend on the errors you have identified?
