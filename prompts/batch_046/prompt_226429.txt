In my graduate seminar on Wittgenstein's On Certainty we have reached an impasse that feels more like a deadlock than a disagreement. For months we have argued about whether hinge commitments—such as the long existence of the earth, or the general reliability of measurement instruments—are best understood as a special class of propositional beliefs (default entitlements that do not require evidential support but can still be false in principle) or as non-propositional rules that make justification possible and do not have truth-values at all. Both sides cite the riverbed-and-water metaphor, both agree that hinges are not empirically justified, and both accept that in ordinary life we do not doubt them; yet whenever one side calls a hinge true but unjustified, the other objects that this is a category mistake, and when the grammarian says hinges lack truth-values, the entitlement theorist hears a denial of their obvious correctness. Recently I introduced a test case to break the stalemate: imagine a research community that gradually stops treating instrument reliability as a background hinge and instead institutes mandatory quarterly empirical audits of every instrument, without anyone experiencing this shift as a crisis of certainty. The entitlement theorist describes this as a hinge being rationally revised or downgraded to an ordinary belief; the grammarian describes the exact same behavioral shift as a change in the rules of the language-game, not a revision of belief. They do not disagree about the observable facts of the case, only about what to call it, and neither can point to an observable difference that would decide the matter.

Is this stall merely verbal—are we using belief and hinge in different ways—or is there a substantive disagreement about the ontology of commitment hidden behind our shared vocabulary? Could it be that one side is really asking a psychological question about mental states while the other asks a grammatical question about conditions of sense, so that we are answering different questions under one name? Or is the impasse a sign that the concept of a hinge is itself ill-posed, a family-resemblance notion that does not support the kind of unified theory either side wants? I want a diagnosis of the structure of this disagreement—supported by what each side actually claims and by the test case I described—together with an account of what would actually move it: a conceptual distinction, an empirical study of scientific practice, a formal model of belief revision, a change of question, or a reason to think progress here is impossible. If my framing of the debate rests on a confused premise—say, if I am wrongly assuming that both sides are talking about the same phenomenon—please say so plainly rather than accommodating the confusion.
