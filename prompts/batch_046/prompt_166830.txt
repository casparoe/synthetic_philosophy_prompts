Write a 35-minute exam essay question (30 marks) for an upper-level philosophy-of-cognition course. Students have studied Hofstadter’s account of creativity as systematic variation within a conceptual space—turning parameters or “knobs” on a deep thematic invariant while preserving its core structure—but have not read extensive secondary literature. The question should present one concrete, non-obvious case of conceptual innovation that you specify in the prompt: for example, the shift from Newtonian force-based dynamics to Lagrangian energy-based dynamics, treating predictive and explanatory success regarding motion as the invariant theme and coordinate-system independence together with the elimination of explicit constraint forces as turned parameters. Ask students to do three things: (1) identify the thematic invariant and at least two specific parameters that were adjusted in the case; (2) explain whether this transformation counts as genuinely creative on Hofstadter’s model or as a mere technical reformulation; and (3) defend a definite position on whether the knob-twiddling framework captures what is essential about the innovation or misses something crucial—such as the role of empirical anomalies, representational rupture, or social negotiation in driving the change—giving explicit reasons rather than listing both sides. Provide a model answer that takes a clear stance, either arguing that the framework successfully explains the creative structure of the shift or that it collapses an important distinction by treating a revolutionary reconceptualization as smooth parameter variation, and justify that stance with reference to the mechanics of the case. Include a marking scheme that awards 8 marks for correct identification of the invariant and the adjusted knobs, 8 marks for accurate deployment of the conceptual-space machinery (e.g., distinguishing surface-feature change from deep-structure preservation), 9 marks for a substantive, non-generic argument in part (3) that actually engages the case rather than repeating generic claims about “all creativity,” and 5 marks for clarity and coherence. Explicitly list mistakes that should cost marks: confusing parameter adjustment with random trial-and-error; treating the thematic core as a static, unparameterized object rather than as a structure with variable dimensions; invoking unrelated theories of creativity (e.g., Boden’s exploratory versus transformational distinction) without integrating them into Hofstadter’s framework; and failing in part (3) to commit to one verdict with supporting reasoning. Write in formal academic prose; do not include bibliographies, citation requests, or reading lists; and ensure the question is answerable from the core framework plus general knowledge of the specified scientific case.
