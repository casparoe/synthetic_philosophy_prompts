hey—working on a feature about how philosophers think we understand strangers, and a source sent me this gloss on Davidson's radical interpretation. He says Davidson's core claim is that to understand anyone at all you have to apply a 'principle of charity' that treats them as mostly rational and mostly right about the world, but that this charity is basically just a psychological guess we make to bridge the gap. He also says Davidson's view is really just Quine's indeterminacy of translation with extra steps—since we're charitably assigning beliefs, there's no real fact about what someone means, just our best fit. And he claims Davidson used this to kill the idea of incommensurable conceptual schemes, because radical interpretation forces every foreign belief into our own framework.

I feel like he's smushing together Davidson and Quine, and maybe treating charity like a mental shortcut rather than a constitutive condition. Is this summary fair? Where does it get Davidson right, where does it simplify too hard, and what's flat wrong? I'm especally interested in whether Davidson's anti-psychologism about belief-attribution is being ignored, and whether his idea of triangulation—between speaker, interpreter, and world—gets left out in a way that changes the whole picture. Also, does the incommensurability argument actually hang on charity the way he describes, or is there a different move there?
