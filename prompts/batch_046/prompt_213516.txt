In philosophical debates about the emergence and persistence of social norms, there is a long-standing stall between defenders of a Lewis-style rational-choice analysis—according to which a regularity becomes a convention because it solves a coordination problem that participants prefer solved in that particular way, with common knowledge of that preference—and evolutionary-game-theoretic accounts that derive the same regularities from replicator dynamics, learning, or selection without attributing to agents any explicit common knowledge of rational preferences. Both sides accept the same formal apparatus (coordination games, equilibrium selection, payoff structures) and the same empirical cases (language conventions, traffic norms, etiquette rules). Both concede that norms carry some normative weight, in the sense that deviation warrants criticism. Yet for decades neither side has persuaded the other: the Lewis camp insists that evolutionary models fail to capture the specific ought of convention, because they cannot account for why an agent feels first-person entitled to demand compliance rather than merely expecting punishment; the evolutionary camp responds that this ought is nothing more than an internalized expectation with no independent ontology, and that Lewis illicitly imports rationalist psychology unsupported by behavioral data.

My working hypothesis is that the impasse concerns not the causal mechanism of emergence but what I will call normative residue—the claim that once you have fully explained why a regularity is stable and why deviation is sanctioned, there remains an unexplained remainder in the form of an agent's authoritative entitlement to demand conformity, a remainder that evolutionary dynamics can model as a behavioral disposition but cannot constitute as a normative reason. The evolutionary side treats this residue as a projected fiction onto stable dynamics; the Lewis side treats the dynamics as constitutively insufficient without it.

Is this diagnosis correct? Or is there a hidden empirical disagreement—for instance, about whether common knowledge is psychologically realistic in large-scale populations—that is actually doing the work? Are the two camps answering different questions under one name: one seeking a constitutive explanation of normative authority, the other a causal explanation of behavioral regularity? Is the question ill-posed, or is it merely hard? I want a diagnosis supported by what each side actually says, together with an assessment of whether anything—a formal result showing that evolutionary dynamics necessarily generate first-person entitlement structures, an empirical study that distinguishes predictive expectations from normative demands, or a conceptual distinction that either dissolves or vindicates the residue—could actually end the stall, or whether no progress should be expected because the sides measure theoretical adequacy by incommensurable standards.
