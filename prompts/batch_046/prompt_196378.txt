I spent most of last evening in a long, rather heated argument with a close friend about whether the sciences genuinely carve nature at its joints, and I have tried to turn our disagreement into the outline of a short philosophical essay. The core claim I want to defend is that the joint-carving metaphor smuggles in an anatomical picture of reality—discrete parts separated by clear boundaries—that is actively misleading in both physical chemistry and microbiology. In chemistry, I argue that the status of isotopes and synthetically produced superheavy elements undermines any simple essentialist account of the elements: we do not discover a pre-given essence of element 118, but rather stabilize a cluster of nuclear and chemical interactions that we then classify. In microbiology, I press the point that horizontal gene transfer makes the species concept—already contested in macrobiology—practically useless for prokaryotes, so that the very idea of a joint between kinds dissolves into a continuum of genetic exchange. From these two cases, I draw a more general conclusion: natural kinds are better understood not as discovered joints nor as purely conventional categories, but as interaction-stable clusters that emerge from the coupling of our measurement practices with regions of relative stability in nature. I am particularly interested in whether this middle position is genuinely different from Richard Boyd's homeostatic property cluster account, or whether I am merely restating it in the language of practice rather than essence.

Would you be willing to read these notes and offer comments? I am especially interested in three things. First, does the move from chemical elements to microbial taxonomy work as a unified argument, or does it conflate two quite different failures of essentialism—one about the ontology of matter, the other about reproductive isolation? Second, is the interaction-stable cluster framing philosophically substantive, or does it collapse back into either essentialism or conventionalism once pressed? Third, I want you to distinguish carefully between what is broadly settled in the philosophy of science—say, that strict Kripke-Putnam-style essentialism is widely rejected in biology—and what remains genuinely contested, such as whether quantum-mechanical structure provides an essentialist foundation for chemical kinds, whether Boyd's cluster theory itself survives recent critiques, and whether my critique of the anatomical metaphor is original or a well-known objection I have inadvertently rediscovered. Please do not present a disputed view as the consensus, and please avoid treating my framing of the joint metaphor as obviously correct if it is in fact already standard in the literature.
