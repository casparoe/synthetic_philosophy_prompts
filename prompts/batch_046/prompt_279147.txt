I am a retired physicist who has only recently begun to read systematically in moral philosophy, and I have drafted notes for a short essay that I hope to refine. The essay concerns intergenerational justice in climate policy. I want to argue that framing climate obligations in terms of rights held by particular future individuals fails because of the non-identity problem: altering our emissions trajectory changes which people will eventually exist, so no specific future person can claim to have been harmed by our current choices. Rather than abandoning intergenerational obligations altogether, I propose that we reconceive them as duties to preserve what I call functional ecological identity—the continuity of large-scale climate-regulating processes, such as ocean circulation patterns and carbon-cycle feedback dynamics, that form a kind of structural inheritance independent of the particular humans who eventually live within them. On this view, damaging these processes is wrong not because it violates the rights of identifiable descendants, but because it disrupts a systemic continuity that carries normative weight analogous to the maintenance of a shared scientific apparatus across successive generations of researchers who never individually own it. I am particularly uncertain about two points. First, does this move genuinely escape the non-identity problem, or does it merely relocate the difficulty, given that these ecological processes are themselves partly constituted by human activity? Second, is the analogy to scientific instruments misleading, since instruments are designed to serve human purposes, whereas ecological processes have no such teleology? I would welcome your comments on the coherence of this reasoning, especially on the transition from individual rights to process-based obligations, and on whether my scientific analogies illuminate or obscure the underlying moral structure. Please respond in continuous prose without headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables, writing as you would in a letter, and please say explicitly what argument or empirical consideration would lead you to revise your judgment—where in particular you think my reasoning is most likely to fail.
