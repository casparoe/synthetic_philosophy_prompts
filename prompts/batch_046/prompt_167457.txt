Here is an argument I sketched for a metaphysics seminar, and now it looks like it does not merely challenge the identity of indiscernibles—it seems to prove that nothing can ever move. (1) The principle of the identity of indiscernibles (PII) says that if x and y share every property, then x = y. (2) In Black’s two-sphere universe, the spheres are qualitatively identical in size, color, and material; they differ only relationally—say, sphere A is two miles north of sphere B. (3) So a defender of PII must treat “being two miles north of B” as a genuine property of A, and “being two miles south of A” as a property of B. (4) If spatial relations are properties in this sense, then being located at spacetime point p is a property of whatever occupies p. (5) Now take a ball that rolls continuously from point p at t1 to point q at t2 (q ≠ p). The ball-at-t1 has the property “being at p”; the ball-at-t2 has “being at q.” (6) Because p and q differ, these two share no spatial property. (7) Applying PII across these two, the ball-at-t1 is not identical to the ball-at-t2—so motion is never persistence of one object, but replacement by numerically distinct objects at each point. (8) Generalized, continuous motion is impossible; every apparent movement is a succession of different things. (9) But we observe continuous motion and treat it as real. (10) Therefore PII is false.

I would rather keep (1) and (9) than accept that motion is an illusion of replacement. Which step should I reject—(3)’s treatment of relations as intrinsic monadic properties, (6)-(7)’s diachronic application of PII, or something else? And if I reject it, what do I pay: must I deny that location is ever a property, become a four-dimensionalist about persistence, or concede that Black’s spheres are genuinely indiscernible and that PII needs a modal or qualitative restriction?
