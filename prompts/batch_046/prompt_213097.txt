What is your credence—stated as a specific number between 0 and 1—that G. E. Moore's account of intrinsic value, if applied with full consistency to his own claim that personal affection and the appreciation of beauty are the supreme goods, entails the following comparative judgment: no accumulation of solitary aesthetic appreciation, however intense or widespread, can outweigh even the minimal instantiation of mutual friendship, because friendship alone involves an organic unity between the appreciation of another's qualities and the affection directed toward them, whereas solitary appreciation lacks any analogous relational structure?

Please do not merely answer yes or no. Decompose your credence across at least four sub-questions:
(i) whether Moore's principle of organic unities is intended to govern relations between distinct goods (inter-good) or only internal to a single state of consciousness;
(ii) whether friendship, on Moore's view, necessarily comprises an organic whole in which affection and the cognition of the friend's value are inseparable and jointly greater than their sum;
(iii) whether solitary aesthetic appreciation is genuinely devoid of any comparable internal complexity or relational dimension that could generate its own organic value;
(iv) whether Moore's framework permits quantitative comparison across different categories of supreme goods at all, or whether such comparisons are conceptually blocked.

For each sub-question, assign a rough probability that Moore's view resolves in the direction supporting the comparative judgment above. Then combine them—explicitly or implicitly—into your final number. Finally, identify which single sub-question your final credence is most sensitive to, and what specific feature of Moore's argument or conceptual framework would cause you to revise that sub-probability by the largest margin.
