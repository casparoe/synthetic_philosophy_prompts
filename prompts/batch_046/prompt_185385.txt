I am preparing questions for a recorded panel interview with a moral and political philosopher—let us call her Dr. Elena Voss—who has recently advanced an account she terms “diachronic transparency” in the ethics of public commemoration. Rather than assessing statues or street names by the moral purity of the figure honoured, Voss argues that the central ethical question is whether a commemorative object currently makes visible, or instead obscures, the historical conditions of its own installation and the institutional reasons for its continued presence. She maintains that monuments and named streets function as “standing invitations” to civic identification, and that retaining them creates a “structural misalignment” when an institution’s present normative commitments conflict with the values implicitly embedded in its symbolic landscape. Crucially, she distinguishes focal, intentional monuments (statues) from infrastructural, accretive designations (street names), and she introduces the concept of “mnemonic collateral”: the claim that every retained contested monument borrows or depletes symbolic authority from other objects in the same commemorative economy, so that the ethics of retention cannot be assessed case by case.

Please draft ten to twelve interview questions that engage directly with the mechanics of this view. I require coverage of: (i) the statue versus street-name distinction and why infrastructural embedding complicates the demand for transparency; (ii) cases where the original installation was itself contested, coerced, or made without democratic mandate; (iii) what “mnemonic collateral” explains that a simpler harm-based or offence-based framework cannot capture; and (iv) whether her position requires some form of negative or counter-commemoration—contextual plaques, renamed shadows, or archival displays—as a necessary condition for legitimate retention rather than removal. For each question, provide an anticipated reply consistent with her view and a sharp follow-up that anticipates her standard deflection—particularly her tendency to reframe removal not as erasure but as a change in the mode of transparency. Avoid biographical or generic philosophical questions; keep the tone rigorous but accessible to an educated non-specialist audience. Please write in British English.
