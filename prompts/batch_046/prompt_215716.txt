Write a dialogue between Dr. Elena Vasquez, defending a causal-role account of biological function, and Dr. Marcus Chen, defending a selected-effects (etiological) account. The scene is a workshop on synthetic biology; they are debating the proper function—if any—of a rationally engineered protein scaffold they call Stabilon, inserted into the endoplasmic reticulum of human induced pluripotent stem cells. Stabilon was designed de novo to bind misfolded collagen and was never present in evolutionary history, yet once integrated it recruits endogenous chaperones and reduces ER stress in a manner that precisely mirrors the causal contribution of naturally evolved quality-control proteins. Vasquez should argue that Stabilon’s function is chaperone recruitment and stress reduction, since on her view function is fixed by the contribution a component makes to the current capacities of the containing system. Chen should reply that without an evolutionary history of selection for that contribution, Stabilon possesses only causal effects, not proper functions, because etiological accounts tie function to the historical reason the trait was retained. The exchange must be genuinely responsive: Chen should explain why an identical causal profile is insufficient, and Vasquez should explain why appealing to evolutionary history risks conflating function with origin story in ways that misclassify evolved parasites or vestigial structures. They should also examine together whether a “design-selection analog” could ground function in synthetic contexts, refining or rejecting it on shared grounds rather than treating it as an easy compromise. At the end, each speaker should state explicitly what empirical finding or conceptual argument would change their verdict on Stabilon—Vasquez should specify what observation about its system integration or evolutionary analogues would force her to concede that selection history is indispensable, and Chen should specify what discovery about naturally evolved chaperones—say, one that performs the identical causal role but was never selected for stress reduction—would undermine his reliance on selection history. Finally, each should identify the weakest point in their own reasoning and say where the argument is most likely to fail. The response should be self-critical throughout: it should flag which considerations could lead to a different verdict about Stabilon’s function, and it should openly mark where its reasoning depends on contestable assumptions about the boundary between designed artifacts and biological traits.
