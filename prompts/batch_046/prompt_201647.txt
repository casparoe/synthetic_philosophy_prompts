I'm taking an advanced course in jurisprudence that has me revisiting the question of legal moralism, and after a long discussion with friends I realized my intuitions about when law should enforce moral norms form a pattern I can't explain. I think a municipal ordinance banning the display of a flag tied to a well-documented regime of systematic violence is justifiable, even if the display occurs on private property and no direct physical harm results. I also think a statute criminalizing private, consensual adult relationships that depart from traditional norms is clearly wrong, even when supporters argue it protects social cohesion. Similarly, I reject a city ban on a laboratory-grown protein that is nutritionally identical to conventional meat, despite widespread disgust at what people call its unnatural origin. But I feel differently about symbolic acts: prohibiting the public desecration of monuments honoring soldiers who fought in a war now widely regarded as unjust seems appropriate to me, while I would oppose banning private rituals in which people burn replicas of a revered text, even though believers experience deep moral offense. The tension is that I seem willing to restrict some expressive or norm-violating conduct but not others, and I cannot tell whether the relevant factor is public visibility, the presence of a historically vulnerable group being symbolically targeted, the kind of disgust involved, or something like the difference between expressing contempt and merely violating convention. Is there a coherent principle that captures all five of these verdicts without simply restating them? If not, which verdict should I drop for the sake of consistency? And applying whatever principle emerges, what should I think about a proposed public museum exhibition that recreates the sensory conditions of a documented historical famine in order to provoke moral reflection, fully aware that it will cause severe distress to descendants of survivors?
