Read Abbott's Flatland as an extended meditation on metaphysical grounding and fundamentality rather than only as social satire. Focus on A Square's encounters with Lineland, Pointland, and Spaceland: does Abbott present the lower-dimensional worlds as ontologically dependent on higher-dimensional ones, or does dimensionality function as a relational property that carries no implication of fundamentality? Consider the Sphere's intrusion into Flatland and A Square's inability to perceive three-dimensional objects without being physically lifted out: does this suggest that grounding relations are epistemically opaque to the entities they ground, and that a derivative being cannot grasp its own derivative status from within? I want you to take seriously the distinction between full and partial grounding, and the debate over whether grounding must be well-founded, while resisting the reduction of Abbott's theological and social dimensions to a mere illustration of ontological dependence. Where does the novel resist this framing—particularly in the figure of the zero-dimensional Point, who denies all other dimensions and claims self-sufficiency? Does the Point serve as a reductio of the claim that there must be a fundamental, ungrounded base? Conclude by saying what Flatland contributes to contemporary debates about fundamentality and ontological dependence that formal arguments about well-foundedness tend to overlook.
