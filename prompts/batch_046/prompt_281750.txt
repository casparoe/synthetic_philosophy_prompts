I'm giving the opening remarks—roughly eight minutes—at tomorrow's interdisciplinary workshop on intelligence and alignment, co-hosted by our philosophy and computer-science departments. The room is deliberately mixed: formal epistemologists and decision theorists, ML engineers working on agent architectures, and university administrators who approve funding but do not build models. I want the philosophical content to do real structural work, not decoration. Specifically, I want to treat the orthogonality thesis and instrumental convergence not as complementary warnings but as concepts in tension: if final goals can vary without bound, then the standard convergence on shared instrumental subgoals—resource acquisition, self-preservation—may fracture under conditions I'm framing as "resource pluralism," where agents pursue genuinely incommensurable goods and therefore compete for non-substitutable resources. The remarks must be rigorous enough that specialists do not dismiss them, translated enough that the administrators grasp the governance stakes, and architecturally disciplined: they should open by framing the day's central question, and they should close by ruling out one class of questions we should not spend the workshop asking—namely, any question that assumes high intelligence carries implicit moral orientation or benevolence. Please write the speech with that tension and that closing exclusion intact.
