I've been in this philosophy of history reading group for a semester now and we keep circling the same drain about moral revolutions—like, why did dueling basically vanish in Britain within a generation, or why did Atlantic slavery collapse when it had been totally normal for centuries? Some people in the group are realy into the Appiah-style story where it's all about honor codes shifting: dueling stops being honorable, owning people stops being a badge of gentleman status, and the norm just dissolves from the inside. Others say that's just dressing up material interests in fancy clothes—elites changed because the economics stopped working or they wanted to avoid war costs and trade disruptions. Then there's the third camp that says no, people actually heard the moral arguments, they just needed time for the reasoning to percolate through institutions. We've been going round this for like six sessions and nobody's moved an inch.

My hunch is that we're not even disagreeing about history anymore; we're stuck in a conceptual knot about what counts as "the explanation" for a norm dying. The honor people are talking about internal reasons—what makes sense to the people inside the practice—while the interest people are talking about external causal pressure, and the argument people are asking about rational justification that could convince anyone regardless of social position. So maybe it's not one debate but three different questions shoved under one heading, with maybe a hidden verbal dispute about the word "reason" doing all the work, plus a bit of the Williams-style confusion between internal and external motivation. But I could be totally wrong—maybe there realy is an empirical disagreement hiding in there about whether elites changed their public rhetoric before or after their private behavior changed, and we just don't have the archives to settle it. Is this stall basically a category mistake where we're answering different questions, or is there a real substantive disagreement about causal mechanism?

Given your read, is there any conceptual distinction—like separating status equilibria from normative framing, or clarifying what "honor" means as a social mechanism versus a rational justification—that could actually unstick us? Or should we just admit the question is ill-posed and that no new historical fact is going to resolve it because we're mixing explanation with justification? I want an honest diagnosis of what kind of disagreement this is, supported by what each side actually says, and whether anything short of digging up new primary sources could count as progress—or whether the impasse itself tells us something about how moral change works. I don't need a literature review or a list of books, just a direct answer about the structure of the stall.
