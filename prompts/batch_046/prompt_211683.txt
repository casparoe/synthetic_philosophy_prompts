There's this long-running fight in philosophy of mind and ethics about whether sentience is yes/no or comes on a sliding scale—like, maybe a beetle is 0.02 conscious and a smart thermemostat is 0.0001, or maybe there's just a hard cutoff somewhere between crickets and wall clocks. People have circled it for years: one side points to continuous neural gradation, the other says without self-report you can't assign partial moral staus. My hunch is the stall isn't about brain science at all but about whether 'being sentient' is being used as a biological category or as a moral license, and those are two different questions wearing the same hat. Is that right, or is there a hidden empirical disagreement—like, do some formal models of cognition predict smooth curves while others demand sharp jumps? I don't want a survey; I want a diagnosis of the disagreement structure. Are they talking past each other on definitions, fighting over which organisms count as data, or stuck on an unarguable intuition that suffering can't be fractional? And could anything actually break it—maybe splitting 'phenomenal gradation' from 'moral gradation', or building an artificial sensor that mimics insect reflex arcs without any inner life? Or is this deadlocked for good because our concept of pain was built for humans and just breaks when stretched to insects and appliances?
