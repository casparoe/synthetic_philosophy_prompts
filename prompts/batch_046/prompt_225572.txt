In popular neuroscience, the brain is routinely likened to a computer — a comparison encouraged by John von Neumann's late reflections, in which he mapped electronic circuits onto nervous tissue while noting profound architectural divergences. The brain, he observed, operates through statistical, error-tolerant processes without discrete addressing or a central clock, relying on parallel self-repair rather than deterministic instruction sequences. Audit the brain-is-a-computer analogy in light of these specific reservations. Set out the correspondences (memory, processing, feedback loops); identify where the mapping fractures, especially regarding noise tolerance, parallel regeneration, and the absence of stored-program architecture; and determine whether conclusions about artificial cognition or human uniqueness that the analogy is used to license actually survive once von Neumann's caveats are fully registered.
