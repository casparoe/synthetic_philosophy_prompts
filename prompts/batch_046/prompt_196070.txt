Consider the institutional practice, common in university syllabi and course-design policies, of mandating content warnings for materials that contain explicit, literal descriptions of violence or trauma, while systematically exempting texts that invoke the same themes only indirectly—through hypothetical framing, philosophical thought experiments, metaphor, ironic distance, or allusive narration—even when students report indistinguishable emotional or cognitive reactions to both kinds of material. The policy thus treats a direct propositional description and an implicature-laden invocation as belonging to different categories of institutional risk.

What philosophical commitments about speaker meaning and communicative harm does this boundary presuppose? Does it implicitly depend on a Gricean separation between what a speaker literally says and what they merely implicate, treating harm as attaching only to the explicitly said content? Or does it presuppose that a speaker’s communicative intention is recoverable solely from semantic content, so that indirect framing falls outside the scope of responsibility?

Reconstruct the implicit theory of communication and harm that licenses this distinction. Is the practice coherent with views on which audience uptake—including all inferred implicatures—determines communicative effect, regardless of explicit formulation? Could an intentionalist or relevance-theoretic account of speaker meaning justify the same boundary, or does the practice quietly rule out such alternatives? And if one adopted a view on which harm tracks the total propositional and implicational content recovered by a reasonable audience, irrespective of whether it was directly said, what specific changes would be required in the design and application of these warning policies?
