A colleague in a debate about meritocracy and cognitive stratification invoked Aristotle’s doctrine of natural slavery—specifically the idea that certain human beings lack the deliberative faculty and therefore function as living instruments for those who possess it—to argue that steep hierarchies of labor and authority are not merely efficient but metaphysically appropriate. Rather than asking for historical scholarship, I want to understand what precise claims that slogan is actually carrying. Can you lay out, as a discrete menu, the distinct theses embedded in the notion of natural slavery: for example, a purely descriptive psychological claim about reasoning capacity; a normative claim that governance by others benefits those who lack full deliberation; a functional or economic claim about comparative advantage in social organization; a metaphysical claim about fixed human kinds divided into ruler and ruled; and any other interpretation you think is genuinely available in the text or in modern appropriations of it. For each thesis, indicate whether it collapses into a near-platitude once translated into modern vocabulary, whether it is a substantive claim that could be defended with contemporary evidence, or whether it is false—particularly in light of modern cognitive science, disability ethics, and the history of using such categories to justify domination. Then specify which of these readings my colleague was most likely relying on to justify present-day economic stratification, whether that reading actually follows from Aristotle’s position, and exactly where it breaks if pressed.
