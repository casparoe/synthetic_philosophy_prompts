You are delivering the opening remarks at an interdisciplinary workshop held at a university centre for memory and international law. The audience is deliberately mixed: legal scholars and historians sit alongside family members of survivors and postgraduate students with no specialist background. The workshop examines how atrocities are named, and you have been asked to frame the day without delivering a conventional welcome. Your remarks must do real structural work: they should reflect the gap between Raphael Lemkin's original moral conception of group destruction — which encompassed cultural annihilation and the targeting of political collectives — and the narrower legal category adopted in the 1948 Convention, shaped by geopolitical compromise. You should also note that contemporary efforts to recognise ecological destruction and to recover the dimensions excised from the treaty text have revived interest in Lemkin's wider, largely unpublished taxonomy, making the tension between legal precision and lived moral experience especially acute today.

Because the room contains people for whom this is not abstract, you must avoid false consolation: do not suggest that legal naming resolves moral injury, but neither dismiss the category as mere political artifice. The philosophical problem should shape what you can and cannot claim — for instance, you may not assert that harms falling outside the Convention's definition are therefore unreal, nor that recognition alone delivers justice.

Please present your remarks as exactly five points, each consisting of one or two sentences, with no introduction or conclusion. The points should speak simultaneously to the specialists and to the non-specialists present, moving from the politics of naming, through the structural limitations imposed by the 1948 exclusions, to the present revival of Lemkin's broader vision, without offering closure.
