I'm preparing a feature on distributed cognition and want to avoid the standard functionalist-versus-biological-chauvinist rerun. Taking the ant-colony case—where no single ant represents the colony's global state, yet the system solves routing problems, allocates labor, and corrects errors—what are the genuine desiderata any adequate theory of collective mind must meet? Specify the phenomena it must explain (integrated problem-solving without central representation; colony-level error correction that does not reduce to summed individual adaptations; behavioral coherence across generations despite complete turnover of members); the distinctions it must preserve (between intelligent aggregation and unified consciousness, between causal coupling and representational integration, and between genuinely having mental properties and being a mere target of anthropomorphic projection); and the empirical findings from swarm robotics or neuroethology it cannot violate. Then introduce a deliberately niche condition: imagine a colony that modifies its foraging network only after a simulated disruption is presented in a virtual pheromone layer—no ant experiences the disruption, yet the colony exhibits a sustained, generalized avoidance pattern. Must this be explained by individual-level associative learning, or does it force a genuinely collective representational state? Identify which single desideratum eliminates the most popular existing accounts—functionalism, integrated information theory applied to the swarm, and biological naturalism—and say which requirements are negotiable versus which, if dropped, dissolve the problem entirely. A concise table scoring the main contenders against the list is welcome; please do not include bibliographies or citations, since the argument must stand on first principles.
