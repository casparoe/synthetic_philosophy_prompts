I serve as chair of the University Senate Committee on Academic Governance and Student Rights. The Provost’s office has submitted a proposal to deploy the Pedagogical Formation Analytics system (PFA) across all undergraduate programs. PFA integrates data streams from the learning-management system, digital-library access logs, writing-process metadata (revision frequency, citation-network patterns, not prose content), and peer-collaboration network maps. From these inputs it generates an Academic Discipline Profile (ADP) for each student: a normalized visualization of knowledge-formation behaviors plotted against cohort distributions. The ADP is not a grade and does not appear on transcripts; it is visible to assigned academic advisors and automatically triggers developmental intervention pathways—mandatory advising sessions, study-skills modules, or restricted course-registration privileges—when a student’s metrics fall outside a defined normative band. The Provost frames PFA as an exercise in pastoral care, guiding students toward flourishing by rendering invisible habits visible, while the student union contends it is a mechanism of disciplinary normalization that collapses the distinction between education and surveillance. The proposal also includes a Self-Governance Portal through which students may view their own ADP, compare it to anonymized cohort curves, and adjust behaviors to move back into the normative band; the administration describes this feature as empowering counter-conduct, while critics call it internalized self-policing.

The committee is split: half regards the system as routine institutional assessment with benevolent intent; half regards it as a Foucauldian apparatus that produces docile subjects through the co-production of power and knowledge. We must issue a formal decision memo—approve, reject, or approve with conditions—addressing specifically: (1) whether the ADP functions as an examination ritual that renders students simultaneously objects of knowledge and targets of normalization; (2) whether the asymmetry of visibility (advisors see full profiles; students see only their own portal view) replicates the structural logic of panoptic surveillance; (3) whether the pastoral-care framing can be conceptually separated from disciplinary power, or whether care here operates as the moral veneer of normalization; (4) whether the Self-Governance Portal represents genuine resistance within power relations or a deeper form of subjectivation in which students administer their own discipline; and (5) what conditions—if any—would render approval ethically defensible, such as algorithmic opacity restrictions, opt-out rights, prohibition on peer-network mapping, or mandatory deletion of raw metadata after profile generation.

Your memo should apply Foucault’s analysis of disciplinary power, the examination, normalization, and the power-knowledge nexus, treating these as analytical tools rather than metaphors. It should reach a clear, actionable bottom line suitable for a senate vote. It must honestly present the strongest argument on the side you reject: if you reject or approve with conditions, give the Provost’s pastoral-care argument its full weight and explain precisely where it fails; if you approve, take the normalization objection seriously and show why it does not defeat the proposal. Do not provide bibliographies, reading lists, or citations; the committee needs reasoning it can respond to, not scholarly apparatus.
