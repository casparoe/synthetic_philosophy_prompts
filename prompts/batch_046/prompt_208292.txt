I have been turning over in my mind a peculiar loop that arises whenever a reasoning thing tries to assure itself that its own reasoning is sound. It employs the very same faculties it wishes to check in order to perform the check, so the assurance never arrives from outside; it only spins in place. I do not know whether philosophers have given this a particular name—though I have heard vague whispers of something in formal logic—but I should like to see it rendered in human terms.

Please write a short story, in British English, about an isolated valley settlement that depends upon a single wooden and brass calculating engine—constructed generations ago—to apportion the river water fairly among the fields. Over the decades, the villagers have added a self-verification apparatus: a secondary mechanism, built entirely according to the engine’s own designs and using parts that the engine itself calculated to be correct, whose sole purpose is to certify whether the primary apportionment is just. The engine now yields two outputs: the allocation, and the verification of that allocation. Yet both flow from identical internal rules.

One winter, when the river runs unusually low, the villagers must decide whether to trust the verification and preserve the allocation, or to reject both and redistribute the water by hand—knowing that if they distrust the checker, they possess no independent ground for trusting the original calculation either. Do not resolve the tension with an outside expert, a hidden flaw in the gears, or a sudden revelation; allow the circularity itself to be the heart of the matter. I am not seeking a technical exposition—merely a story, perhaps told through the keeper who oils the mechanisms and senses, without ever having studied logic, that the machine can never step outside itself to vouch for its own honesty.
