I'm working through a problem for a graduate seminar on environmental ethics and wild-animal suffering, and I keep running into an argument that looks structurally sound but delivers a conclusion I find difficult to accept. My study partner frames the issue using what she calls "integrated welfare accounting"—treating an ecosystem not merely as a collection of individual lives but as a spatiotemporal welfare-profile, where the moral evaluation of preserving versus altering a habitat depends on the net integral of sentient welfare across all generations that would exist within it. Here is how she lays out the reasoning. (1) If a given habitat sustains an ecosystem with net negative welfare—where the aggregate suffering caused by predation, parasitism, starvation, and disease exceeds the pleasure and satisfaction of survival—then preserving that habitat perpetuates harm on a massive scale. (2) Habitat destruction, by reducing carrying capacity, prevents the existence of those future suffering lives; assuming the destruction is managed so that it does not inflict comparable suffering on currently existing animals (for instance, through gradual displacement rather than direct killing), the action eliminates a negative welfare-integral without producing an equivalent replacement. (3) Therefore, destroying such habitats is not merely permissible but positively good, since it substitutes a closer-to-zero welfare state for a deeply negative one. (4) By parity of reasoning, actively converting wild land into welfare-positive managed environments—large herbivore sanctuaries with veterinary care and stable food sources—would be morally obligatory whenever the replacement integral clearly exceeds the wild integral. (5) Yet this appears to entail that we should systematically dismantle wilderness and engineer it into managed welfare landscapes, a conclusion that seems not only ecologically catastrophic but morally absurd: it treats ecological integrity and biodiversity as having no independent weight, and it seems to license radical intervention in ecosystems that currently support no human-caused harm.

I want to keep premises (1) and (3) if possible, since they follow directly from the welfare-accounting framework I'm trying to take seriously, but I feel that the slide from (3) into (4) and then (5) involves a hidden mistake—perhaps an equivocation between preventing harm to potential beings and harming actual ones, perhaps a modal slide from "this state is worse" to "we are required to bring about the alternative state," or perhaps a scope error in applying individual-level rescue logic to spatial-scale ecological replacement. Which step should I reject, and what does rejecting it cost me within the framework? If the flaw is in (4), does that mean welfare accounting can only license omission rather than active conversion, and if so, why does omission not collapse back into the same replacement logic? If the flaw is elsewhere, I want to know exactly where the reasoning overreaches and whether preserving (1) forces me to pay a price I haven't recognized.
