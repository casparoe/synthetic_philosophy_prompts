In discussions of scientific revolutions, people often compare Kuhnian paradigms to mutually untranslatable natural languages: scientists in rival paradigms are said to speak past one another because core terms—“mass,” “planet,” “force”—shift meaning, and there is allegedly no neutral meta-language or dictionary to adjudicate the dispute. The analogy is regularly used to license the conclusion that paradigm choice is not rationally decidable but is closer to religious conversion or a gestalt switch. I want you to audit this analogy precisely. First, provide the correspondence table: what in the source domain—native speakers, bilinguals, translation manuals, loanwords, creoles—maps onto what in the target domain—scientists, textbooks, anomalies, the transition period, shared instruments? Second, identify the disanalogies that matter for the conclusion: scientific practice often involves hybrid vocabularies, partial translations, and “bilingual” researchers during transitions; does the language analogy obscure this? Does it quietly import a view of meaning as holistically fixed by an entire linguistic community, and is that how scientific terms are actually anchored (e.g., by measurement procedures or causal interaction)? Third, examine whether local incommensurability—a few terms changing—illegitimately collapses into global incommensurability, where entire worldviews become opaque, and whether that collapse is driven by the analogy itself. Fourth, say whether the non-rational-choice conclusion survives once these breaks are visible, or whether the analogy was doing the argumentative work. Finally, state what would change your mind: which feature of actual scientific practice, if shown to be widespread, would make you regard the language analogy as apt rather than misleading, and where is your reasoning most likely to fail?
