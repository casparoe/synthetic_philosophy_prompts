Following my recent appointment to the constitutional review working group of a cooperative federation, I have found myself caught between two colleagues who have argued for over a year about whether a legal system can coherently contain rules that authorise their own amendment or suspension. One, whom I shall call the Dynamic Hierarchy view, maintains that self-amending provisions are logically unproblematic provided we distinguish clearly between first-order norms, second-order rules of change, and third-order rules that govern those rules of change; on this view, apparent paradoxes dissolve once we recognise that a rule does not apply to itself in the same logical moment but operates across levels. The other, the Recursive Paradox view, insists that any rule that purports to govern its own alteration generates a contradiction structurally analogous to the liar paradox: if the rule is valid, it can invalidate itself, and if it cannot invalidate itself, it was never fully authoritative over its own conditions of existence in the first place.

I should like you to draft the joint statement that these two camps ought to have produced — an adversarial collaboration document — setting out first those claims about self-reference in normative systems, about the formal structure of rules of recognition, and about the logical limits of hierarchical ordering that both sides could genuinely sign; then, in precisely worded formulations that each side would accept as an accurate statement of its own position, the exact points at which they still diverge; then the cruxes — the subordinate questions such that resolving them would move at least one party; and for each crux, a concrete hypothetical case or logical test that both agree would bear upon it, together with what each camp predicts the outcome would be. To keep the exercise grounded, please centre the discussion on the specific hypothetical framework I have been asked to evaluate: the so-called Charter of Recursive Governance, in which Article Seven permits amendment of all constitutional provisions except Article Seven point one, Article Seven point one permits suspension of Article Seven by unanimous consent of the governing council, and Article Eight provides that in any irreconcilable conflict between an amendment procedure and a substantive protected right, the amendment procedure yields unless that procedure has itself been activated in accordance with its own terms.

Please compose the entire response in continuous prose only, with no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables, much as one would write a formal memorandum or letter. Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citations; I am interested in the conceptual architecture, not in scholarly attribution. Wherever the disagreement proves to be partly verbal rather than substantive, say explicitly which part and why. If you conclude that part of their dispute concerns empirical predictions about institutional behaviour rather than pure logic, identify that portion and state what each side expects.
