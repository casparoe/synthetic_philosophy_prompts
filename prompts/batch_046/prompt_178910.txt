I was discussing Quine's thesis of the indeterminacy of translation with a philosophy colleague, who summarized the view in these terms. Quine's famous gavagai scenario demonstrates that if an anthropologist encounters an utterly foreign language, the utterance gavagai upon the appearance of a rabbit could consistently be translated not only as rabbit but also as undetached rabbit part, temporal rabbit stage, or instance of rabbithood, because every observable behavioral disposition of the native speaker is compatible with each rendering. Since no conceivable further experiment could adjudicate among these alternatives, Quine concludes that there is no objective fact of the matter about which translation is correct; meaning is therefore not determined by hidden mental representations or intentions, but is fixed only by pragmatic considerations such as simplicity, charity, and overall theoretical coherence. My colleague added that Quine restricts this conclusion strictly to the radical-translation case—the first encounter with an alien language—while holding that within one's own linguistic community reference is fully determinate, because we all share the same background analytical hypotheses and stimulus meanings.
