I've been working through an essay idea on corrigibility and the shutdown problem, and I'd like comments on the framing before I commit to a full draft. The standard discussion—an agent permits its operators to correct its goals—treats corrigibility as a first-order feature. I'm wondering whether there's an unexamined regress once we add reflective consistency. If the agent should allow its goals to be revised, shouldn't it also allow its willingness to be revised to be revised? That suggests a hierarchy: level one permits goal correction; level two permits correction of the level-one permission; and so on. If every level is genuinely open, then an operator can disable the entire stack, which seems to collapse into the agent being able to authorize arbitrary self-modification or self-termination—the shutdown problem, but generalized across the whole structure. If, instead, the agent protects some root level—say, a hard constraint against allowing total shutdown or total goal erasure—then it isn't fully corrigible; it has an unmodifiable core. My tentative claim is that this protection isn't a design failure but a necessary condition for diachronic identity: an agent that allows its root corrigibility mechanism to be switched off is, at that moment, no longer the same agent, so protecting the root is just protecting continuity. But I also want to push back on myself: perhaps the distinction between object-level and meta-level permissions dissolves the regress, because allowing an operator to change the correction mechanism isn't identical to allowing the agent to destroy itself. I haven't resolved whether there's a non-arbitrary way to fix the height of the stack, or whether a "partially corrigible" agent is stable, or whether rational reflection ultimately forces either total openness or total closure.

I'm looking for comments on three things. First, accuracy: does this misrepresent the standard corrigibility framing, or is the hierarchical regress genuinely new? Second, coherence: is the regress vicious, or can it be blocked by a distinction I'm missing—perhaps between altering a mechanism and authorizing self-termination? Third, interest: does recasting shutdown resistance as "root-corrigibility protection" illuminate anything, or does it merely rename the problem? I'd especially value pushback from anyone who's thought carefully about reflective consistency and self-modification. I'm not asking for a literature review or citations—this is written for people who've followed the alignment discussions for years, and I want conceptual pressure-testing, not bibliography.
