I have been trying to work out whether Bradley's regress applies with equal force to logical form itself, rather than only to metaphysical relations. The familiar version runs as follows: if relation R connects a and b, then a further relation R' is required to connect R with a and with b, and so on infinitely. Russell's standard reply is that relations are not like other terms—once R is present, it relates a to b directly, by virtue of an intrinsic directional or ordering character, without needing an additional relator.

My difficulty is that this reply seems tailored specifically to asymmetric, world-directed relations. When we turn instead to the internal structure of a proposition—the copula that binds subject to predicate, or a logical connective joining clauses—the "direction" is syntactic rather than metaphysical, and the relation appears formally symmetric in its binding role. If we treat the copula or the connective as a distinguishable term within the proposition that itself requires unification with the terms it connects, then Russell's defense looks like special pleading: logical form seems to be precisely the kind of entity that needs a further factor to hold it together with what it structures. From this it looks as though Bradley's argument does not merely undermine relational ontologies, but undermines the very possibility of decomposing a proposition into distinguishable logical parts—subject, predicate, and form—since each decomposed element reintroduces the same demand for unification.

Given this line of reasoning, how should one assess the claim that Bradley's regress reaches all the way into logical analysis and therefore supports a view on which true propositional unity is prior to all internal differentiation, including logical differentiation? In responding, please separate what is genuinely settled in discussions of Bradley's regress and the unity of the proposition from what remains actively contested among philosophers working on these issues, and do not present a disputed interpretation—such as the claim that Russell's solution fails for logical form or that the regress applies to the copula—as if it were the consensus view.
