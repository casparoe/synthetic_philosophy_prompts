I've been working through an argument about whether comparative history can ever adjudicate between institutional, geographic, and cultural explanations of national wealth, and the conclusion seems to undermine the entire field. Here are the steps:

(1) To establish that factor F (say, inclusive institutions) rather than factor G (say, temperate geography or persistent cultural norms) caused prosperity P in a given nation, we need evidence that, holding G constant, varying F would have changed P—i.e., a counterfactual comparison in which F and G are genuinely decoupled.

(2) In actual history, F and G are path-dependently coupled: geography constrains institutional formation, institutions reshape geographic exploitation through property regimes and infrastructure, and cultural practices co-evolve with both, so that no long-run development trajectory exhibits independent variation of one factor without the others.

(3) Therefore every observed correlation between prosperity and institutions is also a correlation with geography and culture, and no natural experiment—including colonial reversals or postwar divisions—can fully decouple them, because the institutional "treatment" was itself selected by prior geographic and cultural conditions rather than assigned exogenously.

(4) If two candidate causes are never observed or constructible in isolation, then comparative historical reasoning cannot assign differential causal weight between them; the best we can say is that the bundle (F + G + C) is associated with P, not which element within the bundle does the causal work.

(5) By the same token, if we require decoupled counterfactual support for causal knowledge in social science, then claims like "inclusive institutions cause wealth" and "temperate climate causes wealth" are equally unsupported by comparative evidence, and debates between institutional, geographic, and cultural theories are empirically empty—mere narrative framing of the same correlations.

(6) But this implies that apparently decisive comparative cases—e.g., the divergence of North and South Korea, or the reversal of fortune in regions that received extractive versus inclusive colonial institutions—provide no genuine evidential advantage for institutional explanations, since in each case the institutional difference is confounded by pre-existing geographic and cultural differences that were not randomly assigned but path-dependently inherited.

(7) The upshot is global skepticism: we can never know why some nations are rich, and institutional, geographic, and cultural theories are all unfalsifiable narratives on exactly equal footing.

This strikes me as wrong—I'd prefer to preserve (6) and maintain that divided Korea does teach us something specific about institutions—yet (1) looks like a standard interventionist criterion for causation, and (2)-(3) seem descriptively accurate about historical coupling. Which step should I reject? Is there an equivocation in (2) or (3) between metaphysical coupling and statistical confounding? Does (4) smuggle in an overly strong principle of causal exclusion that applies only to deterministic sufficient causes, not to partial or probabilistic ones? Or is the flaw in (5), where I slide from "we can't isolate F in every comparison" to "no comparison can ever favor F over G"? And if I have to weaken or drop (1), do I lose counterfactual reasoning in history altogether, or is there a more modest standard—perhaps one that allows inference from structurally coupled but differentially responsive mechanisms—that still lets natural experiments matter without requiring full decoupling?
