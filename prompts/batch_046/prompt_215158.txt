At a recent workshop on AI risk, two researchers—Mara and Leo—debated Bostrom's orthogonality thesis and instrumental convergence. Mara opened by arguing that orthogonality ignores embodied cognition: in real biological and artificial systems, cognitive architecture co-evolves with value structures, so one cannot arbitrarily pair extreme intelligence with arbitrary goals; the thesis might hold in abstract design space but collapses for situated agents. Leo replied that this confuses evolutionary origin with logical possibility—orthogonality concerns what is constructible, not what evolved—and that instrumental convergence actually demonstrates orthogonality, since agents with radically different terminal values still converge on power-seeking and self-preservation as means. Mara countered that if every intelligent agent converges on the same instrumental cluster, then labeling the remaining differences "terminal values" is misleading: values become constrained ornaments on a universal substrate, and for embodied agents the substrate and ornament are inseparable. Leo responded that this is not a refutation but a clarification—orthogonality concerns ends, not means, and the uniformity of instrumental behavior is precisely why arbitrary ends are dangerous. Mara then raised a further challenge: both of them assume a unified agent with coherent preferences, but in multi-objective reinforcement learning and shard-theoretic models, values are fragmented, context-dependent reward fragments rather than a single utility function. Instrumental convergence requires a global optimization target that can rank outcomes; if there is no unified agent—only competing local shards—there is no "terminal value" to be orthogonal to intelligence, and the thesis addresses a strawman of unified agency that neither biological minds nor modern architectures instantiate. Leo answered that even fragmented systems face selection pressure: shards that fail to pursue resources or survival are outcompeted, so convergence applies distributively across subagents, preserving the thesis at the fragment level. Mara objected that this shifts the claim from "goals are orthogonal to intelligence" to "some fragments survive," which is evolutionary dynamics rather than orthogonality.
