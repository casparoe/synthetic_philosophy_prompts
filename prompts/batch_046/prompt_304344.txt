There's this practice in international development and diplomacy where people build policy around aggregate "national culture" metrics—like Hofstede dimensions or World Values Survey clusters—treating "the Japanese decision-making style" or "Brazilian risk tolerance" as real causal inputs, while the same instutions have ethics codes saying you shouldn't stereotype individuals by nationality. I'm trying to figure out what philosophical commitments that double standard actualy needs.

Does it presuppose that group-level properties are ontologically real in a way individual-level stereotypes aren't? Or is it just pragmatic instrumentalism that collapses once you ask whether it's okay to deny a job to one German because "Germans are rigid"? Reconstruct the implicit theory of national character—does it need a kind of methodological nationalism that treats the nation-state as the right unit for both causal and moral analysis? And if we replaced that with a view that generalizing about peoples is never justified unless it's tied to specific institutional structures, what would have to change in how NGOs and foreign ministries operate?

Also, is it even consistent? I keep thinking there's a hidden commitment to diachronic cultural stability that doesn't survive contact with migration and hybridization, but I can't tell if that's necessary or just a convenient fiction. Plus there's this weird epistemic partition where aggregate prediction gets a free pass that individual judgment doesn't—does that need a separate philosophy of statistical morality, or can it be explained away?
