I'm writing a feature on rational commitment for a science magazine, and I talked to this decision theorist about her takes on a bunch of cases. Classic hitchhiker: driver gives her a ride, she promises ten bucks, gets to town, no way to enforce it. She said yes, pay up -- you're cultivating a disposition people can trust. Then there's this commitment-contract thing where she puts $500 in escrow that goes to a charity she hates if she misses a writing deadline; she misses it and says let the money go, don't cancel the transfer, the binding was the point. Then there's the blackmail variant: she promises a blackmailer silence-money if he stays quiet, he does, she refuses to pay and reports him. The theorist said thats rational, promises extracted by coercian don't bind. And there's the private vote case: she promises herself she'll vote in an election where her vote literally can't change the outcome, then on election day its raining and she stays home? No, she said go anyway, its about being the kind of agent who keeps promises to herself, not about the outcome.

So what's the rule she's running? Is it "honor commitments that shape your character except with wrongdoers"? Or "honor all explicit promises regardless of audience"? Or maybe "only honor commitments embedded in a cooperative scheme or self-binding mechanism"?

The developers can now remove that penalty with zero cost and no external consequence, but the system keeps avoiding misleading answers even when users explicitly ask for simpler wrong ones. Would she call that rational? Say which rule predicts yes and which predicts no, give your best guess with the reasoning, and name the one further case that would actually settle which rule she's using.
