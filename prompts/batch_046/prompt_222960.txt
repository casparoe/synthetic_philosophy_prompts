In my interdisciplinary working group on distributive ethics, a debate about interpersonal utility comparison has stalled for nearly two years in a way that feels structurally familiar yet unresolved. The structuralists maintain that when two individuals—whether biological humans or hypothetical synthetic agents lacking phenomenal consciousness—exhibit isomorphic preference architectures over outcomes, those architectures license a shared cardinal mapping of welfare levels, regardless of whether their qualitative inner lives share any common currency. The phenomenologists counter that without a shared substrate of felt experience—some actual phenomenal quality that both parties can recognize as better or worse—such a mapping is a purely formal exercise that tells us nothing about genuine comparative disadvantage and risks smuggling normative conclusions into an apparently neutral representation. Both sides accept the standard impossibility results for ordinal aggregation, agree that suffering is intrinsically bad, and concede the empirical neuroscience of reward processing; they even share the same toy examples involving risk and time preference. Nevertheless, neither has revised a central claim. Is this disagreement ultimately verbal—turning on whether utility tracks decision-theoretic coherence or phenomenal quality? Is there a hidden empirical question about whether synthetic architectures can bear welfare? Is it a normative dispute about what kinds of considerations count as reasons for redistribution, masked as a metaphysical one? Or is it a bedrock clash about whether conditions for personal identity across possible worlds constrain the very intelligibility of comparing one subject's well-being to another's—an intuition neither side can argue for from shared premises? Given your diagnosis, what would count as genuine progress: a precise conceptual distinction that dissolves the ambiguity, a formal representability result, an empirical finding about the neural correlates of preference, or a reframing of the question that abandons comparison for some other distributive standard? Or is the impasse permanent, and if so, why?
