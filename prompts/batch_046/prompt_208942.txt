In my reading group on the metaphysics of perception, we have spent the better part of a year on the argument from illusion and the disjunctivist response without anyone altering their view. The representationalists concede that in Macbeth’s hallucinatory dagger there is no object present, yet they maintain that there must be a shared mental item — a representational content or intentional object — common to that case and the corresponding veridical perception, and that this common factor alone explains their subjective indistinguishability. The disjunctivists deny that hallucination furnishes any model for understanding genuine perception: in veridical experience the subject stands in a direct relational acquaintance with the world, and there is no mental common denominator between that relation and its failure. We all accept the phenomenological datum that the two cases can be introspectively indistinguishable, and we agree that any adequate account must preserve that fact. Yet the positions remain fixed.

I have started to think the impasse is not empirical but turns on a hidden disagreement about what counts as an adequate explanation of phenomenal presence. The representationalists treat the need for a shared explanatory substrate — something that remains constant across success and failure — as a non-negotiable constraint, while the disjunctivists treat direct object-dependence as a primitive explanatory kind that requires no decomposition into internal representational vehicles. Neither side has independently justified why their explanatory demand should govern the taxonomy, rather than simply restating their metaphysical commitment. There may also be a divergence in what each side takes as the primary data to be explained: one emphasizes the presence-to-absence structure characteristic of hallucination, the other emphasizes the success-conditions and epistemic accessibility of veridical judgment. Are they therefore answering different questions — one about the ontology of conscious states, the other about the conditions of perceptual knowledge — under the single rubric of what perception is? Or is the disagreement verbal, hinging on whether experience denotes a relational achievement or a representational vehicle?

Please diagnose the structure of this stalemate: is it a substantive ontological dispute, a difference in standards of explanation, a hidden empirical assumption about phenomenological description, or a case of two fields speaking past one another? In your answer, separate what is settled in the philosophical literature — for example, that subjective indistinguishability does not by itself entail a common mental factor — from what is genuinely contested among those who work on these issues, and do not present any particular interpretation of disjunctivism or representationalism as the consensus. Finally, indicate what kind of development might actually move the debate: a formal distinction between phenomenal presence and representational content, a phenomenological clarification of the absence experienced in illusion, or an argument that the framing itself is ill-posed because it forces perception and hallucination into a single explanatory kind that neither requires.
