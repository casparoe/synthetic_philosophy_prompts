I've been trying to understand why the United States handles parental leave so differently from countries like Sweden or Iceland. Here, the only federal guarantee is the Family and Medical Leave Act, which provides unpaid time off for eligible workers, and any paid leave is usually an employer benefit tied to seniority or hours worked. In several states, maternity leave is actually administered through short-term disability insurance, which means childbirth is classified as a temporary medical disability rather than a social or economic event. By contrast, in the Nordic systems, leave is a tax-funded individual entitlement, with specific months reserved for each parent that generally cannot be fully transferred to the other.

I'm wondering what philosophical assumptions about the family, gender roles, and distributive justice are built into these institutional choices. Does the American practice of tying leave to employment status, and framing childbirth through a disability-insurance lens, presuppose that child-rearing is essentially a private consumption choice—something individuals budget for like a vacation or a medical problem? And does the fact that the system is rarely designed around a stable dual-earner household—where both parents are expected to maintain labor-market attachment—reveal a hidden commitment to a modified male-breadwinner model, even when public rhetoric celebrates working mothers?

On the other side, does the Nordic individual-entitlement model depend on a genuinely different theory—perhaps that care work is socially necessary labor that should not depend on one's employer, marital status, or gender? Or does it simply apply the same liberal framework of individual autonomy through a different administrative mechanism?

Please reconstruct the implicit theory of value that each practice presupposes: what view of fairness, gender equality, and the relationship between market work and care work makes each system intelligible. Distinguish what each practice strictly requires from what merely fits well with it, and say whether the two systems are philosophically incompatible or just differently implemented versions of the same underlying view. If the American system's hidden assumptions were replaced with the theory you think underlies the Nordic model, what specific features—funding source, eligibility rules, transferability, framing—would have to change, and why? I'm not asking for a policy proposal; I want to know whether these are administrative variations or expressions of genuinely different conceptions of what a society owes to parents and children.
