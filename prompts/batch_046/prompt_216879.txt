In debates over whether anti-corruption norms drawn from Western legal-rational bureaucracy apply universally, the disagreement has stalled for decades. Universalists treat clientelism and ritual gift-giving as disguised instrumental exchanges—quid-pro-quo transactions that undermine impartial institutions—while culturalists treat the same practices as forms of constitutive reciprocity, in which the gift creates the social bond rather than corrupting it. Both sides now share extensive ethnographic descriptions and agree that exploitation is harmful, yet neither has moved. Is this impasse substantive—about whether there exists a context-independent institutional harm—or is it partly verbal, turning on whether “corruption” must entail an instrumental exchange rather than a relational obligation? Or are the sides answering different questions altogether, one about optimal institutional design and one about the ontology of social bonds? Diagnose the structure of the disagreement, indicate whether a conceptual distinction, an empirical finding, or a normative framework could actually move it, and say whether progress should be expected or whether the stall indicates that the question is ill-posed.
