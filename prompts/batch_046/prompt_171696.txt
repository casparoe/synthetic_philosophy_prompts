Read Never Let Me Go through the lens of incommensurable values and hard choices, but with a twist: focus on Kathy H's narration as a kind of moral accounting that never actually settles the debt between the clones' autonomy and society's need for organs. The novel presents a world where human life and saving lives through donation are treated as if they can be balanced, but they're genuinely incommensurable. Is Kathy's refusal to rebel — or even fully acknowledge the horror — best understood via Ruth Chang's idea of a hard choice resolved through commitment to her carers and friends, or does the novel show that when a society hides the choice from you there's no rational agency left and the philosophical framework collapses? You could also ask whether Ishiguro is implicitly critiquing the idea of moral residue — that leftover guilt after a tragic choice — by having Kathy feel nostalgia rather than guilt, and what that implies about experiencing incommensurable values without an explicit choice being made. I'm writing an essay for a philosophy of litterature programme and want to avoid reducing the book to a thought experiment, so please point out where the literary details resist the philosophical reading.
