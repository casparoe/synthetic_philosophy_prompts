I am preparing an interview feature for a quarterly that covers the intersection of moral psychology and historical sociology. My subject is an unnamed historian who has recently defended a published position arguing that the familiar debate between the situational account associated with Christopher Browning’s study of Reserve Police Battalion 101 and the deep-cultural account associated with Daniel Goldhagen’s thesis of eliminationist antisemitism is structurally misframed. Rather than forcing a choice between passive peer pressure and pre-existing ideological hatred, she proposes what she calls a “role-embedded character” framework: perpetrators developed localized moral dispositions—reliability, camaraderie, professional pride, and a sense of collective duty—that were structurally coupled to killing tasks without requiring either global personal conviction or mere robotic conformity. She contends that this explains the documented pattern in which the same men displayed ordinary domestic tenderness and escalating on-duty brutality, and that it relocates culpability from private psychology to the architecture of bureaucratic segmentation, producing what she terms “manufactured willingness” rather than discovered willingness.

I need eight to ten interview questions that treat her view with genuine historical and philosophical pressure rather than asking her to restate it. Specifically, I want at least one question that probes how her framework handles the Józefów massacre, in which a notable minority initially refused to shoot but the majority eventually complied and in many cases grew enthusiastic—include a follow-up keyed to her likely reply that such enthusiasm was performative solidarity and role-maintenance rather than ideological conviction. I want at least one question pressing whether her “localized virtue” model can genuinely accommodate Goldhagen’s archival examples of voluntary, gratuitous cruelty—acts that went well beyond orders—and a follow-up anticipating her claim that even gratuitous violence served to stabilize the perpetrator’s position within the group’s moral economy rather than expressing personal animus. I also want a question on moral responsibility: if character is distributed across institutional roles, does her view diffuse individual blame in ways that conflict with postwar legal practice, and what should she say to a prosecutor who adopts her framework? Finally, include a methodological question addressing how her model treats the reliability of postwar testimony and perpetrator self-explanation—sources central to both Browning and Goldhagen—given that perpetrators were operating within a “localized moral world” they may not have possessed the conceptual vocabulary to articulate at the time.

Please draft the sequence so that it builds from the concrete mechanics of Battalion 101 toward the broader implications for assigning culpability when ordinary people are embedded in lethal bureaucratic structures. Avoid generic biographical openings; every question should turn on the actual content of her position and anticipate the standard reply with a targeted follow-up.
