So my school just rolled out this new three-lane discipline system and its been driving me nuts since I had to sit through orientation. Lane one is the old honor council - like, you cheat, you face your peers and the punishment is basically social death, expulsion, very public. Lane two is the dignity office, which handles harrassment and discrimination; they talk about inherent worth and restorative justice but also have formal hearings with rights and appeals. Lane three is this victimhood support track for identity-based harm where the complainant gets a dedicated advocate, theres no cross-examination, and the goal is validation and safety not really punishment.

What theory of the person or of moral wrong does each lane presuppose? Does the honor lane assume people are basically rational agents who can bear public blame? Does the dignity lane pre-suppose that wrongs violate some universal human status? And does the victimhood lane treat harm as essentially subjective and tied to group identity? Or are these just administrative conveniences?

More specifically: is the institution implicitly saying that one of these cultures is morally superior? Like, is dignity culture actually better than honor culture, or is victimhood culture just a corruption of it? Or can they all be consistent if we think of them as applying to different kinds of wrong?

Also, if we took the victimhood lane's logic seriously - that harm is defined by the affected person's experience within their identity group - could we apply that to cheating? Or does the honor lane's logic of public accountability actually depend on denying that harm is subjective?

I'm not asking for a bibliography, just want to understand what philosophical commitments are baked into having all three running side by side in one school. Is there a coherent view of human value that licenses all three, or is the school just philosophically incoherrent?
