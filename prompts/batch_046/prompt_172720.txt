I'm a high school senior writing an independent study on philosophy of art, and for the past semester my reading group has been stuck on aesthetic testimony. The pessimists argue that if I have never seen a particular abstract painting and a trusted friend tells me it evokes sublime melancholy, I do not thereby acquire genuine aesthetic knowledge about the work; at best I hold second-hand belief, and at worst I have outsourced my judgment in a way that undermines my autonomy as a perceiver. The optimists reply that aesthetic testimony functions like testimony in science or history: if the speaker is reliable and I grasp the description, I can genuinely learn that the painting possesses the property in question, even if my own perceptual experience remains thin.

What makes the stall puzzling is that both sides accept the same boundary cases. They agree that someone born blind cannot gain visual-aesthetic knowledge through verbal description alone; they agree that an expert's verbal guidance can help a novice notice formal features she would otherwise overlook; and they agree that judging a work beautiful carries a different normative commitment than judging it blue. Despite this, the pessimists classify the expert-guidance scenario as pedagogy rather than testimony, while the optimists treat it as a central case of testimony. After months of discussion, neither camp has revised its position.

My working hypothesis is that the disagreement is not genuinely about epistemic norms but about whether aesthetic properties are response-dependent in a way that makes first-person affective calibration epistemically prior. The pessimists appear to treat my own emotional response as a necessary condition for aesthetic knowledge, not merely as one piece of evidence among others; the optimists treat it as correctable data, analogous to a thermometer reading that can be overridden by a more accurate instrument. If that diagnosis is correct, then the debate is substantive but hidden: it concerns the ontology of aesthetic properties, disguised as an epistemological dispute about knowledge transmission.

Yet I am uncertain. Could this instead be a verbal disagreement about the term knowledge, with pessimists applying a stricter, experience-bound standard and optimists applying an ordinary, socially mediated one? Or are the sides actually answering different questions—one asking whether testimony can replace direct experience, the other asking whether it can supplement it—while using the single phrase aesthetic testimony? And if the core issue really is response-dependence, is there a conceptual distinction, a formal model of perceptual learning, or an argument about coherence that could resolve the matter, or should we expect permanent impasse because the competing intuitions about autonomy and expertise are bedrock?

Please diagnose the structure of this disagreement rather than simply picking a side. Use the shared acceptance of the blindness case, the expert-guidance case, and the normative difference between aesthetic and color judgment to determine whether the sides share one question or several. Identify whether the hidden driver is ontological, semantic, or methodological, and specify precisely what form of progress—an analytical distinction, a revised framing, or a demonstration that one side's conditions are internally inconsistent—would be needed to break the stall, or argue that no progress should be expected and explain why.
