I'm planning a single seventy-five-minute seminar for second-year philosophy students who have completed basic epistemology but have no background in aesthetics or philosophy of art. The session must tackle the problem of verisimilitude—not the shallow question of whether art should be realistic, but the structural puzzle that a representation can be maximally like its object and yet fail to be true of it, while another representation can be highly unlike it and still succeed referentially. To prevent the discussion from collapsing into a generic realism-versus-anti-realism stalemate, I want to organize the entire session around what I will call the "indexical asymmetry": the phenomenon that verisimilitude aids belief and recognition when the audience knows the represented object exists, but can actively trigger suspicion or disbelief when the audience knows it does not, because excessive likeness in the absence of an external referent reads as artifice rather than testimony. I need you to design the explanatory arc, not to lecture me on the concept itself.

Lay out the exact dependency order in which the pieces must be introduced—what distinction or framing has to be secured before the indexical asymmetry can even be formulated, and why reversing that order causes the idea to be misread as a psychological quirk about viewer confidence. Select one carrying example that must illustrate both poles of the asymmetry in a single paired comparison; specify what formal properties it needs (what kind of object, what kind of medium, what kind of audience knowledge-state) and what properties it must lack so that the students cannot resolve the tension by appealing to common sense or craft skill. Then map the predictable misconceptions: I expect they will conflate verisimilitude with accuracy, and I expect they will try to dissolve the problem by saying that art simply does not aim at truth—identify the precise point in your sequence where each misconception should be intercepted and the specific counter-move that heads it off. Provide one check-for-understanding question I can pose in the final ten minutes that would reveal whether the students have actually grasped the asymmetry rather than merely memorized the vocabulary. Finally, state explicitly which portion of the standard philosophy-of-art literature on representation and resemblance I should openly refuse to introduce at this level and declare that refusal to the class.

Take definite positions on every element—the sequence, the example choice, the misconception map, the check, and the exclusion—rather than presenting alternatives for me to select among. Give your reasons for each choice, and keep the plan self-contained: do not ask for readings, do not cite specific texts, and do not invoke theoretical apparatus that depends on knowledge of niche debates the students have not encountered.
