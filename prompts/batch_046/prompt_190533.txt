I am working through an independent study on functional explanation in philosophy of biology, and I have become stuck on a point that feels like it ought to have a canonical resolution, yet I cannot seem to frame it correctly. As I understand the standard landscape, etiological accounts—associated with thinkers like Wright and Millikan—identify the function of a biological trait with the effect for which it was historically selected, whereas causal-role accounts, in the tradition of Cummins, identify function with the trait's present contribution to the capacities of the system that contains it, leaving evolutionary history aside. These positions are typically introduced as rival answers to a single question: what is the function of this trait? To test whether I truly grasp the distinction, I have been thinking about a deliberately engineered scenario—let me call it a "historical echo system"—in which researchers synthesize an artificial valve analog that replicates, at the molecular level, the structure of a naturally evolved valve that was selected for unidirectional flow. The replica is then embedded in an artificial circulatory architecture where its geometric properties accidentally generate a counter-flow turbulence that proves indispensable for distributing a synthetic nutrient; without that turbulence, the system collapses. From an evolutionary-design perspective, the blueprint clearly derives from a unidirectional-flow selection history; from a present-systemic perspective, its indispensable contribution is precisely the opposite flow pattern. When I present this to colleagues, the evolutionary biologists invoke etiological language about the design origin, while the bioengineers invoke causal-role language about the mixing contribution, and neither group treats the other's description as wrong—only as addressing a different concern. This makes me wonder whether I am missing a more subtle tension. Is there a hidden presupposition that functional attribution must be unique—that for any given trait in a given context there is exactly one correct function statement—such that the peaceful coexistence of these two descriptions actually reveals an irreducible ambiguity in the concept of function itself? Or, conversely, is there a principled meta-criterion—perhaps drawn from mechanistic explanation or interdisciplinary design reasoning—that allows us to declare one of these attributions primary and the other derivative even in such hybrid, synthetic contexts? I would be grateful for help in seeing exactly where the confusion lies, because at the moment it feels as though both answers are empirically adequate yet conceptually incompatible.
