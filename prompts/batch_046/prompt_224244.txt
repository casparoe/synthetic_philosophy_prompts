Exam question: Hume, in Of the Standard of Taste, argues that judgments of beauty and deformity, though founded on sentiment rather than reason, are not arbitrary because human nature supplies a standard of taste. Some interpreters have compared this standard to the calibration protocols used in physical measurement—where an instrument must be in proper working order, correctly positioned, and shielded from interfering conditions in order to yield valid data. Assess whether this calibration analogy illuminates or distorts Hume's account. In particular, address (i) what the five qualities of the ideal critic—delicacy of imagination, practice, comparison, freedom from prejudice, and good sense—actually do in Hume's framework; (ii) whether the standard makes beauty objective in a manner analogous to temperature or pressure; and (iii) whether legitimate disagreement among qualified judges is, for Hume, always evidence that at least one party is defective.

Student answer: Hume argues that beauty is not a primary quality like solidity or extension, but a sentiment aroused in an observer by an object's form and design. The five conditions—delicacy of imagination, practice in comparison, freedom from prejudice, and good sense—function like calibration checks on an instrument. Delicacy permits fine discrimination of parts and relations; practice builds a comparative database of works; comparison situates the present object within that database; freedom from prejudice removes distorting personal interests; and good sense applies general reasoning to verify that the sentiment is proportionate to the object's intended purpose. Once these conditions are satisfied, the resulting sentiment is normatively correct, and disagreement between two qualified critics must mean that at least one instrument is malfunctioning. This makes beauty objective in precisely the same intersubjective manner as temperature: there is no beauty independent of all observers, just as there is no temperature independent of measurement, but within the calibrated system the values are fixed and errors are correctable. Hume therefore anticipates Kant's analysis in the Critique of Judgment, where universal subjective validity is achieved through the free play of cognitive faculties. The only exceptions Hume permits are temperamental or cultural differences that do not violate the core conditions, which confirms rather than undermines the objectivity of the standard.
