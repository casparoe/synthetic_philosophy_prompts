Next semester I present a dissertation chapter to a philosophy-of-science committee, defending the claim that Popper’s demarcation criterion collapses not for the usual reasons—verificationist residues, the Duhem-Quine problem, or conventionalist stratagems—but because it tacitly requires an unjustified modal-stability condition: a theory is scientific only if it remains falsifiable across a range of counterfactual experimental conditions. I argue that certain quantum-mechanical measurement contexts violate this condition, yet the affected theories are still treated as scientific. The audience includes a historian of science skeptical that philosophers should legislate practice, a philosopher of physics fluent in Bell-type arguments and measurement independence, and a generalist worried I conflate demarcation with confirmation. Give me the objections in the order they will come, phrased in each member’s voice, with what I should concede, what I should hold, and—especially—the weakest point in my modal-stability argument that I should defend openly rather than hope they miss.
