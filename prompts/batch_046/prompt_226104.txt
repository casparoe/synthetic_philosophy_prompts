My father has been diagnosed with early-stage dementia, though he still experiences occasional periods of clear reasoning. A research coordinator is recruiting him for a study of a novel sleep medication. Knowing about his cognitive condition, she abandons the standard thirty-page consent document and instead uses an interactive tablet program that explains risks through animated diagrams. The program covers common side effects in detail but entirely omits a rare, severe adverse reaction that occurs in approximately one percent of participants. During one of his lucid afternoons, my father interacts with the tablet, asks coherent questions about dosage, and signs the electronic agreement. By the following morning he retains no memory of the risks, yet he insists he wishes to contribute to science. I am present throughout and feel a profound duty to protect him, though I have no financial interest in his participation. Given these circumstances, can his agreement be treated as genuinely binding? I want you to examine whether the coordinator's decision to withhold the rare risk undermines the informational basis of his choice; whether his intermittent lucidity means he lacks the stable understanding required for such a decision; and whether my own emotional involvement and sense of duty, despite the absence of explicit threats, subtly constrain his freedom to refuse.
