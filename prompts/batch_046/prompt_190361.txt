I am a doctoral student in philosophy of science, working at the intersection of decision theory and the philosophy of collective action, and I am drafting an essay on Douglas Hofstadter’s superrationality experiments. Below is an outline of the argument I want to develop; I would welcome critical comments on its accuracy, its conceptual coherence, and whether the framing is worth pursuing.

The essay compares the Platonia dilemma—where twenty agents, forbidden from communicating, each decide whether to send a telegram such that exactly one sender receives a billion dollars, with superrational reasoning prescribing randomization at probability 1/20—with what I understand as the Luring Lottery. In the latter case, a single agent faces a lottery structured so that entry promises an enormous payoff, yet the agent’s own predictive reasoning about whether they will be “lured” into entering seems to undermine the rationality of entry itself. My thesis is that these two cases pull Hofstadter’s notion of superrationality in structurally opposite directions. Platonia requires symmetric agents to treat one another’s reasoning as perfectly correlated but causally independent, using probabilistic coordination to solve a collective-action problem. The Luring Lottery, by contrast, appears to require a single agent to treat their own future decision as an object of predictive reasoning, generating a self-binding constraint in which the agent must reason themselves out of an option they anticipate taking.

I develop this contrast in three steps. First, I formalize Platonia as a problem of evidential correlation without causal interaction: each agent conditions on the fact that all twenty share an identical decision procedure, then selects a randomization strategy that makes the collective expectation match the success condition. Second, I interpret the Luring Lottery as testing whether the same correlation logic can operate intra-personally—whether an agent can achieve a “superrational” refusal to enter by predicting that a rational version of themselves would enter. Third, I ask whether the distinction between inter-personal symmetry-breaking (Platonia) and intra-personal self-binding (the Luring Lottery) survives formalization in causal versus evidential decision theory. Specifically, I wonder whether both cases reduce to the same underlying structure—reasoning about correlated copies of a decision procedure—such that Platonia is merely the Luring Lottery distributed across twenty bodies.

My concerns are as follows. On accuracy: I am relying on my recollection that the Luring Lottery involves a predictor-like or self-predictive mechanism tied to entry, but I am unsure whether the standard presentation includes an external predictor or whether the “lure” is generated entirely by the agent’s internal reasoning about expected value. If my characterization misrepresents the case, the central contrast collapses. On coherence: I worry that framing both experiments as testing “correlation reasoning” obscures the fact that Platonia involves strategic interdependence (even if non-causal), whereas the Luring Lottery may be a pure Newcomb-style problem with only one decision node. Is there a meaningful philosophical distinction between distributed correlation and self-prediction, or does causal decision theory dissolve both? On structure and interest: I want to avoid writing a mere taxonomy of puzzles. Would the essay be stronger if I argued explicitly that Hofstadter’s experiments reveal an ambiguity in the concept of rational correlation—namely, whether correlation is treated as a resource for coordination or as a threat to autonomy?

Please treat these notes as a preliminary sketch rather than a polished draft. I am especially interested in whether my comparison of the two experiments illuminates something about superrationality that is not already obvious from treating them separately, and in whether I have mischaracterized the mechanics of the lottery scenario. I do not need bibliographic suggestions or a literature survey; I am looking for conceptual and structural feedback on the argument as outlined.
