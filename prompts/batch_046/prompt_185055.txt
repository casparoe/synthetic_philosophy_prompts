Since Fitch published his 1963 result—showing that if every truth is in principle knowable then every truth is in fact known—the literature on the knowability paradox has cycled through a familiar family of responses: restrict the principle to contingent truths, deny closure of knowledge under conjunction, appeal to anti-realist semantics, or argue that the modal derivation misapplies epistemic operators across worlds. Despite decades of formal refinement, the exchange feels static; each camp possesses a well-rehearsed maneuver, and the debate rarely advances beyond restating it. I am trying to understand whether this stalemate is genuinely substantive, or whether it is structural—rooted in hidden disagreements about what counts as solving a paradox.

Specifically, I suspect that part of the impasse comes from an unacknowledged divergence over explanatory standards: one side treats the problem as belonging to modal epistemic logic and considers a formal restriction of the principle sufficient if it blocks the derivation, while the other treats it as a reductio of verificationism and demands an account of why unknown truths exist in a world where they are epistemically accessible. There may also be a verbal or framing split—whether "knowable" means theoretically discoverable in some world, practically achievable by actual agents, or de dicto accessible—together with a hidden question about whether indexicals like "unknown" shift evaluation across modal contexts in ways the derivation ignores. Diagnose the disagreement concretely using what each side actually maintains: is it a dispute about facts, about which cases count as data, about the correct logic of the knowledge operator, or about starting intuitions that neither side can argue for? Are the sides answering different questions—one about logical consistency, one about the metaphysics of truth—under the single heading "Fitch's paradox"? And if the impasse is real rather than ill-posed, what would count as progress: a formal result demonstrating that no restriction avoids omniscience without unacceptable cost, a conceptual distinction separating the logical from the metaphysical issue, or an argument that the paradox dissolves once we distinguish epistemic possibility from epistemic achievement? Give me the diagnosis, supported by the actual moves made on each side, together with your view of what should happen next—whether that is a change of question, a targeted formal result, or a reason to expect no further resolution.
