Argue that the standard framing of hard choices involving incommensurable values is conceptually inadequate because it treats the agent as a unified preference-ranking system facing an external conflict between goods. Instead, defend the position that such choices are better analyzed as cases in which an agent's practical identity is constituted by commitments that generate genuinely conflicting reasons of comparable weight, and that this internal-constitution model explains why rational regret remains appropriate after resolution in a way that the external-conflict model cannot accommodate. Use the case of a physician-researcher forced to choose between clinical duty and theoretical investigation not merely as illustration, but as a structural test: show how your model predicts specific features of the agent's normative landscape—such as the persistence of undefeated reasons on both sides and the absence of a compensatory metric—that the incommensurability-as-external-conflict view must dismiss as irrational residue. In doing so, address whether your account requires that the values in question be literally incomparable, or whether it can function with values that are merely non-substitutable within the agent's self-constituting commitments.
