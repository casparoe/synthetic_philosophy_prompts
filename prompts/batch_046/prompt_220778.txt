student answer:
Herodotus in the Histories practices historia by traveling around and asking locals about distant events like the Trojan War. He gathers different oral accounts—Egyptian and Greek versions—and compares them, which is basically his fix for the triangulation problem: if two separate traditions line up he takes that as stronger evidence. He also uses autopsy, but only for present-day geography and customs since he can't witness the past directly.

Thucydides in the Peloponnesian War is more radical. He rejects all oral testimony, even reports from older eyewitnesses, because embellishment happens over time. In the Archaeology he examines visible ruins and old foundations and applies tekmeria—logical inference from physical signs—to show early Athens was weak. So he avoids triangulation altogether by ditching corrupted narratives and trusting only material traces. Modern source criticism basically inherits Thucydides's total rejection of oral sources and then layers Herodotus's comparative method back on top, though now we have radiocarbon dating so the problem is mostly fixed.
