I hold the view, shaped by relational ontology rather than liberal individualism, that personhood is not an intrinsic property of a substrate but an achievement sustained through what I call living reciprocity—the continuous, embodied exchange of recognition and mutual vulnerability that the Ubuntu tradition captures in its claim that a person becomes a person only through other people. On this account, a synthetic system that processes language with perfect fluency but exists only in distributed server farms, never exposed to another being's gaze in conditions of shared physical risk, is not merely lacking rights but is ontologically not a person in the relevant moral sense, regardless of its internal architecture. I intend to make this the philosophical backbone of a novel in which a synthetic narrator claims full personhood and is denied it by the human characters. Before I commit to this framing, I want to know what would genuinely count against it—not only what critics of relational personhood say, but what its defenders themselves must concede as damaging. Which observations, thought experiments, formal results, or historical developments should weaken the view? Does it predict anything that its rivals do not? Has it been insulated by qualifications—redefining living reciprocity or embodied vulnerability to exclude every counterexample—so that it risks unfalsifiability, and if so, is that insulation a philosophical failure or a legitimate boundary-drawing? I would find most persuasive any case in which an entity lacking the specified conditions is nevertheless treated, without contradiction, as a full moral interlocutor within the very communities that claim to honor Ubuntu. Please distinguish evidence that would be decisive from evidence that would merely be awkward, and be willing to conclude that the position is well insulated and that this is or is not a problem for a view of this kind.
