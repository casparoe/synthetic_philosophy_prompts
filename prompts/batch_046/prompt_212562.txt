I work as an acquisitions consultant for a mid-size museum, and I must evaluate proposed acquisitions quickly—under thirty minutes, with only the object's file—across disputed-attribution paintings, digital replicas of destroyed works, and reconstructed artifacts built from fragments with modern materials. Write a reusable decision procedure that tells me whether to treat an item as carrying original-value (justifying conservation and display resources), as an authorized substitute (representational value only), or as a deceptive fake (no independent value). Ground the procedure in the distinction between causal-historical provenance and present functional integrity, and include a specific branch for reconstructed originals, where the physical causal chain is broken but institutional continuity is restored. For each step, state the test, the evidence that satisfies or fails it, and whether failure is fatal or sends the case to human judgment. Apply it to three cases: a canvas with an authentic period but a forged signature; a 3D-printed ceramic rebuilt from scan data of a destroyed original; and a reconstructed Baroque instrument assembled with modern metal but performed with historical technique. Include one case where the procedure grants original-value despite a broken causal chain, and flag exactly where institutional values—not the procedure—must settle the outcome. Keep it practical: no bibliographies, no appeals to named theorists, and an explicit note on where the procedure will misfire, especially with conceptual or performance-based works.
