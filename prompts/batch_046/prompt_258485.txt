I'm prepping for a panel next friday on informational citizenship — it's half comparative-law people, half poli-sci theorists, and there's always that one computer-science grad student who asks whether encryption is even relevant anymore. I'm defending this synthetic view I'm calling strategic opacity: citizens shouldn't go full german Datenschutz-minimalist or full american FOIA-radical, and definitely not chinese social-credit transparent, but should instead maintain selective hiding in financial, medical, and associative domains while being radically open about political speech and civic participation. The core claim is that hiding from the state is domain-specific, not binary, and that democratic accountability actually improves when the government can't see certain categories of citizen data even though it can see others.

My audience is mostly constitutional scholars who think I'm just smuggling in american liberalism with extra steps, plus a few german legal theorists who treat their data-protection framework as a constitutional baseline rather than one pole in a spectrum. There's also a political philosopher who specializes in transparency and will probably argue that any selective opacity collapses into a kind of naive value-pluralism. I need the objections they'll actually raise — not the ones I'd get from a privacy-activist crowd or a rationalist forum — ordered by how likely they are and how much damage they'd do, each phrased the way that specific person would put it, with a two-minute reply, a note on where the reply is soft, and what I should concede versus hold. I also need the question I'm hoping nobody asks, which is probably whether strategic opacity is technically feasible now that data brokers can infer medical status from grocery receipts and location traces, and I want to be honest about the boundary problem — who decides which domains stay hidden — rather than pretend I've solved it. Oh, and there's probably a typo in my slides somewhere about "recieve" instead of "receive" so don't hold that against me.
