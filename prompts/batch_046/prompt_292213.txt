I am a novelist completing a manuscript about institutional collapse and collective agency, and I am applying for a six-month interdisciplinary fellowship that pairs philosophers with writers. I need an eight-page research proposal—rigorous enough for a philosophy panel but framed by a question that directly shapes how I understand character and institutional behavior in fiction. My half-formed idea concerns the metaphysics of dispositions: the standard debate over C.B. Martin's "finks" and the subsequent antidote or masking responses (associated with figures like Johnston and Lewis) is almost always conducted with discrete individual objects in mind—a brittle glass, a poison with an antidote—where the bearer has a single intrinsic manifestation condition. I want to push this further and ask whether the very distinction between a finked and a masked disposition depends on an ontology of intrinsic, individual properties, or whether it can survive when applied to distributed, temporally extended, or representational entities—such as an institution "disposed" to regulate markets, or a fictional protagonist whose narrative arc structurally blocks the manifestation of a trait. The project should take a committed position: either the distinction requires reconceptualization for collective and representational bearers, or it holds firm and thereby reveals something non-obvious about narrative agency.

Please turn this into a formal proposal with a precise, answerable research question—something like: does the fink/mask taxonomy presuppose an ontology of discrete intrinsic properties, and if so, does it collapse, bifurcate, or require relational reconstruction when mapped onto institutional and fictional-character dispositions? Explain exactly why the existing literature leaves this open: the classic cases assume a single bearer with a single manifestation condition and do not test whether the "intervener" in a fink case and the "blocker" in a mask case remain distinguishable when the bearer is composite or when the conditions of manifestation are partly constituted by an observer or author. Set out the method in monthly stages for six months: Month 1, formalize the taxonomy with explicit conditions for finking versus masking; Month 2, construct the institutional case study; Month 3, construct the fictional-character case study; Month 4, construct a collective-action control case; Month 5, test whether the taxonomy applies cleanly or forces a relational/intrinsic split; Month 6, write up the argument and assess failure conditions. Specify what success looks like—a reconstructed distinction or a proof that intrinsic ontology is indispensable—and be explicit about failure, such as finding that all cases map onto the existing taxonomy without any ontological pressure.
