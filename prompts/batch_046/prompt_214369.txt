As a novelist preparing a dialogue between an archival historian and a counterfactual novelist, I want to understand whether historical objectivity can survive its classic sceptics. Please give me your credence — a specific number between 0 and 1 — that Ranke's epistemic ideal of showing the past as it actually occurred remains defensible in some non-trivial form after accounting for Carr's present-minded selection and Hayden White's tropological constructivism. I am not asking for a bare opinion; I want the reasoning decomposed into explicit sub-questions.

Break your answer into at least three probabilistic terms. First, assign a rough probability to Carr's strongest claim: that because every historian selects evidence according to present concerns, there can be no meaningful correspondence between a narrative and a past reality independent of that selection. Second, assign a rough probability to White's strongest claim: that the rhetorical modes of emplotment — romance, tragedy, comedy, satire — determine historical meaning so thoroughly that causal anchoring to actual events is always subordinate to narrative form. Third, assign a rough probability to the counter-position I will call causal anchoring: that even when two accounts share identical selection biases and identical tropological structures, there remains an epistemically significant distinction between the one that traces actual causal dependencies and the one that substitutes counterfactual dependencies, and that this distinction allows a non-trivial defense of Ranke's ideal.

Show how these terms combine into your overall credence. Then identify which single term your final number is most sensitive to, and describe — without citing specific texts, scholars beyond those named, or bibliographic resources — what kind of philosophical or evidentiary shift would most move you on that term.
