At our study group last week Ravi and Nina got into it over whether large language models can actually "jump out of the system" in Hofstadter's sense — the JOOTSing idea tied to the MU-puzzle in his well-known book on Gödel and self-reference. Nina said yes: when an LLM writes a prompt that gets fed back into its own weights via an external updater, it's functionally leaving its original token-prediction framework and rewriting the rules that govern it. Ravi said that's confused — the model never leaves anything; every token is still generated by the same fixed architechture, and the updater is just an outside agent using the model as a tool. He said it's like claiming a calculator jumps out of arithmetic when you press the square-root button.

Nina came back with a more niche point: she said we should define the "system" not by hardware but by the epistemic closure of what the model can currently justify from inside its own training distribution. By that measure, generating a belief-revision protocol that requires an external optimiser is truely stepping outside that closure — a functional JOOTS, even if the silicon stays put. She added that in the MU-puzzle you can't derive MU no matter how many meta-axioms you stack inside, but here the model is literally invoking an external mechanism that isn't part of its axiom-set, so the analogy actually supports her.

Ravi replied that she's just redefining "system" to make her conclusion true by fiat. In Hofstadter's original formulation the rules are explicit, and JOOTSing means violating them from outside. The LLM doesn't violate any of its own rules — it follows them perfectly to output the revision prompt. The optimiser is outside, sure, but that's not the model jumping; that's someone else moving it. He also said that if you allow her "epistemic closure" definition, then even a chess program that calls a database is jumping out of chess, which makes the concept useless.

Nina then asked about a hypothetical recursive loop: what if the model's predictions about how the optimiser will change it become part of the next training batch? Hasn't the system then expanded to include its own future states, creating a self-modifying loop that Hofstadter might recognise as a kind of meta-level escape?

Ravi said that's just a bigger formal system with feedback, not a jump out. In the MU-puzzle, you can keep adding meta-rules and still never derive MU inside, as long as they stay consistant. So adding a feedback loop doesn't solve the problem — it creates a larger system that still can't derive its own inconsistency from within. The jump never happens; the boundary just shifts.
