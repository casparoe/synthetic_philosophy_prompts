At a recent philosophy workshop on collective ethics, two attendees—Leo and Nina—debated whether a small municipality should keep funding an open digital archive of survivor testimonies from an industrial accident that occurred forty years ago. The archive is rarely accessed, its upkeep is costly, and several descendants of survivors have requested that it be quietly decommissioned. Their exchange proceeded in four rounds.

(1) Leo argued that letting the archive decay would be a second moral injury. He appealed to the idea that dense “thick relations” in a small community generate obligations of remembrance that do not vanish when present residents find them inconvenient. The victims, he said, have a claim on recognition; to treat the records as disposable is to treat the dead as lacking standing. He added that reconciliation without remembrance is not forgiveness but avoidance.

(2) Nina replied that Leo treats remembrance as an unqualified good, when institutionalized memory often serves power rather than victims. She drew on the argument that forced collective remembrance can lock a community in a permanent identity of victimhood, and that descendants have an autonomy-based right to move on. Maintaining the archive against their explicit wishes, she said, is paternalism disguised as piety.

(3) Leo countered that Nina conflates private grief with public accountability. The duty, he insisted, is owed to the dead themselves, not calibrated to the current emotional preferences of survivors. Even if some descendants want silence, the community’s shared failure created a standing obligation to preserve an accurate public record. Without it, future citizens lose the evidentiary basis for judging whether justice was ever done.

(4) Nina said Leo’s position lacks any limiting principle: by his logic, every past harm demands perpetual archival maintenance, which would paralyze civic life. She proposed a “threshold of living harm” criterion—remembrance is morally obligatory only when its absence would actively mislead present citizens about ongoing risks, structural injustices, or unresolved liabilities, not merely when it honors past suffering. Without such a boundary, she concluded, Leo collapses into an impossible duty to preserve every trace of every wrong.

Please adjudicate this exchange move by move: rate each of the four moves from clearly mistaken to clearly right and briefly justify each rating. Then say who is right about which specific point, who is more right overall, and what—if anything—both parties are missing. Treat this as a point-by-point evaluation of the arguments as actually made, not as a request for personal advice.
