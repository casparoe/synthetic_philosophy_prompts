Question: Moore's paradox is often introduced with first-person present-tense assertions such as "It's raining, but I don't believe it." Philosophers standardly distinguish an omissive formulation, in which the speaker denies holding the belief that corresponds to the asserted content, from a commissive formulation, in which the speaker affirms holding the belief that contradicts that content. Explain the difference between these two formulations, and discuss why G. E. Moore treated the first-person case as paradoxical even though it involves no formal logical contradiction. In your answer, address whether the source of the paradox is best understood as a structural feature of belief itself—such as transparency or self-knowledge—or as a merely pragmatic feature of assertion, and note whether the omissive and commissive cases present exactly the same kind of problem.

Student answer: Moore's paradox arises in sentences like "It's raining, but I don't believe it's raining," which sound absurd in the first person but entirely unremarkable in the third person. The omissive form is when you assert p while denying that you believe p; the commissive form is when you assert p while saying you believe not-p. Both are paradoxical for the same underlying reason: belief is transparent, so that considering whether p is true just is considering whether you believe p. Because of this, you cannot sincerely assert p without the corresponding belief, and you certainly cannot sincerely assert p while holding the contradictory belief. Moore originally introduced the puzzle with his example about going to the movies yesterday, treating the statement as absurd but not logically contradictory. Later philosophers—particularly Wittgenstein and Shoemaker—developed this insight into a full theory of first-person authority, showing that belief reports have a special status that makes self-contradictory mental states impossible in practice. Therefore both the omissive and the commissive versions involve a direct doxastic contradiction between the asserted content and the speaker's mental state. Once we recognize that belief is governed by transparency, the paradox is fully resolved, because it becomes impossible to sincerely utter either formulation. So the paradox reveals a deep structural feature of first-person belief rather than a mere pragmatic glitch in how we talk.
