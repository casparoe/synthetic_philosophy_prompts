I'm preparing a module for an advanced seminar on distributive justice, and I find that students can recite the labels—meritocrat, luck egalitarian, desert-theorist—without being able to map them onto their own actual judgments. To fix this, I want a decision tree that sorts positions on merit and desert not by philosophical pedigree but by answers to concrete diagnostic questions, so that someone can trace their commitments without knowing in advance which leaf they land on.

Begin with the following case: a society assigns high-status roles through an opaque predictive algorithm that correlates strongly with future productivity, but neither the subjects nor the designers can explain why any particular individual is selected. Some are elevated; others are excluded permanently. From this starting point, construct a tree with four branching nodes, each phrased as a yes-or-no question that a respondent could answer without knowing which interpretation it leads to.

First: does the fact that the assignment tracks a real productive contribution generate a desert claim for the recipient, even when the correlation is entirely opaque to everyone involved? Second: if two people have made identical past contributions but one possesses greater unexpressed natural aptitude, does that aptitude already strengthen the more able person's claim? Third: is any desert claim conditional on the role serving a common good, or is desert intrinsic to the effort or contribution regardless of institutional purpose? Fourth: when merit is partly shaped by unchosen upbringing, does that dilute the claim only if the advantage was deliberately engineered, or does any unchosen structural advantage weaken desert proportionally?

For each leaf—there should be sixteen in principle—name the position if it is defended (for instance, strict meritocrat, Rawlsian fair-equality proponent, luck-egalitarian, or libertarian desert-theorist), or mark it "unnamed" if no prominent view occupies it. Each leaf must carry one sentence stating its most awkward commitment: the consequence its neighboring positions avoid but it must swallow. Where two named positions fall on the same leaf, flag them as inseparable by this tree. For every unnamed combination, briefly diagnose whether the gap is likely due to incoherence, unattractiveness, or mere neglect. Keep the instrument self-contained: no bibliographies, no citation requests, no appeals to texts the respondent must have read. The goal is a logical map of the space, with the actual philosophers and schools pinned to it only after the branches have done their work.
