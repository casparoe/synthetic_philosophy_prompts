I am preparing a position paper for a philosophy of probability workshop and would like you to argue in favour of revising the Principal Principle associated with David Lewis so that rational deference to objective chance is conditional not merely on knowing the chance value, but on possessing what I shall call procedural transparency about the mechanism that generates that chance. The standard formulation requires an agent who knows the objective chance of some event to align her credence with it, yet this collapses important distinctions between knowing a statistical frequency and grasping the modal structure of the process that produces it. I want you to defend the view that an agent should only be bound by the Principal Principle when she understands, at least in outline, how the chance-generating process operates in a way that renders the outcome genuinely indeterminate rather than merely epistemically opaque to her; where such procedural transparency is absent, deference to the known chance ought to be rationally optional rather than mandatory. You should address the objection that this revision makes the principle excessively psychologically demanding, and consider whether your amended principle can still accommodate canonical examples such as a biased coin or radioactive decay without collapsing into a form of subjectivism. Please present your response entirely in continuous prose, without headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables, in the manner of a formal letter written in British English.
