Please act as an expert philosopher of history and collective identity. Here is the argument:

(1) Following Anderson, a nation is an imagined community: its members sustain a mental image of shared belonging with strangers they will never meet, bounded and sovereign.

(2) Following Hobsbawm, the rituals, origin myths, and commemorative practices that sustain this image are largely invented traditions—deliberately constructed or retrofitted in modern times rather than organically ancient.

(3) Now suppose members become broadly aware that these sustaining practices are recent fabrications; call this reflexive membership.

(4) If the imaginative bond depends on treating these practices as genuinely ancient and continuous, then widespread reflexive membership should dissolve the bond.

(5) But empirically, widespread awareness rarely dissolves national identification; citizens often maintain strong patriotic attachment while knowing the history is constructed.

(6) Therefore either (4) is false—the bond does not depend on perceived antiquity—or the imagination in Anderson's account is causally inert, leaving only material or institutional forces to do the work.

(7) If the imaginative component is inert, then Anderson's thesis is not an explanation of national persistence but an epiphenomenal description, and the same reasoning applies to every collective identity sustained by historical narrative—religious communities, scientific disciplines, civilizational identities—predicting either their dissolution or the irrelevance of their founding myths, neither of which occurs.

(8) Hence the Hobsbawm-Anderson framework, applied consistently, undermines itself.

If I must choose, I prefer to keep (5)—that awareness does not dissolve identity—rather than accept (8), but I want to know what rejecting (4) or (6) costs conceptually. Which step should I give up?
