I am a doctoral candidate in philosophy of mind and logic, and I have been asked to moderate a colloquium Q&A with a philosopher who has recently defended what she calls the strict Gödelian thesis. On her view, Hofstadter's analogy between Gödel's self-referential formulas and human self-awareness is valid only for representational systems that are strictly first-order and operate with a fixed, non-modal interpretation; she argues that once we allow meta-cognitive states that reference their own modal status—for instance, recognizing that one's own belief might be erroneous—the formal conditions for Gödel-numbering collapse, and with them the analogy to consciousness. Far from seeing this as a limitation, she treats it as an explanatory virtue: it explains why artificial neural networks have not achieved genuine self-awareness, since their dynamics lack the syntactic fixity required to encode representations of representations in the literal manner that Gödel's proof demands.

Please write me a sequence of four interview questions that turn on the actual content of this position rather than on generic biography or background. The first should ask her to delineate the exact boundary she draws between syntactic self-reference in formal arithmetic and what she terms semantic reflexivity in conscious thought, with particular attention to whether stable neural attractor states could genuinely instantiate the former or merely approximate it. The second should anticipate her reply that biological brains achieve sufficient syntactic fixity through dynamical stabilization, and should include the follow-up: if approximation is enough, then her analogy applies to existing AI architectures after all; if only literal encoding counts, then her claim about biological brains risks being insulated from empirical check. The third should press the objection that her first-order restriction seems to exclude precisely the phenomenon she wants to explain, since human self-awareness characteristically involves second-order evaluation—revising commitments, doubting perceptions, assessing one's own rationality. The final question should ask what specific formal result or empirically accessible finding—one that does not depend on unobservable neural encodings—could genuinely count against her view, rather than being reinterpreted as further evidence that the analogy's domain is narrowly circumscribed.
