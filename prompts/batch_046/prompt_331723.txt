As a voting member of the exhibition-oversight committee for a large public natural-history museum, I must circulate a formal recommendation before Thursday’s meeting on a proposed permanent gallery. The curatorial team wants the gallery, titled Selection and Explanation, to frame evolutionary biology through Karl Popper’s philosophy of science rather than through the conventional empirical-science narrative. Specifically, the design adopts Popper’s characterization of Darwinism as a metaphysical research programme—valuable for explanatory power though not strictly falsifiable as a universal law—includes an interactive panel on the tautology objection that natural selection is circular, paired with Popper’s rebuttal that selection functions as a situational logic rather than a circular definition, and applies his anti-historicist arguments about historical explanation to caution visitors against reading evolutionary history as a predictive law of development. The committee is split: one side holds that presenting an established science through an explicitly metaphysical lens will confuse the public and weaken trust in scientific institutions; the other side holds that suppressing this framing misrepresents how working biologists actually reason about non-predictive, historical explanations. Draft the committee’s decision memo—approve, reject, or approve with binding curatorial conditions—that recommends a concrete course of action, specifies any required revisions or display constraints, treats the public-trust objection with full seriousness rather than dismissing it, and explains whether this Popperian framing belongs in a museum setting. The document should be something the curators could respond to with concrete adjustments rather than a free-standing philosophical essay.
