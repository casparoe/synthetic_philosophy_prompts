(1) I accept Hofstadter's core critique that statistical machine translation is shallow: it manipulates surface forms without intentionality, without world-reference, and without grasping an author's conceptual intentions or cultural context.

(2) At the same time, I think "understanding" is not binary but comes in degrees, and that large statistical language models possess a minimal but genuine form of distributional comprehension—tracking semantic relations through correlation structures in a way that is more than mere symbol-shuffling, even if radically thinner than human cognition.

(3) I hold that translation correctness is essentially a relation between source and target texts—preserving propositional content, pragmatic force, and register—so a system can produce a fully correct translation without possessing understanding.

(4) Nevertheless, I believe there is a philosophically significant distinction between translations produced with understanding and those produced without it, particularly regarding ambiguity resolution, error correction, and creative adaptation to novel contexts.

(5) I apply the mechanistic inspection argument familiar from Hofstadter's discussion—looking inside and seeing only weights, tensors, and next-token predictions, never a "mind" perceiving meaning—to modern LLMs, but I reject its conclusion, since I take understanding to be an emergent organizational property rather than a property of the system's constituent parts.

Please audit this package. Which pairs are genuinely in tension, which only appear to be, and what is the least costly revision?
