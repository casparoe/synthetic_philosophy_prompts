In our interdisciplinary philosophy seminar we have been working through a sequence of stylized cases about evidential cooperation in large worlds — that is, whether causally isolated but evidentially linked agents (for example, copies of a reasoner distributed across a vast multiverse or high-fidelity simulation space) ought to treat one another’s choices as mutual evidence, and whether such cooperation is rationally obligatory, merely permissible, or irrational. Our collective rulings so far are these: (1) in a scenario with ten thousand copies facing an identical prisoner’s dilemma, all with shared preferences for mutual cooperation and symmetric reasoning, each copy ought to cooperate, since defection is evidentially self-defeating; (2) in a case where copies share the decision structure but have causally independent, randomly assigned utility functions — some altruistic, others purely egoistic — we held that no evidential cooperation is warranted, because there is no stable correlation between one copy’s choice and the interests of another; (3) when there are only two copies separated by cosmological distances but with perfectly symmetric belief-formation and a common goal of averting a shared existential risk, we judged cooperation obligatory, yet we noted that introducing even minor asymmetry in reasoning procedures weakens or dissolves the obligation; (4) in a small-world baseline with no copies whatsoever, we agreed the concept of evidential cooperation does not apply, though analogical reasoning to future selves might support cooperation on independent grounds.

We now face a new case that divides the seminar. There are exactly seven copies distributed across causally disconnected regions, each facing the same binary decision: contribute to a public good at a small personal cost, or free-ride. The good is achieved only if at least four copies contribute. All seven share identical reasoning architectures and a common preference for the good being funded, but their prior credences that the other copies will contribute vary in a smooth gradient — copy one assigns 0.85, copy two 0.80, copy three 0.75, copy four 0.70, copy five 0.68, copy six 0.66, and copy seven 0.65 — reflecting slightly different local observational noise in each region. One participant argues we should predict full cooperation, extending our ten-thousand-copy ruling; another contends that the prior divergence breaks the symmetry required by rulings (1) and (3), predicting partial defection with no clear obligation for the lower-credence copies; a third proposes that we need a threshold principle, holding that cooperation is required only when inter-copy prior agreement exceeds some cutoff, which this case straddles.

Please predict what verdict our seminar should reach if it wishes to remain consistent with its earlier track record, identifying the closest analog among cases (1) through (4) and being explicit about where our precedents genuinely underdetermine the outcome. Then articulate the two or three competing principles that best systematize our rulings — for instance, a pure evidential-symmetry principle, an interest-alignment-constrained symmetry principle, or a prior-convergence threshold principle — noting which earlier cases each principle accommodates well or poorly. Finally, specify the single further case, with precise conditions on copy number, the range of prior divergence, and the alignment of interests, that would most cleanly distinguish your preferred principle from its strongest rival, and say what each principle would predict in that case.
