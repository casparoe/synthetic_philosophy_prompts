I am drafting an essay with the following thesis and outline, and I would like your critical comments before I develop it further.

Essay thesis: In his monetary essays, particularly Of Money and Of Interest, Hume treats currency not merely as a quantitative token but as the object of a habitual, associative belief structurally parallel to his account of necessary connection in the Treatise of Human Nature. On this reading, the acceptance of paper or debased coin rests on a custom-hardened projection of exchange-value onto an otherwise valueless token—what I call fiduciary psychology—rather than on intrinsic metallic worth or on an explicit institutional assignment of status-function. I then ask whether this makes Hume an unwitting precursor to modern social ontology (for instance, Searle-style institutional facts) or, conversely, whether his skepticism about causation renders monetary belief more fragile than contemporary institutional theories allow. If belief in money can dissolve in the same way belief in necessary connection can be philosophically undermined, then monetary crises might be epistemic collapses of customary association rather than purely economic disruptions.

Notes for the argument:
- Hume describes money as representing labor and commodities, comparing it to oil that smooths the motion of trade. He insists that national prosperity depends on labor and industry, not on the stock of gold or silver, because prices adjust to the quantity of currency.
- Despite this quantitative neutrality, people continue to treat specific tokens—coins, banknotes, bills of exchange—as possessing value even when they know these tokens lack intrinsic utility. Hume explains this persistence by appealing to convention and habit, not to rational contract.
- In the Treatise, Hume argues that necessary connection is not observed in objects but is projected onto constant conjunctions by a feeling of determination in the mind. I want to map this onto monetary acceptance: repeated successful exchange produces an associative habit that projects purchasing power onto paper, just as repeated conjunction projects causal power onto events.
- Against a Searlian reading, I argue that Hume lacks any notion of collective intentionality assigning a status-function. For Hume, the convention is not a rational collective act but an accumulated custom, vulnerable to skeptical dissolution. A run on a bank is not a failure of institutional assignment but a breakdown of associative confidence.
- The essay concludes by asking whether modern fiat systems depend more on Humean custom or on explicit institutional framing, and whether recognizing the Humean element makes them more vulnerable to epistemic panic than economists typically admit.
