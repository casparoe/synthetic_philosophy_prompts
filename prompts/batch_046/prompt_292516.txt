As a journalist who covers philosophy and public affairs, I serve on my university's Committee on Honorary Degrees and Institutional Memory. Next month we must vote on whether to confer an honorary doctorate on a retired defense strategist—call him Dr. Voss—who designed the decision-architecture for autonomous drone campaigns conducted in the 2030s. The campaigns eliminated designated targets but produced documented civilian casualties that the architecture's own risk-models had classified as probable rather than intended outcomes. In a white paper circulated to the committee, Voss proposes that moral evaluation should turn on a "threshold of direct algorithmic intent," not on aggregate casualty ratios. Half the committee treats this as a defensible refinement of consequentialist reasoning; the other half believes it evades the kind of absolute prohibition on deliberately killing innocents that G.E.M. Anscombe invoked when she protested Oxford's decision to honor Harry Truman for ordering the atomic bombings of Hiroshima and Nagasaki.

Please draft the committee's formal decision memo—approve, reject, or approve with conditions—addressed to the university senate. The memo should recommend a concrete course of action, specify a timeline for any subsequent review or public statement, and treat the strongest argument on the opposing side with intellectual honesty rather than dismissal. In particular, engage Anscombe's distinction between directing one's action toward the death of the innocent as an end or means, and accepting such death as a foreseen side effect, and ask whether that distinction remains coherent when the relevant intention is distributed across machine-learning classifiers, human oversight boards, and rules of engagement drafted years before any particular strike. Do not request further sources, bibliographies, or citations; work solely from the conceptual resources available to you. I need this circulated before Thursday's meeting so that members have a shared text to amend.
