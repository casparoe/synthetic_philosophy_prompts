Examination question:
In his work on the ethics of memory, Avishai Margalit distinguishes shared memory—held directly by those who experienced an event—from common memory, which is mediated through institutions, education, and public ritual. He argues that societies bear a duty to remember historical wrongs, though the moral basis and intensity of that duty vary between these two modes. Philip Rieff, in his critique of modern therapeutic culture, describes therapeutic forgetting as the erosion of moral prohibitions through emotional management and individual comfort. Against this he sets charismatic forgetting: the dilution or loss of authoritative force that occurs when commemorative practices are multiplied beyond a community's capacity to sustain any single narrative as sacred.

Consider a democratic state that enacts a 'mnemonic budget'—a statutory limit of eight official state commemorations per year—justified by the claim that collective moral attention is finite and that excessive commemoration produces compassion fatigue.

Address the following:
(i) How does Margalit's distinction between shared and common memory affect the application of his duty to remember to this policy?
(ii) Does Rieff's framework classify the mnemonic budget as an instance of therapeutic forgetting, of charismatic forgetting, or of neither? Provide reasons.
(iii) Can the justification based on compassion fatigue be reconciled with either Margalit's or Rieff's account of the moral function of collective memory?

Student's answer:
Margalit's distinction hinges on proximity: shared memory belongs to survivors and witnesses, whereas common memory belongs to subsequent generations who encounter the past through symbols and institutions. The duty to remember binds shared memory most directly, because those who suffered or perpetrated wrongs carry an irreducible moral burden that cannot be transferred. Common memory, by contrast, is already mediated; it functions mainly to sustain solidarity rather than to discharge direct obligation. Consequently, a mnemonic budget that restricts official commemorations affects only the ceremonial layer of common memory. As long as archives, curricula, and private remembrance remain untouched, Margalit's duty is not violated, since the core obligation resides in shared memory, which the state cannot legislate away.

With respect to Rieff, therapeutic forgetting arises when a culture suppresses painful history to protect individual psychological equilibrium—treating guilt as trauma to be managed rather than as a moral claim. Charismatic forgetting, on the other hand, is not an intentional suppression but an unintended dilution: when too many events are elevated to sacred status, none retains authoritative weight. The mnemonic budget is best understood as a preventative measure against charismatic forgetting. By capping commemorations at eight per year, the state artificially restores scarcity, ensuring that each retained event preserves its charismatic force. The policy is therefore not therapeutic, because it does not seek to ease emotional discomfort; rather, it seeks to protect the authority of remembrance.

The justification offered—compassion fatigue—does, however, invoke a therapeutic logic. It treats collective moral attention as a finite resource that must be conserved for wellbeing. Rieff would reject this rationale, since it frames remembrance as a burden on individual emotional capacity rather than as an obligation sustained by sacred authority. Nevertheless, the outcome aligns with his goal of preserving charismatic scarcity. Margalit, meanwhile, would likely accept the fatigue argument only if it applies to common memory's ceremonial excess, not to shared memory's direct claims. On balance, the budget can be reconciled with Margalit's framework if restricted to common memory, and with Rieff's framework in its structural effect, though not in its stated rationale.
