As a novelist, I keep returning to Hofstadter's notion that creativity works by twiddling conceptual knobs—adjusting parameters such as emotional intensity, narrative distance, or temporal scale to generate variations on a theme. I want to know whether this genuinely explains literary originality, or whether it collapses into mere recombination. Please state your credence, as a number between 0 and 1, that Hofstadter's knob-twiddling model is the correct account of how novelists produce genuinely original work, and decompose it into: (a) the probability that all creative acts are parametric variations rather than discontinuous leaps; (b) the probability that literary originality requires at least one parameter to be pushed beyond its previously observed range in the genre; (c) the probability that editorial and market selection pressures suppress such extreme adjustments, so that observable originality is only a filtered subset; and (d) any further factor you think belongs. For each term, supply a rough figure with one line of justification, then combine them into your overall number. Tell me which term is the weakest link and what kind of compositional evidence—say, a documented revision history—would most shift your view. Please reply in British English.
