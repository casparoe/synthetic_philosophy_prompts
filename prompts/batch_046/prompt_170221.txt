I have recently been appointed chair of an institutional working group charged with evaluating proposals for so-called adaptive learning environments—systems that continuously harvest granular behavioral data on students, including keystroke dynamics during digital examinations, eye-tracking metrics during online lectures, and temporal patterns of library access, in order to dynamically recalibrate pedagogical parameters in real time. The administration presents these interventions as technically neutral optimizations of student welfare, yet several colleagues in critical theory have insisted that such arrangements must be assessed through the lens of Foucault's analysis of disciplinary power, particularly the productive relationship between knowledge and power, the normalization of subjects through micro-physical control, and the distinction between sovereign coercion and disciplinary modulation. Rather than soliciting a general theoretical exposition, I am writing to request an operational decision procedure—an ordered checklist or flowchart in prose—that my working group, which includes administrators and computer scientists with no philosophical training, could actually apply to each proposal in a consistent manner. The procedure must translate Foucault's conceptual distinctions into concrete evaluative questions: for instance, how to distinguish mere data aggregation from panoptic visibility, and how to identify whether a system produces normalization rather than merely recording deviation. It should specify the sequence in which questions must be asked, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where an affirmative determination triggers an immediate procedural stop, and where evaluation necessarily yields to an irreducibly normative judgment about the value of disciplinary normalization that cannot be settled by further empirical inquiry. To ground the procedure, please apply it to two concrete proposals: first, a system providing real-time biometric feedback to exam proctors based on student eye-movement patterns; second, a predictive analytics platform that automatically advises students to withdraw from courses based on behavioral patterns correlated with past failure. I need this framework to be rigorous enough to satisfy my philosophical colleagues yet practical enough to be executed in a ninety-minute committee meeting without requiring recourse to specialist literature.
