Read John Milton's Paradise Lost through the lens of Galileo Galilei's hermeneutics, particularly the distinction between the book of nature and the book of scripture and the principle of accommodation developed in his letter to Christina of Lorraine. Concentrate on Book VII, where Raphael describes creation and cosmology to Adam: does Milton treat the Genesis account as accommodating the limited understanding of its original audience, or does he tether scriptural authority to a specific cosmological model? Point to particular scenes—Raphael's cosmological instruction, Satan's passage through chaos, or the physical layout of Heaven, Hell, and Earth—where the poem either anticipates Galileo's separation of theological and scientific claims, or where it fuses them in ways Galileo would reject. Then ask whether the epic's theological architecture ultimately depends on that cosmology being literally true, or whether it can survive the interpretive flexibility Galileo demands of scripture. Finish by saying what this literary case reveals about the limits of accommodation that philosophical argument alone usually misses.
