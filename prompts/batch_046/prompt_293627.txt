I have been working through variations on the separateness-of-persons objection to utilitarian aggregation, and my intuitions seem to pull in different directions. Here are my verdicts on five cases.

First, the classic transplant case: a surgeon can kill one healthy patient and harvest his organs to save five dying patients. I judge this impermissible, even though the net lives saved are positive.

Second, the targeted statistical rescue case: a public-health authority knows that mandating a specific intervention for one particular person will certainly cause that person a severe but non-fatal reaction, but it will prevent an outbreak that would kill five specific others. Here I think imposing the burden is permissible, despite actively harming one for the sake of five.

Third, the labor-redistribution case: one worker is compelled to work an extra twenty hours per week for ten years so that five coworkers can each reduce their workload by four hours per week, with no net change in total labor but a massive, sustained concentration of burden on the one. I feel this is impermissible, even though no life is at stake and the aggregate burden is unchanged.

Fourth, the triage-omission case: a doctor has resources sufficient to save either one critically injured patient or five others with equally critical injuries, and she saves the five, letting the one die. This seems not just permissible but correct.

Fifth, the judicial-scapegoat case: a government can prevent a riot that will kill five innocent people only by executing one innocent person as a public spectacle. I judge this impermissible, like the transplant case, even though the numbers are identical.

What strikes me is the tension between the second and third cases. In both, one identifiable person bears a concrete, foreseeable cost so that five avoid a comparable or greater cost, yet I treat them differently. Meanwhile, the first and fifth share a verdict despite very different institutional contexts, while the fourth seems to license exactly the kind of interpersonal comparison that the first forbids.

Is there a single principle—perhaps involving the distinction between using someone as a mere means, the structure of distributive burdens over time, or the difference between token-targeted and type-level harm—that can accommodate all five verdicts? Or must I drop at least one? Please state the principle precisely, indicate which of my judgments it forces me to revise, and apply it to two further cases I have not mentioned: (a) a scenario in which one person must be physically restrained without lasting injury for one hour so that five people can escape a fire, and (b) a scenario in which one person's modest lifetime tax increase funds medical care that saves five strangers from fatal illness. Please provide your answer in American English.
