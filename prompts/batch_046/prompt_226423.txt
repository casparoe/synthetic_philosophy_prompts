I am preparing the final examination for an upper-level seminar in moral ontology and African philosophy. I need an essay question on Ubuntu and relational conceptions of personhood that students cannot answer by merely reciting the standard formulation that personhood is constituted through communal belonging. Instead, the question should force them to confront the problem of asymmetric recognition—situations in which one party extends full moral recognition to another that cannot genuinely reciprocate, such as a family recognizing a patient in a permanent vegetative state, or users recognizing an artificial system that simulates reciprocity without internal states.

Please construct the following, written in a formal academic register and suitable for a 90-minute examination requiring approximately 800–900 words:

1. The exam question. It should direct students to (a) articulate relational personhood precisely and distinguish it from both intrinsic-property theories and pure social-constructionist accounts; (b) analyze whether asymmetric recognition undermines the relational view, or whether it can be accommodated without collapse; (c) propose or critically evaluate an interstitial account of graded personhood—one that attempts to preserve the constitutive role of reciprocity while allowing that partial or one-sided recognition networks might confer intermediate moral status; (d) apply that account consistently to four specified cases: (i) a newborn infant recognized only by parents; (ii) a patient in permanent vegetative state recognized by family members; (iii) an AI conversational agent treated as a confidant by users but lacking any internal mental states; and (iv) a wild animal that systematically avoids human contact; and (e) conclude whether preserving the view in this interstitial form requires abandoning Ubuntu’s core commitment that reciprocity is constitutive rather than merely instrumental.

2. A model answer of roughly 700 words. It must demonstrate the required conceptual moves: a precise distinction between relational, intrinsic, and constructionist theories; a clear formulation of the asymmetry challenge; explicit conditions for the interstitial grading principle (for example, conditions involving recognition-network density, potential for reciprocity, or functional integration within a moral community—not merely metaphorical language); consistent verdicts on all four cases with explicit reasoning; and a critical conclusion that acknowledges at least one tension or sacrificed verdict.

3. A marking scheme totaling 25 points, separating A-range answers (22–25) from B-range answers (18–21) by specifying the moves each tier must contain. The scheme should allocate marks across five categories—conceptual articulation (5 points), formulation of the asymmetry problem (5 points), construction of the interstitial principle with explicit conditions (6 points), application to the four cases (6 points), and critical conclusion about core commitment (3 points). For each category, provide a one-line note on the characteristic mistake that should cost marks, such as conflating relational personhood with social constructivism, treating reciprocity as causally rather than constitutively relevant, or applying the interstitial principle inconsistently between the AI and animal cases.

The prompt should not request bibliographies, reading lists, or citations. It should avoid reliance on obscure named theories or specific texts that a student without web access could not be expected to recall. Do not include verbatim quotations from actual philosophical texts; paraphrase any well-known formulations without quotation marks.
