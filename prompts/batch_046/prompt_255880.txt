A philosophy of economics blog offered the following summary of the backward-induction resolution to the centipede game: 'Because rationality is common knowledge, each player knows the other will defect at the very next node, so by backward induction the first player should take the initial payoff immediately. This proves that rational agents can never reach the Pareto-optimal outcome in any finitely repeated interaction with perfect information, which is why experimental subjects who cooperate longer are simply failing to apply the logic consistently. It follows that backward induction is a normative benchmark that evolutionary dynamics must eventually converge upon, and therefore all evolutionary accounts of sustained cooperation are undermined.'

I have a nagging sense that this gloss conflates normative prescription with descriptive prediction, and that it misstates what backward induction requires about beliefs and about the class of games to which it applies. Could you audit the summary? Please separate what is accurate about the logical structure of backward induction, what counts as simplification or overstatement (especially regarding common-knowledge conditions, the restriction to perfect-information games, and the claim about evolutionary convergence), and whether the blogger's final conclusion about evolutionary explanations actually follows from the corrected premises.
