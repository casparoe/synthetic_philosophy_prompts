I've been thinking that karma might actually be defensible as a theory of moral desert if we strip out the reincarnation stuff and treat it as a claim about causal persistance — like, moral actions create structural conditions in social and institutional networks that feedbak onto agents over long time horizons, so desert isn't tracked by a cosmic ledger but by the durability of the patterns you set in motion. It's not just "what goes around comes around" as folk wisdom; it's that moral character gets embedded in relationships, reputations, and institutional roles that persist and constrain future outcomes. So karma, on this view, explains why we intuit that people should get what's coming to them without needing divine judgment or metaphysical souls.

But I'm worried I've just insulated it too well. What would actually count against this? Not just the obvious "people often don't get what they deserve" objection — I want the deeper stuff. Which formal results or empirical patterns in sociology or economics would have to be false? What intuitions about desert does this view mispredict — say, about immediate punishment versus delayed feedback, or about desert for people who change character radically? Does it risk collapsing into plain consequentialism or into some kind of social contract theory? And which defeaters would a defender of this naturalized karma view concede are fatal versus just awkward? I want an honest map of where it's actually exposed, including whether it's been qualified into unfalsifiability and whether that's a problem for a desert theory specifically.
