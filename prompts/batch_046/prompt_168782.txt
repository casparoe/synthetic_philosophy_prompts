I am a retired physicist who has only recently begun studying philosophy of mathematics in earnest. At a seminar last month, a speaker offered the following summary of intuitionistic logic and mathematical constructivism for a mixed audience: intuitionistic logic is essentially classical logic with the law of excluded middle removed, so a constructivist treats any statement that has not been explicitly constructed as lacking a determinate truth-value rather than being false. From this logical base, the speaker continued, Brouwer replaced the classical real continuum with free-growing choice sequences—mental constructions that are never completed as a set—and this substitution yields two striking analytical results. First, the continuity principle: every total real-valued function on the closed unit interval is automatically uniformly continuous. Second, the intermediate value theorem is false in this setting, since one can define a continuous intuitionistic function that moves from negative to positive values without ever attaining zero. The speaker concluded that, because these outcomes contradict the calculus used in physics and engineering, intuitionistic analysis is at best a philosophical curiosity with no real bearing on working science.

I suspect this account weaves together genuine technical claims with subtle distortions—perhaps conflating Brouwer’s particular ontology with constructivism generally, or misstating what follows from the rejection of excluded middle—but I lack the training to identify the errors with precision. Would a careful intuitionist or constructivist accept these characterizations of the logic, the choice-sequence ontology, the continuity principle, and the status of the intermediate value theorem? Which parts are accurate, which are overstatements or misrepresentations, and does the speaker’s dismissive conclusion about scientific irrelevance actually follow once the account is corrected?
