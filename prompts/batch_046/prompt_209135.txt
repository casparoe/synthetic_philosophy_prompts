Here is my philosophical profile on Arendt and bureaucratic evil for a reading group consistency check. I hold that: (a) Arendt was correct that Eichmann's evil was banal—driven by careerism, cliché, and the absence of ideological conviction rather than by fanatical hatred; (b) moral blame for atrocity requires that the agent possessed and culpably failed to exercise relevant moral capacities, not merely that they caused harm; (c) the capacity in question is what I term counterfactual moral sensitivity: the ability to represent one's actions as they would appear outside institutional roles; (d) modern bureaucracies structurally suppress the exercise of this sensitivity without destroying the underlying capacity itself; (e) where a capacity is structurally suppressed in this way, the agent's failure to exercise it does not warrant full moral blame, though it may justify a non-blaming form of accountability I call functional culpability; and (f) Western liberal frameworks that centre individual intent are not undermined by this analysis, since identifying structural suppression itself requires the individualist vocabulary of agency and capacity. Which commitments genuinely pull against one another? In particular, do (b) and (c) together commit me to blaming Eichmann, whilst (d) and (e) together commit me to excusing him? Is the apparent conflict between (e) and (f) resolvable on closer inspection? Please rank the tensions by severity, state the least costly revision for each, and indicate what that revision costs me in terms of the other commitments.
