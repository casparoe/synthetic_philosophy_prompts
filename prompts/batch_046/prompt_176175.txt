As someone recently appointed to an advisory board shaping transnational governance protocols for generative cultural systems, I find myself returning to Goethe’s political writings—not out of antiquarian interest, but because the problem before us seems to replay tensions he diagnosed. Goethe witnessed the collapse of the Holy Roman Empire and the rise of Napoleonic rationalization; he distrusted revolutionary abstraction, insisted that legitimate authority grows organically rather than being engineered by constitutional design, and yet embraced a kind of cosmopolitan Weltbürgertum that refused to reduce culture to national possession. He also spoke of the demonic in politics: those overwhelming, quasi-natural forces—embodied for him in Napoleon—that reshape institutions faster than deliberation can absorb them. Today we face a comparable dynamic: regulatory frameworks for artificial intelligence are being drafted by bodies with no direct democratic mandate, operating across jurisdictions, attempting to impose uniform ethical constraints on technologies that evolve faster than legislative cycles. The tension is between engineered rationalization and organic adaptation, between cosmopolitan governance and democratic rootedness. Given Goethe’s commitment to organic development, his skepticism toward abstract universalism in constitutional form, his qualified cosmopolitanism, and his notion of the demonic as an irresistible historical energy that must be channeled rather than denied, what position would he likely have taken on the legitimacy and structure of such supranational AI governance? Would he have seen these regulatory bodies as necessary channels for a demonic technological force, or as a dangerous repetition of the bureaucratic rationalism he associated with post-revolutionary France? And how might his concept of Weltliteratur—culture that transcends borders without losing local soil—inform the design of governance that is neither purely national nor purely technocratic?
