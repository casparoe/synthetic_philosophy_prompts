I hold a version of associative nationalism that grounds special obligations among compatriots not in shared ethnicity, culture, or bare institutional membership, but in what I call co-authorship: citizens are jointly responsible for the shape of a shared political life in a non-voluntary but enduring way, generating associative duties analogous to those among co-parents or long-term collaborators who did not choose one another but remain mutually accountable for outcomes they jointly produce. What would count against this view? I want more than the standard cosmopolitan objections about moral arbitrariness or global distributive failure; I want to know which specific cases, conceptual distinctions, or empirical findings would undermine the analogy between citizenship and joint authorship. Which predictions does this co-authorship view make that pure institutionalism or pure cosmopolitanism do not? Has it been qualified into unfalsifiability — for example, by treating any absence of fellow-feeling as evidence that the associative bond hasn't been "activated"? Rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, distinguishing those a committed defender would concede as damaging from those they would dismiss as missing the point. I am especially interested in whether there is a principled boundary that prevents the logic from extending to all humanity (collapsing into cosmopolitanism) or contracting to subnational groups (making the view unstable), and whether that boundary is defensible or ad hoc.
