I am drafting a short philosophical essay on split-brain patients and the unity of consciousness, and I would like comments on its coherence, its fidelity to the clinical phenomena, and whether its central analogy holds. Rather than treating commissurotomy as proof that one brain can host two conscious subjects, I want to argue that the well-known left-hemisphere confabulations—rationalizing actions apparently initiated by the right hemisphere—are not pathological exceptions but exaggerated versions of the narrative integration that occurs in intact brains. Specifically, I propose extending a multiple-drafts picture spatially: in the normal brain, parallel streams generated in both hemispheres are continuously reconciled by subpersonal integrative mechanisms, so that consciousness is not a pre-given unity but the product of successful interhemispheric editing. In split-brain cases, that editing is disrupted, and the left hemisphere publishes its draft unrevised, producing the illusion of an independent right-hemisphere mind. I then address the objection that this collapses into the standard divisibility thesis—if poor integration yields multiplicity, then split-brain patients do have two minds after all—by arguing that genuine multiplicity requires not merely failed editing but competing, unreconciled drafts with independent affective and attentional substrates, which are absent because both hemispheres still share brainstem and limbic regulation. I conclude that the philosophical debate should shift from counting minds to assessing the robustness of synthetic mechanisms. Would you assess whether my characterization of the interpreter phenomenon is accurate beyond the classic visual-field experiments? I also welcome criticism of whether applying an editorial-revision model across hemispheres is conceptually coherent, and whether my reply to the divisibility objection genuinely distinguishes synthetic unity from mere causal interaction.
