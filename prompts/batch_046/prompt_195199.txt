I have just returned from mediating a local dispute between two communities with incompatible ritual obligations, and I am now presenting a job-market paper at the Workshop on Normative Pluralism arguing that moral compromise between incommensurable value systems can yield genuine normative gains—analogous to gains from trade—not merely through mutual concession but through what I term normative arbitrage: each system relaxes a prohibition it regards as fungible in exchange for the other system’s protection of a value it regards as sacred, producing a composite arrangement that neither could generate alone. The audience includes Kantians who treat moral duties as non-fungible, virtue ethicists concerned that arbitrage institutionalises insincerity, comparative ethicists familiar with Confucian-Islamic dialogue who will stress power asymmetry, and an anthropologist of Maussian gift economies who will ask whether this is merely disguised reciprocity. Give me the objections in the order they are likely to arise, phrased precisely as each camp would phrase them, with for each: what I should concede, what I should hold, the weakest point in my reply, and—crucially—the objection about the distinction between arbitrage and reciprocity that I am hoping nobody asks. Lead with the bottom line: the three objections that would most seriously damage the thesis if left unanswered, then the reasoning and reply strategy for each, with no preamble and no restating of the question.
