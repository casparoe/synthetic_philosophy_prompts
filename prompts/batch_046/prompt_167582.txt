I'm writing a feature on whether culture can genuinely cause things, and I've got stuck on Weber's Protestant ethic thesis. From what I gather, Weber argued that Calvinist beliefs about predestination and worldly asceticism actually caused modern capitalism to take root — not just that they happened alongside it. But here's my confusion: if values are genuinely causal in that hard sense, shouldn't we be able to isolate the 'Protestant effect' in historical datasets the same way we'd isolate a drug in a randomised trial? And if we can't run that kind of experiment — which we obviosuly can't with 16th-century Geneva — doesn't that mean culture is never really a cause, just a narrative we slap on afterwards?

There's another thing that's niggling me. People keep using Weber to claim every economic system needs a matching spirit or value-set to survive. But if Protestant values genuinely caused capitalism, and then capitalism eventually made those same values redundant, doesn't that create a kind of paradox? The culture builds the machine that then destroys the culture. So either values don't really cause anything, or Weber's thesis collapses into contradiction.

I think part of my problem is that I'm treating Weber's idea of an elective affinity between religious ideas and economic practice as if it were straight-line causation. Can you lay out exactly where that analogy breaks — what Weber meant by adequate causation versus meaningful adequacy — and whether the fact that capitalism outgrew its Protestant origins actually damages his argument, or whether I was just asking the wrong question from the start?
