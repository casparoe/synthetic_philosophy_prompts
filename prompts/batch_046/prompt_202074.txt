I'm teaching a short unit on Tolstoy's The Death of Ivan Ilyich and I want to pair it with the Epicurean argument that death can't harm the dead person since they don't exist to experience it. But I'm also trying to work in the modern deprivation account and its timing problem — if being dead deprives you of future goods, when exactly is that harm supposed to occur? Before you die? After? There's no subject then either, so it's weird.

Read the novella through both lenses. Ivan spends the story terrified of death and then, right at the end, seems to accept it and even feel a kind of relief or light. Does Tolstoy present his fear as rational — is Ivan actually being harmed by his impending death in a way the Epicurean misses — or is the harm all in the dying process and in the wasted, inauthentic life he realizes he's led?

Specifcally, look at the scene where he falls and the black sack metphor, and the final moment where he stops fighting and feels compassion instead of fear. Does that ending vindicate Epicurus (death is nothing, stop fearing it) or does it smuggle in a different view, maybe that death is a release or that the harm was never death itself but the life he lost? And where does the deprivation account fit — is Ivan deprived of a good future he could have had, or is that idea undermined by the fact he's already lost everything that made life worth living?

I'm trying not to mislead my students into thinking Epicurus just says don't worry be happy, so I'd like a reading that takes both the novella and the philosophical arguments seriously, and says where Tolstoy complicates them. Which parts of Ivan's arc would you point the class to?
