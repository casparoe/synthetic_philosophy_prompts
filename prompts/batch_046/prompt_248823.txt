I am taking an upper-level seminar on political liberalism and we have been working through the standard literature on public reason, but I want to design a session that moves past the familiar framing of whether religious citizens ought to bracket their comprehensive doctrines. Instead, I want fifteen students in a ninety-minute workshop to feel what I am calling the asymmetry of translation burden: the fact that secular citizens can advance policy reasons in a shared vocabulary without altering the normative ground of their commitments, whereas religious citizens must either suppress that ground or render it in secular terms that often strip away its binding force. Please design a structured deliberation or role-play staged around a concrete proposal—perhaps an exemption for religious conscientious objection to a mandatory civic education curriculum—whose rules initially appear neutral but are arranged so that participants discover only mid-process that one side’s reasons are systematically subjected to a translation requirement while the other side’s are accepted as already public. Specify the roles, the timeline, the stakes or payoff structure that does not require real money, and the rules of admissible speech so that the group runs into the problem through their own attempts to coordinate rather than through any explanation I give up front. Then include a debrief that maps specific moments in the deliberation onto parts of the public-reason framework, predicts what will predictably go wrong the first time the activity is run and how to handle it, and is frank about which feature of the translation asymmetry—likely the internal experience of normative loss that accompanies rendering a sacred reason in secular language—this classroom simulation cannot reproduce and that the facilitator must supply. Write everything in continuous prose only, with no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables, as if in a letter.
