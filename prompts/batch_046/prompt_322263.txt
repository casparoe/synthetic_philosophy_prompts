I sit on my university's interdisciplinary research ethics board for digital media and virtual-environment studies. This month we received a proposal from a cognitive-science team that intends to run controlled experiments inside a high-fidelity virtual world: human participants will be instructed to perform actions that cause severe distress to non-player characters rendered with realistic emotional and physiological expressions, and the study will measure the participants' own emotional and attitudinal responses. The researchers classify the protocol as ordinary human-subjects research requiring no special safeguards, on the grounds that the characters are purely fictional artifacts lacking consciousness, interests, or moral standing. A minority of the board argues that if these entities are engineered specifically to elicit genuine moral responses—if they function as what might be called proxy moral patients, borrowing representational weight rather than possessing intrinsic moral status—then inducing harm toward them raises ethical concerns analogous to indirect harm, and the study should be subject to enhanced consent procedures, debriefing, and a stricter justification of scientific necessity.

My own working hypothesis is that the stalemate turns on whether moral patiency can be relationally borrowed through realistic representation without being intrinsically possessed, but I am not convinced this distinction is well-formed, and I worry that both sides are answering slightly different questions under the single heading of whether video-game characters matter morally. Before Thursday's meeting, please draft the board's formal opinion—recommend approve, approve with conditions, or reject—together with a concrete course of action and an explicit time frame for implementation or follow-up review. The memo should treat both the researchers' minimal-risk framing and the minority's proxy-patiency objection with equal philosophical seriousness, not dismissing either as conceptual confusion. Crucially, please separate what is genuinely settled in philosophical work on virtual and fictional moral patients from what remains actively contested among informed specialists, and do not present any contested position—such as the claim that representational moral patiency is incoherent, or that realistic virtual suffering automatically generates obligations—as though it were the established consensus. You may use the proxy-versus-intrinsic distinction to structure your recommendation if it proves useful, but please do not supply bibliographies, reading lists, or citations, and avoid appealing to highly technical results that a well-informed generalist in philosophy or ethics could not reasonably be expected to know.
