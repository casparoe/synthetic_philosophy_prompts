The pattern is this. For centuries, a practice exists inside a narrow social and geographic container—say, a harvest ritual performed only by a specific religious minority in one valley, or a ceremonial garment worn exclusively by members of a local craft guild. When a modern nation-state consolidates, it selects that element, strips away its original boundaries of class, region, and belief, and promotes it as emblematic of the entire national culture. Over generations, the state-sanctioned version becomes so dominant that the older, localized form fades from public memory or is treated as a quaint deviation. The critical twist is temporal: when historians or local communities later attempt to recover the original context—restoring, for example, the religious meaning or the guild restrictions—they are often accused of politicizing heritage, of inventing divisions that never existed, or of corrupting an authentic national tradition. In other words, the curated copy is experienced as the timeless original, and the recovery of the actual original is treated as modern fabrication.

This feels related to work on invented traditions and on how nations imagine themselves into being, yet it does not quite match either frame.
