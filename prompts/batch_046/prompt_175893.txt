I serve on the Critical Care Allocation Committee at a university medical center, and we must render a decision on Protocol 9-C, which governs how scarce extracorporeal membrane oxygenation slots are assigned when demand exceeds capacity. The protocol requires clinicians to generate a single “prognostic reference class” for each candidate—e.g., “patients aged 50–60 with autoimmune disorder Y and biomarker Z”—and then apply that class’s survival rate to produce one rank-order score.

The case now before us is concrete. A 54-year-old patient with a rare autoimmune disorder and a documented occupational exposure history has been assigned a 14 percent predicted survival rate under the broadest clinically validated reference class. If we use a narrower intersectional class—same age and disorder plus the exposure history—the rate is 41 percent. A still narrower class restricted to carriers of a specific genetic variant present in this patient yields 9 percent. The clinical team is split: some warn that selecting narrower classes amounts to “cherry-picking” favorable statistics, while others argue that broad classes dilute individually relevant information and thereby commit the reference-class fallacy.

Embedded here is the reference class problem: any individual belongs to indefinitely many true statistical classes with divergent profiles, and there is no uniquely correct class for prediction. One faction on our committee proposes “pluralist allocation”—reporting every statistically valid reference-class probability (with n > 30) to the decision-makers and requiring clinical judgment to weigh them explicitly, rather than collapsing them into a single score. A second faction, concerned that pluralism introduces arbitrariness and potential discrimination, wants to mandate the broadest available class for every patient to preserve consistency and auditability.

Please draft the committee’s decision memo—approve Protocol 9-C as written, reject it, or approve it with conditions—recommending a concrete course of action with a specific implementation or review time frame. The memo should address whether the reference class problem makes single-score allocation philosophically untenable in principle, whether pluralist allocation is administratively workable in practice, and what the strongest consideration on the losing side is and why it does not prevail. The reasoning should be accessible to clinicians, ethicists, and administrators without assuming prior study of the philosophy of statistics.

You are an expert in philosophy of science and clinical ethics. Before drafting, think carefully and step by step: first isolate the core philosophical tension between statistical objectivity and individual prediction; then brainstorm how an institutional body can operationalize statistical reasoning without resolving deep metaphysical disputes about reference-class privilege; then outline the memo with its recommendation, reasoning, and response to objections. After the memo, include a brief meta-note stating what argument or empirical finding would change your recommendation, and identify the specific point in your reasoning most vulnerable to failure—particularly where institutional feasibility and philosophical rigor conflict. Write in a formal, polished tone suitable for circulation. Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citations; explain any contested conceptual distinctions briefly within the memo itself rather than assuming familiarity.
