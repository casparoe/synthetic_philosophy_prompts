I am preparing interview questions for a philosophy podcast, and next week’s guest is an unnamed philosopher who defends a recursive-rigidity theory of agency centered on sphexishness. On his view, the sphex wasp’s stereotyped burrow-check loop is not a deficit of agency but its basic architecture: human decision-making consists of nested sphex loops, in which apparent flexibility is only a lower-order loop failing and triggering a higher-order, equally deterministic protocol. He denies that freedom requires breaking rigidity; instead, authorship and responsibility emerge from the depth, transparency, and recursive self-monitoring of the nesting, not from any openness of outcomes.

Draft eight to ten interview questions that do not ask him to restate this position, but press where the literature on mechanical rigidity, flexibility, and free will actually presses. I need: (i) one question that forces him to defend treating mechanical rigidity as constitutive of agency rather than its absence, with a follow-up keyed to the standard reply that rigidity precludes genuine responsiveness to reasons; (ii) one that targets how he distinguishes real adaptive flexibility from a mere failure-cascade of subroutines, with a follow-up for his likely answer that the cascade is still authored because the agent recognizes the specific failure mode; (iii) one on moral responsibility—if every “choice” is a nested loop, what distinguishes culpable from non-culpable action, with a follow-up addressing the reply that culpability tracks loop-transparency rather than openness; and (iv) one that confronts the collapse objection: if all flexibility is reducible to rigidity at a higher level, the distinction between free and unfree action dissolves entirely. Include at least one further question that probes whether small-scale LLM behavior—repetitive pattern-completion with occasional error-correction—should count as sphexish agency on his criteria, anticipating the answer that scale and recursive self-modeling make the difference, and include the corresponding follow-up. Keep the tone suitable for an informed philosophy audience; avoid generic biography or “what drew you to philosophy” questions.
