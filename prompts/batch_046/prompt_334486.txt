In a policy seminar on eldercare funding, a speaker kept returning to the line: “Dependency is not a failure of autonomy but its precondition.” She deployed it to dismiss any argument that state subsidies should be restricted to those who have demonstrated prior economic self-reliance. Before I respond, I need the slogan disambiguated. Lay out the precise theses it might express: the empirical claim that human development requires sustained care; the conceptual claim that autonomy presupposes relations of dependency; the normative claim that dependency generates unconditional entitlement; and any others you see as distinct. For each, indicate whether it is a platitude, a substantive but defensible position, or false, and specify whether it actually undermines a merit-based allocation criterion or merely sounds as if it does. I am particularly interested in whether there is a concealed move from descriptive dependency to distributive justice — and whether that move can be justified without importing a full liberal theory of rights. Write in British English. Finally, say what would change your mind: which empirical finding about childhood development, or which argument about the coherence of relational autonomy, would cause you to reclassify one of these readings, and identify where your reasoning is most vulnerable to error.
