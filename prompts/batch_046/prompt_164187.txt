I am drafting a novel that moves between the ruins of 1945 Berlin and the courtrooms of the Nuremberg Military Tribunals, and during a recent conversation with fellow writers about how a legal system can turn its own machinery against its citizens, I found myself returning to the 1947 Judges' Trial—the prosecution of former Nazi jurists who had enforced racial hygiene statutes and issued death sentences for minor offences. The defendants maintained that they had merely applied valid positive law, enacted and published according to the constitutional procedures of the Reich; the tribunal, meanwhile, had to navigate the charge of retroactivity, since international law had not explicitly criminalised their judicial conduct before the war. I should like to understand whether this episode can be read as a test case for the debate between legal positivism and natural law.

Specifically, I want the analysis to run through the lens of Hart's separation thesis and Fuller's account of the inner morality of law—those procedural principles of generality, publicity, prospectivity, and congruence between official action and declared rule—without reducing the trial to a mere illustration. How does Fuller's framework assess a system that, by most formal measures, satisfied the inner morality (laws were codified, promulgated, applied through courts) yet served an ideology of extermination? Does the fact that the Nazi legal order retained procedural regularity undermine Fuller's claim that the inner morality acts as a moral filter, or does it force us to locate the failure elsewhere—perhaps in the absence of a minimal moral purpose that Hart, in his discussion of the minimum content of natural law, might concede is necessary for a legal system's survival?

Furthermore, I would like you to consider whether Gustav Radbruch's formula—that where positive law conflicts with justice to an intolerable degree it must yield—operates as an unacknowledged natural-law premise in the tribunal's reasoning, even though the court formally relied on the positive law of Control Council Law No. 10. Does the episode confirm Hart's warning that conflating legal validity with moral worth leads to conceptual confusion, or does it show, as Fuller might argue, that when a system's rules are systematically used to destroy human dignity, the claim that those rules are valid law becomes not just immoral but incoherent? Please say where the theory is silent—whether it can distinguish between a corrupt but technically competent judiciary and one that is merely incompetent—and what the case reveals about whether judges have a moral duty that can override their legal duty.
