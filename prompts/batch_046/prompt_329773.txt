I am preparing material for a reading group that sits at the intersection of machine-learning alignment and philosophical anthropology, and I keep encountering the same repetitive standoff about inverse reinforcement learning and the broader project of inferring human preferences from behavior. On one side, researchers working in formal decision theory and Bayesian inverse planning treat preference inference as an essentially statistical challenge: given adequate behavioral traces, a well-specified state space, and appropriate priors, one can recover a reward function up to an equivalence class that is practically sufficient for predictive alignment, so the live questions should concern sample complexity, prior selection, and tightening identifiability bounds. On the other side, scholars influenced by interpretive sociology and critical data studies regard the same project with sustained suspicion, arguing that behavior is always embedded in institutional framing, performative identity work, and context-dependent meaning-making, which means any inferred reward vector is not a discovery but a normative projection disguised as mathematics.

My working theory is that the disagreement is not really about the formal theorems—both sides largely concede the identifiability results—nor about the descriptive richness of social context, but about what an explanation of human action is required to accomplish: the first camp demands predictive coherence and instrumental control, while the second demands hermeneutic fidelity and resistance to reductive quantification. Is that diagnosis correct, or is there a concealed empirical or formal disagreement doing the hidden work—perhaps about the actual information content of behavioral traces under richer observation models, about whether certain equivalence classes collapse when social framing is made explicit, or about which episodes should even count as data for preference inference? I want an honest structural diagnosis rather than a survey of interpretations, supported by what each side actually says and does, together with what kind of progress—a conceptual distinction, a formal result, an experimental protocol, or a change of question—could actually end the impasse, or a reasoned argument that no progress should be expected because the sides are answering different questions under the single heading of inferring preferences from behavior.
