I've been thinking about why large language models can reproduce the surface patterns of analogical reasoning yet seem to miss the structural flexibility that humans show when concepts stretch across contexts. In software engineering, we constantly deal with this: the boundary of what counts as a "clean interface" or "proper abstraction" shifts almost imperceptibly when we move from an object-oriented codebase to a functional one, yet we don't treat that shift as mere error—we treat it as a feature of how design knowledge actually travels. This reminded me of Douglas Hofstadter's Copycat work, where concepts in letter-string analogies don't have fixed extensions but slide contextually, and where that very fluidity is supposed to be what makes analogical mapping possible rather than what undermines it.

From this, I've come to hold a specific view: that conceptual boundaries are necessarily unstable in any representational system capable of genuine analogical reasoning, and that attempts to eliminate this instability through stricter formalization—whether in ontology engineering, strongly typed knowledge graphs, or rigid category-theoretic frameworks applied to cognition—don't just fail to capture intelligence, they actively destroy the mechanism that permits novel generalization. I further suspect that what looks like "ambiguity" or "category error" in cross-paradigm reasoning (say, applying an object-oriented design pattern in a purely functional context and having the concept of "inheritance" subtly warp) is not epistemic noise but structured gradient slippage of exactly the kind described in microdomain analogy research. But I'm aware that this risks becoming unfalsifiable: any failure of formalization can be read as proof that fluidity is indispensable, and any success can be dismissed as mere pattern-matching without true analogical understanding.

So I want an honest assessment: what would have to be true for this view to be wrong? Which empirical results in artificial reasoning, which formal results about analogical mapping, or which observations about human conceptual stability would actually count against it? I want the defeaters ranked from fatal to merely awkward, including things that have arguably already happened—for instance, whether recent advances in neural-symbolic integration or in rigid ontology-driven reasoning show that analogical flexibility can be preserved without fluid boundaries. I'd also like to know which objections defenders of fluid concepts would themselves concede as damaging, and which they would dismiss as missing the point. And finally: has this position been qualified into unfalsifiability, and if so, is that a genuine problem for a claim of this kind, or is it acceptable for a structural claim about representational systems?
