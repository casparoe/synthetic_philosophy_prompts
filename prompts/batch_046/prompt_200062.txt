I write novels that move between moral worlds—my current project involves a protagonist raised in a high-honor, high-purity culture whose reasoning reads as genuinely alien to my likely readers. For a workshop I am leading with six other fiction writers, all literate but none with training in moral psychology or philosophy, I need to teach, in a single hour, the empirical reality of cross-cultural variation in folk morality (the WEIRD/non-WEIRD divide, domain differences in harm, loyalty, purity, and authority) and its genuine philosophical stakes for moral objectivity—not to turn them into ethicists, but so they can calibrate how much moral strangeness a reader can bear without either collapsing into relativism or imposing an invisible universal frame. Plan the pedagogical sequence: which conceptual dependency must come first—the descriptive anthropology or the philosophical problem—and why reversing them collapses the lesson; the single concrete comparison or scenario that should carry the idea, specifying what it must contain (a moral judgment that genuinely splits across cultures rather than one culture merely lacking vocabulary for it) and what framing it must avoid (narrative cues that render one side monstrous, childish, or simply ignorant); the misconceptions this specific audience will generate—particularly the slide from "moral emphasis differs" to "no shared ground exists," and the mirror error of treating deep variation as mere costume or performance—and the precise moment in the sequence where each should be interrupted; a quick diagnostic question or exercise that reveals whether they have grasped the difference between descriptive pluralism and normative anti-realism; and which philosophical moves—evolutionary debunking arguments, full-blown metaethical realism, formal moral epistemology—I should explicitly bracket and name as beyond this hour's scope. The deliverable is not the explanation itself, but the architecture of its delivery: order, carrying case, misconception map, check for understanding, and the boundary of refusal.
