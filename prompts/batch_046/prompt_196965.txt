Draft the joint statement of an adversarial collaboration between a defender of cognitive penetration of perception and a defender of strong perceptual modularity. The collaboration concerns whether high-level cognitive states—beliefs, expectations, or domain-specific expertise—can alter the phenomenal character of perceptual experience itself, as opposed to influencing only post-perceptual judgment, attention, or interpretation.

Structure the document as follows:

1. Numbered agreed-claims section. Include at least four claims both parties can sign—for example: that there is a distinction between seeing and judging; that top-down effects on attention and expectation are empirically well established; that ambiguous figures (e.g., the duck-rabbit) yield variable reports across observers with different prior knowledge; and that primary visual cortex receives feedback projections from higher cortical areas.

2. Disagreements table. Give the precise formulation of the core dispute in wording each side accepts. The penetrationist should state that in some conditions a cognitive state genuinely changes what-it-is-like to perceive. The modularist should state that all apparent cases are explained by changes in judgment, interpretation, or attentional selection, with the perceptual experience itself remaining invariant.

3. Two cruxes. First: whether predictive-processing frameworks—in which precision-weighted priors shape sensory representations—constitute cognitive penetration or merely intra-perceptual dynamics. Second: a specific hypothetical case. Consider a trained radiologist viewing a noisy, low-contrast mammogram who, after learning the patient has a strong family history, reports seeing a spiculated mass where novices report only noise; suppose neuroimaging reveals that her early visual cortex (V1/V2) shows differential activation patterns relative to novices viewing the identical stimulus. Specify what both sides agree this case tests, and record each side's prediction: does this pattern indicate penetration of experience, or only of attentional weighting and subsequent judgment?

4. For each crux, identify the kind of evidence or argument both sides agree would be relevant, and state whether either side considers the dispute partly verbal—particularly around the boundary between "perception" and "perceptual judgment"—with an explanation of which portion, if any, is merely terminological.

Keep the wording neutral, avoid citations or reading lists, and do not invoke specific named theories beyond the general predictive-processing framework mentioned above.
