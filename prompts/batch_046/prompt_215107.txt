Construct a decision-tree taxonomy for a graduate seminar on whether an "I" can be distributed across brains, using Douglas Hofstadter's pattern-based view of selfhood as the central provocation rather than the default answer. The instrument must sort respondents by what they say about concrete cases, not by which philosophical label they prefer.

Root commitment, phrased as a direct yes/no question without naming a view: does the persistence of a particular self require spatiotemporal continuity of one specific living biological substrate?

From there, branch through three diagnostic nodes, each a scenario with two or three answer options that a respondent can choose without knowing where it leads:
1. Gradual neural replication in a friend: if a close companion receives implants that copy not merely your episodic memories but your core reasoning patterns, emotional valences, and habitual responses, do you judge that part of your "I" has migrated into them, that they have merely become highly similar to you, or that something else has occurred?
2. High-fidelity functional simulation: if a running computational system replicates your cognitive architecture with sufficient fidelity that it reports first-person continuity with your life and reacts to your history as you would, do you have reason to treat harm or welfare directed at it as continuous with harm or welfare directed at you?
3. Total amnesia with social absorption: if you suffer complete autobiographical amnesia but your long-term partner continues to enact your values, tell your stories in the first person on your behalf, and make practical decisions as you consistently did, is some portion of your self persisting in them?

Derive leaves only after the branching logic is fixed. The tree must include at least these four positions: strict biological singularism (the self is bound to one living body and cannot be shared, copied, or socially transferred); Hofstadter-style pattern pluralism (the self is an abstract informational pattern that can be instantiated in multiple substrates); narrative-social persistence (the self survives through recognition, role-continuation, and emotional resonance in others without requiring literal overlap of brain-patterns); and a deflationary option that denies there is a substantive "I" to distribute in the first place.

In constructing the tree, inject one non-standard distinction that standard taxonomies usually collapse: separate "replicative persistence," in which a self-pattern is directly copied into another substrate, from "resonant persistence," in which two brains mutually shape each other over time so that parts of each self-pattern come to inhabit the other without direct copying. Use this distinction to split one branch that would otherwise merge two different answers.

Then identify one logically possible combination of answers that appears to be unoccupied by any named view—for instance, accepting replicative persistence for artificial substrates while rejecting it for biological brains, or accepting resonant persistence while denying both replicative persistence and narrative-social persistence—and briefly diagnose whether the gap reflects incoherence, unattractiveness, or mere neglect.

Present the result as a text flowchart: each node labeled with its separating question, each branch marked by the answer that leads downward, and each leaf named only after its path is fully defined. Do not include any question that asks a respondent to identify themselves with a pre-existing label (e.g., do not ask "are you an animalist?" or "do you accept Hofstadter's view?").

Finally, flag explicitly any pair of leaves that this three-case tree cannot genuinely separate, and explain why the ambiguity remains. Specify which distinctions matter for the seminar's purpose: in particular, whether distribution requires functional isomorphism, emotional resonance, social recognition, or some combination, and whether the "I" in question should be treated as a token tied to one history or as a type that can be multiply instantiated.
