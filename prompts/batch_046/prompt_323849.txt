Write a formal dialogue between a proponent of Scanlonian contractualism and an opponent who grants the framework's appeal for pairwise cases but denies that it can accommodate the aggregation of harms—the so-called numbers problem—without abandoning its core commitments. Situate their exchange around a concrete policy dilemma: a planning authority must decide whether to authorise fifty separate, small-scale industrial installations, each of which imposes a statistically negligible but non-zero respiratory risk on every member of a discrete, identifiable community. Taken together, the installations will, with near-certainty, cause severe collective harm; yet no single installation can be shown to wrong any particular individual, since each contribution is below any reasonable threshold of perceptible injury.

The contractualist should defend the position that wrongness is a matter of what can be justified to each affected person, and should explain why the separateness of persons blocks straightforward aggregation of harms across individuals. The critic should press the claim that this either renders the framework incapable of condemning the collective outcome—thereby licensing a morally catastrophic result through a series of individually permissible acts—or forces the contractualist to smuggle in aggregative reasoning under the guise of a modified principle, such as a threshold-sensitive rule that treats cumulative risk differently from isolated risk. Both interlocutors should address whether such a modification genuinely preserves pairwise justification or merely collapses contractualism into a disguised form of rule-utilitarianism or prioritarianism.

Require that the dialogue exhibit genuine back-and-forth: each speaker must engage with the specific reply just offered, particularly on whether an appeal to the separateness of persons explains why numbers ought not to count, or only restates that intuition. The critic should not be reduced to a crude consequentialist, and the contractualist should not dismiss aggregation concerns as conceptually confused. The response should be written in British English.
