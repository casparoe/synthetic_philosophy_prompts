I am preparing a single ninety-minute seminar for first-year philosophy graduates who have just completed an introductory philosophy-of-mind module but have never encountered Dennett’s notion of the self as a center of narrative gravity. The audience is philosophically literate but includes several students with neuroscience backgrounds who are suspicious of any language that sounds non-physicalist, as well as others who tend to hear “the self is a fiction” as straightforward eliminativism. I want a teaching plan—not the lecture itself—that specifies the exact dependency order in which the components must be introduced: why the idea of narrative structure has to be established before the gravity metaphor can do any work, why the distinction between the self being fictional and being a useful fiction must wait until the audience grasps what a center of gravity actually denotes in physics, and where in that sequence the biological system that generates the story should first appear. For the carrying example, I am considering using an artificial system that produces consistent first-person reports without a unified processing substrate, but I want your judgment on whether that example is stronger than a clinical case involving disrupted autobiographical continuity, together with an explicit account of what the chosen example must include and exclude so that it does not collapse into either a Cartesian theater or pure social constructivism. Then map the predictable misconceptions: the audience is likely to conflate narrative gravity with a hidden homunculus, with a sociological construct in the strong sense, and with the Humean or Buddhist claim that the self is merely an illusion to be dissolved; specify the precise point in the sequence where each misconception should be anticipated and defused, and explain why defusing it earlier or later would fail. Include a brief written check for understanding—something they could answer in ten minutes—that would reveal whether they genuinely understand that the self is not spatially located but is a dynamical pattern in interpretation rather than a thing interpreted. Finally, state openly what should be left out and acknowledged as such: perhaps the full ontology of fictional objects, the neuroscience of the default mode network, or the comparative evaluation of rival theories of personal identity. The goal is not to defend Dennett against every competitor, but to make the idea available to these students as a live, structurally precise option rather than a slogan.
