I am a third-year undergraduate in comparative constitutional law and political philosophy, currently taking a seminar titled “Church, State, and Fiscal Religion: England, Germany, France, and the United States.” During a recent session on Germany’s Kirchensteuer system, my professor used the phrase “asymmetry of recognition” in passing—referring to how being a Körperschaft des öffentlichen Rechts confers fiscal privileges that smaller religious communities rarely secure—and I have been thinking since about whether that asymmetry is a feature of neutrality or a hidden form of establishment. The standard classroom debates about secularism feel too abstract now, and I want something I can actually use.

This semester I am interning with a municipal grants office that receives quarterly applications from religiously affiliated organizations seeking public co-funding for administratively secular social services—homeless shelters, eldercare facilities, addiction-counseling centers—whose mission statements often contain spiritual language but whose budgets are technically non-religious. The staff has no training in philosophy of law, so the office needs a reusable decision protocol rather than an ad hoc opinion: a checklist with ordered tests, explicit nodes where judgment is unavoidable, and clear flagging of cases the procedure cannot settle. I want this protocol grounded in the comparative philosophical literature on state neutrality—specifically the distinctions between positive neutrality (state cooperation with religion for secular public ends), negative neutrality or strict laïcité (state non-interference and non-funding), and disestablishment with accommodation (no official church but permissive exemptions)—translated into steps a non-expert administrator can apply in under thirty minutes per file.

The procedure should be applied to three hard cases that recur in our docket: first, an application from an established Lutheran welfare association in a German-model jurisdiction requesting church-tax revenue to fund a nursing home that employs staff chaplains and displays religious iconography in common areas; second, an application from a recently registered Islamic cultural center in a French-model jurisdiction seeking a restoration grant for a community hall used both for prayer and for municipally contracted food-bank distribution; and third, an application from an evangelical charity in a U.S.-model county asking for direct municipal funding to run a substance-abuse program in which participants are invited, but not required, to attend optional Bible-study sessions. For each case, run the full protocol, identify the point at which it yields a definite recommendation, mark explicitly where the administrator must make an unavoidable value judgment, and show where the principle demands an answer the applicant will likely find unwelcome. Finally, state precisely what the procedure cannot do—which kinds of hybrid applications it will systematically misclassify, and which deep disagreements about secularism are so fundamental that any procedural translation necessarily begs the question rather than resolving it.
