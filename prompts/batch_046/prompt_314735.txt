The debate between endurantists and perdurantists over how material objects persist through change has settled into a familiar deadlock. Endurantists hold that an object is wholly present at every time it exists, explaining change through temporary property instantiation or adverbial modification rather than by multiplying entities; perdurantists hold that objects are four-dimensional “worms” composed of momentary temporal parts, and that what we call change is just different parts having different properties. Each side knows the standard moves — the endurantist complaint that perdurantism makes intrinsic change mysterious and multiplies entities needlessly, the perdurantist reply that endurantism either collapses into contradiction or secretly relies on time-indexed properties that are temporal parts in disguise — yet for decades neither camp has conceded ground, and the literature repeats the same dialectic with minor variations. I suspect the stall is not accidental. Is this disagreement substantive (about what exists), verbal (about the meaning of “wholly present”), cross-purpose (one side doing ontology, the other conceptual analysis of tracking), or rooted in bedrock intuitions about identity? I also want you to consider a less-discussed pressure case: institutional persistence, as when every member, charter clause, and physical site of a university is replaced over centuries — does this push us toward one view, or reveal that both are answering the wrong question? And does the cognitive-science literature on object-file tracking — which seems to presuppose enduring individuals — bear on the metaphysics, or is it a separate domain?
