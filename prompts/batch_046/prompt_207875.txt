I'm designing a handout for an advanced political-philosophy seminar on democratic legitimacy. I want a decision tree that sorts students from a single starting commitment — they accept that some reference to the will of the people is necessary for democratic legitimacy — to named theoretical positions, but sorted by what they actually believe about concrete cases rather than by which label they prefer.

Please build the tree as a taxonomy: each internal node is one yes/no or either/or question that genuinely divides the positions; each branch is labeled with the claim that separates it from its siblings. The tree should have no more than seven internal nodes and should terminate in leaves carrying (1) the name of the position, or unnamed if no prominent theorist occupies that combination, and (2) one sentence describing its most awkward democratic commitment.

The following positions must appear as leaves: a Rousseauian view that treats the people as a corporate moral agent with a single general will that can be wrong only when deliberative conditions are corrupted; a Schmittian view that defines the people by substantive homogeneity and requires an enemy for democratic identity; a Laclauian view that treats the people as an empty signifier constructed through hegemonic articulation with no pre-political content; an agonistic-pluralist view that denies any final unity of the people and treats democratic politics as the management of irreducible conflict; and a liberal-proceduralist view that denies any collective agent and treats popular will as the aggregated output of fair institutional rules.

To keep the tree from being answerable by encyclopedia lookup, inject the following two niche distinctions into the branching logic: first, a diachronic-revisability branch asking whether a democratic will formed at time t can be legitimately overturned by a differently constituted people at t+1 without breaking democratic continuity; second, a negative-constitution branch asking whether the people's will is constituted primarily by opposition to an elite or out-group rather than by a positive program. Use these to split combinations that standard taxonomies usually collapse.

For every leaf, include a brief diagnosis of why it is occupied or unoccupied. Where two named positions end up in leaves that your tree cannot genuinely separate — for example, if Schmittian homogeneity and Rousseauian corporate unity both answer the negative-constitution branch the same way — flag that pair explicitly and say which extra criterion would be needed to split them.

Phrase every node as a concrete claim or case that a student could answer without already knowing which interpretation it leads to. Do not ask whether the student is a Rousseauian or whether they accept Schmitt. The sorting should be implicit in the path of answers; after the tree, provide a one-paragraph summary mapping the five required answer patterns onto the named positions.

Finally, note any combination of answers that lands in an unoccupied leaf and briefly diagnose whether it seems incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored.
