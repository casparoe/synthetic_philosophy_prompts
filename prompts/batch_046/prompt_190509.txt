I'm trying to identify whether there's an established concept for the following pattern in discussions of negative causation. In complex systems—say, engineered safety networks or organizational protocols—responsibility for covering a given risk-range is divided among subsystems, each assigned a specific domain. When one subsystem fails to act, the absence is treated as causally significant not because it violates a general norm or statistical expectation about that subsystem, but because the failure creates a gap within another subsystem's assigned domain, and the causal attribution is directed at the misallocation of structural responsibility rather than at the bare absence itself. For example, if Module A is tasked with monitoring ranges 1–10 and Module B with ranges 11–20, but Module B's sensor silently fails within range 5, the omission is analyzed as B's failure to respect the division of labor, borrowing causal force from the systemic allocation rather than from B's own expected behavior. I encounter versions of this in distributed failure analysis, but I don't know whether philosophers of causation distinguish it from standard normative-deviation accounts of omissions or from interventionist counterfactual analyses that treat omissions as absences of actual variables. Does this configuration have an established name—perhaps in collective causation, organizational epistemology, or distributed agency—and if not, how should it be defined so that it is clearly separated from both normative and interventionist treatments of omission?
