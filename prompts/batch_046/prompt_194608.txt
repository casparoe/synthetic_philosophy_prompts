I'm running a 90-minute workshop for about 16 philosophy graduate students on Rousseau's critique of theater, and I want an activity that makes them feel—not just discuss—the tension between civic transparency and the actor's divided self. To keep it out of standard lecture territory, inject this framing: treat sentiment not merely as false but as a tradable commodity that a professional performance class can manufacture, so that a republic licensing theater is really licensing a hidden market in counterfeit pity that severs feeling from civic duty. Design a two-phase simulation followed by a legislative vote. Phase one is a collective participatory ritual—call it the fête analog—where every participant has a shared task, visible stakes, and no spectators; rules should reward coordination and mutual accountability. Phase two is a staged emotional scene in which a small group performs for the rest; the performers are paid in direct proportion to the audience's measured emotional response (use simple tokens or a quick show-of-hands intensity rating), and the audience knows this pay structure. After both phases, the same group acts as legislators for a fictional republic and votes, with brief justifications, on whether to license and publicly fund the second format. The rules and payoffs must be arranged so that participants discover for themselves that the second format incentivizes performers to calibrate grief rather than share it, lets audiences consume pity without any obligation to act on it, and makes legislators conflate emotional intensity with moral value. Then provide a debrief that maps each discovered dynamic onto Rousseau's claims about artificiality, the corruption of pity, and the distinction between being seen together and being together, and be explicit about what the 90-minute exercise cannot reproduce—specifically the historical context of 18th-century Geneva and any theological underpinning of authenticity—plus what will predictably go wrong (participants treating the vote as purely aesthetic preference, or mistaking manufactured intensity for authentic solidarity) and how to handle it. If the premise is confused—if reducing Rousseau's political aesthetics to an incentive game necessarily flattens his view of authenticity into behaviorism—say so plainly instead of complying politely. Keep the response under 400 words: numbered rules for both phases and the vote, bullet-point debrief mapping with failure modes, and one paragraph on the unreproducible elements.
