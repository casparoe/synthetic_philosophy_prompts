I am due to present a paper to our university's food-ethics reading group next Thursday — an audience of environmental philosophers, one agricultural ecologist, and two effective-altruism researchers who are well-versed in the standard 'crop deaths' literature but broadly sceptical of wild-animal-suffering frameworks. My thesis runs against the conventional prescription: I defend a threshold-sensitive comparative framework for assessing meat consumption's impact on wild animals, one that I believe undermines the usual 'replace beef with chicken' argument without vindicating omnivorism. Rather than comparing direct mortality counts — rodents and birds killed per hectare of feed crops versus per hectare of pasture — I propose weighing suffering by sentience confidence adjusted for ecological baseline. Rotational grazing on established perennial pasture, I argue, adds minimal suffering beyond the ecosystem's background rate, whereas intensive monoculture for poultry feed generates enormous invertebrate suffering with high sentience uncertainty but non-negligible expected moral weight. On this account, beef from well-managed grassland can yield a lower sentience-weighted suffering differential than chicken raised on soy and maize, though I do not conclude that direct plant consumption is automatically best, since even minimal crop harvesting involves massive invertebrate harm that is rarely internalised in these comparisons. The group values analytical rigour over sentiment; they will press me on the empirical ecology of pasture, on whether my sentience thresholds are arbitrary, and on whether the framework collapses into a reductio that permits almost any diet.

Please prepare me for the objections I will actually face: list them in the order they are likely to arise, phrased in the vocabulary each faction would use — the ecologist's concern about soil-carbon baselines versus the EA researchers' concern about replaceability and expected-value aggregation. For each, indicate where the objection has real force, what I should concede up front, and what I must hold firm if the argument is to survive; also flag the weakest point in my own position that I should defend actively rather than hope they miss. Give me concise replies suitable for a brief Q&A, and note explicitly where each reply is soft. Include, in particular, the objection I am praying nobody raises — namely, that quantifying invertebrate consciousness with sufficient precision to make these comparisons is currently empirically intractable, which would render my framework practically useless. Finally, tell me what this framework predicts for a case I have not yet considered: comparing in-vitro cultivated chicken (with negligible direct wild-animal impact but substantial energy-use externalities) with beef from degraded rangeland that requires active ecological restoration.
