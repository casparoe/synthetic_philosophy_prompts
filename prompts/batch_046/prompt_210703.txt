I stayed out of it, but I want an outside judgment because neither convinced me completely, and I need someone to break down exactly where each stands.

Leo started with the idea that without general moral principles—things like "don't cause unnecessary harm" or "keep your promises"—morality collapses into personal preference. Sam answered with the case of helping a friend move: normally good, but wrong if you're helping them flee a fraud investigation. Leo said that just shows a hidden principle—"don't assist wrongdoing"—overriding the first. Sam argued that this means you'd need an endless list of principles for every combination of circumstances, which nobody actually carries around in their head. She proposed instead that moral reasons are holistic, more like recognizing colors in a painting: you can't isolate one feature and call it good or bad outside the whole context, and adding one new fact can reverse an action's moral status entirely rather than just creating an exception. She used the term "non-monotonic" for that flipping effect. Leo pushed back that if reasons can reverse so easily, moral learning is impossible—you'd have to start reasoning from scratch every morning. Sam said you don't start from scratch; you build moral perception, like learning to recognize a face, not like memorizing a manual. Leo replied that perception needs correction, and only stable principles can correct it when intuition goes wrong. They went back and forth several rounds without resolving it, and I could see merit on both sides.
