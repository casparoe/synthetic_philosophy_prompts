A colleague sketched the following argument in a workshop on archival epistemology, and I have been unable to locate the exit. (1) Historical knowledge is mediated entirely by archives, and archives are not neutral repositories but products of institutional power: they preserve what dominant groups value and neglect or destroy what subordinate groups produce. (2) This generates what we might call negative archival space—the absence of records for subordinate groups is not mere missing data but an active silencing that shapes the evidentiary landscape as powerfully as presence does. (3) Therefore, any historical judgment about events involving subordinate groups is formed under conditions where the evidentiary base has been systematically distorted in a direction serving dominant interests. (4) If one's evidentiary base is systematically distorted in a particular direction, rational belief formation requires either suspending judgment or adopting a hermeneutic of suspicion that treats dominant narratives about those events as unreliable by default. (5) But the claim in (1)—that archives are products of institutional power—is itself a historical claim about institutions, supported by the same archival and historiographical record that (3) declares distorted. (6) Applying (4) consistently therefore requires treating (1) itself with the same suspicion, undermining the very premise that motivated the distortion worry. (7) So either we reject (1) and deny that archives are power-laden, or we accept (4) and end up unable to justify the claim that they are; critical historiography appears self-defeating.

The colleague concludes that we must choose between naive objectivism and self-undermining skepticism. I feel that something slips between (3) and (6)—perhaps an illicit scope shift from object-level events to meta-level claims about archives, perhaps a confusion between first-order historical evidence and second-order sociological evidence about preservation practices, or perhaps a failure to see how correction for bias can operate without global suspension of judgment—but I cannot name it precisely. Which step should be rejected, and what does rejecting it cost? I would prefer to keep (1) and (2) intact, since the asymmetry between presence and absence in institutional records seems undeniable, but I want to know whether saving them forces me to weaken (4) or to distinguish levels of evidentiary assessment in a way that looks ad hoc.
