I write fiction, and I have been asked to serve as an outside grader for an interdisciplinary graduate seminar that pairs philosophy of mind with narrative theory. I have been given one exam response and asked to evaluate it, but I have not formed my own final judgment; please deliver your assessment without presuming that I share your conclusions.

The exam question was as follows. Analyze Daniel Dennett's brain-transplant scenario—frequently discussed under the heading Where Am I—in which the philosopher imagines his brain, called Yorick, extracted and sustained in a vat, while a duplicate brain, Hamlet, is implanted in an exact replica of his body. The original body, now animated by Hamlet, walks away. Students were asked specifically to explain why Dennett holds that the first-person question of which of these subjects is the original Dennett becomes unstable in this setup; to distinguish that instability both from the Lockean psychological-continuity criterion and from a straightforward bodily-continuity criterion; and to evaluate whether the scenario genuinely dissolves personal identity or merely exposes a practical limitation in applying the concept.

The student answer is reproduced below in full. Please provide a letter grade and point-by-point commentary that identifies precisely what is philosophically accurate, where a view is subtly misattributed or conflated, and whether any inferential step fails to hold. I am not asking for a bibliography, a reading list, or citations; I want an assessment of the reasoning itself. Do not offer a vague overall impression—tie each comment to a specific claim in the answer.

Student answer:

In Dennett's scenario, the philosopher's brain, Yorick, is removed and kept alive in a vat, while a perfect duplicate, Hamlet, is placed in an identical copy of Dennett's body. The original body, now animated by Hamlet, walks away. From the vat, Dennett asks where he is.

The standard Lockean account identifies personal identity with continuity of consciousness and memory. Because both Yorick and Hamlet possess Dennett's memories up to the moment of duplication, this criterion seems to entail that both are Dennett. That violates the logical requirement that identity be one-to-one, so psychological continuity appears to break down.

Dennett's own response is to embrace that breakdown. He argues that there is simply no deeper fact about which brain is the real Dennett; personal identity is a useful fiction, a position closely aligned with the reductionism that Derek Parfit defends. Once we see that identity is not a substantial relation but merely a matter of psychological connectedness, the puzzle dissolves.

A bodily-continuity view, by contrast, would locate Dennett with Hamlet, since Hamlet occupies the original body. But Dennett resists this because from his first-person perspective inside the vat he still experiences himself as Dennett, which demonstrates that bodily location is neither necessary nor sufficient for identity.

In the end, Dennett resolves the tension by adopting a narrative conception of the self: the self is a center of narrative gravity, and since both bodies can construct the same autobiographical story, there is no objective fact about which one is the true subject. This result has direct implications for fiction. If the first-person point of view can be duplicated without any loss of narrative authority, then the reliability of first-person narration in novels is fundamentally undermined, because the narrator's privileged access to their own identity can be replicated mechanically.

Please conclude with the grade and the specific, point-by-point justification.
