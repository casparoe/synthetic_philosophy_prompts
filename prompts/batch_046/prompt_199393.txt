I serve on the data-stewardship committee of a neighborhood mental-health cooperative. We hold de-identified transcripts from peer-support circles, shared with consent strictly for staff training. A colleague now proposes running language-analysis software on these texts to generate predictive “risk signatures” — scores estimating which participants will likely enter crisis — so staff can intervene before serious harm occurs. The identities remain encrypted; staff would see only a flag, never the raw text or a name. I believe we should reject this, but my reason does not fit the usual privacy vocabulary of confidentiality or anonymity. From thinking about it on my own, I think there is a separate harm in having your intimate speech mined to construct an institutional model of your probable future: even if no one knows it is you, you are turned into an object of predictive foresight that you never authorized. The violation is predictive, not informational. I need a short written opinion for Thursday’s meeting recommending a concrete course of action — approve, reject, or approve with conditions, with a time frame if conditions apply. The response should begin with the bottom-line recommendation in its very first sentence, then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of the question. The reasoning should treat seriously the strongest argument for approval — that anonymized predictive screening could save lives and that the data is already in our possession — and should explain the predictive-privacy distinction in practical terms the committee can apply to future proposals, without requiring anyone to read philosophy. Please do not include citations, bibliographies, or references to specific philosophers or texts; build the argument from the scenario and the concept alone.
