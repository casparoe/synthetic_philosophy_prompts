I sit on the promotions board of a medium-sized engineering consultancy. One candidate—someone with whom I have collaborated directly for nearly two years—has consistently delivered technical solutions that strike me as unusually creative and reliable, far beyond what I typically observe at her career stage. Based on this close working relationship, I am inclined to support her advancement to principal engineer. At the same time, the consultancy maintains a statistical review showing that, over the past fifteen years, approximately seventy per cent of promotions made on the strength of individual managers' direct advocacy—rather than on standardised competency metrics—have led either to project failure or to the promoted engineer leaving within thirty-six months. Complicating matters further, I maintain a private calibration diary in which I record my confidence level, on a ten-point scale, for every promotion I have endorsed, and then review the actual outcomes two years later; the record shows that my high-confidence endorsements succeed about seventy per cent of the time, which is notably better than the institutional base rate but still far from perfect. I worry that treating myself as a special case is precisely the kind of self-flattery that produces the institutional failure pattern, yet dismissing my direct observations entirely seems equally irrational. How ought I to weigh my particular, first-hand knowledge of this candidate against the broader statistical pattern? Is there a principled framework for combining these perspectives—perhaps by adjusting the base rate with my personal calibration data, or by applying a systematic discount to my own confidence—rather than simply choosing the narrative that feels more flattering? Please respond in American English.
