I serve on the research-funding review board of the Interdisciplinary Institute for Social Policy. We are deciding on a five-year grant proposal titled "Preference Divergence Under Egalitarian Architecture: Re-examining the Gender-Equality Paradox in Career Selection." The applicants build on the cross-national finding that highly egalitarian societies—those with extensive parental-leave quotas, gender-neutral early-education curricula, and strong anti-discrimination enforcement—often exhibit stronger occupational gender segregation in STEM and care-work sectors than societies with weaker egalitarian institutions. Rather than interpreting this pattern as evidence of innate biological differences or of residual hidden structural bias, they propose a third explanatory mechanism they call "liberated preference divergence": the hypothesis that when economic coercion and institutional gender-typing are minimized, gender-linked social identity becomes a more salient axis for self-selection, producing greater career divergence without any corresponding injustice or capability deprivation.

The proposed study compares actual career-choice satisfaction scores with counterfactual scores generated under a modeled "neutral-choice-architecture," using matched cohorts across three countries with differing equality regimes, and it includes a secondary analysis testing whether observed divergence correlates with subjective reports of autonomous preference formation rather than with perceived social pressure. Half our board argues that the study is methodologically sound but politically hazardous: if its results are interpreted as showing that egalitarian policies "backfire" by amplifying gendered preferences, they could be weaponized to justify reducing gender-targeted hiring mandates, dismantling quota systems, or redirecting resources away from structural-intervention programs. The other half argues that rejecting funding because of feared interpretations violates the principle of institutional neutrality, and that the underlying philosophical questions—whether justice in this domain requires equality of outcome, equality of opportunity, or respect for autonomously formed preferences—cannot be settled by suppressing empirical investigation.

Please draft a formal decision memo recommending approval, rejection, or approval-with-conditions. The memo should specify clear, actionable criteria governing study design, data-interpretation protocols, and public-dissemination guidelines that respond to both scientific-integrity concerns and normative-risk concerns. It should explicitly engage the philosophical stakes: what the paradox, if genuinely present, implies about the relative explanatory weight of nature, culture, and justice; whether an institutional funding body has legitimate grounds to restrict research whose conclusions might complicate egalitarian policy commitments; and under what conditions, if any, that risk becomes acceptable. The memo must include an honest and substantive treatment of the strongest consideration favoring the position you do not ultimately take, and it should conclude with a bottom-line recommendation the board can act upon.
