I'm writing a long-form feature on whether mass starvation belongs in the category of natural disaster or in the category of political crime, and I'm struck by how Amartya Sen's entitlement analysis—drawn from the Bengal famine of 1943, the Irish famine of the 1840s, and the famine conditions during the Great Leap Forward—seems to settle an empirical pattern without resolving the underlying conceptual dispute. Sen shows that in each case aggregate food availability was not the binding constraint; rather, the collapse occurred in the legal, economic, and political mechanisms that entitled populations to access food. Yet the scholarly response remains stalled: one camp treats this as proof that famine is necessarily a crime of governance, while another treats it as a descriptive framework that still permits exogenous climatic shocks and bureaucratic incompetence to operate as causal factors without moral culpability. My working hypothesis is that the disagreement is not primarily empirical but taxonomic—about what conditions must obtain for a famine to count as political rather than natural, and about whether attributing political causation requires demonstrable intent or merely structural negligence. Does this diagnosis hold? More specifically, do Sen's three cases genuinely support a unified entitlement theory, or does the heterogeneity among colonial extraction in Bengal, export-driven market failure in Ireland, and authoritarian information suppression in China reveal that the framework needs an internal distinction—perhaps between active deprivation and passive institutional neglect—that Sen himself does not supply? And if the impasse is indeed conceptual rather than empirical, what kind of clarification—of agency, of causation, or of moral taxonomy—would actually move the debate forward?
