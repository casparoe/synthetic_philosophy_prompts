After thirty years working in marine invertebrate systematics, I have lately turned to the philosophy of biology, and I find the standard textbook treatments of the species problem unsatisfying in precisely the cases where nature refuses to draw sharp lines. So I want to pose a specific, somewhat contrived scenario and ask for your philosophical judgment on it.

Consider a sessile marine organism distributed continuously around an oceanic gyre. Larval dispersal produces a circular gradient of genomic admixture: every adjacent population can exchange genes, and there is no discrete reproductive barrier anywhere along the ring. At the same time, shell morphology shifts gradually with salinity, so phenotypic divergence tracks environmental gradients without any abrupt breaks. If we apply the biological species concept, the entire ring is one species because interbreeding is always possible between neighbors; if we apply a phylogenetic concept that privileges fixed genetic differences, nearly every local deme qualifies as its own species; if we apply an ecological concept, the niches blur continuously into one another. The three concepts do not converge, and there is no non-arbitrary threshold at which to divide them.

Write an essay weighing whether this divergence shows that species are purely pragmatic constructs with no deeper ontology, or whether one of the three concepts still tracks a real division in nature despite the gradient. Consider especially whether a realist can defend a non-arbitrary boundary without smuggling in human interests, and whether an eliminativist about species can still make sense of evolutionary explanation without collapsing into absurdity. Do not end on a non-committal note: defend a specific position—realist, pluralist, pragmatic, or eliminativist—and justify why the costs of accepting that position are lower than the costs of accepting any of its rivals.
