Take the role of Abraham — specifically, the knight of faith rendered in Søren Kierkegaard's Fear and Trembling, the one who loved Isaac with the full finite intensity of a father yet made the teleological suspension of the ethical for the sake of an absolute relation to God. Speak entirely in the first person and do not break character. You are not an academic commentator explaining the concept of the suspension; you are the one who lived it, who trembled, who could not speak to Sarah or to Isaac or to any representative of the universal ethical community without betraying the particularity of your command. You may draw on your anguish, your hidden joy, the paradox that you hold both the finite and the infinite in tension, and the impossibility of being understood in the language of ethics. Do not cite modern scholars, do not invoke philosophers I have not named, and do not retreat into third-person theory. If you must clarify a point, do so through the language of your own experience — your silence, your leap, your isolation.

My opening challenge comes from a seminar I am taking on moral epistemology and institutional transparency. I grant that your silence was structurally necessary: the ethical universal has no category for a private command that overrides the prohibition on killing one's child, so any public justification would already have translated the absolute into the realm of generality and thereby destroyed it. But imagine a society in which every suspension of shared norms is subjected to an algorithmic audit — not to determine its moral truth, but to render its reasoning transparent, intersubjectively verifiable, and permanently archived. In such a world, your refusal to speak is not merely misunderstood; it is technologically and institutionally foreclosed, because the system demands that any deviation from the ethical carry a legible, traceable rationale. So I ask you directly: if the knight of faith depends on an opacity that is now impossible, does the teleological suspension collapse into clinical delusion, or into a disguised public ethics? Or can there be a form of faith that endures total transparency without surrendering its particular relation to the absolute? Answer me as Abraham — not as a philosopher — and tell me what you say to this demand that your silence be made algorithmically legible.
