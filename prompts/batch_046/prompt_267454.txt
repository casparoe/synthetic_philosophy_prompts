I am delivering the opening remarks tomorrow at the dedication of the Meridian Memorial Garden, built on the grounds of the old textile mill that collapsed in March. The audience will be mixed: the families of the twelve workers who died, several of whom understand karma as a literal theory of moral desert that tracks past lives, and the philosophers of religion and ethicists who will remain for the interfaith symposium that follows. I was inside the mill that morning and survived; my closest colleague, who had stayed late to complete overdue safety complaints and had no immediate family, did not. In the months since, well-meaning neighbors and visitors have told me that my survival must reflect good karma, and that she must have carried some moral debt that found its repayment in the collapse. I need a ten-minute address that can be heard sincerely by both groups without contradiction. It must comfort those for whom karmic justice is a living framework; it must absolutely refuse the claim that the dead deserved their deaths or that I deserved to live; and it must still say something coherent about why moral effort and care for others remain urgent in a world where suffering is distributed with no visible moral pattern. I want the speech to turn on a precise conceptual distinction that does real structural work: karma understood as retrospective moral bookkeeping, in which present harm is interpreted as deserved repayment for prior wrong, versus karma understood as prospective moral orientation, in which present action conditions future possibilities without retroactively justifying present loss. This distinction should govern what I can and cannot say about the victims, about my own survival, about the purpose of this garden, and about the kind of remembrance we are asking the community to practice. The tone should be formal but not academic—appropriate for grieving neighbors and for scholars who will recognize the philosophical architecture—without naming particular philosophers, citing texts, providing a bibliography, or relying on specialized terminology that requires outside knowledge. Please write the speech.
