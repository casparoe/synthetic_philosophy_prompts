Consider the thesis Directional Asymmetry (DA): for any grounding relation R, if a R-grounds b, then it is metaphysically impossible for b to R-ground a, even when a and b are co-fundamental with respect to some other relation. (3) Therefore pluralists must allow grounding loops unless they impose an external constraint like DA. (4) However, DA is overly strong: if a and b are both fundamental with respect to R, then neither R-grounds the other, yet they may still stand in some other dependence relation. (5) So DA is unnecessary, because well-foundedness can be secured relation-by-relation without a global directional asymmetry. Is this argument sound? In your answer, discuss (i) whether pluralists really deny transitivity across relations or only within them; (ii) whether DA correctly captures the difference between intra-relational asymmetry and inter-relational independence; and (iii) whether well-foundedness requires a global constraint or can be purely local.

The student submitted the following answer:

Grounding is irreflexive and asymmetric within each specific relation, so within any single kind—say, physical grounding—no loops can occur. Pluralists reject transitivity altogether, which is why they need DA: without it, a physical fact could ground a logical fact, which then grounds another physical fact, creating a loop across relations. Schaffer is right that this is a problem for pluralism, and he defends DA as the solution. But DA is actually redundant because each relation is well-founded locally; if no physical fact is grounded in anything physical below it, then cross-relation loops are impossible too, since you can't have a cycle if every chain eventually stops at a local fundamental. So the argument fails at step (3): pluralists don't allow loops, they just deny global transitivity, and local well-foundedness is enough. Also, DA confuses intra-relational asymmetry with inter-relational independence, because being co-fundamental in R doesn't tell you anything about other relations.
