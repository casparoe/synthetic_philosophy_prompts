I spent thirty years in marine biology and now I'm getting into philosphy of technology in retirement. There's an analogy I keep running into: people talk about von Neumann's self-replicating machines — the ones that could spread through space long after we're gone — as if they were biological seeds or maybe autocatalytic loops, the kind of chemical sets that keep copying themselves even when the original flask is broken. The idea seems to be that replication is just what they do, like life, so technology will naturally outlive the civilization that built it. Can you audit this analogy for me? Give me the correspondence table — seed vs probe, soil vs interstellar medium, evolutionary pressure vs what? — then the places it breaks. In particular, does it smuggle in biological autonomy or goal-directedness that mechanical copies don't have? And once you strip away that baggage, does the conclusion that technology survives us still hold, or was the analogy carrying the whole argument? If there's a better analogy from engineering or chemistry that doesn't import life-metaphors, I'd like to hear it too. Also, be honest about whether I'm just being an old biologist who sees organisms everywhere, and please don't just recieve my framing without pushing back on it.
