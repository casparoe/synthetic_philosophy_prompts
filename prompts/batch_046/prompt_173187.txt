I'm preparing an interview for a philosophy podcast with a philosopher who defends a view she calls narrative transworld identity. On her account, concrete persons maintain literal identity across possible worlds not through bare substrata or pure psychological continuity, but through the persistence of a self-constituting narrative that can survive substantial variation in physical and mental properties. She acknowledges the resemblance to Kripkean essentialism and to MacIntyre's account of narrative unity, but insists that her position avoids the fragmentation of moral agency that she takes to be a fatal cost of Lewisian counterpart theory, while escaping Chisholm's paradox by restricting identity-preserving narratives to those that do not entail contradictory essential properties. I want a sequence of interview questions that go well beyond having her restate the view. In particular, I need questions that press on the mechanics of narrative continuity—how she distinguishes a genuinely continuous narrative from a merely similar counterpart-narrative in another world; whether she is committed to essentialism about the narrative's core episodes; and how she handles radical narrative interruption or revision, such as severe amnesia or deep religious conversion. I also want a question on whether her refusal to apply counterpart relations to persons undermines the standard Lewisian treatment of accidental intrinsics, and for each of these lines I want a follow-up keyed to the reply she is most likely to give—especially her probable appeal to the normative stakes, namely that moral responsibility requires literal identity rather than counterpart similarity. Please conclude with a question about what kind of conceptual or empirical finding could genuinely persuade her to abandon narrative transworld identity and adopt a counterpart-theoretic account of persons instead. The tone should be rigorous but conversational, appropriate for an audience of philosophers and advanced students.
