So I'm a retired biologist—spent thirty years in a genetics lab—and only started reading philisophy seriously after I hung up the coat, mostly because my old grad student kept sending me books. Lately I've been stuck on this well-being stuff, specifically Nozick's experience machine and these so-called objective-list theories. I was talking with my friend Dave over coffee and I tried to build an argument against objective lists, but now it feels like a rubber band that snaps back and hits me in the face. I can't see where the knot is, and Dave just shrugged and said sounds like you proved hedonism by accident.

Here is the chain I wrote on a napkin. (1) If hedonism is true, then living inside Nozick's experience machine is just as good as real life, because every inner experience is identical. (2) But nearly everyone says they'd reject the machine, so hedonism must be missing something; well-being needs real-world achievements, real relationships, actual knowledge—things with causal roots outside your head. (3) Now picture an adaptive objective-list machine: it doesn't just pump in pleasure, it also rewires your preferences and gives you false but deeply believed achievements, calibrating your desires so you genuinely value exactly those virtual outcomes. (4) If objective goods only matter when they're tied to the right mental states—valuing them, feeling satisfied—then this adaptive machine delivers the goods plus the mental match, so it should be equally good. (5) Therefore, if we still reject the adaptive machine, we have to claim that objective goods boost well-being even when you're totally unaware of them and don't care at all. (6) But then a life where you secretly cure diseases in your sleep but never know and never care is better than an equally happy life without those hidden achievements, which seems absurd. (7) So either we accept the machine and give up objective goods, or we bite the bullet on unconscious achievement, which collapses the objective list back into hedonism because mental states end up doing all the work.

If I have to pick, I'd rather keep (2) and the intuition that there's something wrong with both machines, but I don't want to pay the price of (6). Where does the slip happen? Is there a hidden equivocation between having a mental state about a good and the good contributing only via mental states? Or am I just confusing causal history with experiential value? I'd appreciate if you could point to the specific step that looks fine but isn't, and tell me what giving it up costs—especially since I'm trying not to embarrass myself at the next philosophy meetup where they actually know the literature.
