Having spent a career in the natural sciences and only recently begun to study philosophy and political economy in earnest, I have become particularly absorbed by the socialist calculation debate—above all the exchange between Ludwig von Mises and Friedrich Hayek on one side, and Oskar Lange on the other. I should like you to write an essay that treats this controversy not as a settled textbook episode, but as a live conceptual problem.

Most introductory accounts frame the dispute in narrowly computational terms: could a central board solve the simultaneous equations required for rational allocation without an actual market? I want you to set that framing aside and concentrate instead on the epistemic dimension. Specifically, examine whether Lange's proposal for iterative, trial-and-error adjustment of prices by a planning authority genuinely reproduces the distributed, tacit knowledge-function that Hayek ascribed to competitive markets, or whether it merely displaces the knowledge problem into a bureaucratic control loop afflicted by feedback latency—a structural delay in error-correction that profit-and-loss accounting in competitive markets avoids.

You should also address Mises's argument that in the absence of private ownership of capital goods there can be no genuine market prices for them, and assess whether Lange's distinction between rules governing production and rules governing distribution succeeds in answering this objection. Weigh the main arguments carefully, but do not finish with an indecisive compromise. Reach a definite conclusion—whether Lange's market-socialist model resolves the calculation problem, or whether Hayek's epistemic critique retains its force—and justify it with rigorous reasoning.

Please write your response in formal, polished British English.
