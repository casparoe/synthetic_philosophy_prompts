I have been thinking about how different institutional protocols for end-of-life decision-making implicitly take sides in the philosophy of mind, particularly regarding higher-order theories of consciousness. Consider the following contrast: in Protocol A, used in several European neurocritical-care units, a patient who shows reflexive but non-purposeful behavior may still be maintained on life support if family members report that the patient might still be experiencing things from the inside, and the clinical team is required to document specifically whether there is evidence of meta-cognitive monitoring—that is, any indication that the patient is not merely responsive but aware of being responsive—before withdrawal is approved. In Protocol B, used in comparable North American institutions, the same behavioral profile permits withdrawal once two neurologists agree that there is no behavioral marker of consciousness, with explicit instructions that staff should not speculate about hidden inner experience or ask families about the patient's possible awareness of awareness; the justification given to families is purely autonomy-based, referring to the patient's prior wishes, and the protocol explicitly forbids invoking suffering or its absence as a reason.

What philosophical commitments about consciousness does Protocol A presuppose that Protocol B quietly rules out? Specifically, does Protocol A's demand for documentation of meta-cognitive monitoring commit it to a higher-order view—perhaps something in the neighborhood of Rosenthal's—on which a mental state is conscious only when it is the object of a suitable higher-order representation? Or can a first-order representationalist or global-workspace theorist accommodate that documentation requirement as merely a pragmatic proxy for integrated information processing? Conversely, does Protocol B's prohibition on speculating about hidden inner experience presuppose that phenomenal consciousness without access or higher-order tracking is either impossible or ethically irrelevant? And if a society adopted both protocols in different hospitals without any explicit philosophical justification, what tension—if any—would arise in its broader legal treatment of moral status, given that the same jurisdiction might grant legal personhood protections to artificial systems that pass behavioral integration tests but deny them to biological patients who fail only the meta-cognitive monitoring check?
