I have just finished a long, somewhat heated argument with a close friend about Maimonides' explanation of animal sacrifice in the Guide for the Perplexed, and I am trying to determine whether my judgments hang together or whether I am merely defending inconsistent intuitions. The friend kept insisting that Maimonides offers a unified accommodation thesis—God permitted sacrifice only because ancient peoples could not have been weaned immediately from idolatrous practice—while I kept feeling that some details resist that reduction. I want to know whether there is a single principle behind my verdicts, or which ones I must surrender for coherence.

Here are the cases as they came up in our discussion, with my verdicts.

One: The prophetic texts that reject the blood of bulls and fattened beasts seem clearly to support the accommodation reading; they suggest God never desired the practice for its own sake. Two: The restriction of legitimate sacrifice to specific domesticated species—cattle, sheep, and goats—also fits accommodation, since these were precisely the animals central to the surrounding Canaanite and Mesopotamian cults the Torah was redirecting. Three: The replacement of Temple sacrifice by prayer after the destruction of the Second Temple confirms, in my view, that the institution was always dispensable scaffolding; once the Temple fell, worship continued without it, which is hard to explain if sacrifice had intrinsic necessity.

But then my verdicts turn. Four: The requirement that sacrificial animals be unblemished and of the highest quality imposes an economic and selective burden that looks nothing like a mere concession to weak human habits; if anything, it raises the stakes of the practice, which suggests some intrinsic pedagogical or symbolic function. Five: The specific prohibition against offering leaven or honey with sacrifices, contrasted with their explicit allowance in first-fruit offerings, introduces a technical distinction that does not map cleanly onto either accommodating weakness or expressing true worship; it feels like a residue that neither thesis explains well. Six: Modern kosher slaughter laws preserve certain sacrificial rules—most notably the covering of blood—outside any Temple context; if sacrifice was purely temporary scaffolding, I do not see why its procedural residues should retain independent legal weight rather than fading away entirely.

My tentative attempt at a principle runs as follows: a religious practice is best explained by accommodation when its form is borrowed from the surrounding culture and its abolition leaves the core of worship intact, but by intrinsic value when its internal rules impose costs or distinctions that have no parallel in that surrounding culture. Under this principle, cases one through three fall on the accommodation side, case four falls on the intrinsic-value side, case five is an outlier, and case six is ambiguous—perhaps a historical residue that has been re-rationalized.

This strikes me as ad hoc. So I ask: is there a more coherent principle—perhaps one that distinguishes levels of law, or temporal phases of revelation, or symbolic versus functional dimensions—that can accommodate all six verdicts without multiplying distinctions arbitrarily? If simplicity forces me to drop one verdict, which should it be? Furthermore, what does the best principle predict for a case we did not resolve: Maimonides' indication that sacrifices might be restored in a future Messianic age? Would such a restoration undermine the accommodation thesis, or can it be interpreted as the re-erection of scaffolding for a different pedagogical purpose?

Finally, apply the resulting principle to one further case I have not mentioned: the ritual of the red heifer, which is explicitly classified in the tradition as a statute whose reason exceeds human understanding. Does this ritual belong to the accommodation framework, stand outside it as an exception, or reveal that the framework itself needs revision?
