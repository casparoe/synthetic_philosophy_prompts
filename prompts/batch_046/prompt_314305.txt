Two colleagues in a practical-reason reading group debated Parfit’s hitchhiker scenario—the stranded traveler who promises to pay a rescuer in exchange for being driven to town, but who, once safely delivered, has no further need of the driver and every instrumental reason to break the promise. They agreed on the basic setup but diverged on what philosophical work it actually does.

Maya argued that the case reveals a genuine structural feature of rational agency: an agent can be rational in adopting a policy of promise-keeping ex ante, yet rational in violating that policy in a particular instance ex post. She insisted this is not resolved by simply noting a difference in temporal perspective, because even a single agent who fully remembers her earlier commitment faces a live conflict; the point, she said, is that instrumental reason can mandate dispositions that are locally self-defeating, and that is what “rational irrationality” means.

Jonas objected that Maya conflates prudential reputation with normative status. He said that once the driver is gone and no future interaction is possible, the rescued agent has no instrumental reason to pay, and calling her refusal “irrational” merely relabels the presence of a moral reason rather than demonstrating a paradox within rationality itself. He then introduced a further twist: if we extend the case to a collective agent—say, a democratic state that signs an environmental treaty for immediate aid, but whose later generations bear the compliance costs without having received the benefit—the supposed “rational irrationality” collapses into ordinary intergenerational conflict, because there is no single diachronic agent whose interests genuinely span both moments.

Maya replied that Jonas misses the normative point by reducing it to being seen: Parfit’s claim is not about reputation, but about being the kind of agent capable of securing cooperation in the first place. She then added her own twist: suppose evolutionary game theory explains promise-keeping as an evolutionarily stable strategy. Even then, she said, the individual in the moment faces a real normative tension between her current best reason and the policy that makes her cooperative; evolutionary origins explain the disposition’s persistence but do not dissolve the local conflict for the agent who now contemplates breaking her word.

Jonas countered that this proves the “conflict” is illusory. If the disposition is an adaptive heuristic, then the local “violation” is not a rational failure but a misfire of a generally sound strategy, analogous to a visual illusion. He concluded that what Maya calls rational irrationality is better understood as the occasional local cost of a globally optimal heuristic, and that importing collective agents or evolutionary explanations only obscures the basic point that, ex post, breaking the promise is fully rational.
