I'm presenting next week at my department's philosophy of mind workshop a chapter arguing that the meta-problem of consciousness—why we think there is an explanatory gap between neural processes and phenomenal experience—is not an independent explanatory challenge but a symptom of how phenomenal concepts work. Specifically, I defend a recognitional-demonstrative account: phenomenal concepts function like bare demonstratives ("this quality") that carry no descriptive or theoretical content capable of entering into explanations, which is why no physical account ever seems to "explain away" the feel of pain. The gap intuition is therefore a conceptual limitation, not a metaphysical one, and once we see that phenomenal concepts are explanatorily inert in this specific way, the meta-problem dissolves without requiring us to solve the hard problem first.

The audience is precisely this mix: two senior faculty who are naturalistic dualists and have written on Chalmers-style explanatory gaps, one eliminativist in the Dennett tradition who thinks phenomenal concepts are already theory-laden confabulations, two cognitive scientists who study introspective reporting and believe phenomenal judgments are reliable but conceptually mediated, plus several graduate students who treat the hard problem as genuinely open but are skeptical that "meta-problem" framing adds anything. Give me the objections in the order they're likely to come—starting with the eliminativist's opening salvo, then the dualists' counter, then the cognitive scientists' empirical challenge, then the grad students' confusion—each phrased as that specific person would phrase it. For each, tell me what I should concede, what I should hold firm on, how to reply within the time available, and where my reply is weakest. Include the objection I am secretly hoping nobody asks—probably the one about whether my view collapses into a form of eliminativism or whether it makes phenomenal knowledge impossible to share across subjects—and tell me how to defend it rather than hope it is missed. I do not need a bibliography; I need a prep sheet.
