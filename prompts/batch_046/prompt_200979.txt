At a rationalist meetup last month, two people I know had a sharp, good-faith exchange about whether hypocrisy cancels standing to blame. They were working through a hypothetical case rather than calling out anyone present: a prominent public intellectual who has spent years advocating aggressive carbon regulation, yet who maintains a second home and takes frequent long-haul leisure flights, issues a vigorous public denunciation of a fossil-fuel firm for lobbying against climate legislation. I listened without taking sides. Here is the argument as it unfolded.

(1) The first speaker said that hypocrisy directly undermines standing in exactly these cases. Blame is not a bare factual report that someone did wrong; it is a form of moral address that presupposes the blamer is not themselves participating in the same wrong at a comparable scale. If the blamer is equally complicit, the address becomes structurally self-exempting and therefore lacks normative authority—not merely rhetorically weak, but normatively void.

(2) The second speaker replied that this confuses standing with credibility or moral purity. Standing is a matter of being wronged or having a legitimate stake in a norm being upheld, not of being a moral exemplar. By the purity argument, only saints could blame anyone, which would paralyze collective accountability. The intellectual is still vulnerable to climate harms and still lives under the regulatory framework she advocates; her personal emissions do not erase her interest in the company’s conduct.

(3) The first speaker conceded that standing does not require sainthood, but insisted on a domain-specific condition: one retains standing to blame for wrongs in domains where one is not similarly complicit, yet loses it within domains of shared, proportionate complicity. He introduced a distinction between global moral standing and domain-restricted standing, arguing that this explains the intuition that a chain-smoker may blame a tobacco executive for deceptive marketing but not for smoking itself. Hypocrisy, on this view, does not eliminate all standing but selectively nullifies it inside the relevant domain.

(4) The second speaker countered that this domain-restriction view smuggles in an unprincipled metric of proportional complicity with no non-arbitrary boundary. She proposed that the real mechanism is not a normative deficit but a coordination failure: blame functions partly as a signal that reinforces shared norms, and hypocrisy introduces noise that allows targets to deflect and audiences to rationalize inaction. Therefore the problem is not that the hypocrite lacks standing, but that her blame is strategically self-defeating and may trigger defensive rationalization in listeners. She concluded that the first speaker mistakes a pragmatic, signal-corruption failure for a structural absence of authority.

Please adjudicate move by move: for each of the four moves, rate it from clearly mistaken to clearly right and briefly justify the rating. Then state who is right about which specific point, and identify what both parties are missing—especially whether there is a coherent middle position that preserves the normative force of blame without requiring global moral purity, and whether either speaker has adequately addressed the possibility that hypocrisy might undermine standing selectively through a mechanism other than either pure credibility loss or coordination noise.
