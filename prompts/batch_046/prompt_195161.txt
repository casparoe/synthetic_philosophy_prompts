I'm writing this after a tense departmental meeting in which I accused a senior colleague of selectively reporting data in a recent study, and he replied, "People in glass houses shouldn't throw stones," citing an old, uncorrected error in one of my own papers. The room seemed to treat the proverb as a decisive reply rather than a mere deflection, and I've been trying since then to understand exactly what logical work it could be doing in an argument about blame. Could you lay out the distinct precise principles hidden in that slogan—separating, for example, a claim about moral standing from one about epistemic authority, from one about pragmatic hypocrisy, and from one about the comparative gravity of wrongs? For each reading, say whether it actually supports dismissing my accusation, whether it applies only when blame is public or institutional, and whether it requires that my fault be of the same kind or merely that I be morally imperfect in general. I'm particularly interested in whether there is a defensible reading that connects to philosophical work on "standing to blame"—where hypocrisy specifically undermines one's entitlement to blame—without collapsing into the implausible view that only the morally perfect may ever criticize anyone, and I want to know which of these readings the people in that room were probably invoking when they let the line end the argument.
