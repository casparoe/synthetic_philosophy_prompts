If Lewis analyzes conventions as arbitrary, self-sustaining regularities that solve coordination problems—where any regularity is replaceable by a functionally equivalent alternative provided mutual expectation shifts—then combining his framework with evolutionary models of norm stabilization suggests that long-entrenched moral norms are merely path-dependent equilibria selected for stability rather than normative content. Applying this synthesis to norms governing reciprocal obligation, one might conclude that a historically unprecedented but coordination-equivalent replacement should be normatively interchangeable with the evolved prohibition against unilateral defection. Yet in practice, such replacements are resisted not as impractical disruptions but as violations of substantive normative authority. Is this resistance evidence that Lewis's arbitrariness thesis cannot extend to moral norms without collapsing the distinction between conventional coordination and genuine obligation, or does it instead reveal that we misapply the framework by treating reciprocal norms as pure coordination problems when they were never merely coordinative?
