I have recently been appointed to the long-range governance board of a cooperative housing trust, and we must draft binding fifty-year covenants that will constrain future residents who do not yet exist and cannot vote. In reading decision theory and normative philosophy, I have become convinced that legitimate collective choice should not simply aggregate our current, possibly transient or self-interested preferences, but rather a form of idealized preference—what is sometimes called coherent extrapolated volition—the set of preferences we and affected parties would hold if fully informed, free from predictable cognitive distortions, and able to weigh interests across generations. I need a reusable decision procedure my board can run in a single two-hour session without philosophical training: an ordered checklist of questions, with clear stopping rules for rejection or deferral, explicit flags for steps that are judgment calls rather than operational tests, and criteria for distinguishing correctable bias from legitimate value pluralism. The procedure must apply both to conflicts among current members and to conflicts between present stakeholders and counterfactual future ones, and it must say where it hands authority back to human deliberation because coherence-testing cannot settle the issue.

Please construct this procedure and apply it to three cases. First, whether to mandate solar-panel installation that alters the historic facade of our buildings, where current members split between climate urgency and aesthetic preservation. Second, whether to maintain an income cap that keeps units affordable but excludes middle-income families who might stabilize the community financially—a case where current residents’ revealed preferences may diverge sharply from what they would endorse under fuller information about long-term social dynamics. Third, include one case where the procedure yields a recommendation the board is likely to reject: for example, that a beloved communal garden should be converted to high-density units because the extrapolated housing needs of future residents outweigh present recreational attachments. For each case, show exactly where the procedure issues a directive, where it stalls, and what residual judgment remains. The board must sign off collectively, includes no philosophers, and has only limited time, so flag any step requiring expertise we lack, and be explicit about what the procedure cannot do—particularly whether it risks disguising substantive value commitments as technical coherence checks.

Finally, please state how confident you are that such a procedure can genuinely distinguish legitimate preference idealization from hidden value imposition, and identify the specific step—whether the coherence test across generations, the modeling of counterfactual stakeholder preferences, or the boundary between correctable bias and irreducible pluralism—where you are least sure the translation from theory to practice survives without distortion.
