I'm trying to work out whether my views on explaining perpetrator behavior in the Holocaust—specifically regarding Reserve Police Battalion 101—form a stable package, or whether I'm smuggling in incompatible motivations from different historiographical traditions. I find Browning's emphasis on peer dynamics, bureaucratic distancing, and incremental escalation convincing for the immediate mechanism of the killings, but I also think that without the specific ideological and bureaucratic framing of the Nazi state, these particular men in this particular unit would not have carried out sustained mass shootings. At the same time, I want to preserve a genuine explanatory role for individual moral character, but only in a restricted sense: I think it explains the minority who refused, not the majority who complied. More precisely, I conceive of this character factor not as a stable personality trait but as situationally activated moral residue—a residual capacity for refusal that becomes causally effective primarily when institutional pressure weakens or is absent. I reject Milgram-style obedience studies as explanatory analogs, not because authority was irrelevant, but because genocide involves sustained, ideologically embedded action over months, which lacks the discrete, time-bounded structure that makes Milgram's paradigm intelligible. I also hold that adequate explanations must satisfy what I call counterfactual robustness: they must identify a factor F such that, holding other conditions constant, the absence of F makes the atrocity significantly less likely. Finally, I think clinical psychopathy should be excluded—removing clinically psychopathic individuals from a unit would likely not change the unit's behavior, since replacements would fill the roles, so psychopathy fails counterfactual robustness—but I am open to subclinical narcissistic traits, specifically a diminished empathic identification with victims, explaining why some individuals initiated violence without explicit orders, without this counting as exculpatory pathology.

My worry is that these commitments pull in different directions. On the one hand, if moral character only operates as negative residue that activates in low-pressure contexts, then I seem to have restricted character to explaining refusal alone; on the other hand, if I allow subclinical narcissism to explain active initiation, I seem to be using character positively to explain compliance, which undermines my restriction. Meanwhile, I assign primary explanatory weight to situational factors, yet I also claim that the specific Nazi ideological framework was necessary—which makes the situation look less like an independent cause and more like a background condition that requires the framework to be active. There is also an apparent conflict between rejecting Milgram because of temporal and ideological extension and still relying on a broadly situational explanation: one might think that if sustained action undermines the Milgram analog, it should also weaken the case for peer-pressure dynamics that operate over months. I do not think that apparent conflict is real, but I am less sure about the others.

Can you audit this package? I want to know which tensions are genuine and which dissolve on inspection, ordered by severity, and for each genuine tension, what the cheapest way to restore consistency is and what it costs me. In particular, do not give me a general lecture on Browning versus Goldhagen; I want to know whether my specific combination—situational primacy plus necessary ideological framing, restricted moral residue plus permitted subclinical narcissism, counterfactual robustness applied selectively—is jointly tenable, and if not, which commitment is the least defensible outlier.
