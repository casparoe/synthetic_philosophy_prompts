I am a retired experimental physicist who, since leaving the laboratory, has taken up moral philosophy as a serious amateur. In a recent discussion of what is called tranquilism—the thesis that only the absence of craving and of suffering carries intrinsic moral value, while pleasure, joy, and flourishing carry none—I encountered the following summary, offered by a fellow member of my reading group. I should like your assessment of its fairness.

The summary holds that tranquilism asserts a strict axiological asymmetry: suffering and craving are positively bad, but pleasure is not positively good, being at most the neutral absence of an aversive state. From this it follows, the summary claims, that a universe containing only permanently anesthetized beings who lack all occurrent desire is morally perfect, and that there is no reason to prefer a world with flourishing, creative lives over one with mildly tranquil but inert ones, so long as neither contains suffering or craving. The summary further states that tranquilism distinguishes only occurrent mental states, treating dispositional traits—such as a cultivated tendency toward equanimity—as morally irrelevant except as instruments for preventing future occurrent suffering. Finally, it reports that critics object that tranquilism cannot differentiate between a genuinely liberated sage and a chemically suppressed patient, and that defenders answer by saying that the patient retains suppressed dispositional cravings, which still count negatively, whereas the sage lacks them entirely. The summary concludes that, on this view, the optimal moral policy is to prevent all future suffering even if that requires extinguishing all potential for future pleasure or consciousness itself, since pleasure adds no value to offset the risk.

Please examine this summary carefully. Indicate, point by point, which elements are accurate to the position as its defenders understand it, which involve subtle misrepresentations—particularly any conflation with negative utilitarianism or any mischaracterization of how the occurrent-dispositional distinction is drawn—and which are outright errors. Where specialists genuinely disagree, please separate the settled core of tranquilism from the contested interpretive extensions; do not present a disputed reading as if it commanded consensus. As I do not have access to external databases or libraries, please do not ask me to verify claims against specific texts or bibliographies, and avoid citing particular authors or works unless you are certain of the attribution; I am relying solely on your knowledge.
