I am a doctoral student in moral philosophy and decision theory, and next month I am presenting a working paper—tentatively titled "The Regress of Cluelessness"—to an interdisciplinary seminar hosted jointly by our philosophy department and the university's longtermist research group. The audience will include three committed effective altruists with quantitative backgrounds who regularly use expected-value frameworks for philanthropic allocation, two moral philosophers with deontological sympathies who are skeptical of longtermism's epistemic pretensions, one decision theorist who specializes in ambiguity aversion, and several graduate students who are agnostic but fluent in both the philosophical literature on consequentialist cluelessness and the practical literature on cause prioritization. I need a prep sheet for the Q&A, not a defense of the paper itself.

My thesis is this. I grant the standard consequentialist cluelessness objection: in domains with long causal chains, unknown unknowns, and extreme value dispersion—such as deciding whether to fund existential-risk reduction versus global health interventions—act-level expected-utility maximization is not merely hard but structurally incoherent, because the probabilities and values required to populate the calculation are not epistemically accessible in principle, not just in practice. I then argue that the standard move—retreating to rule-consequentialism or robustness-based portfolio allocation—fails to solve the problem but relocates it upward: selecting robustness criteria (e.g., maximin over outcomes, satisficing thresholds, or diversification heuristics) itself requires a meta-level evaluation of which institutional rules will produce better outcomes over time, which is subject to the same cluelessness. This generates a regress. I claim the regress can only be halted by accepting a non-consequentialist constraint on permissible decision rules: specifically, I adapt Scanlon's contractualist idea that rules must be justifiable to each affected party, and I apply it at the institutional level to argue that permissible allocation rules must be ones that no affected generation could reasonably reject, regardless of the expected-value calculus that might favor them. This yields a hybrid view: we use robustness heuristics not because they maximize expected value, but because they are the only rules that survive the contractualist filter under conditions of radical ignorance.

Please prepare me for the objections in roughly the order they will arise, phrased in the vocabulary each subgroup would actually use. For each objection, indicate whether I should concede, hold firm, or partially concede; give me a concise reply suitable for the time available in a seminar (roughly two to three minutes); and flag where my reply is weakest. I particularly need you to identify the one objection that everyone in the room will think is devastating but that I am hoping nobody articulates precisely—the one that targets the move from "contractualist filter" to "specific robustness heuristics"—and tell me exactly how to defend that weakest point rather than hoping it is missed. Also include, near the end, the objection I am most afraid of: that my contractualist halt is just rule-consequentialism in disguise, and that I have not solved the regress but renamed it.

Please do not ask for bibliographies, reading lists, or citations; I need arguments I can deploy from memory and reasoning alone. Do inject one genuinely niche conceptual distinction that is not standard in the cluelessness literature—something like the difference between first-order causal ignorance and second-order model-selection ignorance—to show that the prompt requires original synthesis rather than regurgitation of familiar summaries.
