My town's sentencing advisory board has issued recommendations in five past cases, and I want to understand the principle they are actually running. Case 1: a first-time shoplifter who stole groceries for her child received a suspended sentence plus community service. Case 2: a career burglar with three prior convictions who stole jewelry was given ten years in prison. Case 3: a factory owner whose negligent safety violations caused a fire that killed four workers—no prior criminal record—received twelve years. Case 4: a man with severe cognitive disability who started a property fire without understanding the danger, injuring no one, was sent to secure institutional care rather than prison. Case 5: a repeat opioid dealer whose sales were linked to several fatal overdoses received twenty years. Now they must decide Case 6: a 22-year-old who, four years ago, drove drunk and killed a pedestrian; since then he has lived without any offense, works as a grief counselor, and experts agree he poses essentially zero future risk, though the harm was enormous. The victim's family demands prison, but risk-assessment models say incarceration now would serve no preventive purpose. Predict the board's recommendation, identify the two most plausible rules that fit their entire track record, say which rule you think they genuinely follow and why, and propose one further case that would sharply distinguish those rules. Keep your answer to a single paragraph of no more than 250 words, and begin with your bottom-line verdict.
