Consider the regulatory practice of a national biotechnology ethics board that oversees gene-editing research on farmed livestock. The board approves projects that diminish nociceptive transmission—for instance, edits reducing the density of peripheral pain receptors—but routinely rejects proposals that would leave nociception intact while altering its affective valence, so that tissue damage is registered but not experienced as aversive. Its written justification states that an organism in which nociception and suffering have been decoupled falls outside the scope of welfare science, because behavioural and physiological welfare indicators presuppose a unified subjective state; without that unity, the board claims, we lose epistemic access to the animal's welfare altogether. At the same time, the board requires that all approved livestock programmes include environmental enrichment measures aimed at promoting positive affective states, measured through the same behavioural proxies it regards as unreliable in the decoupled case.

What philosophical commitments about the nature of suffering, the conditions for welfare attribution, and the epistemology of animal minds does this practice presuppose? Reconstruct the implicit theory: does it treat suffering as necessarily tied to nociception, or does it allow that suffering could exist without nociception or vice versa? Is the board's appeal to epistemic access a genuine methodological constraint, or does it mask a deeper metaphysical commitment about what kind of being can be a proper welfare subject? Examine whether the practice is internally coherent—particularly whether its reliance on behavioural proxies for positive welfare is consistent with its rejection of those proxies in the decoupled case. Finally, say what would have to change in the board's regulatory framework if one replaced its implicit view with a purely functionalist account of suffering that defines welfare solely in terms of behavioural and neural function, with no requirement of unified subjective experience, and whether such a replacement would strengthen or weaken the case for abolishing suffering through biotechnology.
