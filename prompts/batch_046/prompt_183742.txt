I recently began apprenticing in traditional bookbinding, and a discussion about craft mastery led me to an essay summarizing the philosophical distinction between knowing how and knowing that. The author writes: Ryle’s distinction proves that practical skills are entirely non-cognitive; when an expert binder executes a perfect headband stitch, she represents no steps to herself, and her hands move through pure bodily intelligence that involves no mental states whatsoever. The essay further claims that modern neuroscience confirms this separation, because procedural memory localized in the basal ganglia operates without any involvement of the prefrontal cortex, which demonstrates that skill is neurologically isolated from propositional thought. From these points the author concludes that teaching craft through verbal instruction is fundamentally misguided, since knowing how can never be translated into knowing that, and any attempt to do so mistakes the nature of expertise.

Some of this sounds like an overstatement of Ryle’s anti-intellectualism, and the neuroscience claim strikes me as suspiciously absolute, but I am not sure exactly where the summary slides from defensible simplification into error. Could you audit this characterization with care? Please separate, first, what Ryle actually argued about the so-called intellectualist legend and whether he truly denied all mental or representational involvement in skilled action; second, which parts of the summary are reasonable popularizations; third, which are subtle misrepresentations—particularly regarding Ryle’s relation to cognitive states and whether the basal-ganglia claim is accurate; and fourth, whether the conclusion about the futility of verbal instruction in craft education actually follows from any corrected version of the preceding claims.
