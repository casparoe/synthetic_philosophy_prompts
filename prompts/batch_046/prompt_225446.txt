I am a doctoral candidate in comparative constitutional law, and for my dissertation I am reconstructing the normative logic underlying a sequence of arbitration rulings on the boundary between democratic sovereignty and constitutional monarchy. The body I study—call it the Council—has issued consistent judgments in four documented cases, and I need to determine whether its logic genuinely determines a verdict in a fifth, technologically novel case, or whether the precedents underdetermine the outcome and force the adoption of a new principle.

The Council's four precedents are these. First, it upheld a statute that abolished the monarch's formal power to withhold royal assent to bills, holding that democratic authorization supersedes any reserve power that conflicts with parliamentary sovereignty. Second, when the monarch endorsed a specific party's environmental platform in an official address, the Council ruled that this violated constitutional neutrality, stating that the dignity of office does not encompass expressive autonomy when speech is made in official capacity. Third, Parliament legislated that all royal appointments to ceremonial offices must be made solely on ministerial advice, but the Council carved out a continuity exception: the monarch retains an unreviewable discretion to reject any candidate who has publicly advocated the abolition of the monarchy, on the ground that such a candidate undermines the institutional preconditions of the office itself. Fourth, the Council rejected a proposal to replace the monarch's live annual address to parliament with a pre-recorded video message drafted entirely by the prime minister's office, holding that live delivery by the monarch constitutes a minimal expressive presence necessary to maintain the symbolic separation between democratic government and state continuity.

The new case is this. A democratically elected government proposes an emergency protocol—triggerable only during declared national crises—under which an artificial-intelligence system, trained on the monarch's past speeches and approved in real time by cabinet ministers, generates and delivers the monarch's public statements. The monarch would be physically present but silent; the message would carry the monarch's formal imprimatur. The government argues that this preserves the visual symbol of continuity while ensuring that all substantive content remains under democratic control. The monarch objects that the protocol eliminates irreducible expressive agency and transforms the office into a passive avatar.

If I want to be consistent with the Council's own track record, what should the verdict be? Identify the closest analog among the four precedents, and say which principle—full democratic subordination, protected institutional preconditions, or neutrality-only constraint—best explains the pattern. Then say what each principle predicts about this AI-mediated protocol, and whether the precedents genuinely underdetermine the case. If they do, specify exactly what principle the Council would be adopting with each possible choice. Finally, propose one further hypothetical case that would best distinguish the principle you think is operating from its nearest rival.
