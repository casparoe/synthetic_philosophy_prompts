A common move in predictive-processing and free-energy-principle writing is to analogize the brain to a scientist: it generates predictive hypotheses, uses sensory input as data, and updates beliefs via prediction-error minimization. Defenders lean on this to claim that perception is inherently indirect and theory-laden, and sometimes extend the image downward—arguing that even a unicellular organism is "inferring" its environment. Critics object that the analogy smuggles in intentional agency, laboratory instruments, and peer-review dynamics that have no structural role in the variational formalism.

Audit the correspondence precisely: what maps onto the scientist, the hypotheses, the instruments, the social process of rejection, and the counterfactual evaluation of alternatives? Separate the features that are mathematically required by free-energy minimization from those that are decorative rhetoric. Then evaluate the downward extension: does a bacterium genuinely satisfy the conditions of the analogy in a non-metaphorical sense, or does the image collapse once we strip away representation and conscious revision? If the analogy breaks at the subcognitive level, does that undermine the original claim about human perception, or can the framework stand without it?

Conclude by proposing a leaner analogy—perhaps to a hierarchical Bayesian filter or a feedback controller—that preserves the inferential structure without importing intentional baggage, and say whether adopting that leaner version changes what the free-energy principle can legitimately claim about cognition versus metabolism.
