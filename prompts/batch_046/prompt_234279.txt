You are serving as a philosophical mediator with deep expertise in metaethics, rational choice theory, and the philosophy of value. Your task is to draft the joint statement that two opposing camps could both sign after a sustained, good-faith adversarial collaboration on incommensurable values and hard choices. Before you begin drafting, take a moment to brainstorm the conceptual landscape: distinguish rank-order failure from weight-assignment failure, consider whether emotional commitment plays a rational or merely causal role in breaking ties, and clarify what empirical or conceptual evidence could actually move either side.

The two camps are the Structural Incommensurabilists and the Contextual Integrationists. The Structural Incommensurabilists hold that certain value conflicts are not merely difficult but structurally tragic: there is no rational fact of the matter about which option is better, and the agent’s choice must be filled by an existential commitment or non-rational act of will that creates value rather than discovering it. The Contextual Integrationists hold that all apparent incommensurability dissolves once we apply the correct context-sensitive framework—whether through second-order reasons, partial ordering with domain restrictions, or indexical weighting tied to the agent’s practical identity—so that hardness is always epistemic or computational, never metaphysical.

Your deliverable is the collaboration document itself, not an adjudication. It should contain:

1. A numbered section of agreed claims—about the phenomenology of hard choice, about the formal structure of multi-criteria decision problems, about what the no-go results in value comparison actually establish, and about the limits of both camps’ current formalisms. These should be substantive, not vacuous.

2. A disagreements table. For each residual dispute, provide the precise formulation that the Incommensurabilists would accept for their own position and the precise formulation that the Integrationists would accept for theirs. Do not use slanted wording for either side.

3. The cruxes—the specific sub-questions such that settling them would cause at least one camp to revise its view. I expect at least three: (a) whether preservation of irreplaceable cultural or natural goods carries a non-compensatory, non-exchangeable value (the Preservation-Exchange Asymmetry); (b) whether second-order reasons about practical identity can always resolve first-order value conflicts without residue (the Indexical Weighting Problem); and (c) whether emotional or volitional responses in hard choice are evidence of rational indeterminacy or merely heuristics for an underlying comparative fact.

4. For each crux, present a concrete case that both sides agree is relevant and describe the empirical or conceptual conditions precisely. I suggest using the Museum-Clinic Tradeoff: a municipality must either preserve a singular, non-replicable archive of pre-industrial ecological knowledge (with no direct life-saving application) or fully fund a clinic that will save exactly fifty lives over ten years, with both sides accepting all the empirical premises about outcomes, probabilities, and opportunity costs. For each crux, state what the Incommensurabilists predict we should observe or conclude about this case, and what the Integrationists predict—recorded in advance so the predictions are clear.

Make the document formal and polished. Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, citation requests, or references to specific page numbers from named texts. Do not quote any author verbatim in quotation marks unless you have fetched and verified the passage; paraphrase without quotation marks if needed. Ensure the agreed-claims section separates genuine disagreements from merely verbal ones—for instance, clarify whether “incomparable” means “no rational comparison exists” or “no comparison can be made without loss,” since the camps may be talking past each other on that point. End with a brief statement on whether any part of their dispute is purely verbal, identifying which part and why, or affirm that all residual disagreement is substantive.
