You serve as senior ethics counsel and resident philosopher on a national reproductive-policy review board. Before you draft anything, take a few minutes to think step by step: identify the exact philosophical tension at stake, sketch the two strongest opposing arguments, and decide what institutional safeguards would be necessary regardless of the bottom line. Then write the board’s formal decision memo.

The board is evaluating a pilot proposal from the Institute for Developmental Optimization called “Resilience Selection Plus.” The program would offer state-subsidized preimplantation genetic testing that screens embryos not only for severe monogenic disorders but also for polygenic scores predicting two composite traits the Institute labels “cognitive resilience” and “empathic accuracy”—constructs it describes as moral-cognitive capacities that influence long-term flourishing. The Institute claims this fulfills the principle of procreative beneficence, understood as a parental reason to select the child expected to have the best life, while remaining fully non-coercive because parents may opt out and standard IVF remains available. Critics on the board argue that the program collapses therapeutic selection into liberal eugenics, risks generating a “genetic meritocracy” that erodes social solidarity, and relies on psychometric composites that lack established clinical validity or stable predictive value across diverse environments.

Your memo must recommend a concrete course of action—approve, reject, or approve with specific conditions—and include an explicit implementation timeline (for example, a conditional approval with a 24-month moratorium pending independent validation). It should engage the Institute’s beneficence argument and its non-coercion defense on their own terms rather than caricaturing them. It should also take seriously the critics’ concerns about solidarity and construct validity, explaining why those concerns do or do not defeat the proposal in this institutional context. You must include an honest account of the strongest consideration on the losing side: if you recommend approval, explain precisely what moral cost the board is accepting and how the recommended conditions mitigate it; if you recommend rejection, explain precisely what opportunity or parental liberty the board is forgoing and why that cost is acceptable. Write in a polished, formal institutional voice suitable for circulation to clinicians, legal counsel, and lay representatives. Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citations of any kind. Do not invoke obscure technical frameworks that would require external lookup; if you use specialized terms—such as “polygenic score,” “procreative beneficence,” or “liberal eugenics”—briefly explain them in context. Keep all reasoning accessible to a non-specialist decision-making body.
