In discussions of longtermist resource allocation, I keep encountering the line "the expectation swamps the cost," used to justify committing enormous present resources to outcomes that have both vanishingly small probability and astronomically large value—specifically, AI-related scenarios that might affect vast numbers of future lives. I am unsure whether this is a precise decision-theoretic principle or a rhetorical compression of several distinct claims. Could you lay out, as a menu rather than a single thesis, the precise principles this slogan might stand for? For instance: a formal claim about the linearity of expectation and unbounded utility functions; a normative claim about how rational agents ought to treat probabilities near zero; a practical claim about resource allocation under extreme uncertainty; a claim about the difference between epistemic and practical reasoning; and any other distinct reading you can identify. For each, state whether it is tautological, defensible with standard premises, false, or dependent on hidden assumptions (such as whether utility is bounded, whether probabilities are sharp, or whether aggregation across worlds is legitimate). Then say which of these readings actually supports the inference from "there is a tiny chance of enormous payoff" to "we should allocate massive resources now," and which ones are being smuggled in without argument. Finally, indicate whether the hidden premises required in AI-risk contexts differ from those required in the classic theological framing of Pascal's wager, and whether the slogan does the same work in both domains.
