In the course of drafting a philosophical scene for a novel, I observed a prolonged disagreement between two acquaintances—Celia, a cognitive scientist drawn to emergent systems, and Elias, a philosopher of mind skeptical of distributed cognition—concerning Douglas Hofstadter’s ant-colony parable, in which the figure of Aunt Hillary functions as an analog to individual neurons inside a larger mental architecture. I reproduce their exchange below, reconstructed as faithfully as memory allows, and ask that you adjudicate it with precision.

Celia opened by arguing that the colony itself can bear a mind. No single ant, she noted, possesses the colony’s overall plan—temperature regulation, foraging allocation, defense—yet these ends are pursued with flexible, adaptive coherence. The colony is an integrated information-processing system whose global dynamics are not reducible to any ant’s local behavior. To deny it mental status solely because its substrate is chitin rather than neural tissue, she added, is mere substrate prejudice.

Elias replied that the emergence of organized behavior is not the emergence of a subject. A thermostat regulates temperature adaptively, he observed, yet we do not impute consciousness to it. The colony, he insisted, lacks a singular point of view, lacks unified phenomenal experience, and lacks anything corresponding to a first-person perspective. He further argued that Aunt Hillary is a narrative fiction—a rhetorical personification projected onto an ant that is, in reality, a reflex-driven module. Calling the colony a mind, he concluded, confuses functional organization with subjective presence.

Celia then introduced a specific hypothetical: imagine the colony is physically bisected by a permeable mesh that allows chemical signaling but prevents direct physical contact between the halves. Both halves, she proposed, would continue foraging and eventually reconstruct the colony’s architecture. If we accept that split-brain patients in whom the corpus callosum has been severed can, under some descriptions, harbor two centers of consciousness within one body, then consistency demands we ask whether the bisected colony now houses two minds. Our reluctance to answer yes, she argued, reveals not a principled metaphysical boundary but a bias toward continuous bodily envelopes.

Elias countered that the analogy collapses upon inspection. Split-brain patients involve hemispheres that were once integrated into a single enduring system within one organism; the colony halves, by contrast, are assemblies of interchangeable modules—ants are replaced constantly, and no ant is a necessary part of the whole. He pressed a further point: if two separate colonies are merged and their chemical signatures blend so that they function as one, should we say that two minds have fused into one? If mental identity can be created or destroyed merely by altering pheromonal proximity, he said, then the concept of a colony-mind becomes arbitrary in exactly the way that renders it metaphysically empty.

Celia answered that brains, too, depend on chemical proximity—synaptic transmission requires physical adjacency—and that neurons are also replaced over time, though more slowly. The replaceability of ants is, she claimed, a difference in rate, not in kind. The genuine issue, she maintained, is whether there exists a global workspace or integrated information structure across the colony, not whether the parts are permanently bound to a continuous body. She accused Elias of smuggling in an essentialist intuition about the correct kind of unity—an intuition he could not defend without circular appeal to the very mammalian architecture he claimed to be avoiding.

They turned to me and asked for a referee’s judgment, but I could not provide one without betraying the ambiguity that serves my scene. Please adjudicate their exchange move by move: for each of Celia’s and Elias’s interventions, indicate where the reasoning is sound, where it overreaches or commits an error, and what relevant consideration each speaker neglects. Then state, with justification, which party is closer to being right overall, whether their disagreement is partly verbal—hinging on competing definitions of mind—and what both are missing that a more complete account of distributed cognition or phenomenal unity ought to include.
