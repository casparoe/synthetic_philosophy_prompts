I have been assembling a philosophical profile on health and diet in a broadly Nietzschean register for a discussion group, and I want an internal audit before I present it. My commitments are these. First, health is not a universal state but an expression of each organism's particular will-to-power, which means dietary prescriptions must be radically individual rather than culturally standardized. Second, digestion is not a mere mechanical process but expresses the body's primary reason; mental and spiritual conditions depend on gut health in a direct, non-metaphorical way. Third, modern industrial eating—heavy alcohol consumption, irregular meals, and rich stimulants—constitutes a form of physiological decadence that systematically weakens self-mastery, regardless of the particular body involved. Fourth, despite this, the philosopher should adopt a strict, almost ascetic regimen of simple foods, regular hours, and avoidance of stimulants in order to preserve the mental clarity required for rigorous thought. Fifth, climate and altitude are not incidental background conditions but constitutive of one's physiological type, so that the same food which strengthens an organism in one geography may weaken it in another. Sixth, fasting and dietary restriction are not denials of bodily appetite but techniques that intensify the body's own selective power, which makes ascetic practice the highest form of bodily affirmation rather than its negation. Seventh, specific alcoholic rhythms alter the tempo of thought in physiologically determinate ways—certain wines deepen and slow cognition, while beer accelerates and flattens it—so preferences among them are not aesthetic but diagnostic judgments about which mental rhythms sustain philosophical work.

These seven commitments feel coherent to me, but I suspect there are hidden tensions—perhaps between the radical individualism of the first commitment and the universal prescriptions embedded in the fourth and seventh, or between the claim that the body is primary and the claim that we must suppress its appetites for mental ends. There may also be an apparent tension between condemning modern diets universally and denying universal dietary norms, which might dissolve once the distinction between structural misalignment and specific content is made explicit. Could you go through the set and tell me which pairs are genuinely in tension, ordering them by severity? For each, state how bad the conflict is and what the cheapest revision would be—whether giving up a thesis, weakening it, or adding a qualification—and what that revision costs. Also identify at least one pair that looks problematic but actually coheres once the underlying motivations are unpacked. I am not asking for bibliographic support or a survey of secondary literature; I want an audit of this particular belief profile.
