my study group tried to apply the tragedy of the commons logic to what we called 'epistemic charity' — the willingness to interpret others charitably rather than suspiciously. here's the argument we ended up with and i can't see the exit.

(1) epistemic charity is non-excludable: if people generally interpret others charitably, even those who free-ride benefit from being understood.
(2) it's also largely non-rival: one person being charitable doesn't reduce the charity available to others.
(3) so epistemic charity is a public good.
(4) for any public good, an individual's contribution is negligable to the overall supply.
(5) therefore each rational agent should free-ride and withold charity, letting others do the work.
(6) if free-riding is rational for each person, then it's rational for everyone.
(7) but if everyone witholds charity, interpretation collapses into universal suspicion, and communication fails.
(8) communication failure is catastrophic for all, including the free-riders.
(9) so rationality requires that everyone witholds charity and also requires that not everyone witholds charity.
(10) apply the same structure to moral dilemmas: resolving a dilemma often requires collective moral attention, but if attention is a commons then rational agents must both contribute and not contribute.
(11) this seems to prove either that there are no genuine moral dilemmas (since rationality can't contradict itself) or that the commons model applies everywhere and rational choice theory is self-defeating.

i feel like there's a slide between (5) and (6), or maybe in treating moral resolution as a commons at all. which step do i reject, and what does rejecting it cost? i'd rather keep (3) and (8) if possible. please answer in british english.
