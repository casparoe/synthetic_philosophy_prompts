ok so I'm putting together my position for a discussion on whether famines are natural disasters or political crimes, and I want to see if my commitments actually hang together or if I'm just cherry-picking cases. Here's roughly where I've landed:

1. I think Sen's entitlement analysis of the 1943 Bengal famine is basically correct - food was available, but purchasing power collapsed and the British administration made things worse with boat requisitioning and price policies that backfired. So it's not a "natural disaster" in any meaningful sense.

2. I think the Irish famine (1845-52) should be classified as a political crime rather than a natural tragedy - yes, the potato blight was biological, but grain exports continued and relief was shaped by ideological commitments that prioritized market principles over lives.

3. Same for the Great Leap Forward famine (around 1959-61): the drought explanation is mostly cover, and the real drivers were inflated harvest reports, grain requisition, and suppression of information.

4. But I also want to insist that "famine" be defined purely by mortality and nutritional thresholds, not by cause - otherwise we can't do comparative work across Bengal, Ireland, and China without smuggling moral judgments into the concept itself.

5. I also think there's a real distinction between a state that deliberately withholds food and one that simply lacks the bureaucratic capacity to distribute it. Only the first counts as a "crime"; the second is closer to a natural disaster overwhelming weak institutions, not a moral failure.

6. Finally, I think there's a general cognitive bias - kind of like an evolutionary debunking thing - where people prefer to attribute mass death to nature rather than policy, and part of the point of Sen's work is to correct that bias.

My worry is that (2) and (3) call specific famines "crimes" but (5) restricts "crime" to intentional withholding, and I'm not actually sure the Irish or Chinese cases involved clear intent rather than ideoligical blindness or bureaucratic caos. Also, Sen's framework in (1) talks about entitlement failure, not necessarily malicious intent - so am I adding a stricter criterion in (5) that undermines the first three points? And does (4) clash with (1)-(3) since I'm using cause-language for the cases but demanding cause-neutrality for the concept? And is (6) even compatible with (5) - if I'm correcting a bias toward nature, but also allowing that some famines really are non-criminal capacity failures?
