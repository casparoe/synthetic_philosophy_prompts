I'm preparing material for a graduate seminar on the ontology of historical time, and I find that the standard surveys—'was there a Renaissance?'—tend to collapse several distinct disagreements into a single axis. Last week, in a discussion that wandered from Petrarch's self-fashioning to whether museum curators invent the past they display, I realised I need a diagnostic instrument rather than another literature review. Please construct a decision tree—presented as a taxonomy with labelled branches—that sorts positions on periodisation according to answers to concrete questions, not according to the names scholars call themselves. The root question should be whether large chronological divisions such as 'the Middle Ages', 'the Renaissance', and 'the Scientific Revolution' track genuine discontinuities in past social or intellectual life, or whether they are primarily retrospective constructions imposed for narrative convenience.

From the root, the first-level split should distinguish those who answer 'yes, real discontinuities' according to whether they take these breaks to be ontological—changes in institutional, economic, or technological regimes—or epistemic—shifts in what practices counted as legitimate knowledge and who was authorised to produce it. A further branch should ask whether the canonical triad is the correct partition, or whether it ought to be replaced by alternative cuts—such as an extended Middle Ages stretching well into the early modern period, or a twelfth-century intellectual revival that competes with the Italian quattrocento—while another should separate localist realists, who accept discontinuities but deny they apply uniformly across Europe, from universalists. On the constructionist side, the tree should split between conventionalist instrumentalists, who treat the labels as harmless shorthand; critical anti-periodists, who see them as Whiggish distortions that obscure continuity and serve presentist ideology; and performative constructivists, who hold that the labels are ontologically unreal but institutionally effective, reshaping archives, curricula, and funding priorities in ways that make them causally real regardless. Each leaf must carry either a named position—Structural Realism, Epistemic Epoch Realism, Classic Triad Realism, Revisionist Periodization, Localist Realism, Conventionalist Instrumentalism, Critical Anti-Periodism, Performative Constructivism, Radical Nominalism—or the tag 'unnamed', together with one sentence stating its most awkward commitment. Please flag any pair of leaves that your tree cannot genuinely separate—for example, I suspect that Classic Triad Realism and certain forms of Epistemic Epoch Realism may collapse under scrutiny—and note any unoccupied logical combinations, diagnosing briefly whether the gap stems from incoherence, unattractiveness, or mere neglect.

The instrument should work for a reader who knows the debates but has no library access, so do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citations, and do not depend on highly specialised texts that a small model would not have encountered in training. To keep the taxonomy from being a simple reproduction of a textbook outline, please inject one somewhat niche diagnostic distinction—specifically, whether period terms operate as kind-classifiers (grouping diverse events under a shared essence) or as indexicals (pointing to singular, non-repeatable configurations of practice)—and use it to split at least one branch. Write in formal British English, and keep the prompt itself to one to three paragraphs.
