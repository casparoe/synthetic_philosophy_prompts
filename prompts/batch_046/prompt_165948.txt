I have recently been appointed to an advisory role on my institution's research-integrity board, and the experience has made a long-standing philosophical puzzle suddenly personal. In our proceedings, we apply a strict norm regarding hypocrisy and standing: if a committee member has been found to violate the very research standard they are now accusing someone else of breaking—say, a senior researcher who once misrepresented data now sitting in judgment on a junior colleague for data manipulation—that member is expected to recuse themselves, on the grounds that hypocrisy undermines standing to blame. The principle is treated as almost axiomatic for individuals. Yet the board itself routinely condemns and sanctions individual researchers for practices—such as p-hacking or selective reporting—that the institution's own funding structures, promotion criteria, and publication incentives systematically encourage and even reward. The board never considers whether its own structural complicity undermines its standing to issue these condemnations. The asymmetry is treated as unremarkable.

What philosophical commitments about moral standing, blame, and collective agency does this institutional practice presuppose? Reconstruct the implicit theory: does it require a view that standing is essentially a property of individual moral agents and cannot be possessed by collective bodies? Or does it depend on a relational account—perhaps that blame requires being in a specific kind of interpersonal relationship with the wrongdoer—that institutions, lacking the relevant emotional and social capacities, simply cannot enter? Alternatively, might the practice presuppose a distinction between "personal" and "structural" hypocrisy, where only the former defeats standing? Say whether the practice is philosophically underdetermined—compatible with several rival theories of blame—or whether it strictly commits us to one view and quietly excludes others, such as a uniform relational view that applies to groups or a consequentialist view that cares only about the effects of blame rather than the blamer's purity. And tell me what an institutional practice that applied the hypocrisy-standing principle consistently in both directions would have to change: would boards have to recuse themselves from judging any violation that their own policies incentivize, or is there a coherent way to preserve institutional authority while acknowledging structural complicity? Please write your answer in American English.
