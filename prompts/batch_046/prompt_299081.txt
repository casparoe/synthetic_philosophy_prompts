After a long and somewhat heated argument with a friend about whether Daniel Dennett's taxonomy of minds—Darwinian, Skinnerian, Popperian, and Gregorian—carves cognitive architecture at its joints or merely sorts behaviors by evolutionary complexity, I have volunteered to lead a ninety-minute session for a reading group of educated non-specialists. They have read popular evolutionary biology and some behavioral science, but none have studied philosophy of mind, and they will arrive assuming that possessing a mind is a single threshold that language crosses.

I do not want you to explain the taxonomy to me. Instead, plan the session as a teaching arc. First, lay out the dependency order: what conceptual separation—specifically between selection across generations and conditioning within a lifetime—must be firmly in place before I can even introduce the Skinnerian/Darwinian distinction without collapse, and why the Popperian layer requires that the audience already understand that a creature can generate and test representations internally rather than merely tracking correlations.

Second, propose the single carrying example. I am inclined toward a navigation task in a maze whose reward rules invert after a hidden auditory cue, but specify exactly what this scenario must include and what it must exclude—particularly how to prevent the audience from interpreting the Popperian response as merely a more flexible Skinnerian one, and how to keep the Gregorian response from being read as just talking about the maze.

Third, map the misconceptions they will bring or form in sequence: that Darwinian creatures are mindless automata, that Gregorian creatures are defined solely by language possession rather than by cultural tool-use, and that Popperian cognition is just smart Skinnerian learning. For each, identify the precise point in the arc where it should be headed off—before, during, or after the example is introduced—and what corrective distinction should be made.

Fourth, design one check for understanding at the end that would reveal whether they have grasped the architectural differences rather than just memorized the four labels. It should not be a definitional quiz.

Finally, state explicitly what element of Dennett's original framing—perhaps his reliance on evolutionary just-so stories or his specific examples from ethology—should be openly left out at this level, with an acknowledgment that it has been omitted.

The audience should leave understanding that these are four different ways a system can generate adaptive behavior, not four rungs on a ladder of consciousness.
