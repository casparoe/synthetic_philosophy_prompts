Analyze the March 2018 fatal collision in Tempe, Arizona, in which an Uber self-driving Volvo XC90 struck and killed Elaine Herzberg, through the lens of responsibility-gap theory for autonomous systems. The salient, broadly established facts are these: the vehicle was in autonomous mode with a human safety driver, Rafaela Vasquez, present; Uber had disabled the factory-installed Volvo emergency-braking system; the perception module detected Herzberg but oscillated among classifications—unknown object, vehicle, bicycle—without triggering braking; Vasquez was distracted by a mobile device at the moment of impact; and subsequent investigations assigned fault to Vasquez and criticized Uber’s safety culture, while no criminal charges were filed against Uber as a corporate entity. Treat this not as an engineering postmortem but as a case study in distributed moral agency.

Using the framework of responsibility gaps—specifically the distinction between backward-looking gaps (no agent satisfies the conditions for blame despite a wrongful outcome) and forward-looking gaps (no agent is clearly assigned remedial or regulatory duties)—together with the concept of moral crumple zones, in which human operators are structurally positioned to absorb blame for failures they lack meaningful power to prevent, proceed as follows.

First, map each relevant agent—the safety driver, the vehicle’s perception and decision modules, the Uber engineers who set classification thresholds and disabled redundant braking, Uber management, and the regulatory environment—against the standard conditions for moral responsibility: adequate control, relevant knowledge, and appropriate causal connection. For each agent, state whether they meet these conditions, fail them, or meet them only in a diluted form, and identify the precise feature of the episode—architectural, organizational, or temporal—that breaks or preserves the link.

Second, determine whether the Tempe case exhibits a genuine backward-looking responsibility gap or merely an epistemic gap (we do not yet know who is responsible). Does responsibility consolidate onto Vasquez, onto the engineering team, onto the corporate entity, or onto none? How does the organizational decision to suppress the Volvo emergency-braking system alter this distribution? Does it generate a new locus of responsibility, diffuse responsibility further across the socio-technical system, or reveal that the apparent gap is actually an artifact of design choices that could have been made differently?

Third, examine Vasquez’s role as a potential moral crumple zone. She was physically present and nominally in control, yet the system architecture—including the disabled braking and the handoff protocol—severely constrained her capacity to intervene in the available time window. Does assigning blame to her depend on a fiction of meaningful human oversight? What does this imply about the ethics of maintaining a human in the loop when the loop is engineered to be unclosable at the critical moment?

Fourth, address the forward-looking dimension. Given the exposed failure modes—classification ambiguity for pedestrians with bicycles, organizational suppression of redundant safety mechanisms, and distracted monitoring—who bears the duty to redesign, and can that duty be coherently assigned within existing legal and moral frameworks? Or does the case show that forward-looking responsibility also fragments across engineers, managers, regulators, and the absence of binding standards?

Finally, evaluate what the episode does to the theory itself. Is it a confirming instance of an unavoidable gap inherent to autonomous systems, an anomaly that demonstrates gaps can be closed through organizational restructuring, or a case the theory never anticipated because it treats the system as a unitary agent rather than as an assemblage of human and technical components? Be explicit about where the theory is silent—where it offers no verdict on the distribution of blame or duty—and where it risks misdescribing the case by forcing it into a binary of human-responsible versus machine-responsible.

Maintain disciplined mapping throughout: identify the specific options available to each actor, the specific evidence they possessed, and distinguish cases where the theory genuinely applies from cases where applying it would be an imposition.
