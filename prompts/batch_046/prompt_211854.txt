Write the opening remarks—approximately four minutes when delivered—for the interdisciplinary workshop Cohorts and Culture, hosted by a university social-science division. The occasion is the opening session, which doubles as the reception for an undergraduate research symposium; the audience is split evenly between quantitative sociologists and demographers who work on age-period-cohort identification, and philosophers of social science together with family members of the student presenters. The speaker, a philosopher of science invited to frame the theme, must treat the ontology of generational cohorts—whether labels like Boomers, Millennials, and Gen Z denote real kinds, statistical artifacts, or something intermediate—as the structural backbone of the address, not as decorative reference.

The philosophical work must navigate a specific tension: on one side, cohort membership can function as an interactive kind that loops back on behavior and self-understanding; on the other, the age-period-cohort identification problem means pure cohort effects are statistically inseparable from aging and historical period, making causal claims about generations methodologically fragile. The speaker should honor the student researchers without claiming that Gen Z names a natural kind like a biological species, yet also avoid collapsing into pure nominalism that would render talk of intergenerational justice or shared historical experience meaningless. The address must comfort parents who rely on these labels to understand their children, respect the sociologists' awareness that cohort causation is hard to prove, and speak to philosophers about what kind of existence these groupings have—without offering bibliographies, citations, or reading lists, and without invoking obscure theories unknown to a well-trained generalist model. Keep the tone appropriate to a formal academic opening: welcoming, conceptually precise, and accessible to non-specialists in half the room.
