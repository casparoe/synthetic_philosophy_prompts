I am facilitating a graduate seminar on archives and power—fourteen students in a history and political theory program, ninety minutes, one session. I want a structured in-class simulation that lets participants feel how archival silences are produced structurally rather than accidentally, but I want to avoid the usual exercise of handing them a “missing” record and asking what is absent. Instead, please design an activity built around a mechanism I am calling reverse provenance: small groups must actively construct the official record of a single historical event—for instance, a 1920s waterfront strike or a post-war resettlement—yet each participant is assigned an institutional role (bureaucrat, family chronicler, corporate archivist, community scribe) with a rigid recording instrument (a standardized form, a genealogical chart, a ledger category, an oral-history protocol) that determines what can be entered. The rules should be arranged so that certain actors’ testimony literally cannot be inscribed within the available fields, producing gaps that emerge from interaction before anyone names them as silence or erasure. Include specific roles, materials, a timeline with decision points, and a consequence structure that makes the omission feel costly rather than neutral.

The debrief should map precise moments in the activity onto theoretical claims—specifically, that silence functions as a positive structuring principle rather than a mere absence, and that the authority to classify is prior to the authority to preserve—without delivering a lecture up front. I also need an honest prediction of what will go wrong on the first run: I expect participants will try to “repair” the archive retroactively by translating excluded testimony into the available categories, thereby reproducing the very epistemic violence the exercise is meant to expose, and I want guidance on how to intercept this move within the rules rather than through facilitator intervention. Finally, be explicit about which feature of real archival power—such as the multi-generational durability of institutional memory or the legal enforceability of record-keeping—the simulation necessarily fails to reproduce, so I can supply that limitation myself in the closing discussion.
