I'm preparing a seminar discussion on Quine's thesis of the indeterminacy of translation, and I'm tired of exercises that stop at the familiar gavagai ambiguity between rabbits and undetached rabbit parts. To push deeper, I'm considering a constructed case: imagine a distributed network of autonomous sensors that coordinate tasks without any natural language, emitting only structured data packets. The token zork appears consistently whenever a particular spatial relation holds among sensors, but one translation manual treats zork as naming a persistent object-tracker embedded in the network, while an alternative manual treats it as naming the transient relational state itself; both manuals predict every observable interaction with perfect accuracy, and there is no behavioral evidence to break the tie.

Write a dialogue between two philosophers—Alex and Blake—debating whether Quine's argument genuinely applies here. Alex should argue that because the system lacks human intentionality and exhibits only functional coherence, there is no deeper fact about whether zork denotes an object or a relation, and that this extends Quine's point: indeterminacy is not a quirk of ethnographic translation but a feature of any semantics fixed solely by use. Blake should counter that engineered systems allow us to appeal to design specifications and internal representational architecture to dissolve the ambiguity, and that treating this as ontological indeterminacy confuses our epistemic access with what is actually fixed in the system. The exchange must be genuinely dialectical—each speaker should address the other's specific point about functional equivalence, design intention, and whether citing internal architecture begs the question against the behaviorist constraints Quine imposes. By the end, take a committed position: either defend that Quine is vindicated even in this artificial setting and identify exactly where Blake's architectural appeal fails, or defend that Blake succeeds in blocking indeterminacy here and show why Alex's functionalist move overreaches. Do not merely present both sides as equally plausible; commit to one and justify it with reasons drawn directly from the dialogue.
