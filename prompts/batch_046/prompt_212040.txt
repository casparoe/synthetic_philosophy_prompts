Consider the paired institutional practices of classical Athenian democracy: the assembly could vote by simple majority on matters of life and death—such as the initial decree to execute every male citizen of Mytilene and sell the women and children into slavery, reversed the very next day, or the decision to launch the catastrophic Sicilian expedition—while serious trials, including that of Socrates, were decided by large panels of ordinary citizens with no professional judiciary, no fixed legal code constraining interpretation, and penalties proposed adversarially by the accuser and defendant alone. What philosophical commitments about collective judgment, moral knowledge, and democratic legitimacy does this design presuppose? Does it require the view that strategic and moral truth is immediately transparent to any gathered citizenry—a kind of synchronic epistemic democracy in which the present will of the assembled demos is self-validating—or can the same practices be sustained without any underlying epistemology? Is there an implicit theory of responsibility, perhaps that punishment is purely an expressive act of communal boundary-setting rather than a calibrated response to individual culpability, that explains why the same body could reverse a mass-death sentence within hours yet condemn a philosopher to death for corrupting youth? Reconstruct the implicit philosophical framework that unifies, or fails to unify, these practices; distinguish what they strictly presuppose from what they merely accommodate; and explain what institutional changes would be required if that framework were replaced by one that treats democratic authority as diachronically accountable and serious moral or strategic judgment as requiring stable, interpretive institutions rather than instantaneous popular will.
