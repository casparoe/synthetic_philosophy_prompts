Since Kripke introduced rigid designators and the necessary a posteriori, philosophers have divided over whether the identity of Hesperus with Phosphorus expresses a metaphysical essentialism or only a semantic necessity fixed by contingent reference-fixing practices. Essentialists treat the modality as de re and world-independent; anti-essentialists treat it as de dicto, arising from how names are anchored rather than from the nature of the planet. The literature is exhaustive, yet the core disagreement has not moved in decades. I want a diagnosis rather than a survey: is this a substantive dispute about the relation between language and reality, a verbal disagreement about what counts as necessary, or a case of philosophers of language and metaphysicians answering different questions under one heading? To sharpen the diagnosis, consider a distinction the debate largely neglects: between term-level rigidity, belonging to an individual expression, and what might be called practice-level modal anchoring, the distributed social and causal mechanisms by which a linguistic community fixes reference across counterfactual scenarios. Does the stall persist because both sides treat rigidity as an intrinsic lexical property and thereby miss that the explanatory work is done by anchoring practices? Given your diagnosis, what kind of result—formal, empirical, or conceptual—would actually count as progress?
