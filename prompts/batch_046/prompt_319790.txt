Having spent thirty-five years in experimental biophysics before turning, rather late, to philosophy, I have been struck by how often a conceptual impasse in my new reading group resembles the stalled interdisciplinary disputes I once observed in science committees. One such impasse concerns what the group calls “free-floating rationales”—the question of whether normative reasons can exist and do genuine explanatory work in the absence of any reasoner, mental representation, or intentional agent.

The dispute is concrete, not merely verbal. One faction—call them the functional naturalists—accepts without reservation the standard evolutionary dynamics and the formal apparatus of evolutionary game theory. They point to niche construction, where an organism modifies its environment in ways that subsequently select for further modifications, and they argue that the stability conditions of these feedback loops function exactly as reasons do: they explain why a trait persists, they can be correct or incorrect (maladaptive loops collapse), and they guide behaviour in a normative sense, all without being entertained by any mind. The opposing faction—the agency-anchored philosophers—agrees entirely with the biology and with the mathematics. They do not deny the causal efficacy of selection pressures. What they deny is that causation here is normatively loaded. For them, a reason is essentially a relation between a mental state with representational content and an action; to call a selective pressure a “reason” is, on their view, either a misleading metaphor or a category error that conflates causal explanation with normative justification.

For more than a year these two groups have read the same papers on evolutionary epistemology, shared the same formal models of rational-choice dynamics in non-cognitive systems, and even conceded that suffering in wild populations is a genuine bad. Yet neither side has moved an inch. The functional naturalists keep insisting that the formal structure is sufficient for rationality; the agency-anchored philosophers keep insisting that structure without representation is mere mechanism.

My working hypothesis is that this is not a hidden empirical disagreement—no one expects a new fossil or a new theorem to settle it—nor is it purely verbal, since both sides understand perfectly well what “reason” means in ordinary contexts. Rather, I suspect they are answering different questions under a single heading: one asking whether normative explanation can be decoupled from psychology in principle, the other asking whether our explanatory practices in biology ought to employ normative vocabulary at all. Alternatively, it may rest on bedrock intuitions about the indispensability of agency that neither side can argue for without begging the question.

Could you diagnose the disagreement precisely? Is it substantive, verbal, or a case of cross-purposes? Do the sides differ about facts, about what counts as data for a philosophical claim, about standards of explanation, or about starting intuitions that cannot be further defended? And, given your diagnosis, is there anything—a conceptual distinction, a formal result about representation in dynamical systems, or a change in the framing of the question—that could actually move the debate? Or should one conclude that progress here is impossible in principle?
