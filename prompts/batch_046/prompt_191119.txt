I've been arguing with friends after reading Dostoevsky's parable of the Grand Inquisitor, and I want to test whether the Inquisitor's logic can be turned against the rationalist culture that often discusses freedom and self-binding. The Inquisitor claims that human beings are psychologically constituted to reject the burden of moral freedom; given liberty, they will invariably exchange it for security, miracle, and authority. From this diagnosis, one can construct a chain that seems to dissolve not only liberal democracy but also rationalist practices of self-improvement. Here is the argument:

(1) Human beings are constituted so that bearing genuine moral freedom causes suffering; left free, they will invariably surrender autonomy for comfort and guided authority.

(2) If a being is constituted to suffer under condition C, then deliberately imposing C upon it is cruel rather than beneficent.

(3) Therefore Christ's offer of spiritual freedom was cruel, and the Church's substitution of authority for liberty was merciful.

(4) Rationalist culture extensively employs "Ulysses contracts" — precommitment devices, accountability mechanisms, and self-binding rules designed to restrict one's own future options and prevent anticipated irrational behaviour.

(5) If even agents who profess to value autonomy routinely construct artificial authorities to escape their own freedom, then they implicitly concede that freedom is not genuinely desired but universally feared, and must be neutralised.

(6) Hence the Inquisitor's diagnosis applies without exception: no one actually bears freedom; it is always experienced as a hazard to be managed or surrendered.

(7) Consequently, any philosophy — liberal, existentialist, or rationalist — that treats freedom as an intrinsic good is not merely mistaken but actively harmful, since it forces people to endure what they are constituted to reject.

(8) The only consistent ethical stance is full heteronomy: an external authority that removes choice entirely and guarantees happiness.

This proves far too much. It makes rationalist self-improvement self-refuting, collapses the distinction between autonomous self-rule and passive submission, and undermines the very reasoning that produces the conclusion. I want to preserve the intuition that precommitment can be rational without conceding that freedom is toxic to all, but I cannot locate the fracture. Is there a hidden equivocation between freedom as absence of external coercion and freedom as absence of internal compulsion? Does step (5) commit a scope or modal slide by treating second-order planning as first-order surrender? Please tell me which premise to reject, explain why that premise initially looked plausible, and price the alternatives: what must the view that treats Ulysses contracts as exercises of second-order autonomy pay, and what must the view that treats them as heteronomous surrender pay? Please answer in British English.
