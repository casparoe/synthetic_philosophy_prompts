Following my father’s recent diagnosis with a rapidly progressing neurodegenerative condition, I have been trying to clarify my own moral stance on physician-assisted dying versus the withdrawal of life-sustaining treatment. In the clinical-ethics discussions I have encountered—both in hospital ethics-committee briefings and in the informal reasoning of family members—a particular mechanical analogy is repeatedly invoked: withdrawing mechanical ventilation is described as releasing the brake on a vehicle that was already accelerating downhill, whereas administering a lethal agent is described as pressing the accelerator toward the cliff. The analogy is meant to establish that withdrawal merely permits an underlying pathological trajectory to unfold, while injection initiates a novel causal path to death; consequently, only the latter counts as killing, and the moral distinction between doing and allowing survives intact.

Can you audit this brake-and-accelerator analogy systematically? First, provide an explicit correspondence table—what plays the role of the vehicle, the downhill slope, the brake, the driver, the accelerator, and the cliff in the clinical case? Second, identify the disanalogies that bear specifically on the intended moral conclusion, particularly whether the downhill slope (the underlying disease) is genuinely independent of the brake (the ventilator), given that mechanical ventilation does not merely resist an external force but actively substitutes for failed respiratory musculature and gas exchange. Third, examine whether the analogy quietly imports a picture of causation—linear, unidirectional, and separable into discrete interventions—that misrepresents biological dying, where the boundary between natural failure and artificial support is often continuous rather than binary. Fourth, assess whether the conclusion—that withdrawal is allowing and injection is doing—survives once these disanalogies are made explicit, or whether the analogy was carrying the entire argumentative load. Finally, if the analogy collapses under this pressure, is there a more precise framework—perhaps one drawn from the philosophy of action that distinguishes causal contribution from agential initiation—that can do the work without analogical scaffolding, or does the doing/allowing distinction itself dissolve in this clinical context?
