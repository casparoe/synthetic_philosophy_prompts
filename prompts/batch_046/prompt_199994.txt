I work in software engineering with no formal background in philosophy, and I have been trying to think through Ubuntu and relational conceptions of personhood—specifically the view that personhood is constituted through communal relationships rather than possessed as an intrinsic property. I want to test an extension I have not seen discussed in the material I have encountered, which I am calling “negative relationality.”

Essay sketch: Standard Ubuntu is built on positive bonds—“I am because we are.” But in distributed systems, a node’s authority is sometimes defined by isolation protocols: identity is secured precisely through structured absences (air-gapped validation, zero-trust boundaries, deliberately missing edges). By analogy, could relational personhood include constitutive absences? That is, might one’s status as a person depend not only on being embedded in specific communal relations, but on recognized, structured non-connections—exile, orphanhood, or deliberate non-belonging functioning not as mere privation but as an active relational mode that shapes who one is? I am not suggesting absence is just the lack of presence; rather, I am asking whether the architecture of relations can include negative edges—defined non-connections that actively structure identity, much as an “outsider” position in certain kinship systems carries its own obligations and recognitions.

Please comment on the conceptual coherence of this extension. Specifically: Does treating absence as a constitutive relational mode violate Ubuntu’s core commitment, or can it be accommodated without collapsing into individualism? Is there a meaningful distinction between “having relational status through absence” and “lacking relational status altogether,” and does the distributed-systems analogy illuminate or obscure it? Finally, if negative relationality is admitted, can the view still preserve the distinction between having personhood status and the qualitative character of one’s relationships, or does it risk reducing the self to private boundaries? I do not need citations, reading lists, or references to specific texts—just an assessment of whether the argument holds together conceptually and where it is most vulnerable.
