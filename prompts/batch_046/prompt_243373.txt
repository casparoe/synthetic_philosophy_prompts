I'm working through a set of commitments for a chapter on integration and state neutrality, and I want an audit of whether they hang together before I settle on the framework. I hold the following:

(a) Liberal neutrality should be interpreted as non-endorsement rather than non-interference: the state must not treat any conception of the good as the official civic identity, but this does not require it to remain passive when minority practices are suppressed by social majorities.

(b) French laïcité correctly identifies that religious neutrality requires state agents—especially teachers—to refrain from displaying symbols that represent state authority, but it errs when it extends this prohibition to students or private citizens in public institutions; meanwhile, Anglo-American multiculturalism errs in treating religious accommodation as a collective group entitlement rather than as an individual exemption grounded in liberty of conscience.

(c) Cultural assimilation is impermissible when it targets voluntary practices of association, dress, or expression, but it is obligatory—indeed, a condition of democratic legitimacy—when it enforces what I call the civic syntax: the procedural norms and shared vocabulary necessary for deliberative equality. This is distinct from cultural grammar, the substantive values and traditions that should remain open.

(d) The harm principle should be the sole criterion for restricting religious or cultural practice in public space: if a practice causes no direct, tangible harm to others, the state lacks standing to prohibit it, regardless of whether it conflicts with dominant norms.

(e) Public recognition is a demand of justice, not a discretionary policy: when a practice is central to a minority group's identity, failure to accommodate it in public institutions constitutes a misrecognition that undermines equal civic standing.

(f) Individual autonomy requires that the state never assign citizens to cultural groups; any accommodation must be justified solely by reference to the individual's chosen commitments, not by an aim of preserving the collective culture.

(g) Dominant cultural practices—such as Sunday closing laws or Christian holiday calendars—must be treated symmetrically: they should either be abolished as non-neutral endorsements, or analogously extended to minority traditions, but they cannot be retained as neutral background conditions.

The tensions I can already see seem partial and possibly resolvable, but I want a systematic check. Which of these commitments actually pull against one another, and which pairs only appear to conflict? In particular, does the combination of (e) and (f) conceal a deeper contradiction between group-based misrecognition and individualist justification? Does (c) sit uneasily with (d), given that enforcing civic syntax may require restricting practices that cause no direct harm? Is the position in (b) genuinely consistent, or does it try to have both laïcité and multicultural recognition without paying the price of either? And is (g) compatible with (a)'s conception of neutrality, or does symmetric treatment collapse into either anti-religious suppression or plural endorsement?

Please rank the tensions by severity, indicate for each what would have to be revised or qualified, and identify whether any of the apparent conflicts dissolve on closer inspection—especially where a distinction I have drawn, such as civic syntax versus cultural grammar or state agents versus private citizens, actually resolves what looks like contradiction. I am not asking for a lecture on multicultural theory; I need an audit of my own position set, with the least costly revision clearly identified and its cost specified.
