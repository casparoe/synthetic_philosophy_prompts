exam q:
Some ethicists argue that comparing historical atrocities (e.g., ranking genocide A as 'worse' than genocide B) is either impossible because values are incommensurable, or obscene because it instrumentalizes victims. Others say that in real-world contexts—museums, school curricula, memorial funding—remembrance is a finite resource, so some form of selection or prioritization is unavoidable. This has led to talk of a 'memorial saturation threshold': the idea that societies can only maintain active, well-funded moral remembrance for a limited number of events at once.

Write an essay addressing: (1) Is there a meaningful difference between comparing atrocities along specific dimensions (scope, intent, speed) and ranking them as 'worse' tout court? (2) Does the existence of a saturation threshold justify dimensional comparison, or does it actually make ranking more dangerous? (3) Can the dignity-based objection to comparison be answered by showing that comparison does not require treating victims as fungible?

student answer:
I think comparing on dimensions is totally different from overall ranking, and it solves the problem. For instance, we can say the Holocaust had a larger absolute death toll than the Rwandan genocide, but Rwanda had a faster per-day killing rate. These are just factual comparisons, not moral judgments about which victims matter more. So the dignity objection fails: comparing numbers doesn't mean we treat people as interchangeable units.

The memorial saturation threshold shows that we have to make choices about what gets taught in schools or displayed in museums. This is like triage in medicine — we allocate limited resources where they do the most good. Because the threshold is real, refusing to rank anything is actually irresponsible; it just lets hidden biases decide what gets remembered.

Some philosophers claim that any comparison is automatically immoral because values are incommensurable. But this is wrong: incommensurability just means there's no common metric, not that we can't talk about differences at all. So we can have multidimensional comparison without a single scale.

One risk is that once we rank things for practical reasons, the public will treat the lower-ranked ones as less important morally. But this is just a communication problem, not a problem with comparison itself. We can fix it by being clear that curriculum space isn't the same as moral worth.
