I am a retired evolutionary biologist, recently turned to philosophy, with working familiarity in population genetics, adaptive dynamics, and the logic of functional explanation, but little patience for unexplained mentalistic vocabulary. I have been trying to understand the thesis of "free-floating rationales"—the claim that normative reasons can exist, guide conduct, and carry justificatory weight without being anchored in the mental states, deliberation, or reasoning processes of any agent whatsoever. Before I assess whether this is coherent, I want to see how much of it survives translation into the conceptual apparatus I actually trust.

Restate the core position of free-floating rationales, together with the principal arguments advanced in its favor and the main objections raised against it, entirely within the vocabulary of evolutionary biology: selection pressures, fitness landscapes, functional design, population-level dynamics, and adaptive explanation. For each component claim—that such reasons exist independently of reasoners, that they can direct behavior without being mentally represented, and that they possess genuine normative authority—state whether it translates cleanly into evolutionary terms, whether it requires distorting those terms (for instance, by treating descriptive fitness relations as prescriptive or by smuggling teleology into mechanistic processes), or whether it lacks any counterpart at all.

Then provide a rigorous residue report. Identify precisely which elements of the original thesis are lost, deformed, or rendered inexpressible in evolutionary vocabulary, and defend a verdict on the source of that residue: does the failure of translation reveal a substantive limitation in evolutionary concepts—their inability to accommodate genuine normativity—or does it instead expose the philosophical thesis as relying on an unacknowledged mentalistic residue that only seemed indispensable because the debate was originally framed in agent-centered terms? Work only with the conceptual structure; do not invoke named authors, texts, or bibliographic references.
