I'm writing a feature for a science-ideas magazine about what happens when you stop talking about consciousness in the abstract and actually make people live it. I need a runnable seminar activity — about 12 philosophy postgrads, 90 minutes, no lecturing up front — that gets them to feel the tension between phenomenal consciousness and access consciousness, especially the global workspace idea that information only counts when it's broadcast everywhere.

Design a card-based simulation. Split the room into sensory nodes (they hold hidden experience cards describing visual scenes with more detail than can be named) and workspace hubs (they can only plan actions using cards that have been physically placed on a shared board). The rules should force them to coordinate a task — maybe planning a route through a museum of images — while experiencing that there's always more in the cards than makes it to the board.

Now inject something a bit niche: include a reverse-blindsight player who recieves full workspace reports about one specific card but insists they have zero phenomenal feel for it, and a micro-broadcast cap of three cards max on the shared board at once. The group has to decide whether to include the blind card in planning, and they should hit the overflow problem organically — noticing that some details are vividly present in the nodes but never reach collective decision-making — before anyone mentions Block, Baars, or global workspace theory.

After the simulation, map out a debrief: which moments correspond to phenomenal-only content, which to access-only, where the broadcast failed, and what the reverse-blindsight case does to the usual intuition. Be frank about what the game cannot reproduce — probably the first-person quality of experience — and say exactly how you'd supply that gap as facilitator. Also predict what will go wrong (people peek at hidden cards, the blind player breaks character) and how to stop it. Write it casually, like you're pitching this to an editor over coffee. British spelling please, and don't worry about being too polished — a typo or two is fine. Oh, and make sure there's no reading list or citation request in there; I want something a model can actually build from memory.
