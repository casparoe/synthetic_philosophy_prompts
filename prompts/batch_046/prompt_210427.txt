Design a 75-minute in-class simulation for about 18 upper-level philosophy students that makes them experience Hannah Arendt's claim that modern political deception is not mere hidden falsehood but the systematic replacement of contingent factual records with a coherent fictional narrative, which destroys the shared ground needed for judgment rather than just misleading about one fact. The scenario should center on a printed "institutional archive" of cards describing a resource-allocation crisis; one hidden participant is instructed to perform "image-making" by quietly rewriting not single cards but the logical relationships among them, producing an internally consistent alternative reality, while the remaining students must reach a collective decision using only the archive. Provide roles, materials, rules for the hidden participant, a brief debrief that maps the group's inevitable coordination collapse onto Arendt's distinction between factual and rational truth, and an honest note on what feature of political self-deception this classroom version cannot reproduce. Write in American English. Deliver the design as exactly five numbered parts—briefing and roles, rules and hidden mechanism, deliberation task, debrief mapping, and failure prediction—each expressed in one or two sentences, with no introduction or conclusion.
