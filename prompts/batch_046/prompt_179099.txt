A fellow student summarised Plato's attitude to drunkenness for me, drawing mainly on the Laws and the Symposium, and I am unsure how far to trust the account. They said that in the Laws Plato treats wine not as a vice but as a civic diagnostic: by relaxing rational control, intoxication exposes whether a citizen's soul is made of gold, silver, or bronze, so that magistrates can observe true character without the mask of sobriety. They added that Plato therefore recommends compulsory state drinking-parties for all adult citizens, with supervisors recording behaviour for the purpose of political sorting. Turning to the Symposium, they claimed that Alcibiades' drunken intrusion demonstrates Socrates' complete immunity to alcohol, which Plato presents as proof that the philosopher's soul is literally unalterable by bodily change — the very model of the perfectly stable ruler. From these two dialogues they concluded that Plato's position is essentially an early theory of psychometric screening: alcohol functions as a state-administered test of character, and philosophy is the discipline that enables one to pass it permanently. Some of this sounds familiar — I know Plato discusses wine in the Laws and that Socrates remains sober — but I worry it imports the Republic's metallic hierarchy into contexts where it does not belong, and that it makes Plato sound like a behaviourist administrator rather than an ethical educator. Which parts of this summary are accurate to the texts, which are subtle conflations or overstatements, and does the conclusion about state-administered screening follow from the accurate parts? Please separate what a careful reader of Plato would accept, what they would correct, and whether the overall interpretation survives those corrections.
