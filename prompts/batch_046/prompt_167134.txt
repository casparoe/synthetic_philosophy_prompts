I am a doctoral student in philosophy of science, and in my graduate seminar on scientific representation we have been arguing about what philosophers call Friedman's F-twist actually commits one to. My supervisor keeps repeating the claim that the more unrealistic the assumptions, the more powerful the theory, and she deploys it whenever anyone raises empirical objections to the premises of a model. Most recently, she used it to dismiss my concern about a particular agent-based macroeconomic framework that jointly assumes (i) homogeneous rational expectations across all agents, (ii) a single global shock transmission rule with no network heterogeneity, and (iii) that behavioral deviations from rationality are purely idiosyncratic noise rather than structurally correlated. I argued that because these three assumptions are false in empirically documented ways that interact — for instance, network heterogeneity actually amplifies the very behavioral deviations the model treats as noise — the model's predictive successes may be spurious artifacts of error cancellation rather than evidence of isolating a true mechanism. She replied that this is exactly why unrealistic premises are virtuous: they strip away complicating detail to reveal the core causal structure, so worrying about empirical accuracy in the assumptions is methodologically confused.

I want to take this back to the seminar with precision rather than polemic. Could you lay out, as a menu rather than a single interpretation, the distinct precise claims that slogan could be making? I am especially interested in: (a) the trivial reading that all models involve simplification; (b) the methodological reading that predictive accuracy alone validates a model regardless of premise realism; (c) the causal-isolation reading that false premises are actively useful because they suppress confounding variables; and (d) any stronger or stranger readings that might be in play. For each, please indicate whether it is defensible, false, or conceptually confused, and — crucially — whether it actually supports dismissing my specific objection about interacting false assumptions in this agent-based setting, or whether it changes the subject. I do not need a bibliography, a history of Friedman's 1953 essay, or citations; I need the logical geography of the slogan so I can see exactly where my supervisor's dismissal lands and what it costs to reject it.
