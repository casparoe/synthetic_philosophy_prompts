I was working through Goodman’s new riddle of induction with a study group, and we ended up constructing a reductio that seems to collapse the boundary between empirical and mathematical induction. The argument runs like this:

(1) Without an independent criterion for projectible predicates, evidence that observed emeralds are green equally supports that they are grue—green before time t, blue thereafter—since both hypotheses fit the data exactly.

(2) The standard reply appeals to entrenchment: “green” is projectible because it figures in past successful inductions, while “grue” does not.

(3) But entrenchment is a feature of linguistic practice, not of the world; it records which predicates a community has used, not which ones track real properties.

(4) Now apply the same structure inside mathematics. Define “blurg” for integers: blurg(n) holds iff n is prime for n < 10^100, and composite otherwise. “Prime” is deeply entrenched in mathematical practice; “blurg” is not.

(5) By (2)–(3), our justification for projecting “prime” beyond observed cases is merely that mathematicians have used “prime” in prior proofs—not that primeness is an objective feature of numbers.

(6) So if entrenchment is the only available defense, mathematical induction is no more justified than grue-projection; both reduce to arbitrary linguistic habit.

(7) Yet mathematical induction is not arbitrary: we treat results like Fermat’s Last Theorem as discovered truths, not as conventions fixed by our vocabulary.

(8) To block (6), one needs a non-circular, non-linguistic criterion of projectibility—perhaps naturalness or simplicity—that distinguishes green and prime from grue and blurg.

(9) Any proposed criterion either applies differently across conceptual schemes (making it language-relative after all) or presupposes the very distinction between projectible and gerrymandered predicates it was meant to explain.

(10) Therefore either mathematical induction collapses into convention, or there exists an unarticulated hidden criterion that saves both domains. The first option is absurd; the second seems permanently out of reach.

Where does this slip? I want to preserve (1) and (7) if I can, and I am inclined to think the damage is in the move from (3) to (5) or in the hidden assumption driving (9). But I need to know exactly which step to reject and what rejecting it costs—whether it forces me to abandon Goodman’s anti-realism about predicates, to concede that entrenchment can do genuine epistemic work after all, or to accept that mathematics and empirical science stand on genuinely different foundations. If I have to choose, I would rather keep the objectivity of mathematics and the inadequacy of pure entrenchment, but I want to understand what I am paying to do so.
