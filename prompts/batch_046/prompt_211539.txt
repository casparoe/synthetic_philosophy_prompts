Next month I present at an interdisciplinary seminar — mostly AI alignment researchers familiar with preference-based frameworks, plus two philosophers of practical reason and one behavioral economist — a revision of how idealized volition should be modeled in alignment theory. My thesis is that the standard counterfactual-psychology framing of coherent extrapolated volition should be replaced by deliberative closure: rather than asking what agents would want after knowing more and thinking faster, we apply a coherence constraint to present practical commitments, requiring they survive recognition of framing and context-dependence without assuming a stable underlying preference ordering. The audience cares about tractability for AI systems, respects formal decision theory, but is divided on whether preference aggregation is philosophically defensible. Give me the objections in the order they will actually come: the decision-theoretic objection that I have only relabeled Bayesian updating, the behavioral-economist objection that framing effects are noise around true preferences that idealization removes, the philosopher's objection that reflective equilibrium governs belief not volition, and the alignment-engineering objection that removing counterfactual psychology eliminates the computational target an AI can approximate. For each, phrase it as that speaker would phrase it, note what I should concede upfront, what I must hold, where my reply is weakest, and flag the one objection I am hoping nobody raises — that my view collapses idealization into constructivism and leaves alignment with no normative anchor. End by identifying the weakest structural point in my own position that I should defend actively rather than hope they overlook.
