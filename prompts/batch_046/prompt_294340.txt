I've spent the last year trying to understand why public arguments over whether an atrocity should be named genocide always stall in the same place: one side insists that without proof of specific destructive intent—the core of the legal framework that traces back to Lemkin's formulation—the label does not apply; the other side argues that the scale and identity-based targeting of the violence make the legal threshold morally inadequate. Rather than taking sides, I want to propose a four-month independent study—roughly one semester—to ask whether this deadlock comes from the definition itself, or from a hidden structural mismatch between binary legal categorization and graded moral assessment.

My half-formed idea is that people in these disputes implicitly work with a gradient model of group destruction, recognizing degrees of severity, while publicly defending a binary genocide-or-not framing because the term carries unique moral and political weight. The niche angle I want to develop is a "structured ambiguity" framework that separates three registers: legal verdict (binary and intent-driven), historical assessment (graded and evidence-driven), and moral condemnation (context-sensitive and scale-driven). The precise research question is: does separating these registers clarify the politics of naming atrocities, or does it only relocate conflict to the boundaries between them?

To keep the project tractable without archival research or legal expertise, I will rely on conceptual analysis, using well-known borderline configurations—cases where extensive group-targeted violence coincides with disputed destructive intent, or where intent seems present but group boundaries are contested—rather than obscure events. Month one will map how the intent criterion functions in legal reasoning compared with how "group destruction" operates in ordinary moral cognition, isolating exactly where the binary-graded tension arises. Month two will apply the three-register framework to these configurations to determine whether the ambiguity is resolvable through conceptual clarification or structurally necessary. Month three will evaluate whether explicit register-separation changes the terms of public debate, and will specify the conditions under which the framework collapses—particularly, if legal intent and moral outcome cannot be separated without incoherence, or if the model applies generically to all atrocity categories rather than specifically to genocide.

What would count as success is a defensible conceptual model showing that some naming disputes are genuinely conceptual rather than purely strategic, together with an honest account of where it breaks down. The project fails if the distinction between registers proves unstable upon analysis, or if the framework offers no distinct purchase on genocide compared to crimes against humanity or ethnic cleansing. The deliverable is a focused conceptual essay of approximately thirty pages, designed to stand on its own reasoning; I will add references and citations separately, so the proposal should not include a bibliography or literature review. I need the architecture precise enough that I can populate it with sources later, but the argument itself must depend only on reasoning that does not require outside verification.
