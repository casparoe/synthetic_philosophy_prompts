I'm giving the opening remarks at the retirement dinner for Dr. Elena Voss, who ran the Morrow Gallery for thirty years. The gallery's whole thing was exhibitions of grief art -- not decorative sadness, but works that actually hurt to sit with. Half the room are philosophers and art historians who know the paradox of tragedy literature inside out; the other half are family and board memebers who just know she spent her career making people cry in public. I want a speech that uses the paradox to do real structural work: it needs to explain why honoring her means honoring the pain she curatated, not smoothing it over into 'it was all beautiful really.' Don't let it fall back on Aristotle catharsis -- that's the answer everyone expects and I want to sidestep it. Instead, build it around this framing I've been working with: call it 'asymetric witnessing.' The viewer suffers with the work but gets something back -- maybe clarity, maybe solidarity -- that the subject in the work doesn't get, and that asymmetry is exactly what keeps the experience ethical rather than voyeuristic. The speech has to land for both halves without losing either. There's probably a typo in here somewhere, don't worry about it.
