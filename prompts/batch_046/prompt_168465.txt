As a software engineer without formal training in philosophy, I have been trying to understand Derek Parfit's repugnant conclusion in population axiology by applying it to a concrete historical allocation problem: the 1962 decision by the selection committee at the Seattle Artificial Kidney Center, often referred to as the God committee, to ration access to a scarce new dialysis machine using criteria of social worth and projected future contribution rather than pure medical urgency. The committee was effectively selecting a subset of existing people whose lives would be extended, which raises the question of whether the moral evaluation of that choice depends on the total welfare summed across all affected lives or on the average welfare per person in the resulting population. If one adopts a totalist view, does the repugnant conclusion imply that the committee should have preferred extending the lives of a larger number of patients with modest quality-of-life gains over extending the lives of a smaller number with very high gains, provided the aggregate sum was greater? And if one adopts an averagist view, does the committee's implicit preference for patients with high social value reflect an unacknowledged commitment to maximizing average welfare, and does that avoid the repugnant conclusion only to run into the related problem that a small population of excellent lives can be outperformed by a vast population of barely worth-living lives? I am also interested in whether the committee's reasoning reveals an implicit reliance on person-affecting intuitions—that it is only wrong to withhold treatment from an identifiable, existing patient—rather than on impersonal total or average calculations, and whether that undermines the practical relevance of the repugnant conclusion for real-world triage. Please analyze the case through the lens of population axiology, specifying where the theory explains or justifies the committee's criteria, where it forces conclusions that contradict their actual reasoning, and what the mismatch reveals about the limits of applying axiological calculus to life-or-death allocation decisions.
