I'm a doctoral student in comparative criminal justice preparing to interview a scholar—let's call her Dr. K. Larsen—who has recently argued that comparing Norway's Halden prison with American mass-incarceration facilities is not merely an empirical comparison of more or less humane regimes, but a category mistake. On her view, Halden and the American supermax are not points on a single punishment-rehabilitation spectrum; they are incommensurable "penal technologies" that construct entirely different carceral subjects—the Norwegian prisoner as a temporarily excluded future citizen undergoing normalization, and the American prisoner as a permanent security risk to be warehoused through temporal collapse and sensory deprivation. I want a sequence of interview questions that forces her to defend the ontological weight of this distinction rather than retreating to familiar praise of Nordic humanism.

First, ask her to specify what makes two penal technologies "incompatible" rather than just different in degree, and include a follow-up keyed to her likely appeal to Foucauldian "techniques of the self": can that framework genuinely distinguish Halden's domestic routines and open kitchens from American vocational and meal-schedule programs without circularly defining normalization as "whatever Halden does"?

Second, press her on the phenomenology of time: Halden's architecture is said to preserve temporal continuity through domestic routine, whereas long-term American solitary relies on the collapse of lived time. Ask whether this temporal difference is constitutive of her claim about incompatible subjects, with a follow-up anticipating the reply that American prisons also have routines—counts, meals, yard schedules—that are merely brutalized versions of normalization, and asking how she distinguishes a brutalized routine from the absence of routine altogether without smuggling in a normative baseline.

Third, ask about the conceptual dependency of rehabilitation on prior punishment: if Halden is not "punishment-lite" but a different ontology altogether, does rehabilitation still presuppose a moral debt being paid through temporary liberty-deprivation? Follow up with the answer she is most likely to give—that Norwegian rehabilitation is not debt-payment but "capacity-building"—and press whether that distinction survives the fact that Halden inmates remain coercively confined, subject to involuntary transfer, and legally stripped of liberty.

Fourth, pose a question about the epistemic limits of comparative penology itself: if Halden and American prisons construct incommensurable subjects, is there any shared metric—recidivism rates, mental-health outcomes, subjective reports of suffering—that can legitimately compare them? Include a follow-up keyed to her probable claim that we can compare outcomes without comparing meanings, asking whether those very metrics do not already embed the American security-subject framework she claims to reject.

Fifth, ask what evidence could, in principle, collapse her ontological distinction: what would architecture, prison law, or first-person phenomenology have to show for Halden and an American facility to count as variants of a single institution rather than different technologies?

Please draft these as polished, spoken interview questions—not bullet points—with each follow-up embedded or clearly paired. At the very end, provide a one-sentence summary of the interview strategy on its own line beginning with "Summary:".
