My position is that Buridan's ass exposes a structural gap in rational choice, not an artificial edge case: when alternatives have genuinely symmetric value and evidential profiles, there is no rational basis for selection, and tie-breaking rules—whether salience, randomization, or temporal ordering—import non-rational determinants. I extend this to credence allocation: symmetric evidential support for competing hypotheses should yield suspended judgment rather than an arbitrary tie-break. What would genuinely count against this? I want more than the standard reply that agents do in fact choose. Which formal results in decision theory—say, about the completeness of preference orderings or representation theorems—would show that symmetric cases always conceal a hidden ordering rather than reveal its absence? Which empirical findings about human or animal choice under parity—perhaps in perceptual decision tasks or multi-attribute selection—would be fatal, and which merely awkward? Would a committed defender concede that ex-ante endorsed tie-breaking rules preserve rationality, or is that concession structurally unavailable? Rank the defeaters: from a formal demonstration that all symmetric choice is representable by a complete ordering, through reliable tie-breaking behavior without apparent irrationality, to the charge that extending this symmetry gap to belief forces an implausible skepticism. For each, say whether the view risks collapse, can absorb it by qualification, or must dismiss it outright.
