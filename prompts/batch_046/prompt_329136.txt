People arguing for reparations or historical accountability often treat a nation like a corporation that never dies -- shareholders come and go, but the legal person stays liable for old debts and crimes, so maybe moral guilt sticks across generations too. Critics say that's nonsense because countries aren't people and you can't inherit blame like a bank account. Audit the corporation analogy for collective guilt: map the parts (citizens = shareholders? the state = board? past crimes = corporate liability?), show where the mapping cracks (birth vs voluntary membership, Jaspers' split between political and moral guilt, legal vs moral responsibility), flag what corporate-law assumptions get smuggled in that don't fit nations, and say whether the analogy actually supports anything like Jaspers-style metaphysical guilt or just collapses into political liability. If it fails, what's a cleaner analogy for guilt that survives generational turnover?
