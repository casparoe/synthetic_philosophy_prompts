I recently finished a long and somewhat circular argument with a close friend about whether pure time preference is ever rational, and I am now trying to turn the disagreement into a clearer research agenda. We kept returning to climate discount rates, personal savings behavior, and the non-identity problem, but never settled what exactly is conceptually at stake. So I want to ask: within the philosophy of pure time preference and intertemporal discounting, which open conceptual questions or lines of work matter most, and in what order should they be pursued?

To avoid generic answers, I want to frame the space with two distinctions that emerged in our argument. First, there is what I will call diachronic partiality: the idea that one's present temporal location might confer special standing on present welfare, analogously to how personal or spatial proximity works in other ethical contexts. Second, there is structural discounting: the claim that the normative shape of discounting should depend on ontological differences among future people—say, between those who will actually exist and those who are merely possible—rather than being a uniform psychological rate.

With these frames in mind, please provide a defended prioritization rather than a survey. Which unresolved problems are genuinely upstream? For example, is settling whether diachronic partiality can be rationally defended more fundamental than resolving the correct social discount rate for climate economics? Which questions are tractable for philosophers using conceptual and logical tools rather than empirical economic modeling? Which ones, if answered, would actually change how we reason about long-term obligations to distant generations, and which are largely internal to welfare economics?

Please rank the directions or open questions explicitly, defend the ordering with criteria you are using—such as upstream dependency, tractability for conceptual philosophy, relevance to policy or personal deliberation, and neglect—and indicate what kind of argument or result over the next few years would cause you to reorder them. I am less interested in a reading list than in knowing where a philosopher's limited time and attention are best spent, and why.
