I'm working on an extended essay for my school's philosophy of ethics program, and I've been reading about whether hypocritical agents forfeit the standing to blame others for wrongs they themselves have committed or permitted. The literature feels stuck: one camp holds that hypocrisy undermines standing because it reveals a failure to take the relevant moral reasons seriously—almost as if the blamer hasn't earned the authority to invoke them—while the other camp insists that standing is essentially a relational entitlement tied to being wronged, so that a tax evader retains full standing to blame a murderer unless the evasion and the murder are structurally linked. Despite decades of refinement—distinctions between acknowledged and unacknowledged hypocrisy, between being "similarly situated" and merely "morally compromised," and between direct and derivative blame—neither side seems to move, and the arguments keep circling the same intuitions about fairness and authority.

I'm trying to diagnose whether this is a substantive disagreement about moral relations, a verbal dispute about what "standing" denotes, or a case of two questions being pursued under one heading—perhaps one about the ethics of blame (whether it is fair or appropriate) and another about its pragmatics (whether it actually achieves correction or recognition). To push the diagnosis further, I'd like to propose a framing I haven't seen in the texts: treating standing not as an all-or-nothing status but as a kind of non-transferable token that can be partially depleted by certain actions without being fully extinguished, which might reveal whether the two camps are actually disagreeing about distributive properties of blame rather than about entitlement per se. Could progress come from separating the ethical and pragmatic questions, from formalizing this token-like structure, or from identifying a hidden empirical claim—say, about whether being blamed by a hypocrite causes a distinctive kind of moral injury that neither philosophical argument captures? I want an honest assessment, supported by what each side actually says, of whether this is an ill-posed question, a deep but tractable conceptual confusion, or a genuinely hard moral problem that needs a different kind of result altogether.
