I am designing a seminar session on the philosophy of labor that moves beyond standard distributive debates and asks whether there is a coherent normative framework governing both the right to work and the right to leisure. For our case study, I want to read Akira Kurosawa's film Ikiru through the intersecting lenses of Max Weber's Protestant work ethic, the institutional logic of the French thirty-five-hour week, and the Japanese phenomenon of karoshi, understood not merely as fatal overwork but as the slow erasure of non-instrumental time within bureaucratic life. The film follows Kanji Watanabe, a mid-level bureaucrat who, upon receiving a terminal diagnosis, recognizes that decades of administrative routine have left him incapable of experiencing anything outside institutional obligation, and who spends his remaining months forcing through the construction of a children's playground despite institutional resistance. I want an interpretation that takes the film's emotional and narrative structure seriously: where does Watanabe's transformation illuminate Weber's claim that modern labor can become a secular vocation, and where does it expose that vocation as a form of living death? How should we understand his playground project—does it represent an exercise of leisure-time autonomy, or is it merely another mode of self-justifying productivity? And how does the wake scene, in which his colleagues briefly resolve to reform only to revert immediately to bureaucratic inertia, bear on the question of whether rights to leisure can be secured by statutory limits on working hours, or whether they require a prior transformation in how individuals value their own mortality?

Please avoid reducing the film to a mere illustration of these concepts. I am particularly interested in moments where the philosophical framework risks misreading the work: for instance, if the Protestant ethic frames Watanabe's pre-diagnosis life as disciplined virtue rather than as structural karoshi, does that distortion reveal something the philosophy misses about bureaucratic subjectivity? Conversely, what does the film's treatment of death-awareness contribute to debates about labor rights that purely institutional or theological arguments cannot capture? Give me an honest critical reading that identifies where the work resists interpretation, and conclude by saying whether Ikiru ultimately supports the view that leisure is a positive entitlement to meaningful non-instrumental activity rather than a negative freedom from labor, or whether it deliberately leaves the normative question unresolved.
