I'm a phd student in moral psych, not philosphy technically but close enough. I've been going through the situationist literature (Milgram, the Good Samaritan study, etc.) and the virtue ethics responses, and I keep noticing this specific argumentative pattern that I don't have a word for.

The pattern goes like this: a study shows people behave inconsistently across situations -- generous in one context, not in another. The virtue ethicist concedes the data but says virtue isn't a global trait, it's situation-sensitive, mediated by practical wisdom. Fair enough. But then they never specify in advance which situational differences count as legitimate excuses and which count as moral tests. Instead, they seem to calibarate the 'situational weight' retroactively: if failure is widespread (like Milgram), they call the situation 'morally overwhelming' and say virtue can't be expected; if failure is rare, they treat it as a genuine character flaw. So the boundary between 'this situation was too hard' and 'this person failed the test' gets drawn exactly where the behavioral data falls, always protecting the theory.

I know this smells like Popper's immunization or just ad hoc reasoning, but those feel too general. Is there an established term for this specific move in virtue-theoretic defenses -- this retroactive calibaration of situational excuses to match observed variance? If not, can someone name and define it precisely, with boundary cases that distinguish it from legitimate situation-sensitive virtue (like Aristotelian phronesis actually being responsive to context) and from ordinary special pleading?

Not looking for citations or a reading list, just whether the carving exists and where its edges are.
