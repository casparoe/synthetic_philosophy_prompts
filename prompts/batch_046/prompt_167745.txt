Write an essay on the ethics of dramatizing real dead people in historical fiction and biopics. Frame the controversy around a distinction I want you to treat seriously but critically: call it "narrative hospitality," the idea that a dramatist owes the deceased not merely factual accuracy (archival fidelity) but a refusal to impose upon them a dramatic arc or psychological interiority that they themselves explicitly repudiated in life—even when the historical record is too sparse to fully justify an alternative portrayal. Weigh whether this is a genuine moral constraint, an indirect duty mediated through living descendants or collective memory, or a sentimental projection onto agents who can no longer be wronged. Focus especially on the hard case where the archive is fragmentary—say, a figure known mainly through hostile institutional records and a handful of private letters—so that any coherent narrative necessarily invents motivation and affect. Does the sparsity of evidence weaken narrative hospitality, because we cannot know what we are violating, or strengthen it, because the dead are then defenseless against our impositions? Reach and defend a clear verdict: either that narrative hospitality binds dramatists independently of archival completeness, or that it dissolves under analysis into either ordinary epistemic responsibility to audiences or into duties we already owe the living. In your essay, sharply separate what is philosophically settled—such as that legal personhood ends at death and that representation shapes collective memory—from what remains genuinely contested among philosophers of art, historians, and ethicists, particularly regarding whether posthumous interests or dignitary harms are coherent notions. Do not present a contested position as the consensus view, and do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citations; the argument should stand on its reasoning alone.
