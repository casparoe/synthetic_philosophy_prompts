Notes for an essay: Are dreams experiences, or only retrospective narratives?

I am drafting an essay for my philosophy seminar on whether dreams count as experiences in the same sense as waking perceptual states. The debate I want to address is usually framed between Norman Malcolm, who argues that the idea of a concurrent dream-experience is logically incoherent because any report of a dream is necessarily produced in a waking state and therefore cannot be a direct report of a mental state occurring during sleep, and Daniel Dennett, who rejects the Cartesian theater model and holds that the dream narrative is constructed at the moment of awakening rather than replayed from a stored experiential record.

Rather than taking either side fully, I want to propose a middle position that I call retroactive phenomenal binding. On this view, REM sleep generates low-level, fragmentary cortical activations—proto-experiential fragments—that lack the unified phenomenal structure characteristic of waking consciousness. These fragments are not yet full experiences. However, upon awakening, memory-consolidation mechanisms do not invent a narrative entirely from nothing; instead, they bind these fragments into a coherent phenomenal field, creating the retrospective impression that one was undergoing a unified experience all along. So Malcolm is correct that there is no fully structured concurrent experience during sleep, but Dennett is also correct that the waking narrative is a construction—except that this construction is constrained by genuinely present, though unbound, phenomenal material, rather than being pure confabulation.

I would appreciate comments and suggestions on the following points:

1. Is my characterization of Malcolm's logical objection—that dream reports are always retrospective and thus cannot report concurrent experiences—accurate and fair?
2. Does retroactive phenomenal binding genuinely split the difference, or does it collapse into Dennett's view once we concede that the fragments are not themselves experiences?
3. Is the distinction between proto-experiential fragments and fully structured dream experiences defensible, or does it rely on an ad hoc division?
4. Is the overall argument interesting, and have I overlooked an obvious objection—for instance, that even these fragments might be post-hoc reconstructions?
