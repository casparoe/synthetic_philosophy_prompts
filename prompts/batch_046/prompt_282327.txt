Since I began working through formal epistemology and long-term decision theory, I have become personally stuck on an argument that seems to show consequentialism cannot survive contact with universal induction. Here is the chain as I have reconstructed it.

(1) Any rational agent embedded in a computable physical world ought to approximate the Solomonoff prior, because it is the only prior invariant (up to a multiplicative constant) across universal Turing machines and computable in the limit.

(2) The Solomonoff prior assigns weight 2^{-K(h)} to each computable hypothesis h, where K denotes Kolmogorov complexity.

(3) For any concrete action A available to me, there exist computable hypotheses h and h' that agree with all my current evidence but differ only in a hidden causal layer: in h, A initiates a vast, low-complexity reward cascade; in h', the same action initiates an equally simple punishment cascade. The complexity difference between h and my current best model is bounded by the size of the hidden layer's description.

(4) Because utility is unbounded and the complexity penalty is only exponential in a linear parameter, both h and h' receive prior weights that are not negligibly smaller than my current best model, and their contributions to expected utility diverge.

(5) Consequently, for every A, the Solomonoff-weighted expected utility is undefined: it contains both an infinite positive and an infinite negative term.

(6) A consequentialist must rank actions by expected utility and choose the highest.

(7) Therefore, a consequentialist using the universal prior cannot rank any actions and is paralyzed.

(8) But an ethical theory that entails universal practical paralysis is self-defeating; consequentialism should not collapse into inaction.

(9) So either consequentialism is false, or rational agents ought not to use the Solomonoff prior, or there is an implicit restriction on which hypotheses enter the expectation.

(10) Abandoning the prior costs us the only formally justified universal induction scheme; abandoning consequentialism costs us the framework I am trying to apply.

Which step should I reject? If the flaw is in (3) or (4), what principled restriction on admissible hypotheses blocks the divergence without looking ad hoc—perhaps a causal or self-locating filter internal to the prior itself? And if I keep (6) and (8), am I forced into a non-consequentialist decision theory, or is there a way to salvage expected-utility computation inside the universal prior that does not simply stipulate the problem away?
