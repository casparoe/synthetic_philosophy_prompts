I am preparing a teaching session for a graduate reading group in aesthetics and philosophy of art. The participants have recently completed a careful study of Kant's Critique of Judgment and are familiar with his distinction between determinative and reflective judgment, as well as with the idea that aesthetic experience involves a feeling of purposefulness that is not directed toward any specific purpose. They are now encountering Schopenhauer's metaphysical claim that music holds a unique status among the arts: rather than representing Platonic Ideas in the manner of painting, sculpture, or poetry, music is said to be a direct objectification of the Will itself, with melody in particular tracing the history of the Will's satisfaction and frustration across its various grades of objectification.

I want a pedagogical plan—not the lecture text itself—for guiding these students through this concept in a single ninety-minute session. The plan should address the following elements precisely.

First, the dependency order: in what sequence must the constituent ideas be introduced—starting from the group's existing Kantian framework—so that Schopenhauer's denial of representational status to music follows necessarily rather than appearing as an arbitrary assertion? Specifically, explain why the doctrine of the grades of objectification must be established before the uniqueness of music can be made intelligible, and where in that sequence Kant's notion of purposiveness should be invoked as a point of contrast.

Second, the carrying example: identify the one concrete structural comparison—between melody and harmony, or between a temporal musical form and a spatial visual form—that must bear the explanatory weight. Specify exactly what features this example needs to exhibit (for instance, temporal linearity without spatial extension, or the absence of determinable conceptual content) and what it must lack in order to show that music is not an Idea-representation but a direct copy of the Will.

Third, the misconception map: given an audience trained to treat aesthetic claims as either phenomenological descriptions or cognitive judgments, and skeptical of metaphysical entities, predict the specific misunderstandings they will generate at each stage—such as identifying Schopenhauer's Will with individual human volition, interpreting the hierarchy of the arts as a normative ranking rather than an ontological scale, or reducing the claim to a proto-psychological account of emotional expression. For each misconception, indicate the precise point in the sequence where it should be anticipated and defused.

Fourth, the check for understanding: propose a brief diagnostic question or exercise—suitable for a whiteboard discussion—that would reveal whether the participants have grasped the metaphysical specificity of Schopenhauer's claim about music, rather than merely memorizing the hierarchy of the arts.

Fifth, the omissions: state explicitly which related elements—such as Schopenhauer's pessimistic conclusion that music offers temporary redemption from the suffering inherent in willing, or the complete taxonomy of the arts—should be bracketed for this session and acknowledged as left aside.

In addition, please incorporate the following framing into the plan: treat Schopenhauer's position not as a straightforward rival to Kant's aesthetics, but as a systematic inversion of Kant's idea of purposiveness lacking a specific purpose. On this reading, music's very absence of representational purpose becomes the ground of its direct ontological significance. Explain how adopting this inversion should reshape the order of presentation so that the audience does not mistake Schopenhauer for a naive realist about musical meaning.

The deliverable is the teaching plan itself, structured according to these five elements and the framing instruction.
