Having spent three decades in nonlinear dynamics and control theory before turning to philosophy in retirement, I am struck by Hofstadter's suggestion that the first-person pronoun is not the label of a hidden executive but a strange loop: a symbol that acquires referential stability only by pointing back to the very substrate that generates it. I would like to lead a single 90-minute workshop with about twelve philosophy graduate students—none of whom have read extensively in cybernetics—so that they feel, rather than merely parse, how a self-referential symbol can stabilize without any central interpreter. The idea must emerge from the structure of the task itself, not from my lecture afterwards.

Design a runnable group activity: specify the physical or symbolic materials needed (cards, assigned roles, or a shared whiteboard protocol), a clear rule-set with a social payoff scheme that rewards coordination but requires no real money, and a staged sequence in which participants first appoint a controller for a simulated distributed network, then discover that every attempt to locate authority collapses into circular delegation, and finally are forced to adopt a collective token—designated by the group itself—that refers only to the pattern of interaction rather than to any individual node. The debrief should map each stage onto the distinction between symbol, substrate, and loop; it should also predict what will go wrong on the first run—perhaps an early cynical refusal to treat the token as genuinely referential, or a premature collapse of the coordination game into pure chaos—and advise how to recover the pedagogical thread. Finally, be frank about which aspect of the self-referential symbol—likely its dependence on a specific physical substrate or its resistance to complete formalization—this simulation necessarily omits and that I must supply in discussion. Do not include a bibliography, citation list, or requests for further reading; the design should stand alone for a facilitator without web access.
