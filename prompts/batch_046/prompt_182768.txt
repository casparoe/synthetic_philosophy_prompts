I've been trying to work out whether "structural hypocrisy"—hypocrisy not in the blamed act but in the cognitive architecture used to deliver blame—undermines standing altogether, and I keep landing on a proof that legitimate blame is impossible. Here is the chain:

(1) If A has standing to blame B for violating principle P, then A must not herself violate P, including any principle Q that P second-order entails.
(2) Every first-order moral principle P (e.g., "do not deceive for personal gain") second-order entails a principle of cognitive impartiality Q: "do not apply moral judgment through reasoning mechanisms that share the same self-serving structure that produced the original violation."
(3) All human moral judgment relies on motivated reasoning, affective tagging, and similar mechanisms that are structurally self-serving.
(4) So whenever A applies P to blame B, A necessarily applies P through mechanisms that violate Q.
(5) Therefore A always violates a principle entailed by P, which is itself a form of violating P; hence A is always structurally hypocritical and lacks standing.
(6) But this implies parents, judges, and friends can never legitimately blame anyone—an absurd result.
(7) So one of (1)–(5) must be wrong, yet each looks defensible.

Which step is the real exit, and what does giving it up cost? In particular, is there an equivocation between using a fallible mechanism and actually violating the second-order principle it entails, or a hidden scope shift in how "violation" travels from Q back to P?

Please answer in four sections:
1. "The weak link" (about 250 words): identify the step and explain the precise logical or conceptual error.
2. "Why it looked fine" (about 150 words): defend why that step seemed unavoidable.
3. "The price of rejection" (about 200 words): explain what we must surrender—e.g., can standing survive without some demand for cognitive purity?
4. "Bottom line" (about 100 words): your verdict on whether structural hypocrisy ever genuinely defeats standing.
