For a reading group on moral psych I'm trying to pin down my Smith commitments so people can poke holes in them. Here they are: (1) sympathy is basically affective contagion — we literally catch the feeling, not just imagine it. (2) The impartial spectator is a cool, dispassionate calculator — zero emotional involvement. (3) Moral approval is just the spectator's verdict; sympathy only feeds it information about others, it doesn't ground judgment itself. (4) The spectator is literally just my internalized neighbors, not some universal rational agent. (5) I think we can symapthize with people we'll never meet — future generations, distant cultures — because affective resonance works through imagination, not shared norms. (6) Once formed, the spectator is impossible to fool about my own motives; self-deception can't happen because it's built on honest social feedback. (7) Lately I'm also flirting with the idea that the spectator is basically a reinforcement-learning model updated by praise and blame.

Do these actually hang together? Go through the pairs, rank the real tensions by severity, and for each say what I'd have to drop to fix it. Also flag pairs that only look conflicting but don't — I don't want to ditch something needlessly. And if one revision cleans up the most mess, tell me what it is. Keep it tight, I don't need a lecture.
