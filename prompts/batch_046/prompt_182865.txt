I've been sitting on this neighborhood board for about two years now, not because I'm an expert on anything but just because nobody else wanted to take minutes, and I've noticed this occurence where someone shouts that "this is the will of the people" and suddenly the room acts like the debate is over. I'm not in academia, I just write up the notes after, and I'm trying to build my own little system so I don't keep writing "the people want X" in the minutes when actually it's just two angry guys and a sign-in sheet with six names. I want you to take whatever philosophy actually says about populism and the different meanings of "the will of the people" and turn it into a practical procedure I can run in about five minutes during a meeting, with quick yes-or-no type questions I can ask myself, and I need it to handle the hard cases where judgment still leaks in. Specifically, build it around the idea that these claims usually mix up three different things: a volitional claim about what people currently want, an ontological claim about who even counts as "the people" being spoken for, and a normative claim about what they ought to want regardless of what they said on the survey. Add a cross-check about whether the speaker treats disagreement as useful data or as betrayal of the group. Make it something I can apply repeatedly — I'm the secretary, the board chair has to sign off, and I need to defend my wording if someone complains. Give me two or three worked meeting scenarios: one that's mostly about preferences, one about group identity and who's being excluded, and one where the procedure tells me the speaker's claim is mostly empty or wrong but I still have to record the objection fairly in continuous prose. Also be honest about what the procedure can't do — I assume it can't tell me if the claim is actually true, only what kind of move it is. Please lead with the bottom line up front in one paragraph, then lay everything else out in continuous prose only, no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables, the way you'd write a long email to a coworker I can read on my phone between agenda items. And definately don't give me a bibliography or reading list since I can't look things up in the middle of a meeting; if you mention a thinker, just drop the name in passing without quotes or page numbers.
