Compare Derek Parfit's psychological-continuity analysis of survival—especially as illustrated by the teletransporter scenario, where psychological connectedness without physical continuity is treated as sufficient for continued existence—with a "unitary-subject" view that holds survival requires preservation of an indivisible, singular first-person perspective that cannot be shared or divided across bodies. Apply both frameworks to the following modified case: the scanner records the original's psychology but does not destroy the body, which is instead placed in reversible suspended animation; forty-eight hours later, a psychologically continuous replica is reconstructed with full awareness that the original might be revived. For each view, state whether the pre-transport person survives in the replica, in the revived original, in both, or in neither, and explain the reasoning. Then identify whether Parfit's branching-tolerant framework is forced to accept "double survival" that violates the uniqueness of the first-person point of view, and whether the unitary-subject view can accommodate the 48-hour interruption without implicitly relying on bodily continuity. Finally, isolate the structural feature—tolerance for branching versus commitment to indivisibility—that drives the divergence, and assess whether this modified case genuinely distinguishes the two positions or merely restages the standard physical-continuity dispute in temporal form.
