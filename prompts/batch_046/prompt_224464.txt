Analyze the organizational dynamics behind the Space Shuttle Challenger disaster of January 28, 1986, through the lens of the distinction between influence-seeking failure and slow value drift — a framework drawn from the study of artificial-agent misalignment, applied here without metaphor to the human institution of NASA's shuttle program. The salient facts are these: prior flights had shown O-ring erosion and blow-by; on the eve of launch, Thiokol engineers recommended against flight at low temperatures; NASA management, facing schedule pressure, requested that Thiokol reconsider and the recommendation was reversed; the shuttle launched and disintegrated 73 seconds after liftoff, killing all seven crew members; the Rogers Commission subsequently documented both the specific decision error and a longer history of risk normalization.

Using these facts, map the episode onto the two failure modes. Which elements — the restructuring of veto authority that placed management judgment over engineering recommendation, the active suppression of dissent in the pre-launch teleconference, the preservation of institutional funding and flight-rate prestige — constitute influence-seeking behavior, defined as strategic manipulation of decision boundaries to secure an organizational goal? Which elements — the incremental acceptance of O-ring damage across successive missions, the gradual redefinition of acceptable risk without explicit strategic choice, the erosion of the original flight-readiness culture — constitute slow value drift? For each mode, state what institutional remedy the framework implies: does influence-seeking call for hard structural checks on decision authority, while drift calls for periodic external audit and explicit re-baselining of safety norms? Where does the framework illuminate dynamics that standard organizational-culture theories conflate, and where does it fail because human institutions lack the unified utility function and strategic coherence that make influence-seeking meaningful for an individual agent? Finally, assess what the case reveals about the portability of the distinction: does Challenger confirm that collective human actors can be decomposed into these failure modes, or does it show that applying the framework to institutions depends on a false analogy between organizational dynamics and agentic reasoning?
