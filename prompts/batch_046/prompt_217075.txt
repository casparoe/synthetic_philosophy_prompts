I'm designing the final examination for an upper-level undergraduate seminar in comparative political philosophy on the relation between person and community in East Asian and Western thought. I want a single essay question that cannot be answered by reproducing the standard individualism-versus-collectivism narrative—namely, that Western liberalism presupposes an ontologically prior, atomistic self while East Asian traditions treat the self as constituted by social relations. To prevent mere summary, build the question around the distinction between constitutive relationality, according to which specific social relations literally form personal identity, and instrumental interdependence, according to which ontologically prior individuals depend on others only for goods, recognition, and coordination. The question should ask students to evaluate the following claim: Confucian role ethics requires constitutive relationality, whereas liberal relational-autonomy theories can accommodate thick interdependence only by collapsing into instrumental interdependence. To ground the evaluation, present a specific case in which an adult child refuses to care for an aging parent, justifying the refusal both by appeal to self-authorship and by arguing that the parent never properly fulfilled the parental role, so the normative force of the filial obligation is void. Students must determine whether either framework—Confucian role ethics or liberal autonomy—can deliver a consistent verdict without tacitly importing premises from the other. Ask for a 75-minute essay of approximately 1,000 words. Provide the exam question, a model answer of about 900 words, and a marking scheme structured around five must-hit analytical moves (total 25 marks). For each move, specify the marks awarded, what distinguishes an A-level execution from a B-level execution, and the one typical error that costs points. Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citation prompts; the assessment should be answerable entirely from conceptual reasoning.
