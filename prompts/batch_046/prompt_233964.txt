I've come to think that the only variety of free will genuinely worth wanting is a refined hierarchical view—call it reflexive coherence—according to which an agent is free just when her first-order desires align with second-order volitions that are neither externally manipulated nor self-deceptively adopted, and where the whole evaluative structure remains stable across relevant counterfactual perturbations and is transparent to the agent herself. This goes beyond Frankfurt's bare hierarchical model by adding Watson's demand that the volitions be genuinely evaluative rather than merely appetitive, and it adds a transparency condition that rules out cases where an agent's self-interpretation is systematically distorted but functionally intact. I want to know what would genuinely count against this position—not merely the standard regress or manipulation objections, but which empirical findings, which carefully constructed thought experiments, and which results in adjacent philosophy of mind or moral psychology it risks being falsified by. For each potential defeater, I want to know whether a committed defender of reflexive coherence would concede it as damaging or dismiss it as question-begging or misdirected, and whether the view has insulated itself through qualifications that make it empirically empty or conceptually tautologous. Rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, and say whether you think the view is well insulated and whether that insulation is a virtue or a vice.
