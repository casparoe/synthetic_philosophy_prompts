I have been following the illusionist–realist dispute about phenomenal consciousness since the early 2010s, particularly the exchanges surrounding the recent monograph-length defence of illusionism and the responses from the anti-illusionist camp. Despite what seems like a direct clash—illusionists claim that the what-it-is-likeness of experience is a kind of introspective misrepresentation, realists insist that there is an undeniable qualitative datum—the debate has not advanced in any recognisable way. Each side treats the other’s core move as either a denial of the obvious or a failure to understand introspection.

I want a diagnosis rather than a verdict. Is this genuinely a substantive disagreement about the ontology of conscious experience, or is it a case where the two sides are answering different questions—one about the causal-functional architecture of self-report, the other about the intrinsic properties revealed in first-person access—under the single heading of phenomenal consciousness? Or is there a hidden empirical commitment doing the work: for instance, whether introspective mechanisms are sufficiently transparent to deliver veridical reports of qualitative properties, or whether all introspective access is necessarily theory-laden in a way that makes the illusionist reinterpretation unavoidable?

Please analyse what each side actually says about introspection and about analogies such as the refrigerator light, and tell me whether the impasse is best understood as bedrock intuition, verbal disagreement over the term seems, or a masked disagreement about the epistemic authority of first-person methods. Given your diagnosis, what would count as progress: a formal model of introspective error, a neuroscientific result about the absence of qualitative correlates, or a conceptual clarification that dissolves the problem? And if none of these are available, should we treat the debate as permanently stalled?
