I have recently begun directing a small civic theater initiative in my town, which has made Rousseau’s critique of spectacle unexpectedly personal. In an essay I read last month—written, I believe, by a historian of republican thought—the author summarized Rousseau’s Letter to d’Alembert in roughly these terms. Rousseau’s central thesis, the author said, is that theatrical representation is intrinsically corrupting because it replaces authentic moral feeling with artificial, vicarious emotion, and this corruption applies to every society without exception. The essay claimed that Rousseau opposed a Geneva theater not because Geneva was a small republic especially vulnerable to luxury, but because any community that prizes virtue must reject spectacle altogether. It added that Rousseau singled out women as naturally more impressionable and therefore uniquely endangered by theatrical vanity, and that his only proposed remedy was direct state censorship of dramatic performances. Finally, the author concluded that Rousseau’s view is therefore a straightforward moral panic about entertainment, plainly inconsistent with his own enthusiasm for public civic festivals and for music, and that it can be set aside once we recognize that modern theater is essentially harmless leisure.

Something about this summary strikes me as too tidy—particularly the universalization of the corruption claim and the framing of Rousseau’s gender argument—though I am not certain where the distortion lies. Could you audit this characterization? Please take a definite position: separate what is an accurate rendering of the Letter, what is a common simplification, what is a subtle misrepresentation, and whether the conclusion that Rousseau’s position collapses into moral panic survives correction. In particular, address whether his objection is genuinely scale-sensitive, whether his concern about women is best read as biological vulnerability or as a structural point about republican domestic life, and whether his proposed alternative—civic festivals rather than representational theater—undermines the charge of simple anti-artism. Do not lay out every possible interpretation; say what the text actually supports and what it does not, and identify the specific conceptual errors that allow the essayist to dismiss the view so quickly.
