I am preparing the interview guide for a colloquium podcast episode with a moral philosopher who defends G. E. M. Anscombe's central argument against the atomic bombing of Hiroshima and Nagasaki—specifically, her claim that deliberately killing innocent non-combatants is always murder, regardless of consequentialist gains—while extending that framework to a problem she never addressed: autonomous weapons systems that authorize strikes through predictive risk models, in which civilian casualties are not individually targeted but are distributed probabilistically across a population. I want a sequence of eight to ten questions that go well beyond asking her to restate the view.

Please include at least one question that forces her to articulate the precise distinction, in Anscombe's terms, between killing civilians as a means to end a war and killing them as a foreseen but unintended side-effect, with a follow-up keyed to her likely appeal to the doctrine of double effect. Include at least one question that presses her on whether Anscombe's view collapses into absolute pacifism or can still license conventional strategic bombing that risks but does not intend civilian death, with a follow-up that anticipates her attempt to draw that line. Include at least two questions on the modern extension: one on whether a statistical risk-pool—where an algorithm assigns a percentage probability of civilian harm rather than selecting specific victims—counts as deliberately killing innocents in the morally relevant sense, and one on whether the presence of a human authorization loop changes the moral analysis; for each, include a follow-up keyed to the answer she is most likely to give, such as that distributed risk is still intended as part of the strike calculus, or that human oversight merely diffuses responsibility without altering intent. Add one question that asks how she responds to the objection that Anscombe's framework ignores the structural coercion of total war—namely, that refusing to bomb left the aggressor's war machine intact and thereby prolonged suffering—followed by a follow-up that asks whether her reply relies on a controversial deontological constraint that she has not independently defended. Conclude with a question that asks what specific empirical finding, historical counter-example, or conceptual revision could genuinely weaken her commitment to the Anscombian prohibition, rather than merely prompting her to refine its application.

Keep the tone appropriate for a philosophy colloquium: precise, adversarial but respectful, and free of generic biographical or career-oriented questions.
