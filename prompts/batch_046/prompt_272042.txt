In a reading group on the opening books of Plato's Laws, where symposia and civic education are discussed, a fellow student keeps invoking the line that "wine strips away the artificial order of law and custom to show the soul as nature made it"—picked up, I suspect, from a secondary gloss—to dismiss any question about whether Plato takes intoxicated conduct as morally revealing or only as temporary distortion of an otherwise rational agent. Before I respond, I want the slogan dissected with real precision. Lay out, as a menu rather than a single interpretation, the distinct theses that sentence could be expressing, ordered from the trivial to the radical: (a) the pharmacological claim that alcohol lowers inhibitions; (b) the psychological claim that the rational part of the soul is an acquired overlay and that its suspension reveals a "true" appetitive or spirited core; (c) the metaphysical claim about physis versus nomos, that law-imposed character is inauthentic compared to one's natural state; (d) the ethical claim that actions performed under voluntary self-impairment are not proper bases for moral evaluation; and (e) the political-legislative claim that symposia should be used as controlled diagnostic tests precisely because they reveal what education has or has not achieved. For each reading, say whether it is a platitude, a defensible thesis within Platonic psychology, or actually false, and flag which pairs are in tension despite often being run together. Then tell me specifically: which of these readings is actually doing the argumentative work when someone uses the slogan to end a discussion about whether drunken actions reveal moral character? And which readings, if any, genuinely cohere with Plato's own proposal to regulate symposia as educational institutions rather than to prohibit them? I am not looking for citations, bibliographies, or references to modern commentators—just the internal logic of the slogan mapped onto the arguments of the Laws.
