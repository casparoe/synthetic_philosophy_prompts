I was recently evaluated and received a formal psychiatric diagnosis as an adult — a category that did not exist in its present form when I was a child — and since then I have been reading Ian Hacking’s work on how classifications can create the very people they describe, particularly his analysis of looping effects among interactive kinds. In clinical and philosophical discussions, a common analogy is that psychiatric classification functions like cartography: the DSM or ICD is a map, mental disorders are territories that exist independently of the map, and the clinician’s job is to match the patient to the correct region without altering the landscape. Some defenders of this analogy use it to argue that expanding diagnostic criteria merely improves resolution — like switching from a coarse topographical map to a satellite image — and that concerns about overdiagnosis confuse better mapping with the invention of new terrain.

Audit this analogy carefully. For the mapping to hold, what exactly corresponds to the cartographer, the survey instruments, the territory, and the map itself? Where does the analogy break down once we take looping effects seriously — specifically, that being classified can change self-understanding and behavior in ways that feed back into the classification criteria? Does the cartographic framework smuggle in an assumption that the territory of mental suffering is value-neutral and observer-independent, and if so, how does that affect the analogy’s ability to account for diagnostic migration — say, the way that broadening criteria for a disorder can produce new symptomatic presentations that then appear to validate the broader criteria? Would a different analogy — perhaps classification as a protocol for social coordination, like traffic signage that shapes the very driving patterns it regulates — do better justice to the interactive nature of psychiatric kinds without collapsing into the claim that all mental suffering is merely socially constructed? And ultimately, can the cartography analogy support the conclusion that psychiatric diagnosis is fundamentally descriptive rather than productive, once these disanalogies are fully on the table?
