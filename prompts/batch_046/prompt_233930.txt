The dialogue between Achilles and the Tortoise that Lewis Carroll published in Mind has been used for well over a century to press a problem about the justification of deductive inference: if every application of a logical rule must itself be warranted by a premise stating that rule, then any valid argument can be expanded into an infinite regress of premises, and deduction never actually gets off the ground. Despite extensive discussion, the debate seems to me to have reached a peculiar stasis. One camp treats the paradox as exposing a category mistake—logical rules are not premises to be believed but norms governing inference—while another camp insists that this response misses the point, because the tortoise is asking not whether we believe the rule but what entitles us to apply it, an entitlement that cannot be discharged by further premises alone. A third group suggests the whole dispute is mislocated: Carroll's scenario is really about the psychology of acceptance, not about logical justification at all.

My working hypothesis is that the stall is not primarily about logic but about an unstated disagreement over what counts as justification—specifically, whether justification must be propositional and discursively articulable, or whether it can include non-propositional competences, procedural entitlements, or practical capacities. But I am unsure whether this is correct, or whether the sides are instead answering different questions—one about epistemic warrant for an agent, one about the normative structure of a logical system—under the single heading of "justifying deduction."

I want a diagnosis rather than a survey of positions. Are the disputants separated by a verbal ambiguity in "rule," "premise," or "justification"; by a substantive disagreement about the nature of logical knowledge; by different standards for what would resolve the regress; or by the fact that they are pursuing distinct projects (e.g., descriptive psychology versus normative epistemology) that share only Carroll's example? If the disagreement is substantive, is there a hidden formal or empirical question—perhaps about the learnability of logical rules, or about whether formal systems can be self-justifying—that could break the deadlock? If it is largely verbal or project-divergent, what conceptual distinction or reframing of the question would actually advance things, and what should we stop expecting the debate to deliver? Please engage concretely with what each side actually says about the tortoise's demand, and be honest about whether anything—an argument, a distinction, or a change of subject—should be expected to end it.
