Here is an argument I drafted for a seminar on distributive ethics, and I find it suspicious because its conclusion seems to be a reductio that consumes more than I intend. (1) If an agent can prevent a catastrophic harm to another at no comparable cost to herself, there is a pro tanto duty to do so; this rescue principle applies regardless of whether the victim is near or distant. (2) National borders are morally arbitrary in the same way racial or ethnic categories are: they distribute life chances by accident of birth, not by any feature that could ground a fundamental moral distinction. (3) Therefore the rescue principle applies with undiminished force between citizens of wealthy states and citizens of poor ones; distance and citizenship do not weaken the duty. (4) The principle should scale from individuals to institutional actors: a wealthy state that can restructure fiscal priorities to fund basic health infrastructure elsewhere, without collapsing its own economy, stands in the same relation to foreign citizens as an individual donor does to a distant victim, only magnified. (5) If so, then not only must individuals give until marginal sacrifice equals marginal aid, but states must redirect domestic spending—subsidies, defense budgets, even internal welfare—until the marginal domestic loss equals the marginal foreign gain. (6) Taken literally, this implies that a government should defund its own police, education, and social insurance before it has satisfied every preventable harm abroad, since there will always be a malaria net or vaccine that saves a life for less than the cost of maintaining domestic public order. (7) That conclusion is implausible: no credible theory of state legitimacy permits a government to abandon its own citizens' basic security in order to maximize global welfare. (8) So either (2) is false—borders do carry moral weight—or (1) is false—the rescue principle does not extend across institutional scales. Yet I want to preserve both (1) and (2). Where exactly does the slide occur? Is it in the aggregation from individual to state duty in step (4), in the marginal-cost calculus of step (5), or in treating state obligations as summed individual obligations? And if the flaw lies in (4) or (5), does that salvage only the institutional conclusion, or does it also weaken the individual-level case for substantial foreign aid? Answer in American English.
