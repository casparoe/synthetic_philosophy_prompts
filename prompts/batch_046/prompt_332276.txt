I'm drafting a fellowship application for a small philosophy-of-science grant, and I want the proposal to sit in a slightly awkward interdisciplinary space—between the history of epistemology and the philosophy of science—without collapsing into either a standard intellectual biography or a generic discussion of evidence standards. The domain is Sidgwick's involvement with psychical research: he was president of the Society for Psychical Research, wrote on telepathy and apparitions, and yet in The Methods of Ethics he insists on extraordinarily rigorous criteria for accepting normative and empirical claims alike. Most existing treatments keep these two sides in separate boxes, treating his psychical work as a biographical curiosity and his ethics as a purely conceptual system.

The research question I want funded is this: did Sidgwick apply structurally different evidential thresholds to anomalous empirical claims (telepathy, survival evidence) than to normative or common-sense claims, and if so, was the asymmetry driven by pragmatic considerations—roughly, what we now call pragmatic encroachment—rather than by a principled domain distinction? More precisely, I want to test whether his cautious openness to psychical phenomena can be reconstructed as consistent with his ethical intuitionism, or whether it reveals an implicit, unstated rule that high-stakes or worldview-disruptive claims face a different burden of proof.

Please write a one-page proposal that contains the following sections in this order, with approximate lengths: (1) The Precise Question (about 120 words), stating the problem and why it is not settled by treating Sidgwick's ethics and psychical writings in isolation; (2) Why Existing Answers Leave It Open (about 150 words), explaining the gap—namely, that scholars either defend Sidgwick's rationality across both domains without analyzing the epistemic mechanics, or dismiss his psychical interest as irrational without asking whether it follows a coherent, if hidden, evidential logic; (3) The Approach (about 200 words), specifying that the project will be conceptual and textual rather than archival, working primarily from Sidgwick's published essays, presidential addresses, and The Methods of Ethics to map his explicit criteria for belief-formation onto his treatment of specific psychical cases, and explaining why this textual-comparative method is preferable to a biographical or purely historical reconstruction; (4) What a Result Would Look Like and What Would Count as Failure (about 150 words), defining success as either demonstrating a unified epistemology with domain-sensitive but principled thresholds, or exposing an unacknowledged pragmatic asymmetry, and defining failure as finding that Sidgwick's texts are too ambiguous to support either interpretation without importing modern frameworks he would reject; (5) Scope and Realistic Deliverable (about 100 words), outlining a three-month timeline, identifying which published texts are central, noting that the deliverable should be useful to philosophers of science interested in evidential standards rather than to historians of parapsychology, and clarifying that I will supply citations and bibliographic references myself—do not include a bibliography, reading list, or specific citation requests in the proposal. Keep the framing formal and suitable for a non-specialist review panel in philosophy, but do not simplify the conceptual issue: the proposal should sound like it is asking for money to do real philosophical work, not historical cataloguing.
