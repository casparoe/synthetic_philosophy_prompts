So I've been thinking about the boundary problem in democratic theory—who actually belongs to the demos—and I'm convinced the debate is stuck in a loop. The all-affected-interests side says anyone hit by a decision gets a say; the all-subjected side says only people under the state's coercive authority count. Every argument I see just rehashes the same moves: affected pulls in foreigners and future generations, subjected misses migrants, rinse and repeat. Nobody budges.

My hunch is it's not really about facts. Maybe the affected camp is doing normative ethics—who ought to have standing—while the subjected camp is doing institutional sociology—who can be held accountable inside existing structures. They're both saying 'demos' but meaning different jobs.

But then I keep coming back to this weird case: imagine a permanent climate sanctuary with no state, no citizenship, but people living there are permanently affected by upstream emissions policy. Both standard principals misfire here. The affected principle wants to pull them into full membership; the subjected principle excludes them entirely; but my intuition says they should have some graded, indirect representation rather than binary in/out status.

So is the stall actually about whether membership has to be binary? Or is there a hidden formal question about how to index membership across time and space that neither side is modeling? And does my sanctuary case show the framing is ill-posed, or just that we need a new category like 'stakeholder without subjecthood'?

Diagnose it for me: is this verbal, bedrock intuition, or a hidden disagreement about what counts as data? And if it's the structure I think it is, what would actually move it—a formal model of graded membership, an empirical study on non-citizen residents, or should we just abandon the binary assumption altogether?
