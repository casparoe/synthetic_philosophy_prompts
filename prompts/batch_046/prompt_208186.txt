Last month, after I redirected part of my income to an overseas famine-relief fund rather than helping my sister with her medical debt, a relative invoked the proverb "charity begins at home" to argue that Peter Singer's drowning-child analogy collapses once we recognize that moral obligations weaken with social distance. I need to understand exactly what that proverb could be asserting before I respond in our family discussion. Could you lay out, as a menu, the distinct precise theses hidden in "charity begins at home" — ranging from tautological descriptions of actual giving patterns, to claims about the moral salience of proximity, to theories of special obligations, to empirical predictions about effectiveness — and for each indicate whether it is trivial, defensible, false, or question-begging? Then say which of these readings, if any, genuinely undermine Singer's argument that distance is morally irrelevant in rescue cases, and which ones merely redirect the debate to a different normative question without touching the analogy itself.
