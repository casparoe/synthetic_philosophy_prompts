I'm preparing a presentation on Zhuangzi's philosophy of death for a course in comparative metaphysics, and I want to avoid the standard comparison with Epicureanism. Instead, please compare Zhuangzi's view of death as continuous transformation (hua) with the Stoic doctrine that death is an indifferent (adiaphoron) that befalls the rational self. Specifically, examine how each framework addresses what philosophers call the deprivation account of death's badness—the idea that death harms us by depriving us of future goods. Zhuangzi dissolves the boundary between self and world, suggesting that after death, I become part of the ten thousand things; the Stoic retains a unified rational agent for whom death is neither good nor bad because virtue alone is good. But there is a subtle tension: if Zhuangzi truly dissolves personal identity, then the deprivation account seems to collapse, not because death is not harmful, but because there is no me to be harmed—yet this looks suspiciously like changing the subject rather than answering the objection. Meanwhile, the Stoic answer retains the self but denies that goods can be lost in death.

Which approach offers the more coherent response to the deprivation problem, and does Zhuangzi's dissolution of the self actually avoid the problem or merely redefine it? Please develop the comparison with attention to whether Zhuangzi's occasional expressions of grief over death, such as his reaction to his wife's death, can be reconciled with his metaphysical dissolution of the self, or whether they reveal an unacknowledged commitment to personal continuity that undermines his theoretical position. I would especially value an answer that does not treat Zhuangzi's grief as mere poetic license, but takes seriously the possibility that his metaphysics and his emotional responses pull in opposite directions.
