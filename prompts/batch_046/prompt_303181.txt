I'm trying to pin down when self-sampling is actually rational and when it's just the presumptuous philosopher move in disguise. Here are my gut verdicts on cases, and they're not allways consistent.

If I'm told I was randomly drawn from every human who'll ever exist, and I notice I'm alive in 2024 rather than 2150, I think that genuinely updates me downward on long-term population growth. That feels okay, like normal conditioning.

But if I apply the same logic to my birth cohort specifically -- like, I'm one of the first eight billion, so there probably won't be eight trillion -- that suddenly feels wrong, like I'm treating causally downstream people as if they're already sitting in the urn.

Then there's the classic case: Theory A predicts 10^40 observers, Theory B predicts 10^10, and I think "well I'm an observer, so A is more likely." Verdict: that's garbage, definately presumptuous.

Now here's one I can't place. I'm told either consciousness requires carbon (very common) or silicon (very rare), and that if silicon is right there are a million simulated copies of me running right now. I check: I'm carbon-based, biological, not in a simulation. Does that update me against the silicon hypothesis? Part of me says yes, that's just Bayes. Part of me says no, that's the same presumptuous structure, just with biology as the filter.

And then there's the urn case: I draw ball #97 from an urn of unknown size, and I update that N is probably big. That seems totally legit.

So what's the actual principle? Is it about causal independence from the sampling pool? Or about whether the selection is evidential vs indexical? Can one account save case 1 and the urn case without saving case 3 or the silicon one? And if not, which verdict should I drop?
