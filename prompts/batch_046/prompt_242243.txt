As someone who spent three decades in meteorological modeling before turning to philosophy of science in retirement, I have grown accustomed to a particular rhetorical move in interdisciplinary seminars: a speaker compresses a deep disagreement into the line, "Chaos proves determinism false—if the laws were deterministic, we could forecast the weather, yet long-range prediction is impossible." I am certain this slogan conceals several distinct philosophical claims rather than one obvious inference, but I lack the conceptual vocabulary to separate them cleanly. Could you disambiguate the precise theses this statement might be advancing—distinguishing ontological determinism from epistemic predictability, in-principle computability from practical tractability given finite measurement resolution, and the claim that sensitive dependence eliminates unique trajectories from the claim that it merely obscures our access to them? For each reading, indicate whether it is defensible, whether it genuinely undermines determinism, and whether it mischaracterizes what nonlinear dynamics actually establish about trajectory uniqueness and Lyapunov divergence. I am particularly interested in whether there exists a rigorous reading on which determinism and chaos are not antagonists but interdependent—one that requires accepting that deterministic laws can generate behavior that is, in principle, unpredictable from any epistemically accessible initial conditions—and, if so, what commitments that reading forces upon our understanding of scientific explanation.
