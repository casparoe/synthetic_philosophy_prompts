Next month I'm giving a seminar to a mixed room — half analytic metaphysicians who think occasionalism died with Hume and was burried by modern physics, half historians of Islamic philosophy who get twitchy whenever anyone maps medieval theology onto quantum mechanics. I'm defending this weird hybrid view: that al-Ghazālī's occasionalism, where God directly causes every event and "causes" are just habits of conjunction, actually anticipates a kind of structural-realist dispositionalism about quantum fields. Strip out the theological packaging, and his denial of necessary connections between distinct existences is closer to how contemporary physics treats effective field theories than Humean regularity is.

Quick sketch of my argument for them to target: in his classic anti-philosophical arguments al-Ghazālī attacks the philosophers' claim that fire necessarily burns cotton. He doesn't deny correlation; he denies it's grounded in an intrinsic power in the fire itself. Now look at quantum field theory — interactions aren't particles carrying intrinsic forces but exchange processes mediated by gauge symmetries, with no "necessary" link between specific field configurations and outcomes except through the Lagrangian structure. So "fire" (the field state) doesn't have an intrinsic power to "burn" (produce a specific excitation). The correlation is real but sustained by deeper structural law — or in al-Ghazālī's framing, by divine custom. So occasionalism isn't anti-scientific; it's just a different metaphysical gloss on the same separation between local properties and global dynamics.

The audience knows Hume, Quine, basic QFT, but they won't have memorized obscure 2020 papers on Islamic philosophy of science. Please prep me: what objections will they actually raise, in roughly the order they'll come, phrased the way a defensive historian or a skeptical metaphysician would put them? For each, tell me what to concede up front, what to hold, and where my reply is weakest. Include the question I'm hoping nobody asks. And please — seperate what's settled in the scholarship from what's genuinely contested, and don't present my hybrid reading as if it's the consensus.
