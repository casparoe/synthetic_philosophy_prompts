I'm a retired field ecologist — thirty years tracking bird populations — and I only started reading philosphy seriously about a year ago, so bear with me if I use terms wrong. Start with this split: is there a moral difference between uttering a direct falsehood and uttering something literally true but arranged so the listener will form a false belief? Then branch on omission — does staying silent when you could easily correct a predictable error count as deceptive? Then include the niche distinction I'm playing with: call it the "intervention asymmetry" — does it matter whether your speech act causally introduces the false belief versus just fails to block one that's already forming? Use that as a node even if it splits weird.

Cover these four cases at the leaves or near them:
- flat lie ("the check is in the mail" when it isn't)
- literally true framing designed to mislead (a grant report highlighting only the data that supports your hypothesis, knowing reviewers will overgeneralize)
- silence/omission (you don't correct a colleague who is about to cite your old retracted study)
- the "pre-emptive correction failure" — you see someone about to act on a false belief and say nothing, but you've previously established a pattern of correcting them, so your silence is read as confirmation

The tree should have named leaves where positions actually exist (maybe direct-falsehood absolutism, Gricean implicature view, consequentialist equivalence, virtue-ethics of honesty) and "unnamed" for any logical combo nobody holds. Flag any pair of leaves your tree can't really seperate — I suspect virtue ethics and some consequentialist views land in the same box on these cases. Also flag empty branches: is there a view that treats omission as worse than commission, or that rejects the intervention asymmetry entirely? If that space is empty, say whether it looks incoherent or just unexplored.
