Having spent three decades in experimental physics before turning, in retirement, to philosophy of mind, I find myself perplexed by a disagreement that appears to have hardened into stalemate without anyone quite acknowledging the deadlock. The dispute concerns the so-called color phi phenomenon—in which a red dot flashed at one location followed by a green dot at another is experienced as a single dot traversing the space and changing hue midway—and specifically whether our philosophical description of it should be Orwellian or Stalinesque. On the Orwellian reading, there is never a genuinely erroneous first draft of consciousness that requires retroactive correction; on the Stalinesque reading, there is an initial experience with incorrect spatiotemporal and chromatic content that is subsequently replaced and rendered permanently inaccessible. Despite decades of exchange, the literature rehearses the same references to backward-masking protocols, to the temporal binding window in early visual cortex, and to the gap between neural latency and reportable experience, yet neither side seems to revise its core commitment.

What strikes me as an unexplored asymmetry is that this entire philosophical architecture is built almost exclusively from visual examples, even though auditory perception exhibits structurally parallel postdictive illusions—most notably the continuity effect, in which an interrupted tone is heard as unbroken and its spectral properties can seem to shift retroactively across the gap. I am unsure whether the Orwellian/Stalinesque distinction even applies coherently to auditory processing, where temporal sequence is intrinsic rather than mapped onto a spatial field, or whether its apparent clarity in vision depends on treating position and color as co-present in a discrete frame that has no acoustic analog. Moreover, predictive-processing frameworks treat both modalities as optimizing prediction error across a sliding temporal window, which raises the possibility that the philosophical dichotomy tracks nothing more than a choice between describing the same neural dynamics in first-person versus third-person vocabulary.

Consequently, I wish to ask whether this long-standing impasse is best diagnosed as a substantive disagreement about the temporal architecture of consciousness, a verbal dispute about the meaning of experience at time t versus experience available for report at t plus epsilon, or a case in which philosophers and cognitive scientists are answering different questions—phenomenological versus mechanistic—under a single heading. Might it be that one camp is concerned with phenomenal content proper and the other with access content, with neither recognizing the divergence? If so, what would genuinely count as progress: a formal distinction between phenomenal binding and access binding that applies uniformly across modalities, an experimental design comparing visual and auditory postdiction that forces the revision/replacement distinction to hold or collapse, or a terminological clarification that reveals the question itself to be ill-posed? Or should we expect no resolution, because the underlying notion of the timing of experience lacks the precision required for empirical or conceptual adjudication?
