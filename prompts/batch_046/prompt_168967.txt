Analyse the Velvet Revolution in Czechoslovakia during the autumn of 1989 as a test case for the competing historiographical commitments of microhistory and elite/state-centred history. Rather than retelling the chronology, examine the episode through two deliberately asymmetrical frames. First, adopt an “infrastructural microhistory” that foregrounds the material practices, local spatial dynamics, and everyday object-mediated decision-making—printing networks, telephone lines, public-transport rhythms—through which ordinary participants in Prague and Bratislava coordinated action without central command. Second, employ the conventional elite/state framework that treats the rapid collapse of Communist Party authority, the formation of Civic Forum around Václav Havel, and Soviet strategic non-intervention as the decisive explanatory variables.

For each frame, identify what it makes visible about the revolution’s dynamics and what it necessarily obscures. Pay particular attention to the problem of scale translation: how, if at all, do micro-level practices aggregate into the macro-level institutional rupture that occurred within weeks? Assess whether the episode confirms that microhistory can account for systemic political change, or whether it demonstrates that such ruptures remain inexplicable without reference to elite decision-making and state structural vulnerability. Reach and defend a specific verdict on whether these two approaches are complementary, antagonistic, or asymmetrical in explaining this particular case, and say what the episode implies about the limits of history from below when confronted with rapid, large-scale transformation.
