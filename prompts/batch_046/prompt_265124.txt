I am a retired experimental physicist who, over the past eighteen months, has begun reading systematically in epistemology without any formal philosophical training. I have composed the following short essay for a discussion group of similarly situated readers, and I ask for precise, point-by-point comments on its reasoning rather than on matters of prose. The essay attempts to draw a structural parallel between the philosophical bootstrapping problem—how one can justify basic beliefs without presupposing the very faculties that produce them—and the practical calibration problems I encountered in metrology.

In the laboratory, one constantly faces circular calibration: instrument A is verified against instrument B, which was itself calibrated by instrument A or by a chain of instruments that eventually loops back upon itself. This circularity is not necessarily vicious, because iterative refinement can produce convergence on stable values; the circle is broken practically, not logically. The philosophical analog appears to be the epistemological bootstrapping problem, especially the challenge of justifying our basic cognitive faculties without already relying on them. Within this problem, I want to examine the notion of easy knowledge—knowledge that requires little or no inferential support, such as direct perceptual awareness or introspective access—and to ask whether it functions like a primary standard in measurement science.

In metrology, a primary standard is defined not by comparison to another artifact but by its direct relation to invariant natural constants. Since 2019, for example, the kilogram has been defined via Planck’s constant using devices such as the Kibble balance, thereby breaking the dependency on the physical prototype kept at Sèvres. If we treat easy knowledge as an epistemic primary standard—beliefs anchored directly to experience without circular dependence on other beliefs—then perhaps bootstrapping is resolved not by constructing linear foundations but by identifying those beliefs that are, in effect, self-certifying in the way a fundamental constant is self-defining.

Yet I suspect the analogy may collapse upon closer inspection. In physics, access to a primary standard still requires extensive theoretical interpretation and instrumental mediation; there is no unmediated grasp of Planck’s constant. The Kibble balance embodies quantum mechanics, electromagnetism, and statistical inference all at once. Similarly, so-called easy knowledge is already conceptually laden: seeing an object as red involves categorization that depends on prior learning and theoretical commitments about color spaces and illumination conditions. Thus, labeling some knowledge easy might be analogous to calling the Kibble balance direct—it conceals the complex machinery that makes the cognition or measurement possible. The risk is that we solve the bootstrapping problem by definitional fiat, declaring certain beliefs foundational rather than demonstrating that they genuinely avoid circular justification.

My questions are three. First, is the parallel between metrological primary standards and epistemic easy knowledge structurally sound, or does it illegitimately confuse practical calibration with normative justification? Second, if easy knowledge is indeed theory-laden in the way I suggest, does that undermine its capacity to serve as a non-circular starting point, or can a coherentist reply absorb this concern by treating convergence rather than linear grounding as the relevant criterion? Third, have I imported an assumption from experimental science—perhaps that predictive convergence is sufficient for justification—that does not belong in philosophical analysis, where normative ground may be demanded rather than mere stability?

Please adjudicate these points with the specificity they deserve, indicate where my scientific background may be leading me astray, and suggest whether the essay’s central analogy should be developed further or abandoned.
