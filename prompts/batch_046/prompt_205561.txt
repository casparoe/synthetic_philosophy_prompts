I am seeking a consistency-based prediction from the case record of my colleague in moral philosophy, who adjudicates questions of communicative ethics across low-context and high-context norms without committing to a single universal framework. Her verdicts on the preceding six cases are these. First, a Dutch engineer who tells a foreign collaborator, without preamble or softening, that the collaborator's design is fundamentally flawed: she judges this not blameworthy, and in fact more respectful than concealment. Second, a Japanese engineer who conveys the same technical judgment by saying the design might benefit from further reflection, never stating the flaw explicitly: she judges this equally permissible, and withholding explicit criticism in that context would violate relational obligations. Third, a physician who tells a patient with terminal cancer that the treatment will likely cure her, knowing it will not: she judges this morally wrong. Fourth, a dinner host who tells guests that a burnt dish turned out well: she judges this permissible, as conventional social smoothing in a low-stakes setting. Fifth, a Dutch invitee who declines an invitation by saying she does not wish to attend, offering no excuse: she judges this permissible. Sixth, a Japanese invitee who declines by saying the date is a bit difficult, leaving the refusal implicit: she judges this equally permissible.

Consider now a seventh case. A Dutch manager assigned to Tokyo, aware of local norms but committed to transparency, tells a Japanese subordinate in private that the subordinate's performance is inadequate, that improvement is required within one month, and that failure will result in dismissal. The message is true, unambiguous, and understood clearly; the subordinate reports severe loss of face and claims that direct disclosure of such high-stakes failure violates relational obligations, though he never misunderstood the content.

Predict her verdict on this seventh case. Identify the implicit principle or principles that organize her first six judgments. Then formulate at least one alternative principle that fits those six verdicts equally well but produces the opposite verdict in the seventh case. Finally, specify an eighth case—one that both principles treat differently—that would best discriminate between them.
