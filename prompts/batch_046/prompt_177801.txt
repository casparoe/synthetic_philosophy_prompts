Analyze the emergence of Nicaraguan Sign Language in the 80s — specifically the split between the first cohort of deaf students in managua who cobbled together a pidgin-like system, and the second cohort who learned from them and suddenly produced full syntax with spatial verb agreement and embedded clauses — through the lens of the poverty-of-stimulus argument for an innate language faculty. Don't just say it proves Chomsky right. I want the mapping disciplined: what exactly was the input the second cohort received (broken pidgin, no native adult signers), what grammatical features they added that weren't in thier input, and whether the standard argument that the stimulus was too poor for induction still holds when the stimulus was actively being engineered by the first cohort's peer interactions. Does this make the case a confirmation of innateness, or does it reveal that the theory silently assumes input comes from adults or nature rather than from peer scaffolding? And where does the mapping break — is there something about the critical period or social dynamics that the poverty argument just doesn't address? Keep it concrete about the grammer, not vague.
