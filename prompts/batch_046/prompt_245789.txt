I am designing the final exam for an upper-level seminar in comparative metaphysics that has spent the past month on Buddhist philosophy of mind and personal identity. I want a single essay question that students cannot answer by reciting the textbook summary of the five skandhas and declaring that there is no self; instead, it must force them into the precise tension between the Abhidhamma elimination of a persisting self (anatta) and the normative architecture of karmic ethics—specifically, how moral consequences attach to actions performed by a being who is, at the ultimate level, only a rapidly arising and ceasing stream of aggregates.

To push the question into genuinely uncharted territory for a standard exam, frame it around the following injected conceptual problem: ask students to evaluate whether the Buddhist causal-continuum (santana) operates as a structure of free-floating rationales, in which reasons for action—such as the reason not to steal, grounded in future suffering—exist and exert normative force within the mental stream without being anchored to any unified, persisting rational agent. The essay prompt should require students to take a reasoned position on whether this framing illuminates the Theravada distinction between conventional truth (sammuti-sacca) and ultimate truth (paramattha-sacca), or whether it illegitimately imports a modern analytic conception of agency that Buddhist philosophy deliberately dissolves. They must address the ownership-without-an-owner problem in karmic retribution, distinguish eliminativist from reconstructivist readings of the conventional self, and explain whether normative grip can survive if reasons genuinely float free of reasoners.

Please write the essay question itself, a model answer of approximately 600 words, and a detailed marking scheme. The marking scheme should explicitly separate an A-grade response from a B-grade response by naming the specific philosophical moves each must contain—for example, a precise analysis of the skandhas as functional rather than substantial, a clear treatment of the conventional/ultimate distinction, an engaged judgment about free-floating rationales in the Buddhist context, and an avoidance of the common error of treating anatta as metaphysical nihilism. It should also list the specific mistakes that should cost marks, such as conflating the stream of consciousness with a hidden substrate or ignoring the normative problem altogether. The exam is intended for a 90-minute sitting with no outside sources.
