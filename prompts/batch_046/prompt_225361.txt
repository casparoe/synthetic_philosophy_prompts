I'm leading a philosophy of time seminar and want to close the session with a poem that holds open the logical tension in Aristotle's sea-battle problem—De Interpretatione 9—without letting it collapse into either determinism or a casual shrug about openness. Write a sestina in which the six rotating end-words are: tomorrow, battle, true, branch, open, necessary. In the first stanza, set up the ancient puzzle: a statement that a sea battle will occur tomorrow already seems to be either true or false, yet if its truth-value is fixed now, then tomorrow's outcome is unavoidable. Through the middle stanzas, let the sestina's compulsory rotation do conceptual work: as branch and necessary cycle through different line-positions, show how assigning true to one future requires singling out that future as actual—invoking the idea of a thin red line that marks one branch among equally real ones—while open keeps reappearing in other positions to resist that selection. The repetition of the same six words should mirror how, without such a line, the claim keeps returning to the same modal ambiguity rather than settling into determinacy. In the envoi, gather all six end-words into a single stanza that stages the unresolved pairing: true should sit beside open, and necessary beside branch, with no cancellation between them. The poem should be accurate enough that someone familiar with both Aristotelian logic and modern branching-time semantics recognizes the structural mapping, but it should read as verse rather than a diagram—no explanatory footnotes, just the argument carried by the rotation and the final, deliberately unsettled convergence.
