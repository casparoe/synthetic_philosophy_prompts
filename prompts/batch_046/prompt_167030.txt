I have come to accept Karl Popper's assessment that Darwinian natural selection, at least in its classical formulation, operates more as a metaphysical research programme than as a genuinely falsifiable scientific theory, largely because the principle that the fittest survive can be read as tautological: fitness is defined post hoc by reproductive success, so the theory appears compatible with any evolutionary outcome. Before I commit fully to this view, I want a rigorous map of its exposure — specifically, what would have to be true for Popper's dismissal to fail, not merely for evolutionary biology to be correct, but for the tautology charge itself to miss its target. Consider the following candidate defeaters and rank them from fatal to merely awkward: first, an operational metric of fitness, such as those developed in quantitative genetics, that is defined independently of survival in a particular environment and thus permits risky predictions; second, evolutionary mechanisms — for instance, neutral drift or constraints arising from evolutionary development — that do explanatory work without reducing to differential reproductive success, yet remain within the scope of Darwinism, thereby showing the theory risks concrete outcomes; third, any later qualification by Popper acknowledging that the tautology charge applies only to a particular formulation rather than to the modern synthetic theory; fourth, the observation that evolutionary biology issues risky predictions about phylogenetic structure, fossil succession, and molecular homology that have survived sustained attempts at refutation. For each, indicate whether a committed Popperian could absorb it by restricting the scope of the tautology claim, by sharpening the demarcation criterion, or by conceding partial defeat, and specify which escape routes are live rather than merely logical. I am also concerned about an internal tension: if I accept Popper's falsifiability demarcation as normative for science, and I also grant that evolutionary biology produces genuine knowledge, am I forced to conclude that evolutionary biology is not science in Popper's sense? Or can the research-programme framework accommodate productive science without collapsing the demarcation? Please address whether this tension is real or only apparent, and if real, which commitment should yield. I do not want a literature survey or a list of commentators; I want an honest assessment of where the position is genuinely vulnerable and where it is well insulated, together with a judgment about whether that insulation is a virtue or a vice.
