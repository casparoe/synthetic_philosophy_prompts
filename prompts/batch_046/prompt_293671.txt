Exam question: John Taurek asks whether, when we can rescue either one person or five people but not both, we are morally required to save the greater number. Present his reasoning. Then apply it to the following case: an emergency-response algorithm can deploy one rescue drone to either Site A (one trapped survivor) or Site B (five trapped survivors). An engineer proposes resolving the tie by a fair coin flip, arguing that this respects the moral equality of all lives. Is this proposal a faithful application of Taurek's view? Note any important limitations.

Student answer:
Taurek's central claim is that in a rescue situation where we can save either one person or five people, but not both, there is no moral reason to prefer the larger group. Each individual has an equally weighty claim to be rescued, and aggregating these claims treats people as fungible, violating the separateness of persons. Applying this to the drone: since Site B's five survivors do not present a stronger collective claim than Site A's single survivor, programming a coin flip respects this equality and avoids counting heads. Taurek's view is further supported by the harm-omission distinction—sending the drone to Site A does not actively harm those at Site B, because the danger there is pre-existing and the unit's absence is merely a failure to save—so random choice violates no negative duty. The only limitation is that Taurek writes about direct human choice rather than code, but the underlying principle transfers without loss. Consequently, the coin-flip procedure is not just permitted but required by impartiality.
