I have been thinking about the recent popularity of Stoicism in everyday life coaching and mental-health applications, particularly the so-called dichotomy of control—dividing the world into what is “up to us” and what is not. The ancient texts also spoke of “preferred indifferents,” things like health or reputation that are not genuinely good but are still naturally worth selecting. What puzzles me is that modern guides often insist that career success, romantic harmony, and financial stability lie firmly outside our control, yet they continue to offer strategic advice on how to pursue them. This strikes me as conceptually unstable. Please argue that this modern revival has smuggled a contradiction into the heart of the dichotomy: either the coaching treats these externals as genuinely within our sphere of influence, in which case the dichotomy is effectively abandoned, or it treats them as truly external, in which case strategic advice about them is philosophically incoherent. Defend the stronger claim that a consistent modern Stoicism must therefore abandon all strategic guidance about externals and restrict itself entirely to the internal discipline of assent and judgement, accepting that this renders the philosophy far less useful as a practical life programme. You need not cite particular authors or texts; work through the conceptual tension itself.
