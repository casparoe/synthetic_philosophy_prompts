I've been working through an argument about fictional emotions that started as a defense of the pretense theory and ended with a conclusion I think is wrong—namely, that taking fiction seriously as a moral teacher requires a kind of systematic self-deception. I want to know exactly where the leak is.

Here is the chain, with each step numbered.

(1) When we respond emotionally to fiction—say, grieving for a character's undeserved suffering—we are not in the same psychological state as real grief, because we lack the belief that the suffering is actually occurring. Call this the pretense condition.

(2) For an emotional response to be rationally appropriate rather than merely caused, it must be calibrated to the properties of its object: real grief is appropriate only if there is real suffering, and its intensity should track the severity of that suffering.

(3) Now, when we treat a novel as morally profound because it makes us feel the weight of injustice, we are treating our emotional response as evidence that the work has successfully represented a real moral pattern—one that applies beyond the fiction.

(4) But if our emotional response is pretense-grief (lacking belief in actual suffering), then by (2) it is not rationally calibrated to any real moral property. It is, at best, a simulated response with no evidential weight.

(5) Therefore, if we take our emotional response to fiction as grounding judgments about moral reality—e.g., concluding that the world contains more cruelty of a certain kind because the novel made us feel it—we are reasoning from an uncalibrated, belief-free affect.

(6) One might reply that fiction teaches by analogy: we map the fictional situation onto real ones. But analogical reasoning requires that the source case be genuinely understood, and if our understanding of the source case is mediated by pretense-emotions that lack rational calibration, then the mapping inherits that defect.

(7) Hence anyone who treats fiction as a source of moral knowledge is relying on emotional states that are, by the pretense condition, disconnected from reality-checking belief. That looks like a form of epistemic self-deception: we let uncalibrated affect shape our beliefs about the actual world.

The conclusion in (7) is absurd—I'm not prepared to say that serious readers of narrative fiction are systematically self-deceived. I want to preserve (1), that fictional grief is not full-blooded belief-based grief, and I want to preserve the idea that fiction can genuinely inform moral judgment. So which premise should I reject?

Specifically, is the error in (2), where I assume that rational emotional appropriateness always requires belief in the actual existence of the object? Or is there an equivocation between 'pretense' as behavioral make-believe and 'pretense' as absence of all cognitive appraisal? Or does (6) overstate the dependency of analogical reasoning on the rational calibration of source-emotions?

I'd like an audit: map out which steps are doing the work, identify any hidden scope shift or modal slide, and tell me whether rejecting (2) collapses the pretense theory or whether there's a more surgical fix—say, distinguishing between first-order emotional response and second-order judgment about representational fit—that lets me keep (1) and deny (7) together.
