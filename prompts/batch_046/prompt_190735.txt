I take seriously the view—famously explored in Hofstadter's Ant Fugue dialogue with Aunt Hillary, but pushed further here—that a mature ant colony is not merely metaphorically mind-like but actually constitutes a single, unified cognitive subject with its own beliefs, desires, and possibly phenomenal states. On this colony-mind realism, the pheromone-mediated information integration, division of labor, and homeostatic regulation across thousands of workers form a non-decomposable system with one point of view, rather than an aggregate of ant-level minds. I want to know what would actually count against this position: not rhetorical dismissals, but the empirical findings, conceptual distinctions, or formal results that should move a defender. Rank the defeaters from decisive to merely awkward—consider, for instance, whether documented failures of global information integration in real colonies undermine the claim; whether the absence of a central neural architecture is fatal or only analogically weak; whether evolutionary arguments about levels of selection show that colony-level "intentionality" collapses into individual-level adaptations; and whether the view risks being qualified into unfalsifiability, say by retreating to pure functional equivalence without phenomenal commitment, or by defining "mind" so broadly that a thermostat qualifies. I also want an honest assessment of whether such insulation is a problem for a view of this kind, plus a clear distinction between objections that defenders of colony-mind realism would themselves concede as damaging and those they would dismiss as category mistakes.
