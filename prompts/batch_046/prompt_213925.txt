Write an essay on whether Aristotle’s distinction between oikonomia (household management oriented toward use) and chrematistike (wealth-getting oriented toward unlimited exchange) retains critical force against contemporary decentralized finance—specifically, yield-generating protocols in which algorithmic lending pools pay returns in stablecoins without any traditional creditor-debtor relationship. First, reconstruct Aristotle’s claim that money is naturally sterile and that interest (tokos) is therefore “unnatural” because it makes currency breed currency. Then examine the objection that smart-contract-mediated yield is not interest in Aristotle’s sense, since no personal loan occurs and the “growth” derives from automated trading fees and liquidity incentives rather than from the reproductive power of capital itself. Consider also the reply that, for Aristotle, the moral fault lies not in the legal form of the contract but in the telos of the activity: treating wealth as an end rather than as a means subordinate to living well. Conclude with a defended verdict: does the Aristotelian framework genuinely distinguish conventional interest-bearing banking from decentralized yield farming, or does it condemn both indiscriminately? And if it condemns both, is that a strength—revealing a shared moral structure hidden by technological novelty—or a weakness—failing to track meaningful institutional differences that matter for regulation and moral assessment? Justify your answer with specific attention to whether Aristotle’s “natural limit” on acquisition can be applied coherently once currency is purely informational and scarcity is algorithmically enforced rather than materially given.
