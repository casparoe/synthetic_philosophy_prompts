As a novelist constructing multi-generational sagas, I find myself implicitly committed to metaphysical claims about personal identity over time that I cannot defend philosophically. Before I settle on a framework for how my characters persist through decades of fictional time—through amnesia, moral reversal, and bodily change—I want the full specification of what any adequate theory of persistence must actually deliver for narrative coherence, not merely for physical ontology.

Write the desiderata that a theory of temporal persistence—whether endurantist, perdurantist, or an alternative—must satisfy to ground long-form character arcs. For each requirement, provide the narrative or conceptual case behind it: for instance, how the theory handles radical psychological discontinuity without dissolving moral accountability for earlier actions; whether it respects the intuition that a single protagonist can endure through transformation rather than being replaced by a temporal counterpart; how it distinguishes physical continuity from narrative-psychological continuity; and whether it allows a first-person narrator to speak with authority about her own past if she is not strictly identical to the subject who lived it. Identify which desiderata are non-negotiable for coherent storytelling and which reflect merely metaphysical preferences (such as simplicity or compatibility with special relativity). Then indicate whether any subset of these requirements is jointly unsatisfiable—perhaps because preserving strict identity through change conflicts with accommodating radical transformation—and tell me which single desideratum eliminates the most popular versions of endurantism or perdurantism. I am not asking for a bibliography or a scorecard of named philosophers; I want the criteria themselves, defended, so that I can see where my narrative commitments actually place me in this debate.
