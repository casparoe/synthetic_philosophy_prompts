I have been working through Kripkenstein's rule-following considerations in a graduate philosophy-of-language seminar, and a fellow student recently proposed an extension that now strikes me as suspicious because it threatens to collapse the skeptical solution back into the skeptical problem itself. I want to locate the exact flaw, if there is one, rather than simply rejecting the conclusion by intuition.

Here is the reconstructed argument. (1) The skeptical argument shows that no finite set of my past mental states, behaviors, or dispositions determines whether I previously meant addition rather than quaddition by 'plus'; hence, absent further resources, there is simply no fact of the matter about my prior semantic intentions. (2) The skeptical solution responds by abandoning the idea that meaning-attributions describe private mental facts with determinate truth-conditions, and instead treats them as assertions warranted only within the normative practices of a linguistic community—my present inclination to correct a deviant computation counts as 'meaning addition now' not because it tracks an inner state with fixed content, but because it expresses my participation in a shared form of life. (3) Yet the skeptical argument's force derives from a general principle: any finite specification of a rule is compatible with infinitely many interpretations, and this vulnerability is not special to past-tense mental access but applies to any finite mental or behavioral specification whatsoever. (4) Therefore, my present corrective inclination—say, my disposition to reject answers exceeding 125—can itself be interpreted in infinitely many ways (perhaps I correct only up to some hidden boundary, or only when observed by specific interlocutors), with no further mental fact available to settle the matter. (5) Consequently, the skeptical solution appears to depend on present mental states that are themselves subject to the very interpretive indeterminacy the argument was meant to dissolve, leaving it without the stable normative ground it requires. (6) From this it seems to follow that accepting the skeptical argument commits us not merely to a deflationary account of past meaning, but to a recursive, global skepticism about all present mental content—including our own adoption of the skeptical framework—which looks self-undermining rather than therapeutic.

I am inclined to preserve step (2) and resist the slide into global skepticism, but I cannot identify whether the error lies in treating present dispositions as equally vulnerable to reinterpretation, in misunderstanding the essentially social character of the skeptical solution, or in an unnoticed equivocation between levels of description. Which step should be rejected, what does rejecting it cost in terms of consistency with the original skeptical challenge, and is there a principled reason why the argument applies to past-tense intentions but not to the present community-oriented practices that constitute the solution?
