Question: Malcolm argues that dreams are not experiences because they fail the criterion of observation: during sleep there is no conscious subject capable of observing events in the manner required for genuine experience-reports. Dennett responds with a narrative-construction model, holding that the dream story is assembled at or near awakening rather than replayed from a prior experiential record. Evaluate whether Dennett's model can accommodate false awakenings—cases in which a sleeper reports having already woken, acted, and then fallen back asleep—without reducing them to mere misremembered waking experiences. Also assess whether Malcolm's observation criterion, applied consistently, should classify brief hypnagogic imagery (vivid sensory fragments at sleep onset accompanied by partial awareness) as non-experiential.

Student answer:
Malcolm's core claim is that for something to count as an experience there must be a possible observation of it, but during REM sleep the subject is unconscious and therefore cannot observe anything; hence dreams are not experiences but rather like unconscious mental processes reconstructed upon waking. Dennett's narrative-construction view says that what we call a dream is composed from memory fragments and current bodily states at the moment of awakening, not played back from a stored record. False awakenings support Dennett because they show the waking narrative can include false beliefs about one's own state—believing one is already awake—without requiring an actual prior waking experience. If Malcolm is right that no observation occurs during sleep, then false awakenings should be impossible, since there would be no observing self available to generate the false belief; yet they are commonly reported, which undermines Malcolm's position. As for hypnagogic imagery, Malcolm should deny these are full experiences because the observation criterion is only partially satisfied during the fading of consciousness. Dennett would treat them as transitional narrative fragments formed as consciousness declines, neither fully experienced during sleep nor constructed entirely upon later waking. Thus both philosophers deny experience-status to hypnagogic states, though Malcolm does so on epistemic grounds while Dennett does so on grounds of narrative formation.
