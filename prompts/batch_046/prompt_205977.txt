Design a 90-minute workshop simulation for 12 philosophy graduate students (no technical prerequisites required) that makes the strategic instability of embedded agency tangible before the concept is named. The target is the loop in which an agent must include its own decision procedure within the world-model it uses to act, and therefore cannot treat that procedure as an exogenous parameter.

Divide participants into three groups of four. Give each group a sealed envelope containing a collective-resource payoff matrix and a single "self-rule card" that states one constraint on their own deliberation—for instance: "If any member invokes efficiency, the group must spend two minutes debating that invocation before voting." Crucially, the card is also listed as an environmental variable in the payoff matrix, so that any optimal allocation strategy must account for it. After an initial five minutes of unstructured discussion, groups open the envelope and must keep the card physically visible on the table. They have twenty minutes to reach a unanimous decision. They may attempt to "rewrite" the card by consensus, but any rewrite attempt activates the original constraint, consuming three minutes of their remaining time. A facilitator-observer tracks whether each group's explicit model of the situation includes or excludes the card that governs how they model the situation.

Structure the rules so that groups naturally try to externalize the card—treating it as hidden information to be solved—then experience oscillation: a rewritten card changes the conditions under which the rewrite was evaluated. Do not lecture on embedded agency in advance; let the failure emerge from the incentives and time pressure.

Provide a precise timeline (opening discussion, envelope opening, decision phase with observer notes, final report), a debrief that maps moments of oscillation and externalization onto the formal distinction between embedded and external agency, and an explicit prediction of what will go wrong on first run: participants will initially frame the difficulty as information asymmetry rather than structural self-reference, and the facilitator should withhold the conceptual framing until roughly the ten-minute mark of the decision phase.

End with an honest assessment of reproduction limits: the simulation captures well the practical instability of treating one's own reasoning as an object inside the world-model, but it cannot reproduce the formal undecidability analog—the boundary on fully transparent self-modeling—which the facilitator must supply conceptually after the practical frustration has been felt. The prompt should not ask for bibliographies, reading lists, or citations, and the facilitator must avoid introducing highly technical literature that participants could not be expected to recall without access to sources.
