As a journalist covering philosophy of mind and theoretical science, I am trying to map a particular argumentative move that recurs whenever Gödelian self-reference is imported into debates about consciousness — particularly the tradition that moves from formal logic to phenomenal self-awareness, often linked to strange-loop accounts of the self. The move I have in mind is narrower than the general claim that minds transcend formal systems.

Here is the configuration I keep encountering. First, an interlocutor notes that a sufficiently powerful formal system can construct a statement referring to its own unprovability. Next, they observe that human beings can grasp truths lying outside any given formalisation. Then comes the distinctive step: they argue that any complete scientific theory of consciousness would necessarily have to include the theorist’s own conscious state within its domain of explanation. By analogy to Gödel’s second incompleteness theorem, they conclude that such a theory could never demonstrate its own consistency or completeness from within the system it describes. I have seen this used to resist the possibility of artificial consciousness — on the grounds that a machine could never fully validate its own understanding — and also in speculative essays that treat reflexive inclusion as an explanatory primitive rather than a formal feature.

What I am after is not the broad Gödelian analogy itself, which is well documented, but this specific configuration: the use of embedded self-reference to block a complete third-person science of mind, on the grounds that the observer is irreducibly inside the observed system. Does this have an established name — perhaps a reflexive-exclusion thesis, or an embedded-theorist argument? If not, how ought one to distinguish it precisely from strange-loop accounts of self-awareness, and from standard anti-reductionist appeals to the explanatory gap? I am especially interested in its boundary conditions: does the move depend on treating consciousness as a formal system with fixed axioms, or on a conflation between syntactic self-reference and semantic self-modelling? And which of these, if either, is essential to its force?
