Write a dialogue between two thinkers—Voss and Kiran—who disagree sharply on whether cooperative outcomes can survive in program equilibrium once agents may selectively encrypt subroutines within their decision algorithms. Voss defends what she calls a modal program equilibrium: she holds that credible cooperation remains achievable even under partial source-code disclosure, so long as the visible modules contain binding commitments about response rules to observed defection, while encrypted layers merely conceal private valuation weights. Kiran rejects this, arguing that selective encryption reintroduces precisely the epistemic asymmetry that full program equilibrium was designed to dissolve; without complete inspectability, commitment mechanisms collapse into strategic ambiguity, reducing the interaction to a conventional game-theoretic standoff. The exchange should take place in a single, confined setting—a late-evening seminar room with rain against tall windows—allowing the physical tension between transparency and concealment to echo the conceptual one. Both voices must be equally rigorous: neither may be reduced to a straw figure. The dialogue must contain genuine back-and-forth, with each speaker responding directly to the other’s actual claim rather than advancing a prepared monologue. Include at least one moment in which Voss concedes a structural limitation of her framework that Kiran then uses to sharpen her critique, and at least one moment in which Kiran acknowledges a feature of partial transparency that genuinely complicates her own skepticism. Maintain the precision of analytic philosophy, but render the cadence with literary care: let pauses, gestures, and the changing light in the room carry part of the argumentative weight. Do not request bibliographies, citations, or references to specific texts; the prompt should stand entirely on its conceptual architecture.
