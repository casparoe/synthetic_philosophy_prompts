I've been thinking about Sor Juana Ines de la Cruz lately—this 17th-century Mexican nun who wrote that famous defense of women studying philosophy and theology instead of just embroidery and prayer. I'm trying to imagine what she'd say about these new AI "curriculum guardians" being tested in some conservative school networks, where the tutor adapts in real time: if a girl's biometic feedback shows stress during calculus, the system quietly redirects her to literature; if she asks too aggressively about church doctrine or politics, the bot steers her back to approved texts. The designers call it personalized learning for girls' wellbeing, but it feels like automating the exact walls she fought against when she argued women should have direct access to knowledge, not just what men think is safe for them.

Would she see this as a total betrayal—worse even than the convent rules she lived under, because the filter is invisible? Or might she be more pragmatic, thinking at least girls get literacy and some science, and an AI gatekeeper is less cruel than a human one? There's also this side question about transparency: should the student know she's being steered? I can't tell if Sor Juana would care more about the hidden steering itself, or about whether the girl knows it's happening and can resist it. I keep going back and forth on whether her core commitment was to women's intellectual autonomy regardless of method, or specifically to tearing down explicit barriers. What's your read on her likely view, especially on whether an invisible algorithimic filter is morally different from the old open rules?
