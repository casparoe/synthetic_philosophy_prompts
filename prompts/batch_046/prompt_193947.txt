Ive been arguing with my flatmate about why some moral norms just vanish while others hang around despite everyone saying theyre wrong, and I think Im trying to map my own gut verdicts onto something like the Williams/Appiah view that its usually interests and honour dynamics rather than pure argument that does the killing — but I keep finding cases that dont sit right.

My verdicts: dueling collapsed across Europe mainly because states banned it and the aristocracy started seeing it as embarassment rather than noble, so thats honour inversion plus state interest, with moral argument playing catch-up. The British end of transatlantic slavery feels messier — yes, industrial interests shifted, and owning slaves became a diplomatic embarassment, but I also think the abolitionists genuinely changed what people felt ashamed of, so argument, interest and honour all seem to do real work and I cant tell which was load-bearing. Foot-binding in China disappeared mostly through nationalist shame and state campaigns, with almost no internal moral debate that I can see, so pure honour/state interest. But then bear-baiting in Britain vanished within a generation once the elite decided it was vulgar, despite being deeply embedded in popular culture, while honour killings in parts of South Asia persist despite criminalisation and widespread moral condemnation. Both are honour-embedded, both faced state bans, yet one died and one survives. My gut says the difference is whether the honour is centralised and publicly visible — bear-baiting status was conferred by elites who could withdraw it collectively, whereas honour-killing status is distributed inside family structures and hidden from the social gaze that would generate shame. So my tentative principle is that norms collapse when the honour they confer becomes publicly non-transferable, which usually happens through elite defection or state restructuring, with moral arguments mainly rationalising the shift afterwards; when honour is decentralised and invisible to outsiders, interests and arguments stall.

Does this principle actually capture my cases, or am I just saving the verdicts at the cost of complexity? What would it predict about a norm I havent considered — say, the modern rapid shift on same-sex marriage, or the decline of sending children to workhouses in Victorian Britain? And which of my verdicts does it force me to drop?
