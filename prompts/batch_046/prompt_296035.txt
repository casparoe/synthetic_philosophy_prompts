Design a 90-minute seminar activity for about 14 upper-level philosophy students on the idea that the self functions as a center of narrative gravity rather than as an inner substance. Do not begin with a lecture; the concept should emerge only from what the rules force the group to do.

Divide students into four “biography committees” of 3–4 members. Give each committee only one quadrant of a dossier describing a single legal adult, “Case J”: quadrant one (years 1–5), quadrant two (6–10), quadrant three (11–15), or quadrant four (16–20). Each quadrant contains first-person journal fragments, behavioral logs, and third-party reports that are internally coherent but carry value-systems incompatible with those in other quadrants—e.g., quadrant one shows severe ascetic self-denial, quadrant three shows unrestrained hedonism, quadrant four shows self-sacrificial altruism. In 25 minutes each committee must: (1) write a two-sentence “center of gravity” profile predicting how Case J will resolve a forced-choice dilemma you provide (saving a stranger requires destroying an object Case J deeply values); (2) assign moral responsibility for an act described in their quadrant; and (3) defend both judgments using only narrative coherence, with an explicit prohibition on appealing to an “inner soul,” “private mental theater,” or biological continuity.

At minute 25, reveal that the four quadrants belong to four different biological individuals whose records were merged by an administrative error into one legal identity; there has never been one continuous body or brain. External observers never noticed because behavioral and narrative continuity are perfect. The committees reconvene for 20 minutes and must reach a binding decision: either (A) there is one self with a fractured but single center of narrative gravity now pulling in contradictory directions, or (B) there never was a self—only a gravitational center constructed by merged records, which has now dissipated into four separate centers.

Debrief (20 minutes): Map the initial prediction task onto locating a self by tracking narrative coherence rather than inspecting an internal essence. Map the post-reveal crisis onto the disappearance of substrate-independent selfhood. Predict the failure mode—students will smuggle in biological continuity despite the rules, or retreat to subjective “it feels like” appeals—and say how to handle it (redirect them to the dossier evidence only). Be explicit about what the activity cannot reproduce: the gradual, evolutionary accretion of selfhood over a lifetime is compressed here into a single bureaucratic shock, so the facilitator must supply that temporal dimension verbally by noting that real narrative gravity builds slowly and can erode slowly, not only by sudden administrative revelation.

What participants should come away seeing is that defending a self as real requires continuous narrative labor, and that when the physical substrate is shown to be discontinuous, the remaining options—splitting the gravity well or denying it ever existed—both feel like genuine losses rather than obvious corrections. Include a clear timeline, the specific dilemma text, and an honest note that the facilitator must verbally supply the missing evolutionary timescale.
