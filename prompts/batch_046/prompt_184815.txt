As a novelist developing a narrative in which the first-person narrator dies at the midpoint yet continues to speak—not as a ghost or spirit, but as a voice undergoing what Zhuangzi describes as transformation (hua)—I need to understand the philosophical architecture of such a voice. Please describe the most important implications of Zhuangzi's views on death, particularly his claim that death is not a harm but a change akin to the seasons, his image of the perfected person who wanders freely beyond the boundaries of life and death without grief or attachment, and the parable in which a skull questions whether transformation into a butterfly is good or bad, for how this narrative should treat continuity of identity, emotional register, and the living characters' responses to the speaker. Specifically, how should the text avoid both conventional elegiac tragedy and metaphysical ghost-story tropes while remaining coherent with Zhuangzi's refusal to privilege life over death?
