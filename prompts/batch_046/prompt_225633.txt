After retiring from a career in experimental particle physics, I have recently turned to philosophy of mind, and I find myself preoccupied with Daniel Dennett's claim that the self is best understood not as a Cartesian substance but as a center of narrative gravity—an abstract point around which autobiographical episodes organize themselves without requiring a central subject. To test the limits of this metaphor, consider a technically precise but currently hypothetical cognitive architecture: a distributed, modular neural-symbolic system that possesses no single integrative processor, yet in which every module maintains continuously updated, first-person-indexed memory traces that are globally broadcast and behaviorally coupled, producing a globally coherent stream of self-referential narrative despite the absence of any unified experiential core. Now suppose David Hume—who argued that introspection reveals no simple, continuous self and that personal identity is a fiction generated by the associative imagination—were confronted both with this architecture and with Dennett's assertion that narrative coherence alone is sufficient for selfhood. How do you think Hume would assess such a system? Would he treat it as possessing a genuine center of narrative gravity, deny that engineered coherence amounts to the natural fiction of identity, or conclude that Dennett's metaphor covertly reintroduces the substantial unity it claims to dissolve? Please reason directly from Hume's principles regarding impressions, ideas, and the construction of identity, applied specifically to this distributed artificial case, and avoid appeals to secondary literature, citation, or bibliographic reference.
