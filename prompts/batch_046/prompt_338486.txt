me and my mate had this massive row last night about whether history can ever be objective, and i'm still not sorted on it. i was trying to lay out my gut verdicts on different historian behaviours and ask if there's one principle behind them, but we just went in circles.

so: when a historian finds a fresh archive document that kills an old myth and reports it straight, no spin, that feels like proper objectivity — the Ranke ideal, y'know, showing things as they actually were. but if someone writes the same events as a tragedy just because Carr's scepticism and Hayden White's narrative tropes say it's all framing, and they drop a dull economic table because it ruins the mood, that feels like cheating even if every included fact is true.

then there's framing. if two historians have the same sources but one casts them as comedy and one as romance, i don't think either is less objective; it's just style. but if either of them quietly leaves out a document that complicates the arc, that's wrong, even if they don't actually lie. and if a historian comes right out and says "i'm selecting for coherence", that feels honest but also like they've given up on Ranke altogether.

there's this other case i'm torn on: a historian recontructs a missing speech from surrounding context and clearly labels it reconstructed. my instinct says that's okay if they're transparent, but my friend reckons it's just fiction with footnotes. i keep going back and forth.

is there a coherent principle — maybe about letting sources push back against your framing? — that gets most of these? and which verdict does it make me drop? especially does it hold together that narrative framing is fine but selecing out inconvenient docs is bad? also what does it say about the recontructed speech case?
