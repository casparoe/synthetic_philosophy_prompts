Next month I present a chapter draft at an interdisciplinary workshop on atrocity law. My position is that Lemkin’s original conception of genocide included cultural destruction—specifically the forced assimilation of children—without requiring physical killing, and that the UN Convention’s narrowing to physical or biological acts was politically motivated rather than conceptually coherent. I further argue that “genocide” now functions as a moral threshold term: applying it triggers obligations that do not scale with severity, which explains why states resist the label even when they admit to the underlying destructive acts. The audience includes two international lawyers who know the Convention drafting history in detail, three moral philosophers skeptical that legal categories should carry moral weight, and a historian of Lemkin familiar with his archival drafts. Give me the objections in the likely order they will come, phrased as those lawyers and philosophers would phrase them, with what I should concede, what I should hold, and the weakest point in my own position—especially regarding my claim about the threshold effect and non-scaling obligations—that I should prepare to defend rather than hope they miss.
