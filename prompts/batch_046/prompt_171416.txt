Political discourse often invokes the idea that the arc of history bends toward justice — most recently I heard it used to argue that present injustices require less urgent intervention because long-run correction is structurally assured, and also to defend gradualism against demands for immediate change. Before I can assess either argument, I need the slogan's logical anatomy.

Please disambiguate the claim that history bends toward justice into a menu of precise, non-equivalent theses. For each reading, indicate whether it is descriptive, normative, metaphysical, or epistemic; whether it is defensible, trivially true, or false; and whether it actually licenses the practical inferences (tolerating present injustice, rejecting activist urgency) that people draw from it.

Specifically, include and sharply distinguish:
(i) a strong teleological thesis, on which history possesses an intrinsic directional vector aimed at justice as its telos;
(ii) a probabilistic selection thesis, on which just institutions enjoy greater long-run stability and thus survive competitive pressures more reliably than unjust ones;
(iii) a normative hermeneutic thesis, on which we ought to interpret the past and future through a justice-oriented framework regardless of actual trends;
(iv) a retrospective narrative-fallacy thesis, on which human cognition selectively weights visible progress over hidden regression, generating an illusion of directed arc that dissolves under systematic accounting.

Also address what is lost or added when the claim is shifted from individual moral agency to macro-historical patterns: does the slogan imply that agents can relax because history will correct things without them, or does it depend on continuous agency to maintain the bend?

Present the readings as a structured list I can use in discussion. Do not provide bibliographies or reading lists; I want to evaluate the conceptual distinctions themselves, not track their textual origins.
