I'm planning a forty-minute seminar for advanced undergraduates on sphexishness, using the Sphex ichneumoneus burrow-checking routine not as a curiosity but as a limiting case for free action. I want them to see mechanical rigidity not as the absence of adaptation, but as a specific failure of counterfactual responsiveness: the wasp adapts within its routine, yet never registers variables—such as a moved burrow—that fall outside the routine's trigger conditions, because the behavior never includes the counterfactual question "what if this had changed?" I need a teaching plan, not the lecture itself. In what order must these pieces be introduced—the ethological observation, the distinction between adaptive flexibility and counterfactual rigidity, the philosophical claim about free will, and the contrast with ordinary human habit—given what each depends on? Which single case should carry the idea, and why should I avoid using something like the knee-jerk reflex for that role? I anticipate two misconceptions: that sphexishness simply means the wasp is stupid, and that any human habit is therefore sphexish; specify exactly where in the sequence each should be headed off. Give one end-of-session check question that reveals whether students have grasped the specific failure mode rather than just memorized the wasp anecdote. Finally, state explicitly what should be left out at this level and why.
