I keep encountering a particular argumentative pattern in debates over who belongs to the demos, and I'm unsure whether it has an established name. A speaker begins with an affected-interests principle—for instance, arguing that long-term non-citizen residents should vote in municipal zoning decisions because the outcomes materially shape their lives. When an interlocutor extends the same logic to foreign workers affected by local procurement rules, or to future generations affected by climate ordinances, the speaker introduces a narrowing filter: only those affected in a "direct, non-mediated" fashion, or only those whose interests are "specifically targeted" rather than "incidentally impacted." Yet this filter is applied selectively. The speaker counts domestic renters as directly affected by housing policy, despite mediation through landlords and markets, but excludes overseas suppliers hit by the same policy's sourcing rules as merely indirect. The boundary of the demos thus stays fixed around the speaker's preferred constituency, while the theoretical criterion shifts to accommodate that fixity. Is there a recognized concept for this specific maneuver—distinct from the general boundary problem, from ordinary motivated reasoning, and from simple ad hoc exception-making? If there is no standard term, how should it be defined and bounded, and what distinguishes it from legitimate distinctions between direct and derivative democratic standing?
