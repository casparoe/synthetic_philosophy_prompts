I have been working through the conceptual structure of secessionist claims with a colleague, using the familiar cases—Scotland’s negotiated referendum framework, Catalonia’s unilateral declaration, and Quebec’s reference jurisprudence—not to settle their empirical histories but to test a distinction I think the standard literature underdraws: that between “remedial exit” (secession justified as a remedy for grave, structural wrong by the center) and “preferential exit” (secession motivated by persistent divergence in political values or economic priorities, absent any clear injustice). We also introduced a further condition we call “archival continuity”: the idea that because political union generates shared institutional memory—joint legal archives, interwoven administrative records, and common historiographic frameworks—any dissolution carries an epistemic duty to preserve access to those shared resources rather than partition them.

My colleague argued as follows. (1) A constitutional pathway for secession—such as a legally authorized referendum—does not independently justify exit; it is merely a procedural vessel, and its use is morally permissible only if remedial conditions are satisfied. (2) Applying this, Scotland’s 2014 referendum was therefore morally dubious, since no grave injustice obtained, whereas Catalonia’s 2017 move, though illegal, tracked closer to a genuine remedial grievance given the suppression of regional autonomy, so its illegality does not settle its moral status. (3) Preferential exit is never sufficient for dissolution, because democratic politics necessarily involves being outvoted; if every persistent minority preference could justify rupture, stable multistate structures would be impossible. (4) The “archival continuity” requirement is aesthetic nostalgia disguised as ethics—institutional memory survives in copies and digital repositories, so political separation need not sever epistemic access.

I replied. (5) The remedial/preferential distinction collapses once we recognize that being permanently outvoted on matters of cultural self-definition and fiscal distribution is itself a form of structural harm; a constitutional pathway transforms this persistent divergence into a remediable grievance precisely by offering a legitimate, non-ruptural mechanism to resolve it. (6) Consequently, Scotland’s legal referendum was morally superior to Catalonia’s unilateral move, because working within shared institutions respects an intergenerational contract that unilateral rupture violates; moreover, a constitutional pathway can embed preservation clauses for joint archives and courts, making “archival continuity” a genuine moral constraint rather than a sentimental add-on, since severing governance without securing shared epistemic infrastructure destroys the conditions for future reconciliation or even accurate historical accountability.

She countered. (7) My claim that democratic loss constitutes harm confuses the normal costs of collective decision-making with injustice—if divergence alone were enough, every regional party with a tax preference could threaten exit, eroding the very possibility of redistribution. (8) Even granting archival continuity some weight, it is a secondary consideration that can be satisfied contractually after secession; it therefore cannot bear primary justificatory weight.

We could not resolve whether the distinction holds, whether archival continuity is primary or derivative, and whether constitutional pathways create obligations rather than mere permissions. Please adjudicate each numbered move (1) through (8): rate each as clearly right, clearly mistaken, or partly right, with brief justification. In particular, determine whether “archival continuity” is a genuine moral desideratum for any adequate theory of secession, or a red herring; and state whether the remedial/preferential distinction survives or needs replacement. Conclude with a single line beginning exactly “Verdict:” followed by your overall assessment in one sentence.
