Two classmates and I were debating dispositions, finks, and masks after our philosophy unit, and we got stuck on a hypothetical case. Alex described a synthetic material—let us call it flex-alloy—that is structurally brittle at the molecular level, but contains an embedded reactive lattice that instantly reinforces it the moment mechanical stress is applied, so it never actually fractures. Alex said this is a masked disposition: the alloy is genuinely brittle, but its breaking is systematically blocked by an interfering factor, just like a fragile vase protected by an external mechanism. Jordan objected that the lattice is not an external mask but part of the material’s intrinsic constitution, so calling the alloy “brittle” is misleading; it is actually ductile by nature, and separating a hidden brittle disposition from its built-in antidote wrongly carves one unified property in two. Alex then argued that if we chemically deactivate the lattice, the material shatters immediately, which proves the brittle disposition was present all along, merely masked. Jordan replied that this counterfactual test changes the object itself, so we are not revealing a hidden property of the original alloy but testing a different substance altogether. They then wondered whether the fink-versus-mask distinction even survives when the interfering mechanism is internal and constitutive rather than accidental and external. Please adjudicate their exchange move by move: is flex-alloy best described as having a masked brittle disposition; does the deactivation argument successfully reveal a hidden disposition; and does the internal nature of the lattice collapse the difference between a fink and a mask? Also say who is more right overall, and point out anything important that both sides overlook—particularly about whether a disposition can exist if its manifestation conditions are permanently and intrinsically blocked.
