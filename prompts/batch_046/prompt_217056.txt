After a long argument with a friend about whether consent can be hollow even when no one lied or threatened anyone, I am preparing a ten-minute defense of a specific thesis for an interdisciplinary workshop next Thursday. My claim is that valid consent requires not only the absence of deception, coercion, and incompetence, but also the absence of what I call "asymmetric conceptual framing"—a condition in which one party controls not merely the information presented, but the very conceptual categories through which the other understands the available options, so that certain risks or alternatives cannot be recognized as such by the consenter despite every literal statement being true and no explicit pressure being applied. I take this to explain why some technically compliant medical consent processes still fail to secure genuine authorization.

The audience is about fifteen people: analytic bioethicists and philosophers of law who are thoroughly familiar with standard informed-consent frameworks, plus two clinicians who are primarily concerned with administrative feasibility and liability. They are broadly autonomy-respecting but deeply skeptical of expanding invalidating conditions beyond deception, coercion, and incompetence; they are likely to view my proposal either as a redundant redescription of deception or as an unworkable, paternalistic expansion that undermines autonomy by treating every framing choice as potentially vitiating. Please draft my Q&A prep sheet: list the eight or so objections they will actually raise, ordered by likelihood and damage, phrased in the vocabulary they would use (for example, concerns about epistemic redundancy, operationalizability, or disguised paternalism). For each objection, provide a concise reply I can deliver in about two minutes, together with a brief note on where that reply is weakest. Finally, include the one question I am hoping nobody asks—likely whether my view implies that ordinary commercial advertising or clinical jargon automatically invalidates consumer or patient consent—and suggest how to deflect it without conceding the core thesis.
