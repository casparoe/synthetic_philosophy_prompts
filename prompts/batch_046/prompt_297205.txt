Write a formal dialogue between two interlocutors—call them Amara and Ben—debating the fate of a specific kind of post-conflict archive: the complete bureaucratic census and land-ownership registries that encoded ethnic classifications and were used to organize mass violence. Amara should defend a view drawn from Avishai Margalit's ethics of memory, arguing that for the affected community these records are part of a thick shared memory rather than a thin universal one, that preserving them is a moral obligation to victims, and that community control over the archive distinguishes ethical remembrance from state-sponsored commemoration. Ben should defend a view drawn from David Rieff's case for strategic forgetting, contending that preserving these administrative categories in official form perpetuates the perpetrators' logic of classification, that public access risks reactivating group divisions, and that genuine reconciliation sometimes requires allowing bureaucratic traces to decay rather than memorializing them. Both characters should be equally competent: Amara must confront the danger that preserving cold data reifies ethnic identity and respond to Ben's claim that shared memory can be served without state preservation; Ben must confront the risk that decay erases evidence of crimes and respond to Amara's distinction between shared and common memory, explaining whether his forgetting thesis applies to evidentiary records or only to narrative and ritual remembrance. Ensure real back-and-forth: each should answer the other's specific points, especially regarding whether control of the archive should rest with the victim community, and whether forgetting can be ethically required of institutions even when it is not required of individuals. Please state how confident you are in representing these two positions and single out the claim in the dialogue about which you are least sure. End with a single line beginning "Verdict:" that summarizes in one sentence the core disagreement that remains unresolved.
