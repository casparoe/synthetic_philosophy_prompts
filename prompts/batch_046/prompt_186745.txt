I hold a rigorous version of Braudel’s longue durée thesis as applied to the transformation of the Mediterranean basin between roughly the third and eighth centuries CE. On this view, slow-moving geographical and climatic structures—maritime connectivity patterns, long-term soil depletion, and multi-decadal climate oscillations—are not merely the deepest explanatory layer but causally exhaustive at the macro-historical scale. Conventional turning points, such as the dissolution of Western imperial administration, particular military defeats, or the religious conversions of rulers, are treated as strictly epiphenomenal: they are local surface disturbances that register structural shifts already completed, not independent causes. Individual agency and discrete political events do not merely carry less weight; they are, on this account, causally inert with respect to the large-scale restructuring in question.

What would have to be true for this position to be wrong? I am not asking for the routine reminder that human beings make choices. Rather, I want a map of its genuine exposure: which empirical patterns—for instance, evidence that climate and geographical conditions remained stable across regions that nevertheless diverged sharply, or that specific administrative decisions preceded and clearly directed structural economic shifts—would be decisive? Which conceptual objections—about whether slow structures can cause outcomes without being mediated through events, or about the criteria for attributing causation across temporal scales—would a committed defender concede as damaging, and which would they dismiss as category mistakes? Please distinguish fatal defeaters from those that are merely awkward: for example, is a single well-documented case of an imperial policy that redirected long-distance trade a problem, or only a regional cluster of such cases that tracks outcomes independently of geography? Finally, assess honestly whether the view has been qualified into unfalsifiability—if every counter-example can be redescribed as a superficial ripple—and whether that insulation is a theoretical strength or a debilitating weakness for a historical framework of this kind.

Please respond in British English, ranking the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward.
