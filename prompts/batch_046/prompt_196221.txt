You serve as the lead compliance auditor for a municipal heritage-preservation board that enforces an ordinance protecting any building deemed "historically significant" from demolition without board approval. The ordinance lists incremental contributing factors—age, density of original architectural ornament, documented association with civic events, physical integrity, and neighborhood contextual importance—but assigns no weights, thresholds, or aggregation rules. Because significance accumulates feature by feature, your board faces a sorites-style boundary problem: removing one decorative cornice or subtracting one year of age never clearly changes status, yet a well-preserved 1920s civic building and a generic 1970s office block are obviously different. You need a reusable, fifteen-minute decision procedure that an auditor with no philosophical background can apply consistently, and that produces a documented result for every case.

Ground the procedure in philosophical analysis of vagueness rather than in statistical methods. Specifically: treat the boundary between "significant" and "not significant" as sharp but epistemically hidden; explicitly break the tolerance principle at designated audit nodes rather than allowing indefinite incremental slide; and establish a formal "borderline zone" (not a forced binary) where classification is suspended pending peer review. The procedure should be ordered: (1) feature inventory with standardized weights; (2) contextual salience adjustment (how neighborhood density changes feature importance); (3) a stability check testing whether the classification survives small perturbations in weight or feature removal; (4) a reverse-sorites removal test (would stripping away the most recently added feature change the result?); and (5) a final audit node that forces a binary decision only if stability and contextual tests both pass, otherwise routing to suspension.

The deliverable must include: a flowchart-in-words checklist with decision gates; explicit flags for judgment calls that cannot be eliminated (e.g., how to weight accidental versus intentional ornament); misfire conditions where the procedure will over-classify or under-classify; and three worked applications—(A) an 1887 courthouse with original facades and documented civic use; (B) a 1972 standard office building with no notable associations; and (C) a 1954 industrial warehouse that acquired ornamental ironwork through three unrelated renovations, scores high on raw feature count, sits in a dense historic district, yet lacks any unified historical purpose. For case C, the procedure should yield a counterintuitive result that the board is likely to resist; identify the exact gate where resistance arises and state whether the procedure permits an override or requires suspension. Finally, state clearly what the procedure cannot do: locate the hidden sharp boundary, resolve weight disagreements without board judgment, or replace democratic review for suspended cases.
