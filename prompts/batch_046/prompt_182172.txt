I am preparing a ninety-minute seminar for first-year philosophy graduate students enrolled in an epistemology course. The session's aim is to teach the inside-view versus outside-view distinction not as a forecasting heuristic but as a normative framework for epistemic modesty in philosophical reasoning. The students are analytically sharp but habitually overconfident: they treat their own intuitive judgments about arguments—say, whether a given objection to compatibilism succeeds—as transparently accessible, and they systematically underestimate how frequently peers with equivalent training hold opposing views with equal conviction. I need a teaching plan rather than the explanation itself. First, specify the dependency order: what conceptual piece must be firmly in place before the inside/outside distinction can function as a tool for self-evaluation rather than as a mere label for cognitive bias. Second, propose the single carrying scenario or example that should structure the entire session; I had considered asking each student to predict whether they personally will revise a specific philosophical belief after encountering a standard objection they have not yet considered, and then comparing that to the base-rate revision rate among philosophers in their reference class, but I want your judgment on whether this case has the right properties and what it must include or exclude. Third, map the predictable misconceptions in the order they will arise—such as conflating the inside view with first-person phenomenology, treating the outside view as simple averaging that ignores relevant differences, interpreting epistemic modesty as blanket self-doubt rather than calibrated deference, or assuming the distinction collapses into subjective versus objective probability—and indicate exactly where in the sequence each should be intercepted and defused. Fourth, suggest what should be explicitly left out at this level, with an acknowledgment that it is being omitted. Finally, design one diagnostic question or exercise for the end that would reveal whether the students have internalized the normative structure of the idea, as opposed to having merely memorized the terminology.
