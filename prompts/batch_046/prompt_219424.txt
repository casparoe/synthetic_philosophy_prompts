I am designing a case-study exercise for my graduate seminar on Spinoza’s political philosophy, and I want my students to treat his institutional judgments not as a single deductive system but as a track record that can be read through competing interpretive rules. To set up the framework, I have reconstructed three of his verdicts from the Political Treatise and the Theological-Political Treatise. First, he approves a monarchy in which a broad, non-hereditary council of citizens shares actual decision-making authority over war and taxation, on the grounds that this distributes power and prevents the king’s will from being absolute. Second, he rejects an aristocracy governed by a small, closed patriciate with hereditary seats and no rotation, arguing that such concentration breeds corruption, faction, and the arbitrary domination of the multitude by the few. Third, he endorses direct democracy—defined as an assembly of all free citizens not under another’s authority deciding laws by majority—as the most absolute expression of sovereignty, even while conceding practical difficulties in large territories. From these three positions I can isolate at least two plausible governing principles: one that makes legitimacy depend primarily on distributing and checking power so that no single will dominates arbitrarily, and another that makes legitimacy depend on the multitude itself actually exercising governing authority rather than delegating it away.

Here is the new constitutional case I have constructed to sit awkwardly between these precedents, and I want a precise prediction or recommendation. Consider a scheme—call it rotational delegation—for a large republic in which legislative and executive functions are not separated. All free adults, without property qualification, select by lot each year a committee of fifty citizens who hold full administrative authority for that term. The committee must report to the full assembly, which retains only the power to recall the committee by majority vote at any time; the assembly itself never deliberates on specific statutes or policies. If Spinoza wants to be consistent with his own track record, should he approve or reject this arrangement? Which of the three precedents is the closest analog—the monarchy-with-council, the rejected closed aristocracy, or the endorsed direct democracy? And if his precedents genuinely underdetermine the case, what principle would he be adopting with an approval, and what different principle with a rejection?

Please render the verdict with the analogies mapped explicitly, justify it with reference to the competing rules I have identified, be honest about any residual ambiguity, and then specify the one further institutional feature—an additional detail not present in the case as described—that would most clearly distinguish whether Spinoza’s judgment is being driven by the anti-arbitrary-will principle or by the direct-sovereignty principle.
