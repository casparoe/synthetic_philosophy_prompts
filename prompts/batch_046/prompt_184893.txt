Consider a pedagogical practice adopted in some classics and applied-ethics programs: instructors assign students to read paired biographies from Plutarch's Parallel Lives—say, a Roman statesman matched with a Greek philosopher—then ask them to write a counterfactual biography in which the same individual is raised entirely within the other culture. The grading rubric rewards preservation of what the syllabus calls the moral silhouette: the structural pattern of deliberative choices, emotional responses, and commitments to others that made the original figure admirable or blameworthy, regardless of how the cultural content of those choices changes. At the same time, these programs explicitly reject hagiographic framing, insisting that biography teaches only if it shows virtue and vice as products of habit, reflection, and circumstance rather than as inborn or divinely conferred gifts.

What philosophical commitments about moral learning, the portability of character, and cross-cultural evaluation does this combined practice presuppose? Reconstruct the implicit theory: does it require that virtues are culturally invariant psychological structures, or only that they are functionally analogous across contexts? Is the rejection of hagiography consistent with treating a moral silhouette as stable across counterfactual cultures, given that the latter seems to treat character as detachable from the social practices that formed it? And if the underlying view of virtue were replaced by a radically historicist or constructivist account—one on which virtues are constituted by the very cultural norms they inhabit—what specific changes would be required in the assignment design, the grading criteria, and the justification for using biography as a moral-educational tool?
