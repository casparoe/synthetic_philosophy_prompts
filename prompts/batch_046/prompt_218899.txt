I have been reflecting on a recurring structure in jurisprudential debates about the separation of law and morality, and I find myself unable to name the exact configuration I keep encountering. The pattern is this: a legal system officially maintains that the validity of its rules depends solely on pedigree—enactment by an institution recognized by a rule of recognition—while insisting that law and morality are conceptually distinct. Yet when courts reach the ambiguous periphery of statutes, they do not exercise raw, arbitrary discretion; instead, their reasoning is consistently channeled by an implicit normative framework, something like a background coherence condition, that determines which interpretations are legally plausible. The result is that substantive moral considerations systematically filter legal outcomes, but they do so through interpretive methodology rather than through explicit validity conditions, so that the conceptual separation remains intact in principle while being systematically circumvented in practice.

This does not seem to be Fuller's internal morality of law, since Fuller's conditions—generality, promulgation, clarity, non-contradictoriness, and the rest—are procedural prerequisites for the very possibility of a legal order, not substantive moral filters applied within particular acts of interpretation. Nor does it fit Hart's model of the core and penumbra exactly, because Hart treats penumbral discretion as a form of judicial law-making that lies outside the law itself, exercised when legal rules run out; here, by contrast, the normative filter operates inside the interpretive machinery and is treated by judges as part of what it means to apply the law correctly. I am therefore asking whether there is an established term for this specific arrangement: a legal order that preserves the separation of law and morality at the level of the rule of recognition, but embeds a substantive moral constraint within its interpretive practices so that the separation holds in theory yet collapses in operation.

If there is no established name, I want to understand precisely where this phenomenon sits relative to inclusive legal positivism, which allows moral criteria to enter the conditions of legal validity, and relative to interpretive theories that deny any strict separation altogether. Should it be understood as a concealed form of natural law, as a distinctive species of structured judicial discretion, or as an unrecognized structural feature of legal interpretation? I would also like its boundaries mapped: under what conditions does such an embedded interpretive filter count as part of the law itself rather than as an external moral correction, and how can one distinguish it from ordinary auxiliary hypotheses about legislative intent or social purpose? I will not be satisfied with a bare label; I want to know what the phenomenon is, what it is not, and where its edges lie.
