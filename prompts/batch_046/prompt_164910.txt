There's this municipal planning model I've seen in a few cities — they call it "neutral optimization zoning," though that might not be the official name. The board takes in quantified inputs only: traffic flow models, energy grid capacity, tax-base projections. If there's a rounding error in the algorithm, that's treated as a real planning failure that can kill a proposal. But if residents say the project destroys the character of their neighborhood or turns the area into something they dont want to live in, that gets filed under preference data, not a planning error — essentially non-cognitive. The idea, I think, is that planning should stay scientific and avoid metaphysical or value-laden claims. It sounds a lot like Carnap's split between theoretical language (truth-evaluable) and practical language (just expressions of attitude or will), and maybe his view that rational planning has to be value-free. But I'm wondering if the practice actually needs that Carnapian framework, or if it's just hiding value judgments inside the metrics it picks. What philosophical commitments about value, rationality, and democratic input does this institutional asymmetry presuppose? Reconstruct the implicit theory, say whether several rival views could license the same rules, and tell me what parts of the board's procedure would have to change if we dropped the fact-value split and treated ethical claims about community character as rationally assessable in the same way as traffic data.
