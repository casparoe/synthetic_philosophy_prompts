Write a dialogue, in American English, between two philosophers of cognition—Dr. Aris and Dr. Bren—debating whether analogy-making is the complete foundation of cognition or whether it depends on a structurally prior, non-analogical operation. Dr. Aris should defend the view associated with Hofstadter and Sander’s Surfaces and Essences: that cognition is fundamentally analogy-making, that categories are clusters of analogical mappings, and that even apparent counterexamples can be absorbed as analogies operating at a different level of abstraction or as mappings to anti-prototypes. Dr. Bren should defend an opposing view centered on what she calls “contrastive delimitation”: the cognitive fixing of a boundary not by mapping similarities onto a prototype, but by specifying the precise conditions under which an analogical mapping must be rejected, using practical examples such as scientists calibrating instruments by identifying systematic disanalogies between sensor outputs and model predictions, where measuring deviation seems to require a non-analogical metric of error rather than a further analogy. Both characters must be equally competent—no strawmen—and the exchange must contain real back-and-forth: each should respond directly to the other’s actual points rather than talk past them. Dr. Aris should specifically attempt to absorb contrastive delimitation into analogical cognition, arguing that rejection conditions are themselves analogies to failure-prototypes or meta-level mappings; Dr. Bren should resist this by explaining why measuring deviation structurally requires a representational frame that is not itself generated by similarity-mapping. The dialogue should remain in the conceptual weeds without asking for bibliographies, citations, reading lists, or references to obscure literature, and it should treat the stakes seriously—including whether a cognition that operates only through positive similarity-mapping can ever generate the negative conditions necessary for its own correction. Conclude without resolving the dispute, but with each character having clearly stated what they take to be the cost of conceding the point.
