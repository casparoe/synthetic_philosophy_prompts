You are a senior philosopher of law with expertise in normative logic, institutional design, and the ontology of rules. Before you begin drafting, take a few minutes to brainstorm in writing: list the conceptual distinctions that must be preserved for this collaboration to be non-vacuous, identify which disagreements are genuinely about legal ontology versus verbal framing, and sketch the concrete institutional cases you will include so that both sides can register predictions in advance.

Your task is to draft the joint statement that two opposing camps — the Hierarchical Entrenchment camp (HE) and the Dynamic Self-Constitution camp (DSC) — could both sign after a good-faith adversarial collaboration on nomic self-amending rules and the paradoxes of legal self-reference.

HE maintains that legal systems avoid reflexive paradox by stratifying norms into discrete authority-levels, so that amendment protocols apply only to lower-level norms and never reflexively to their own enabling conditions; any appearance of self-application is dissolved by assigning the meta-rule to a separate tier. DSC maintains that such stratification either fails or merely relocates the reflexivity, and that amendment rules must be interpreted as performative acts whose validity is constituted through their own exercise rather than derived from a higher-level authorization.

The document you produce must contain the following sections.

These should cover: (a) the formal structure of self-amending rules and what makes them reflexive; (b) the logical form of the reflexivity problem, treated generally rather than through any single canonical example; (c) the distinction between logical contradiction and institutional instability; (d) what each camp accepts as a successful resolution versus a mere relocation of the problem; and (e) shared empirical or institutional predictions about how real legal systems behave when faced with recursive amendment pressure.

Second, a disagreements table. For each remaining disagreement, provide: (i) the claim formulated in wording that the HE side accepts for itself; (ii) the claim formulated in wording that the DSC side accepts for itself; (iii) a neutral one-sentence formulation of the crux.

Third, the cruxes. Identify the sub-questions such that settling them would move at least one side. At least one crux must concern the concept of nomic inertia — the idea that a legal rule’s resistance to self-amendment can be calibrated or measured independently of the rule’s substantive content — and whether this concept is coherent or merely metaphorical. Another crux should address the distinction between synchronic self-reference (a rule referring to itself at a single temporal stage) and diachronic self-reference (a rule referring to its own future or past instances across amendment cycles), and whether this distinction dissolves or deepens the paradox.

Fourth, for each crux, specify a concrete institutional design, hypothetical case, or logical argument that both sides agree would bear on it, together with each side’s predicted interpretation or outcome recorded in advance.
