Write the desiderata that any adequate theory of multi-causal historical explanation must satisfy, with particular attention to cases of granular overdetermination—where a macro-historical outcome is produced not by redundant sufficient causes operating at a single level of analysis, but by cross-scale causal contributions (structural conditions, institutional mechanisms, and individual agency) that are each sufficient for the outcome only under counterfactual dependencies on the presence of the others. For each desideratum, specify the motivating case or conceptual pressure behind it—such as avoiding both explanatory redundancy and the elimination of genuinely efficacious lower-level causes, preserving counterfactual sensitivity across scales, and distinguishing background-enabling conditions from overdetermining ones. Flag which requirements are non-negotiable for any coherent account and which reflect the preferences of particular historiographical camps (for example, the demand that structural explanations always take explanatory priority over agential ones, or that narratives must integrate rather than rank causes). Explicitly state whether any subset of these desiderata is jointly unsatisfiable—especially between maintaining that individual actions remain causally efficacious and maintaining that structural conditions are genuinely overdetermining rather than merely permissive—given standard constraints on causal explanation. Briefly indicate how a covering-law approach, a mechanistic account, and a narrative-integration view stand against the non-negotiable requirements, without scoring every camp in detail. Keep the conceptual weight on the criteria themselves and on their interrelations rather than on an exhaustive evaluation of existing theories.
