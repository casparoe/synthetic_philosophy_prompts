I'm working through the extended-mind literature with a study group and keep running into the same analogy: a notebook, smartphone, or conversational partner is to cognition what a prosthetic limb or cochlear implant is to the body. The people pressing it want to license a parity-style conclusion—if an external resource plays the same functional role as an internal process, is automatically available, and is endorsed by the user, then it literally belongs to the cognitive system, just as a prosthetic belongs to the body when it restores motor or sensory function. I want an explicit audit of the mapping: is biological memory the limb, the notebook the prosthetic, reliability the feedback loop, automatic endorsement the proprioceptive integration? Then I want the fractures laid out precisely—prosthetics are incorporated through a single body's sensorimotor loop, whereas a notebook can be lost, shared, or edited without the user's knowledge, and biological memory is reconstructive rather than archival. Does the analogy quietly import the idea that system boundaries are fixed by integration with one biological agent, and if so, does that undermine rather than support pushing the boundary past the skin? Once those disanalogies are on the table, does the conclusion—that Otto's notebook literally contains beliefs—survive, or does it collapse into the weaker claim that cognition is sometimes scaffolded? And if the prosthetic analogy can't carry the weight, is there a better one—distributed computation, a musician's instrument, something else—that captures what extended-mind theorists want without borrowing features that apply only to bodily extension? I'm less interested in a general defense than in whether this specific analogy can bear the conclusion it's being asked to carry.
