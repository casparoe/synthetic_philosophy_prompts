I hold a specific, non-skeptical reading of the butterfly episode in the second chapter of the Zhuangzi. Rather than treating it as an early form of Cartesian dream skepticism—an argument that we lack a criterion to distinguish waking life from dreaming—I take it to advance what I call transformational identity: the claim that the self is not a persisting substance that remains identical across different states of being, but a transient configuration that can be fully realized in radically different forms (human, butterfly, or otherwise) without any underlying owner that survives the change. On this view, Zhuang Zhou’s uncertainty about whether he is the man who dreamed of being a butterfly or the butterfly now dreaming it is Zhuang Zhou is not an epistemic puzzle about which state is veridical, but a metaphysical demonstration that asking “which one is really me?” presupposes a substantial self that the text denies. The reference to wu hua—the transformation of things—is therefore meant to apply not only to external phenomena but to the first-person subject itself, informed by commentary traditions that read identity as a pattern of qi rather than a fixed essence. What would count against this position? I want the strongest objections—textual, philosophical, and conceptual—that should move someone holding this view. Specifically: which passages in the Zhuangzi or in related Warring States texts resist being read through this metaphysical lens? Are there arguments from personal identity theory—such as psychological-continuity or animalist accounts—that show the self cannot genuinely be identical with a butterfly without leaving some essential residue behind? Does the view collapse into an implausible universalism about consciousness, or does it avoid that collapse only by becoming unfalsifiable? What considerations about memory, agency, or the structure of first-person reference would genuinely threaten it rather than merely being awkward? I also want an honest assessment of whether defenders can absorb standard criticisms—for instance, that Zhuangzi is simply playing with paradox rather than advancing a metaphysics—by adding qualifications, and whether such insulation is a virtue or a vice. Please distinguish decisive defeaters from merely inconvenient ones, say what a defender should themselves concede would weaken the case, and evaluate whether this reading risks anything substantial or has been qualified into safety.
