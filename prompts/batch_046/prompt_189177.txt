I'm trying to determine what I actually think about Kant's universalizability tests—specifically how the Formula of Universal Law and the Formula of Humanity interact—by testing my gut judgments against concrete cases. Here are my verdicts.

Case 1: A borrower makes a false promise to repay, knowing he cannot and will not. I judge this impermissible; universalizing it seems to generate a contradiction in conception, since the institution of promising would collapse.

Case 2: A wealthy bystander refuses to rescue a drowning stranger when she could do so at negligible cost, reasoning that she owes nothing to people she does not know. I judge this impermissible; universalizing indifference seems to generate a contradiction in will, since as a rational being who may someday need aid she cannot consistently will a world of universal neglect.

Case 3: A gifted mathematician resolves never to develop her talents, living entirely in passive consumption, harming no one and asking nothing of others. I judge this impermissible; it seems to involve a contradiction in will with respect to her rational nature.

Case 4: A physician withholds a terminal diagnosis from a patient who has not explicitly asked, because she genuinely believes false hope improves survival outcomes. Her maxim is: "As a physician, when withholding the truth may extend life, I will not disclose terminal prognosis unless directly questioned." I keep reversing on this. It feels structurally close to Case 1—both involve deliberate deception—yet my gut says it might be permissible, or at least not clearly wrong. Would universalizing this maxim undermine medicine, or is there an escape clause?

Case 5: A person volunteers at a shelter solely to gain social prestige, treating the recipients purely as instruments to her reputation, with no regard for their dignity, though the material aid is real. I judge this impermissible, not because of a contradiction in conception but because it violates treating humanity as an end. Yet the recipients are genuinely helped, which makes me hesitate.

Case 6: A professional writer creates false but flattering restaurant reviews for pay, always disclosing her deception to her close friends but never to the public or the clients. Her maxim includes the clause: "I deceive strangers for profit, but maintain transparency within my circle of trust." I think this is impermissible, though the disclosure to friends makes it feel different from Case 1.

Case 7: A government institutes a mandatory contribution to a collective defense fund, with an exemption for anyone who can demonstrate catastrophic personal harm from contributing. I think this maxim can be universalized without contradiction.

The tension that puzzles me most is between Case 1 and Case 4: both center on intentional deception, yet I want to condemn the first and allow or excuse the second. Meanwhile, Case 5 and Case 3 both involve neglect or instrumentalization of rational capacities—one directed outward, one inward—but feel different in severity.

Is there a single principle—perhaps involving both the logical form of the maxim and a condition about practical identity or role-based reliance—that captures Cases 1–3 and 5–7, accommodates Case 7, and either explains why Case 4 is an outlier or shows that my leniency toward it should be overturned? Please state the principle precisely, indicate which clause handles which case, predict its verdict on a case I haven't listed—say, a teacher who simplifies a complex scientific theory for beginners in a way that is technically false but pedagogically necessary, without disclosing the simplification unless asked—and be explicit about which of my verdicts it cannot save. If no unified principle works, present the two best candidates, the case that forces a choice between them, and which verdict each requires me to abandon.
