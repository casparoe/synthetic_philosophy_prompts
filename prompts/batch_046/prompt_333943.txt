Historians have debated for generations whether the French Revolution was driven mainly by class struggle, by the diffusion of Enlightenment ideology, or by sheer contingency—a bankrupt crown, a failed harvest, and a weak monarch. Every camp cites the same turning points—the calling of the Estates-General, the Terror, Thermidor—but assigns them opposite weights: deep structural force, runaway idea, or avoidable accident. I want to know whether this impasse is really about missing evidence, or whether it is a disagreement about explanatory standards—about what counts as a fundamental cause versus a mere trigger—and about whether the pattern in which revolutions devour their children names an unavoidable political dynamic or just a post-hoc narrative we apply after violence subsides. Is there a hidden empirical or formal question that could settle the dispute, or are we using one phrase, "the cause of the Revolution," to ask three different things? I am asking for a diagnosis of the disagreement's structure, not a survey of interpretations, together with an honest view of what—if anything—would actually constitute progress.
