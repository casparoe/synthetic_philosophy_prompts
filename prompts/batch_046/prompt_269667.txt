I am a doctoral student working at the intersection of philosophy of religion and comparative literature, and I am preparing a seminar paper on whether traumatic choice can be modelled through religious categories of obedience. I would like you to read William Styron's Sophie's Choice—specifically the Auschwitz selection scene in which Sophie is compelled to choose which of her two children will live, her subsequent relationship with Nathan Landau, and her eventual suicide—through the lens of Kierkegaard's Abraham and the teleological suspension of the ethical, as developed in Fear and Trembling. Rather than treating Sophie as a simple analogue to Abraham, I want the reading to take seriously the distinction between the knight of faith and the knight of infinite resignation: Sophie receives no divine command, there is no ram substituting for the sacrificed child, and her ethical world is not restored but permanently fractured. Where, precisely, does Kierkegaard's framework illuminate Sophie's choice—perhaps in the moment she suspends the universal ethical duty to protect both children for the sake of a higher, if perverse, telos of saving one life—and where does the novel resist that framework, particularly in the absence of any transcendent justification and in the way her trauma is narrated through Stingo's limited, sympathetic but ultimately inadequate perspective? I am especially interested in whether the work suggests that a teleological suspension without divine restoration collapses into mere arbitrariness or psychological devastation, and what this implies for Kierkegaard's claim that the ethical can be suspended only for the sake of a genuinely higher end. Please treat both the philosophical argument and the novel's narrative architecture with equal seriousness, avoid reducing either to an illustration of the other, and conclude by saying what Sophie's Choice adds to the philosophical debate about obedience and ethical suspension that abstract argument alone does not capture. Please write your response in British English.
