Socrates therefore argues that knowledge is superior not because it produces better action—since true belief guides conduct equally well—but because it possesses diachronic stability. The knower can retain the belief across counterfactual perturbations, whereas the holder of mere true belief might abandon it under slight cognitive pressure. Nevertheless, Socrates implicitly denies that true belief has any practical utility until it is converted into knowledge, which undermines his own concession that both states guide action correctly. Furthermore, the process of tethering should be understood as empirical corroboration through repeated observation, which ties the belief to external evidence rather than to an internal rational account. In this respect, the Platonic answer anticipates modern reliabilist conditions, since both require that a belief be anchored in a robust causal connection to the world.
