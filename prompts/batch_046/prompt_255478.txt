For a consistency check before my seminar presentation, here is my position on Williams's integrity objection and the associated problem of demanding one calculative step too many in moral reasoning:

(1) I accept Williams's claim that asking an agent to justify saving a loved one over strangers by appealing to overall utility introduces an alienating, extra step that violates integrity.

(2) I take this to show that ground projects generate agent-relative permissions—reasons that are structurally resistant to cross-personal trade-offs rather than merely weighted more heavily within an impartial calculus.

(3) I further hold that integrity requires what I term archival fidelity: an agent's practical identity must maintain a non-revisable narrative arc linking past commitments to present action, so that abandoning a ground project for utility is identity-rupturing, not merely costly.

(4) Nevertheless, I think a physician-parent may rationally choose to save two strangers over their own child in a triage case if the welfare gap is large, without losing integrity, provided they feel deep regret of the kind that accompanies unavoidable conflict rather than guilt.

(5) I maintain that this regret does not imply wrongdoing; it registers the collision of incommensurable goods, not a failure of justification.

Which pairs are genuinely in tension, which only appear to be, and how severe is each? In particular, does (3)'s demand for non-revisable narrative arcs conflict with (4)'s permission to override a ground project? Does (2) already rule out (4) regardless of affective response, or does (5) genuinely dissolve the tension? Rank the conflicts by severity, identify the least costly revision if one commitment must be abandoned, and avoid bibliographies or reading lists.
