I am a PhD student in philosophy of science running a 75-minute workshop with about 18 mixed graduate students in philosophy and economics. Design a runnable in-class simulation of rational herding in information cascades that does not reduce the phenomenon to peer pressure. Specifically, structure the private signals so that each participant receives a noisy indicator of an unknown state of the world, but the precision of that indicator is itself drawn from a distribution that is private to the participant—so that observing predecessors' choices reveals information not only about the world but about the quality of their signals, meaning correct Bayesian updating alone can lock the group into a systematically wrong collective decision. Specify the rules, materials, a non-monetary payoff scheme tied to small collective stakes (e.g., points that determine the next session’s discussion topic), and a three-round sequential timeline that lets the cascade emerge without facilitator intervention. The debrief should map each stage onto the formal cascade mechanism—private belief, observed action, belief revision, final action—explain what will predictably go wrong the first time (participants attributing convergence to social conformity rather than rational inference) and how to correct it, and be frank about which formal feature the physical setup cannot reproduce (the continuous common-knowledge-of-rationality belief space) and must be supplied verbally by you. Also include a brief note on what observed behavior during the game would convince you the cascade was genuinely rational rather than socially driven, and identify where your reasoning about the mapping between this simulation and the standard model is weakest.
