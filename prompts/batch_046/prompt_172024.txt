As a retired experimental physicist now working through philosophy of science and epistemology, I have been struck by the long stalemate between Peirce's convergence account of truth—truth as the opinion fated to be agreed upon by inquirers in the indefinite long run—and James's practicalist view, which ties truth to what satisfies experiential demands and proves expedient in belief-adoption. For over a century, commentators have either harmonized the two by diluting Peirce's communal, asymptotic ideal into a vague hope or exaggerated James's position into subjective whim, yet neither camp moves; each treats the other's central cases as either irrelevant or misinterpreted. My working hypothesis is that the disagreement is not substantive about the nature of truth at all, but structural: Peirce is proposing a normative model of scientific inquiry analogous to the convergence of repeated measurements toward a limit value, while James is proposing a decision-theoretic framework for individual belief management under resource and risk constraints, so they are answering different questions—one about the semantics of theoretical claims, one about the pragmatics of belief—under the single label pragmatism. Is that diagnosis correct? Or is there a hidden formal or empirical disagreement about verification conditions, fallibilism, or the role of community that I am overlooking? Please diagnose the stall using what each side actually says about verification, communal inquiry, and individual satisfaction, and tell me what, if anything, would count as genuine progress: a sharp distinction between collective epistemic norms and individual cognitive economics, a reconstructed formal model of Peirce's limit-process, or the recognition that the debate is ill-posed because it conflates descriptive and normative aims.
