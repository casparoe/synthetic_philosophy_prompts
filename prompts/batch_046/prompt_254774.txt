I have been trying to understand Skolem's paradox on my own, without formal training in logic, and I keep returning to what feels like a central tension: if first-order set theory can have both countable and uncountable models that are genuinely indistinguishable from inside the system, then any account of the paradox that claims to resolve it must do more than merely restate this technical result. I want to know what the real desiderata are for a philosophical resolution—what such an account must explain, preserve, or rule out to count as adequate rather than merely clever, and whether some of the demands people place on it are genuine constraints or just prejudices of particular camps.

Specifically, I am wondering whether any adequate theory must satisfy the following conditions, with the case behind each. First, it must be fully consistent with the Löwenheim-Skolem theorem itself and must not deny the existence of countable models of ZFC containing uncountable domains—this seems non-negotiable, but I want confirmation. Second, it must explain why working mathematicians continue to speak of "the" universe of sets or "the" real numbers as if there is a unique intended structure, without dismissing that practice as mere psychological convenience—though I am unsure whether saving this practice is a genuine desideratum or just a commitment of mathematical realism. Third, it must clarify whether invoking second-order logic with full semantics actually solves the determinacy problem or merely relocates it to an equally unsettled question about what fixes the range of second-order quantifiers—this is the part I am most unsure about, and I suspect some subset of these demands may be jointly unsatisfiable. Fourth, it should address whether the paradox reveals a limitation specific to formal languages or a broader limitation in any attempt to determinately refer to an infinite totality from within a finite or formal perspective—a distinction I have been calling "the determinacy gap," though I do not know whether this framing is standard. Finally, the account should not rely on assuming that we have direct, non-formal access to the "true" cardinality of the continuum or other sets, since that seems to beg the question; but it also should not collapse into the claim that there is no fact of the matter about cardinalities at all, unless that conclusion genuinely follows from the other commitments.

If some combination of these requirements—particularly the demand for determinacy together with the refusal to invoke extra-formal intuition—turns out to be impossible to meet together, I would like to know that explicitly, since that would seem to be a result in itself rather than a failure of any particular theory. I am not asking for a bibliography, a reading list, or a survey of named authors; I am asking for the criteria themselves, with the motivating case behind each, and a brief indication of which existing approaches satisfy or violate them, so that I can understand where the real disagreement lies and which requirement does the most work in excluding candidates.
