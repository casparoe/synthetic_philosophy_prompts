I take the following position: in societies where moral standing is constituted by reciprocal visibility rather than by autonomous self-legislation, shame is not merely an alternative to guilt but the structurally appropriate response to wrongdoing. Moreover, a culture can genuinely choose to cultivate shame over guilt—not by explicit decree, but through institutional design: replacing punitive frameworks with rituals of public acknowledgment, organizing moral education around relational honor rather than rule-compliance, and treating failure as a breach of one's visible position within a network rather than as a private debt. For such communities, this choice is not only possible but normatively superior.

What would have to be true for this view to be wrong? I want more than the familiar objections that shame is oppressive or that guilt respects autonomy. Which psychological mechanisms, anthropological patterns, or formal features of moral motivation would genuinely undermine the claim that shame can be institutionally selected and normatively dominant? Are there historical or hypothetical cases where a culture deliberately engineered shame-based institutions and produced outcomes that even defenders of the view would concede as damaging? Which of these potential defeaters would a committed proponent dismiss—say, by attributing harm to corrupt implementation rather than to the emotion itself—and does that risk insulating the position from falsification? Rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, and indicate whether the view risks being qualified into unfalsifiability, and whether that is a problem for a thesis of this kind.
