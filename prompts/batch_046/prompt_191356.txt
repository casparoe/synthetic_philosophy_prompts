As a retired experimental biologist who has recently begun studying philosophy of consciousness, I have noticed a recurring protocol in laboratories that study subjective experience—during binocular rivalry, anesthetic emergence, or psychedelic trials. Subjects are asked to provide free-form descriptions of what they experience. These descriptions are then transcribed, but before entering the quantitative dataset they are translated into a standardized taxonomy that strips away intentional and emotional framing: a report of seeing crimson is coded as a visual-state token at a specified time, and a report of vertigo is coded as a vestibular-state token. The translated strings are treated not as direct windows onto consciousness but as descriptions of a fictional heterophenomenological world, and all statistical modeling is performed on these neutralized representations. The original transcripts are preserved in an archive but are excluded from the formal analysis.

What philosophical commitments does this institutional practice presuppose? Must one already accept that first-person reports are irreducibly theory-laden, or could a direct realist about phenomenal properties adopt the same protocol for purely pragmatic reasons? Does the treatment of reports as descriptions of a fictional world commit the practice to eliminativism about raw feels, to instrumentalism about consciousness, or merely to a methodological bracketing that leaves ontology untouched? Is the distinction between the raw transcript and the translated token philosophically stable, given that the taxonomy itself embeds theoretical choices about which states exist? And if one replaced the underlying view—say, with a view that treats experience as directly self-intimating—would the protocol have to change, or could it survive as a mere administrative convenience?
