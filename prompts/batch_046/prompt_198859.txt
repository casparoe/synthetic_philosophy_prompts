I'm taking this course on Nietzsche's thoughts about history and life, where he talks about three ways people use the past: one where you look for big heroes to copy, one where you just preserve old stuff because it feels like home, and one where you study history mainly to break away from it and start fresh. Anyway, I keep noticing this other move in arguements that doesn't sit right in any of those buckets. It's when someone digs up a long list of past failures -- collapsed revolutions, dead utopias, reforms that backfired -- not to say "tradition is good, keep it" and not to say "we're free to invent something new," but specifically to build a kind of precedent wall that makes any present change feel naive or hubristic. The past isn't inspiring action or protecting identity; it's being used like dead weight to prove that all the possible moves have already been tried and shut down. I've seen this in climate debates ("every past civilization collapsed when they overreached") and in workplace talk ("every attempt to flatten hierarchy eventually failed, so don't bother"). Is there an established name for this specific attitude? Or is it just a sick version of one of the three? If it's its own thing, what exactly distinguishes it -- how is it different from ordinary pessimism, and where does its edge lie with that third attitude, the one that also digs up failures to free yourself?
