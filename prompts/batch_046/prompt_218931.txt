I run a graduate seminar in philosophy of psychiatry and social science, with twelve students meeting for a single ninety-minute session. I want them to experience the pull of Ian Hacking's idea that human classifications are interactive — that labeling a kind of person can change the behavior of those so labeled, which in turn reshapes the classification itself — rather than merely reading about it. To keep the exercise from collapsing into a familiar lecture on well-known diagnoses, I want the group to invent and apply a fictional taxonomy: specifically, categories for attentional drift patterns during collective decision-making in high-noise environments. The simulation should have two phases. In the first, half the students act as observers who must agree on sharp diagnostic boundaries for behaviors they witness in scripted vignettes. In the second, the other half — now assigned one of these invented labels — must complete a collective decision task under hidden rules that differ subtly by category, rules they do not initially know. The loop should become visible when the labeled participants begin to self-monitor or alter their reasoning in ways that confirm the observers' initial distinctions, even though the observers remain unaware of the hidden task differences. Design the full activity: roles, materials, a timeline with checkpoints, and a non-monetary incentive structure that makes compliance with the hidden rules feel natural rather than forced. Then provide a debrief that maps specific moments in the simulation onto the conceptual structure of interactive kinds and looping, predicts what will go wrong the first time — for instance, observers refusing sharp cutoffs, or labeled participants ignoring the constraint — and explains how to handle each failure. Finally, be frank about which feature of looping this ninety-minute exercise cannot reproduce, likely the long-term historical sedimentation of categories across generations, and specify exactly what the facilitator must supply verbally to cover that gap. Do not ask for readings or citations; the point is the lived structure of the activity itself.
