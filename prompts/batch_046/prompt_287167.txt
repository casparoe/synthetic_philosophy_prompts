As a high school student studying international law and moral philosophy, I have become puzzled by a persistent split in how global institutions treat mass atrocities. International criminal tribunals apply the Genocide Convention's definition with strict legal precision: prosecutors must prove specific intent to destroy a national, ethnical, racial, or religious group, which means that large-scale killings directed at political dissidents, social classes, or other collective identities fall outside the charge regardless of scale or cruelty. At the same time, the very same international bodies—in their memorial museums, educational programs, and official commemorative statements—routinely describe those very excluded atrocities as genocide, treating the legal boundary as largely irrelevant to historical and moral naming. What theory of categorization and value does this institutional divergence presuppose? Does it assume that legal precision and moral remembrance serve irreducibly different purposes and therefore require separate vocabularies, or does it tacitly commit to the view that the Convention captures only a subset of a single underlying moral reality that institutions are obliged to name in full? Reconstruct the implicit philosophy here: is the practice consistent with Raphael Lemkin's original conception of genocide as the destruction of human groups regardless of political classification, or does it reveal that the legal category has become a bureaucratic gatekeeping mechanism that separates prosecutable atrocities from others for reasons largely disconnected from the gravity of the harm? And if an institution replaced this dual practice with a unified vocabulary—either extending the legal definition to match its moral usage or restricting commemorative language to match legal precision—what would that change about its understanding of justice, memory, and the politics of naming?
