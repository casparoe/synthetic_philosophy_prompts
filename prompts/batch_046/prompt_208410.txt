As I construct a scene for my novel in which a disillusioned mathematics tutor conducts an after-hours workshop for six graduate students, I need you to design the exercise that drives the scene: a participatory game, lasting seventy-five minutes in a bare seminar room, that makes the participants feel—through physical movement, token manipulation, and hidden-role conflict—the precise tension between Cantorian actualism and strict finitism regarding the infinite. The participants should come away having experienced, not merely discussed, why an actual infinite is not a very large potential collection, and why finitism denies not only magnitude but the coherence of completed pairing itself.

The deliverable should be a fully runnable activity. Provide rules, materials (movable chairs, a central table, and an unending but physically constrained supply of identical tokens), and a hidden-role payoff structure: perhaps three participants secretly hold actualist commitments and three finitist ones, with a collective reward—say, a shared resource—unlocked only by unanimous agreement that a one-to-one correspondence has been definitively completed. The rules must force them to attempt a Cantorian pairing with an ever-renewed supply, then abruptly impose a finitist boundary (a fixed budget of moves, or an inviolable physical perimeter) that makes the correspondence collapse in a visible, social way. They should encounter the failure before anyone names the philosophical problem.

Then provide: a precise timeline (setup, pairing attempts, boundary imposition, crisis, resolution); a debrief that maps each collapse-point onto its formal counterpart—the diagonal argument, the refusal of a completed totality, the asymmetry between addition and removal; an honest account of what will predictably fail the first time (a participant will redefine success, or roles will be exposed prematurely) and how to recover; and a frank admission of which feature—most likely the ontological commitment to a completed infinite set as an actual object—this embodied simulation cannot reproduce, leaving the facilitator to supply it verbally.

Write this in a formal, polished register suitable for a novelist's preparatory notes: unsentimental, theatrically precise about the room's layout and the quality of the participants' silences, and attentive to the moment when abstract mathematics becomes a social fracture. Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citations; the design should stand on conceptual mechanics alone.
