I am working on a novel in which cooperation is treated not as a moral achievement but as a mechanical accident sustained only under specific conditions of error, and in developing its conceptual backbone I have adopted a position I want rigorously stress-tested: in iterated prisoner's dilemmas played under realistic execution noise—where every cooperative act carries a small, irreducible probability of misfiring or being misread—strict reciprocity strategies such as Tit-for-Tat suffer catastrophic error cascades that more forgiving, probabilistic strategies avoid not through generosity but through structural resilience; consequently, the evolutionary persistence of cooperation depends less on punishment mechanisms than on what I call calibrated leniency, and this implies that moral sentiments resembling forgiveness are not evolutionary spandrels but functional prerequisites for maintaining cooperation in noisy environments. What would genuinely count against this view? I want the arguments, formal results, and empirical observations that should move me, arranged by how decisive they would be: whether evolutionary simulations with well-specified noise parameters actually show punitive strategies outperforming lenient ones in sustained interaction; whether the concept of noise I rely on is too vaguely specified to generate falsifiable predictions about real cooperative systems; whether observed patterns in microbial mutualism or human social exchange contradict the claim that leniency is structurally necessary rather than merely advantageous; and whether defenders of classic reciprocal altruism would themselves concede that sustained noise undermines the stability of pure reciprocity. I also want an honest assessment of whether the position risks being qualified into unfalsifiability—for instance, by redefining forgiveness or noise to accommodate any counterexample—and whether such insulation is a fatal weakness for a thesis that purports to explain the origins of moral emotion rather than merely redescribe it.
