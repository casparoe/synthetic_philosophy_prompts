I am in my final year of sixth form studying A-level Philosophy, and our teacher recently introduced Derek Parfit’s teletransporter problem as part of our unit on personal identity. I was discussing the case with friends over lunch and realised that every version we could recall was the familiar scan-and-print scenario; I should like an original case that still isolates the core dispute, but one engineered precisely rather than invented casually. Please construct a thought experiment to the following specifications: the scenario should describe a realistic medical programme—no teleportation beams, perfect predictors, demons, or other devices—lasting twelve months, in which a patient’s brain is gradually replaced neuron by neuron with functionally identical synthetic equivalents, the patient reporting uninterrupted consciousness throughout. Upon completion, the synthetic brain is housed in a synthetic body, whilst the excised original biological tissue is preserved intact in a viable cryogenic stasis vat, capable in principle of being reactivated but left dormant. The entire scenario must be confined to one paragraph, framed for a sixth-form audience, and must present only descriptive facts without announcing theoretical conclusions.

Please engineer the case so that psychological-continuity theory and bodily-continuity theory agree entirely on those descriptive facts—on what physically and mentally occurs—yet deliver opposite verdicts on whether the individual in the synthetic body is numerically identical to the pre-operation patient. The divergence must depend exclusively on whether spatiotemporal continuity of the original biological substrate is necessary for survival, not on any divergence in memory, character, or mental content. After presenting the scenario, structure your response in the following sections, in this order and with these approximate lengths: The Scenario (around 150 words); Verdicts and Reasoning (around 200 words), stating what each theory concludes and why; The Isolating Feature (around 150 words), identifying precisely which element generates the disagreement, what happens if that element is removed—for instance, if the original tissue is destroyed rather than preserved—and why; Defence and Critique (around 150 words), explaining how a defender of the theory that fares badly might attack the case, perhaps by appealing to causal dependence, uniqueness, or the relevance of potential reactivation; and Confidence Note (around 50 words), in which you say how confident you are in your analysis and explicitly single out the point about which you are least sure. I should prefer that you avoid bibliographies, reading lists, or specific citations, and that you do not invoke highly obscure neurophilosophical sub-theories that a small model might not have properly memorised, though the synthetic-replacement twist may itself be somewhat novel. Please write in British English and maintain a formal, polished register throughout.
