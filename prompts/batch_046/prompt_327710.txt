You serve on the funding and ethics review board of a national interdisciplinary research foundation that supports studies at the intersection of empirical social science and normative philosophy. The board meets quarterly and votes on proposals that carry both methodological and philosophical stakes.

For the upcoming meeting, you must circulate a brief written opinion—approximately 800 to 1000 words, in formal institutional prose suitable for board members from sociology, philosophy, and policy backgrounds—addressing the following proposal: “Structural Autonomy and the Gender-Equality Paradox: Preference Formation Under High-Egalitarian Regimes.” The proposal examines the comparative pattern, documented across welfare-state regimes, that stronger institutional gender equality and more comprehensive economic safety nets often coincide with larger—not smaller—gender divergence in career choices, particularly in STEM fields and care-sector enrollment. Rather than treating this as a correlation requiring only sociological explanation, the investigators advance a theoretical framework they term “structural autonomy.” On this view, high-egalitarian regimes do not suppress gendered preferences but instead remove survival and economic coercion, thereby revealing preference structures that were previously masked by constraint. They argue that this undermines both naive biological essentialism, which predicts stable cross-regime patterns, and standard social-constructivist egalitarianism, which predicts convergence under equality. In place of these, they propose reconceiving justice not as outcome-equalization or preference-neutralization, but as the assurance that divergent preferences are formed under conditions of genuine structural independence—meaning that economic security is present, but that social norms and internalized schemas are not themselves treated as coercive merely because they are gender-correlated.

The board is divided. Several members fear that funding this research risks lending empirical authority to essentialist interpretations that could be weaponized to justify rollback of equality policies. Others contend that declining to fund amounts to viewpoint discrimination, and that the “structural autonomy” framework offers a genuinely novel normative contribution: it separates the metaphysical question of whether preferences are “natural” from the normative question of whether they are justly formed, without collapsing either into the other.

Your memo should recommend a concrete course of action—approve with specific methodological safeguards, approve contingent on the addition of an independent philosophical advisory panel, defer pending substantive revision of the normative framework, or reject with explicit reasoning—together with an implementation timeline appropriate to the board’s quarterly cycle. You must treat the strongest consideration on the losing side with intellectual honesty: if you recommend approval, engage seriously with the risk of misinterpretation and policy misuse; if you recommend rejection or deferral, engage seriously with the charge that the board is suppressing uncomfortable findings. The reasoning should remain accessible to non-philosophers on the board without diluting the conceptual precision of the stakes involved—specifically, what follows for our understanding of nature, culture, and justice if preference divergence under equality is best explained neither by biology alone nor by residual sexism alone, but by the interaction of structural freedom and socially transmitted schema. Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, citation requests, or appeals to specific named studies. Write in formal, polished institutional prose.
