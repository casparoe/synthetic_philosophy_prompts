I'm working through my own judgments about personal identity across engineered mind-uploading scenarios, and I want to see whether they converge on a single principle or force me to abandon some intuition. Here are five cases with my tentative verdicts. First, a gradual replacement in which synthetic neurons are substituted one by one over years, maintaining continuous electrochemical and functional integration; the subject reports an unbroken stream of consciousness, and I take this to be genuine survival of the original person. Second, an interrupted scan: the biological brain is destroyed and a digital copy is instantiated only after a forty-eight-hour processing gap during which no running process exists; despite perfect psychological fidelity, I feel this is replication rather than continuation, so the original does not survive. Third, a branching overlap: at sixty percent replacement, the running synthetic-biological hybrid is copied onto an independent server that operates for a week while the original hybrid continues, after which the original is destroyed; I think the hybrid was the continuing person, while the server copy is merely a psychologically identical branch, not the same individual. Fourth, a stochastic atomic reconstruction in which the entire brain is replaced at once not by copying synapses but by regenerating them probabilistically from a compressed statistical model of the original weights; the result is behaviorally and mnemonically indistinguishable, yet because there is no causal chain linking specific prior synapses to specific successor ones, I am inclined to say this is a high-fidelity replica, not survival. Fifth, a chunked hemispheric replacement in which the left hemisphere is replaced gradually over years while the right is replaced suddenly in a single procedure; the subject survives but reports a strange phenomenological asymmetry—continuous selfhood on the left, a felt discontinuity on the right—suggesting to me that continuity is not an all-or-nothing property but can come in degrees without destroying identity altogether.

Is there one precise principle—whether psychological continuity with the right cause, causal dependence of a specific kind, or something else—that delivers all five verdicts, or must I overturn at least one? If your principle saves the gradual and hemispheric cases but rules out the interrupted scan and the stochastic reconstruction, how does it distinguish the branching server copy from the original hybrid without invoking an ad hoc no-branching clause? And what does it predict for a hybrid scenario I have not listed, in which stochastic reconstruction is performed gradually neuron-by-neuron, combining the causal discontinuity of the fourth case with the temporal spread of the first? Please state your principle precisely, indicate which of my verdicts it cannot accommodate, and explain what further case—say, one involving episodic-memory consolidation failure during a gradual upload—would make you revise it. I also want you to say explicitly where your reasoning is most vulnerable: what argument or empirical consideration about first-person memory or causal dependence would cause you to change your mind, particularly about whether the stochastic atomic case might after all count as survival if we take phenomenal continuity seriously.
