As a software engineer with no formal background in philosophy, I am trying to understand a well-known thought experiment—often called Kavka’s toxin puzzle—in which an agent is offered a substantial reward if she can sincerely form the intention to drink a mildly harmful toxin the next day; the reward is granted as soon as an external observer verifies her intention, but once the money is received she has no further reason to drink, since the toxin only causes temporary illness with no compensating benefit. The puzzle asks whether it can be rational to form an intention to perform an act that one knows will be irrational to carry out once the conditions for reward are satisfied.

I want to compare this philosophical puzzle with a structural analog from distributed systems that I have worked with: an intent-verified commitment protocol in which a node broadcasts a cryptographically signed message committing to execute a costly rollback operation later, receives a network token reward immediately upon verification of the signature, and then retains the technical ability to abort the rollback at zero additional cost once the reward is credited, because the protocol verifies only the initial commitment, not the follow-through. In this scheme, rational agents routinely sign without executing, and the system treats the signed intent as credible enough to release the reward.

Please compare Kavka’s toxin puzzle with this distributed commitment protocol, treating both as problems about the rationality of forming an intention to perform a future costly action when verification and reward are decoupled from execution. In particular, address the following: whether the “rationality of intention” problem in the human case is structurally identical to the “credible commitment” problem in the protocol; whether the absence of an external cryptographic verifier in the human case introduces a category error that makes the analogy fail; how the fact that the agent in the puzzle is both the signer and the only observer of her own mental state changes the analysis relative to a network of nodes; and whether the protocol’s reliance on observable reputation effects after an abort has any meaningful parallel in internal human deliberation. Do not provide a bibliography, reading list, or citations; explain both concepts from first principles and assume I have access only to the descriptions given here.
