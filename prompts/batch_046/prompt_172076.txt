I am a retired field ecologist who spent decades studying social insect behavior and have only recently begun studying philosophy of mind systematically. I am preparing a single ninety-minute session for a seminar of about fourteen graduate students in philosophy and cognitive science. I want them to feel the conceptual force behind the question of whether an ant colony can possess a mind—not as an abstract analogy, but as a lived coordination problem. The session should engage Douglas Hofstadter’s dialogue in Gödel, Escher, Bach (1979), in which an ant colony referred to as Aunt Hillary is considered as a potential conscious subject composed of non-conscious ants, though I do not want students to read the dialogue beforehand or hear its title named during the exercise itself.

To prevent the session from becoming a familiar rehearsal, I want the activity to center on a specific, somewhat artificial feature of collective cognition: the colony’s working memory is not stored in any individual ant, but is physically inscribed in the curvature and density of pheromone trails that decay exponentially. Retrieving a past collective decision requires that a critical mass of ants simultaneously occupy nodes arranged in a precise geometric pattern—something no single ant can initiate, represent, or verify. The philosophical tension should emerge organically when the group realizes that successful retrieval depends on a spatial configuration that none of its members can survey or intend.

Design a runnable classroom activity—whether a floor simulation, a structured role-play, or a deliberation with rules and small stakes—that makes this tension visceral. Specify the division of participants into roles (if any), the materials needed, the rules governing individual action and collective outcome, and an incentive scheme that requires no real money. Provide a precise timeline for the ninety minutes, including how the rules should be arranged so that participants encounter the failure of individual knowledge, the emergence of a correct collective move, and the ambiguity over whether that move was guided by a single mind or by distributed mechanics, all before any debrief names these stages.

Then provide the debrief: map specific moments in the activity onto (i) the reductionist claim that only individual ants exist, (ii) the holist claim that Aunt Hillary exists as an integrated subject, and (iii) the intermediate problem of whether geometric trail-retrieval counts as mental representation or merely as environmental scaffolding. Be explicit about what will predictably go wrong the first time this is run—group dynamics, misinterpretation of rules, emotional resistance to the absence of a controller—and how a facilitator should handle each failure. Finally, be frank about which feature of collective mentality the activity cannot reproduce (for example, phenomenal consciousness or a first-person perspective) and must therefore be supplied by the facilitator in discussion afterward.

Do not provide a bibliography, a reading list, or assigned citations. Do not ask participants to reference sources. Keep the framing formal and suitable for advanced students, but ensure that the conceptual difficulty lies in the rules and dynamics of the game rather than in obscure terminology.
