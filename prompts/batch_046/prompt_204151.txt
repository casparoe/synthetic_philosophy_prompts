I'm trying to determine what makes a market repugnant, judging by my reactions to concrete cases. Selling a kidney to save a stranger strikes me as permissible and not corrupting. A pharmaceutical firm raising the price of an essential drug fifty-fold during a shortage strikes me as deeply wrong. Selling one's vote in an election strikes me as wrong in a different register — not merely unfair but degrading. Paying a stranger to attend a funeral so the ceremony looks well-attended strikes me as odd but not repugnant; it seems analogous to hiring an actor. A "moral-repair contract," in which an offender pays a specialist to deliver a court-ordered apology with AI-monitored emotional sincerity and a fee scaled to the victim's measured distress reduction, strikes me as corrupting the apology, though I struggle to explain why this is worse than hiring a lawyer to present legal arguments. Paying for faster elective surgery in a private hospital strikes me as acceptable. Yet parents paying a "priority access fee" to move their child up a publicly funded cancer-trial queue strikes me as wrong, despite the structural similarity to the private surgery case. Is there a coherent principle that captures the wrongness of price-gouging and vote-selling, distinguishes the funeral actor from the apology contractor, and resolves the queue-jumping tension? If not, which verdict should I abandon? Please state the principle precisely, apply it to an unlisted case — for instance, a futures market in which investors bet on verified emotional-distress metrics for named private individuals — and say honestly which of my judgments the principle fails to accommodate.
