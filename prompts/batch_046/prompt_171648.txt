I model entities in distributed virtual worlds through "binding contracts": an object exists only when an explicit contract lists the sub-entities that must be co-instantiated, together with dependency and inheritance rules, but with no built-in assumptions about spatial adjacency, causal interaction, or physical continuity. I am fluent in this vocabulary—contracts, co-instantiation conditions, dependency graphs, null contracts, universal contracts, and contract inheritance—and I have only a basic grasp of metaphysics.

Please restate the special composition question and its three standard answers—unrestricted universalism, nihilism, and restricted composition (e.g., views that require spatial contact or causal integration among parts)—entirely within my contract vocabulary. For each position, specify the corresponding contract conditions: what kind of contract (if any) licenses the existence of a composite, and what rules govern its parts.

Then give the residue report. Identify which philosophical distinctions map cleanly onto differences among contract types; which can be expressed only by distorting my framework (for instance, by treating spatial proximity as a dependency edge or causal unity as shared state mutation); and which have no counterpart at all—especially whether an "arbitrary sum" of unrelated entities corresponds to a null contract, a maximally permissive universal contract, or is structurally impossible in my system. Finally, assess whether the residue indicates that my ontology lacks resources the philosophers genuinely need, or whether parts of the traditional debate depend on presuppositions (continuous space, intrinsic causal powers) that my framework deliberately omits and that therefore dissolve rather than translate.
