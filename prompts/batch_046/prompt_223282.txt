In debates over iterated amplification and distillation, proponents often invoke the craft-guild apprenticeship: a master decomposes complex work into teachable sub-skills, trains apprentices who eventually assume parts of the labor, and over generations the tradition is compressed into an explicit manual that preserves essential technique while shedding the master’s tacit judgment. Critics reply with the successive-photocopy analogy: each round of decomposition and compression introduces noise, so the final manual reproduces the form of the craft without its functional competence. Audit both analogies precisely: map what corresponds to the master, the apprentice, the manual, tacit judgment, and noise in the actual process of recursive amplification and distillation; distinguish disanalogies that matter for the intended conclusion from those that do not; and identify which features of the source domains—social transmission in guilds, physical degradation in copying—are quietly imported into the target but do not apply. In particular, examine whether either analogy captures the specific risk that alignment-relevant properties depend on contextual, non-decomposable judgments that resist being rendered as explicit sub-tasks, and whether such properties survive the compression inherent in distillation. Which analogy, if either, can actually support the claim that decomposition preserves both competence and alignment? If neither survives intact, propose a more accurate analogy that captures the tension between scalability and fidelity in recursive training.
