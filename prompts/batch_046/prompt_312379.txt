After leaving a long-held institutional position that offered security in exchange for a kind of moral quiet, I have found myself returning repeatedly to the question of whether comfort without choice is precisely the happiness Dostoevsky's Grand Inquisitor promises when he offers bread in exchange for freedom. This has made me want to read Ursula K. Le Guin's short story The Ones Who Walk Away from Omelas through the Inquisitor's claim that human beings will always prefer miracle, mystery, and authority to the burden of self-determination.

In the story, an entire city's radiant happiness depends on the abject suffering of a single imprisoned child, and while most citizens accept this bargain, a few silently depart for an unknown darkness. I am asking for a reading that takes both texts with equal seriousness: does the child's confinement function as the bread the Inquisitor offers—material and emotional security purchased by surrendering freedom to another? Do the walkers genuinely reclaim freedom, or does their exit risk being a performance of purity that leaves the structural sin untouched, much as the Inquisitor predicts people will avoid? Where specifically does Le Guin's narrative resist the Inquisitor's psychology—perhaps in the ambiguity of what awaits beyond the gates, or in the way knowledge of the child is both indispensable to the citizens' joy and corrosive to it? I want the analysis to avoid reducing the story to a mere illustration of the parable; instead, identify scenes or narrative choices that dramatize a tension argument alone cannot capture, such as whether the walkers suffer a moral remainder even in departure, or whether their choice represents an adaptive preference for ignorance over complicity. Finally, what does the story add to the philosophical debate about freedom and happiness that Dostoevsky's dialogue, read in isolation, does not?
