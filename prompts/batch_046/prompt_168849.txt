Please describe the most important implications of Amartya Sen's thesis that famines are political crimes rather than natural disasters, using the Bengal famine of 1943, the Irish famine of 1845–1852, and the Great Leap Forward famine of 1959–1961 as comparative cases. Focus specifically on what this distinction implies for contemporary crisis-response protocols: how should an organization decide whether it is facing an entitlement collapse that demands political or institutional intervention versus an aggregate food shortage that demands technical or logistical aid? Address the tension between Sen's emphasis on democratic accountability as a safeguard and the fact that all three cases—colonial administration, limited-suffrage parliamentary rule, and authoritarian central planning—shared a common pattern of suppressing local information about scarcity. What does this similarity suggest about the institutional conditions necessary to prevent entitlement collapse that go beyond the mere presence or absence of formal democratic structures?
