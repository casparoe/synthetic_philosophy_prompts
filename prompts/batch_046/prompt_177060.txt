Consider the passage in Plato's Republic, Book II, in which Glaucon introduces the myth of the ring of Gyges. He describes a shepherd who finds a ring in a chasm that makes him invisible, then uses it to enter the palace, seduce the queen, kill the king, and seize power. Glaucon asks us to imagine that both a genuinely just person and a genuinely unjust person received such rings, and claims that both would behave identically—taking whatever they wished, committing crimes without fear—because justice is practiced only involuntarily, out of fear of punishment and desire for reputation.

I am trying to understand a precise logical tension in this argument. Glaucon notes that the unjust man typically works hard to maintain a false appearance of justice, which suggests that reputation-management requires strategic effort. If invisibility removes all risk of exposure, it also removes the need for that labor. But this raises a problem: the just person, on Glaucon's own account, does not value justice for its social rewards. So if the ring eliminates social rewards and social risks alike, why should it affect his motivation? He should still be liberated from reputation-management, yet continue to act justly, precisely because he never valued justice instrumentally. Does this mean Glaucon has silently collapsed justice itself into reputation-management? Or is there a hidden premise—perhaps that justice is always and only a form of social coordination that requires an audience—that allows him to claim both men would converge on injustice? Can you help me identify the exact point where his analogy breaks down, if it does?
