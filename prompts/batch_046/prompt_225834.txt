Exam question: Malcolm contends that dreaming is not experiencing because a sleeping person lacks the conscious awareness and the behavioral conditions that constitute having an experience. Dennett's cassette theory holds that during REM sleep the brain constructs a narrative and inserts it into memory, so that upon waking the subject reports a story that was never lived through as a continuous conscious stream. First, determine whether Dennett's account escapes Malcolm's conceptual objection or merely relocates it. Second, consider a sleepwalker who, upon being awakened, immediately offers a coherent narrative about what she was experiencing during the episode, despite being in a state of reduced consciousness. Would Malcolm and Dennett classify this case differently from REM dreaming, and does the comparison strengthen or weaken Dennett's position?

Student answer: Malcolm's argument is that dreams cannot be experiences because during sleep a person is unconscious and therefore lacks the sensory awareness and behavioral criteria necessary for experience. He believes that experience requires being awake and able to report events as they occur, so since sleepers cannot do this, they are not experiencing anything. Dennett's cassette theory addresses this by proposing that during REM sleep, brain processes generate a narrative and store it in memory like data written to a tape. When the person wakes up, they retrieve this memory and treat it as a report of past experience. This means Dennett avoids Malcolm's objection by showing that there is indeed a conscious process occurring—namely, the brain's encoding of the narrative—so the sleeper is not entirely unconscious. The actual experience then takes place upon awakening, when the subject becomes aware of the stored story. Thus Dennett relocates the experience from the sleep period to the waking recollection, but this still counts as an experience of dreaming, which means Malcolm is wrong to deny that dreaming involves experience altogether. Turning to the sleepwalking case: a sleepwalker who reports a narrative upon being awakened is similar to a REM dreamer because in both cases there is reduced consciousness followed by a report. However, Dennett might say the sleepwalker is actually conscious during the episode and encoding the narrative in real time, unlike in REM sleep where the encoding happens without consciousness. Malcolm, by contrast, would deny that either case involves experience during the episode itself, since both occur during states that fail his criteria. Therefore the comparison weakens Dennett's position, because if sleepwalkers can genuinely experience and encode narratives while partially conscious, then REM sleep might also involve some hidden consciousness that Dennett's theory unnecessarily denies.
