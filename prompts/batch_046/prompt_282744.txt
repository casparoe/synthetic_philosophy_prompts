I just came out of a long, somewhat circular argument with a close friend about whether the DNA–Gödel isomorphism is a real structural parallel or merely an evocative metaphor that philosophers of biology have overused. We were discussing self-replication in the context of what he insisted on calling “typogenetics”—the idea that a genome contains not only the instructions for proteins but also the machinery (ribosomes, polymerases, tRNA) required to interpret those instructions, creating a loop in which the message builds the reader. At several points he shut down my objections by repeating a line he had clearly picked up somewhere: ‘the genome is not merely a message, but a strange loop that writes its own reader,’ and he seemed to think this settled everything. To him it implied that biological replication achieves a kind of self-referential bootstrapping—what he half-jokingly called “typogenetic closure”—that formal logical systems can only approximate before running into contradiction, as though the cell literally solves the problem of self-reference that Gödel showed was unavoidable in arithmetic.

I am taking a course on philosophy of biology that touches on these analogies, and I find exactly this claim floating around in an unexamined form—part metaphor, part technical assertion about universal computation, part dismissal of formal worries about circularity. Before I return to the conversation, I want to understand precisely what theses are packed into that slogan. Could you lay out the different precise readings it could have: the trivial biological reading (DNA encodes the components that decode it); the analogical reading (transcription-translation is structurally isomorphic to Gödel numbering or to a universal Turing machine interpreting its own description); the literal-constructive reading (that codons function like Gödel numbers assigning arithmetic values to amino acids, so the ribosome acts as a physical proof-checker performing a kind of biological self-reference); and the philosophical-overreach reading (that life has thereby “solved” the liar paradox or escaped the incompleteness theorems). For each, say whether it is defensible, whether it genuinely supports the conclusion that biological self-replication is structurally identical to Gödelian self-reference, and which version my friend was likely invoking when he used the line to dismiss worries about circularity. I do not need a bibliography or a reading list—just a disambiguation with verdicts that I can take back to the argument.
