Having retired from a career in condensed-matter physics, I have lately turned to philosophy of technology and find myself perplexed by a stalemate that seems to pervade discussions of whether artifacts possess politics. On one side stand those who maintain that material designs carry intrinsic political properties: a bridge constructed with insufficient clearance for public buses, or an energy infrastructure that requires centralized oversight for safe operation, does not merely permit but structurally enforces particular social orders, irrespective of the intentions of those who build or use it. On the other side are those who insist that technology is fundamentally neutral, that any political consequence derives from human interpretation, institutional embedding, or contingent patterns of use, and that attributing politics to concrete and circuitry is therefore a category error. The empirical descriptions are not in dispute—everyone grants that certain infrastructures constrain behavior in predictable ways—yet the debate has persisted for decades without either camp conceding ground, and new case studies seem only to reproduce the same division.

My working hypothesis, shaped perhaps by my scientific training, is that the disagreement is not about missing empirical data but about a hidden divergence in explanatory standards: the determinist treats material form as a causal mechanism analogous to a physical law, whereas the constructivist demands an intentional agent or social context before anything can properly be called political. I wonder, however, whether this diagnosis is correct, or whether the dispute is instead verbal—hinging on competing definitions of politics—or substantive, resting on incompatible ontologies of relational properties in non-intentional objects, or even a case of two fields, design history and sociology of use, answering different questions under a single heading. To help me see clearly, I ask you to diagnose the impasse with reference to the familiar infrastructure examples, but also with reference to a more precise hypothetical: a resource-allocation algorithm that distributes goods solely according to physical proximity, with no encoded social categories, yet systematically produces residential segregation. Does this case show that politics resides in the artifact's structural entailments, or only in the interpretive framing of its outcomes? And given your diagnosis—whether it points to bedrock intuitions about agency and naturalness, to an ill-posed question, or to a formal distinction still to be articulated—what would actually count as progress? Would it require a conceptual clarification between structural entailment and interpretive framing, a formal model of technological constraint, or an honest admission that the disagreement is in principle unresolvable?
