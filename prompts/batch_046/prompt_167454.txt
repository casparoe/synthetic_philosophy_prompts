I've been trying to write up a short essay on biological function — specifically the etiological vs causal-role debate, but with a twist I haven't seen discussed much. Here's the gist with some rough notes.

Most people say a heart pumps blood either because that's its current causal role (the Cummins-style view) or because pumping is what hearts were selected for in evolutionary history (the Wright/Millikan-style etiological view). I think both miss a weird middle case. Imagine a fully synthetic heart designed in a lab with zero evolutionary history, implanted and working fine. The causal-role theorist says its function is pumping. The etiological theorist probably says it only has an assigned purpose, not a real biological function, since there's no selection history.

But now imagine we take tissue from that synthetic heart, culture it, and let it evolve in a bioreactor for hundreds of generations under direct selection for pumping efficiency. At some point, doesn't it develop an actual etiological function? Not instantly, but gradually? I propose a "lineage accrual" view: a trait's current causal role becomes its genuine biological function once it's been maintained by selection for some threshold of generations, regardless of whether the original origin was natural or artificial. Before that threshold, it's just engineered purpose; after, it's real function.

I'm worried this is either circular — defining selection in terms of function — or just arbitrarily picking a generation number. I also wonder if I'm misrepresenting the etiological camp: do they allow artificial selection to count? And does my view accidentally collapse back into pure causal-role for anything old enough?

Can you give me comments? I'm especially interested in whether the synthetic-to-evolved heart case is coherent, whether there's a hidden circularity in "selectional independance," and if this is actually interesting or just splitting hairs. Don't worry about being nice — I'd rather know if the central idea is dead on arrival.
