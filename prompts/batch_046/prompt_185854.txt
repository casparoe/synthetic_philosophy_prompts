I was arguing with a friend about the liar paradox and now I'm trying to write down my actual commitments for a philosophy of language study group, but I'm not sure they cohere. Here's my profile: I think truth is basically deflationary—there's no substantive property, just the disquotational schema working across contexts. I accept Tarski's hierarchical solution for formalized languages, but I don't think it applies to natural language because NL is too messy and context-bound for strict stratification. I also think semantic ascent is always legitimate; moving from "snow is white" to "it is true that snow is white" doesn't change the subject matter at all. I hold bivalance—every well-formed proposition is either true or false, no middle ground, no glut. I reject paraconsistent logic completely; contradictions are always errors, never true descriptions.

About the liar sentence specifically, I think "this sentence is false" expresses a fully determinate proposition with real truth-conditions, not meaningless noise. But I also think the paradox is dissolved pragmatically: the speaker is attempting incompatible speech acts at once (assertion and denial), so there's a hidden presupposition failure that makes the utterance self-cancelling rather than false or true. I want to keep classical logic, deflationism, and bivalance all together, and I don't want to give up on the liar expressing a real proposition. Do these actually hang together? Go through the pairs and tell me which tensions are real and which only look like problems. Rank the real ones by severity, say what has to give in each case and how costly that revision is, and then tell me the single change that would clean up the most tension without wrecking everything else.
