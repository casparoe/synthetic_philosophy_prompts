My mate J is pretty rigid about Popper's argument that you can't have proper laws of history, since unique events don't repeat. Here's her track record on proposals people have floated at her. One: she said Marx's claim that all history is class struggle isn't a law at all, just an interpretive framework, so Popper's critique doesn't even apply properly. Two: she allowed that industrialisation is usually followed by demographic transition within a couple generations, but only as a contingent correlation, not a law with neccessary force. Three: she rejected Spengler's idea that every culture runs through an inevitable cycle of birth, maturity and decay — classic historicist prophecy, she said. Four: she accepted the link between grain prices and population density in agrarian societies, but insisted that's an economic law that holds across contexts, not a historical one. Five: she turned down the claim that fiscal crisis always produces democratic revolution, noting that Qing China faced crisis and centralised instead.

Now a colleague proposed: whenever a society introduces standardised weights and measures for long-distance trade, it develops a centralised tax bureacracy within three generations, independant of its political form. What would J say? I'm less interested in the yes/no than in the rule she's actually running, the other rules that fit her five verdicts just as well, and which earlier case is the closest analog. If she rejects it, what principle is she adopting that her past judgements don't already cover? And what further case would best tell those competing rules apart?
