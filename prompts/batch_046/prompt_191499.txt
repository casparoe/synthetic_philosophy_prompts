A close friend serves on the board of a neighborhood association that drafted a meticulous covenant for maintaining common spaces. She recently noticed that the covenant's enforcement depends entirely on members' mutual reluctance to invoke legal penalties; there is no police presence, and a determined free-rider could absorb the benefits while ignoring obligations with little immediate consequence. This led her to a skeptical conclusion: the covenant is not genuinely just, but merely a temporary truce among people of roughly equal influence, and its authority collapses the moment the power balance shifts. At the same time, she insists she can sense a deeper standard of fairness—an ideal distribution of burdens that would apply even if no agreement existed—which the covenant approximates. She is now tempted to justify stricter enforcement by appealing directly to this deeper standard, arguing that using legal coercion in the name of true fairness would finally align power with justice. I told her this risks mixing two kinds of authority in a way that corrupts both, but I could not explain the mechanism clearly. How should she understand the relationship between the covenant's practical authority and this deeper fairness? If she is right that the covenant is ultimately sustained by the members' relative strength and willingness to punish, does that make it arbitrary? And is there a coherent position that admits the covenant is conventional and force-backed, yet denies that it is disposable—while also explaining why invoking 'true fairness' to justify coercion is precisely the error that destroys both?
