I am preparing a classification instrument for a graduate seminar in environmental humanities on whether the Anthropocene denotes a valid historical period, and—if it does—when that period begins and who possesses the authority to declare it. Write a branching taxonomy presented as a decision tree: every internal node should be a concrete claim or question that splits the logical space, phrased so that a student can answer it without already knowing the labels of the positions. The tree must be built from the distinctions themselves, not from a roster of authors.

Begin with the root question: can “Anthropocene” denote a single, globally coherent period at all? From there branch first by the nature of coherence (unitary global boundary versus regionally staggered or conceptually invalid), then by the source of authority (formal stratigraphic commission, interdisciplinary science, or distributed community-based recognition), and then by whether the framing names the human species as a whole or replaces it with a specific structural driver. You must locate exactly eight leaves in this space:

1. A stratigraphic-literalist position that fixes the start in the mid-twentieth century (anchored to physical markers such as radionuclide fallout or plastic deposition) and grants declarative authority exclusively to a formal geological body.
2. An “early Anthropocene” position that pushes the boundary back to the Columbian exchange or to the rise of agriculture, still treating the transformation as globally synchronous.
3. An interdisciplinary, non-stratigraphic position that retains the species-level framing but denies that only geologists may declare the epoch.
4. A structural-replacement position that rejects the species-level framing in favor of a system-specific driver (for instance, the Capitalocene framing) while still treating the transformation as globally synchronous.
5. A diachronic-regionalization position that denies any single global start date, treating industrial-ecological transformation as regionally staggered, with authority resting in interdisciplinary science.
6. A pluralist-epistemic position that combines regional diachrony with distributed authority to affected local communities rather than to centralized scientific bodies.
7. A conceptual-invalidity position that argues the term functions as an epistemic closure, imposing a Western geological ontology that erases indigenous temporalities which do not divide history into epochal boundaries.
8. A gradient-gradualist position that accepts the reality of large-scale ecological change but denies that any boundary is more than an arbitrary convention, rendering “Anthropocene” a rhetorical gradient rather than a period.

For each leaf, provide its label (named if a recognized view occupies it, “unnamed” if the combination is unoccupied) and exactly one sentence stating its most awkward commitment—the implication its defenders must accept but would prefer not to emphasize. Then identify at least two answer-combinations that your tree does not generate—for example, a view that combines strict stratigraphic authority with structural replacement of the species frame, or one that combines diachronic regionalization with insistence on a single global physical marker. For each missing combination, diagnose briefly whether it is conceptually incoherent, institutionally blocked, or merely unexplored.

The instrument should work for someone without web access: do not include bibliographies, reading lists, citations, or verbatim quotations. Phrase every node so that navigation depends on the reader’s judgments about coherence, authority, and framing, not on recognition of author names.

Finally, do not present this as a neutral catalog. Take a committed stance: defend which of the missing combinations are genuinely dead ends and which are live but neglected, and state explicitly which of the eight leaves offers the most defensible framing, giving your reasons. Keep the audience in mind—graduate students who can handle conceptual distinctions but should not need prior familiarity with the literature to navigate the tree.
