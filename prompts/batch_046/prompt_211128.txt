Next thursday I'm defending my view on split-brain patients at our philosophy-of-mind / cognitive-science joint seminer. Thesis: callosotomy patients don't harbor two full conscious subjects, they have one consciousness with a bifurcated executive architecture - the right hemisphere runs behavioral routines with proto-intentional content but lacks the integrative narrative structure required for selfhood, so phenomenal unity survives even though agential access splits. The audience is split: half are philosophers who read the classic Sperry and Gazzaniga experiments and think the "two minds" interpretation is just obvious, and half are neuroscientists who think "unity of consciousness" is a metaphysical ghost philosophers chase. They all know the hand-drawing and visual-field tasks cold, but none have followed the newer work on affect-transfer via the anterior commissure and subcortical pathways, which I'm using to argue there's a shared emotional substrate that keeps the phenomenal field unified. Give me the objections in the order they'll come, ranked by damage, phrased like each camp actually talks, with what I should concede up front, what I should hold firm, and my weakest point - probably the move where I say right-hemisphere emotional responses don't count as full conscious experience without just defining them away. Also include the question I'm hoping nobody asks, probably about whether my view means the right hemisphere can't suffer and what that implies for treating these patients ethically. Keep it tight, no bibliographies.
