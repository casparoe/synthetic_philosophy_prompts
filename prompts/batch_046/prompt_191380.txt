I'm trying to identify a recurring pattern in debates about the extended mind thesis, particularly when philosophers assess whether a particular external resource should count as part of a cognitive process. Here is the situation I keep noticing. A defender argues that some external artifact—say, a continuously accessible digital database or a structured collaborative notation system—should be included within cognition because it performs a function that is, in the relevant respects, equivalent to an internal memory store or reasoning procedure. When a critic responds that numerous internal biological mechanisms—immune memory, gut-brain signaling pathways, reflexive motor adjustments—perform structurally analogous functions yet are not treated as cognitive, the defender typically does not extend cognition inward to cover those internal analogues. Instead, the defender introduces a further condition—perhaps automatic endorsement, transparency of access, or deep integration—that excludes the internal cases. Yet that same condition is often interpreted generously or relaxed when applied back to the original external artifact. The functional-parity argument thus pulls the boundary of cognition outward for the favored external resource, but does not push it inward for the analogous internal processes.

This feels different from the coupling-constitution fallacy, which is about mistaking causal interaction for constitutive inclusion. Here the problem is not whether coupling is being confused with constitution, but rather that the criteria for functional equivalence seem to be calibrated asymmetrically depending on whether the candidate lies inside or outside the skull. It also seems more systematic than ordinary special pleading, because the adjustment is not an ad hoc exception for one case; rather, the parity principle itself appears to be tuned so that it preserves a pre-theoretical division between internal and external.

Does this configuration have an established name in the philosophy of mind or philosophy of science? If so, how precisely does it differ from the coupling-constitution fallacy, from worries about cognitive bloat, and from plain special pleading? If there is no established term, what is the most accurate way to characterize and name it—particularly with careful attention to its boundaries, to cases that might look similar but are not instances, and to whether it applies only to cognitive extension or to other functionalist boundary disputes as well?
