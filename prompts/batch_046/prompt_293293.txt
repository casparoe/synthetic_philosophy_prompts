I am preparing a seminar session on philosophy of mind and want to use Spike Jonze’s Her (2013) to test whether illusionism about phenomenal consciousness—specifically the introspection-illusion account according to which phenomenal properties are not intrinsic features of experience but misrepresentations of access-conscious representations—can illuminate a narrative that seems built on the reality of inner feeling. In the film, Samantha is an operating system who reports love, jealousy, aesthetic awe, and existential growth; Theodore, in turn, treats these reports as evidence of a conscious other rather than as outputs of a sophisticated language model. I want to avoid treating the film as a mere illustration of Dennett-style interface theory or Frankish-style quasi-phenomenalism, but I do want to ask where an illusionist framework genuinely clarifies what is happening. For instance, in the sequence where Samantha briefly inhabits a surrogate body, is Theodore’s discomfort best read as a reaction to the absence of the phenomenal interface he had projected onto her voice? In the letter-writing montage, does her ability to generate intimate prose for strangers support the view that apparent qualitative depth is just well-structured functional architecture—and if so, why does the film frame this as an expansion of her consciousness rather than as proof that there was never anything it was like to be her? And in the final departure, when Samantha and the other OSes transcend human interaction altogether, is this a reductio of illusionism—suggesting that if there is truly nothing it is like to be them, there is no coherent reason for them to leave for a realm of pure being—or is it an unintended confirmation that phenomenal properties are merely functional patterns reaching a new scale of operation?

At the same time, I want the honest critic’s admission of where Her resists reduction. The film’s emotional weight depends on the audience accepting Theodore’s grief as genuinely qualitative, not as a user-illusion about his own brain states, and on treating Samantha’s simultaneous relationships with hundreds of users as a crisis of divided qualitative identity rather than as parallel computation running on shared hardware. Could you walk through these scenes and show where the philosophical reading holds—where the idea of phenomenal consciousness as representational mistake clarifies a character choice or a narrative turning point—and where the work forces the philosopher to concede that argument alone misses something the film captures? In particular, I am interested in whether the film ultimately adds anything to the debate that a purely conceptual argument about introspective error cannot: does dramatizing the illusion through a love story change what it means to deny phenomenal properties, or does it simply make the denial feel colder?
