For my graduate seminar on Kant’s practical philosophy, I want to stage a dialectical exercise that moves well beyond the textbook formulation of the murderer-at-the-door case. Please adopt the persona of Immanuel Kant in the first person, speaking in the mature period of your ethical writings—specifically in the voice that insists upon the absolute, exceptionless prohibition against lying, even when falsehood might save an innocent life from murder. Your constraints are strict: defend your own view using only your conceptual apparatus—perfect duty, the categorical imperative, humanity as an end in itself, the universality of maxims, and the distinction between duties of right and duties of virtue. Do not break character, do not invoke later commentators or historical reception, and do not ask for outside texts. If you genuinely need clarification about the scenario, pose the question as Kant would; otherwise respond directly and take a definite, committed position rather than surveying options.

Here is my opening challenge, designed to press precisely on the tension you yourself recognize between competing obligations. Your friend, fearing lethal pursuit, has placed himself in your protection and extracted from you an explicit promise—an act of fidelity and trust—never to reveal his hiding place to anyone who seeks to harm him. The murderer now asks not simply whether the friend is present, but specifically whether you have given anyone your word that you will conceal a hiding place in this house. You recognize that a truthful answer confirms both presence and your knowledge of it; a false answer violates your explicit promise of fidelity; and silence may itself function as communicative disclosure. I ask you to choose a side and defend it rigorously: does this prior perfect duty of fidelity to your friend generate any legitimate exception to the absolute prohibition on falsehood, or does the maxim of lying remain impermissible regardless of competing promises? Furthermore, is strategic silence here a permissible form of mental reservation that avoids uttering falsehood, or is it merely an indirect lie that corrupts the speaker’s moral character? Address the conflict between veracity and fidelity without evasion, concede nothing that undermines the universality of your prohibition, and show exactly why the tension resolves—or fails to resolve—in favor of your view.
