I have recently been helping my parents evaluate whether to continue a long-term maintenance therapy that has shown consistent efficacy in studies completed over the past decade, and I found myself wondering what exactly licenses the inference that it will remain effective now that the observation window has moved forward. That led me to reconstruct Goodman's grue problem in a clinical register, and I have built an argument that seems to prove that such inferences are never justified. Here are the steps:

(1) Goodman's emerald example shows that observing all emeralds to be green before time t equally supports the hypothesis that they are grue—green before t and blue thereafter—because the two predicates are co-extensive over every observed instance.

(2) In clinical research, observing that Drug D produces remission in every trial completed before 2026 equally supports the hypothesis that D is genuinely efficacious and the hypothesis that D is "grue-efficacious"—effective in trials completed before 2026 and ineffective in those completed after.

(3) Because past data cannot discriminate between "efficacious" and "grue-efficacious," the inductive evidence for the two hypotheses is identical.

(4) If induction requires projecting a past regularity into unobserved cases, then we have no more reason to project genuine efficacy into 2027 than to project grue-efficacy.

(5) Therefore, no body of historical clinical data can justify prescribing D in 2027 rather than withholding it.

(6) Yet we clearly do justify continuing prescriptions across temporal boundaries, and it does not seem irrational to do so.

(7) So either clinical induction is systematically unwarranted, or there is a disanalogy between color predicates like "grue" and causal predicates like "grue-efficacious" that blocks the underdetermination.

(8) But the logical form appears identical: both involve a predicate with a hidden temporal cutoff that is observationally silent in all past cases.

If I must choose, I would like to preserve both that Goodman's puzzle is genuinely threatening in physical domains and that clinical induction is rational, but I want to know precisely which premise I am paying to reject and why that cost is acceptable. Is the error in (2), in treating therapeutic efficacy as the kind of property that can be gerrymandered with a temporal index? Is it in (4), in assuming that induction must be a bare projection rather than an inference anchored in mechanism? Or is the flaw elsewhere—perhaps in (7), by overstating the analogy between observational color properties and intervention-dependent causal properties? Please explain the weakest step, why it initially looks defensible, and what accepting its failure commits me to. Answer in American English.
