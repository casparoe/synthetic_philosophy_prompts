Consider the institutional divergence in how three advanced economies treat the boundary between work and leisure: France legally caps the workweek at thirty-five hours and treats overtime premiums as a structural cost to employers rather than a voluntary wage premium for individual workers; Japan grants substantial statutory paid leave but institutionalizes a culture in which taking it is read as disloyalty, with many firms not tracking unused days and with karoshi—death from overwork—recognized as an occupational hazard; and the United States has no federal mandate for paid vacation, leaving leisure entirely to private contract, while salaried professionals often treat long hours as a signal of merit and commitment. These arrangements are not merely economic; they encode different answers to who “owns” time, whether leisure is a private preference or a public good, and whether overwork is a moral failing, a personal virtue, or an organizational debt.

What philosophical commitments about human flourishing, desert, and the relationship between labor and identity does each arrangement presuppose? In particular, does the French model assume that leisure generates positive externalities that markets systematically undervalue, and if so, what conception of autonomy does that require—mere negative liberty from work, or a positive capacity for non-instrumental activity? Does Japanese presenteeism presuppose that self-worth is constituted by organizational belonging rather than by productive output, and can that view cohere with the legal recognition of karoshi as a compensable injury rather than as an individual failure of moderation? Does the American contractual model presuppose that leisure is a commodity to be traded away like any other, or rather that work is the primary domain in which citizens realize dignity and social standing?

Reconstruct the implicit normative theories—about time, welfare, and the good life—that make each practice intelligible. Distinguish what each system strictly presupposes from what merely fits it well. Identify internal tensions, such as whether recognizing karoshi as an occupational hazard is compatible with treating unused vacation as a voluntary sacrifice, or whether a legally enforced thirty-five-hour cap can be justified without committing to a particular conception of human capabilities. Finally, say what would have to change in institutional design—legal, corporate, and cultural—if we replaced these implicit theories with a straightforwardly contractualist view in which all time allocations are governed by fully informed individual consent and no collective judgment about the value of leisure is permitted.
