ok so i've been retired from field ecology for about five years and lately i've been reading philosophy of ethics, mostly the particularism vs generalism debate. i think i've landed on a package of views but i want a consistency check before i present it to my reading group. here's roughly where i stand:

1) i'm a moral particularist, dancy-style: moral reasons don't travel across cases. what's a reason in one situation can be a reason against in another, and there's no hidden general principle like "avoid harm" that explains the pattern. you have to look at the full particular shape.

2) i reject evolutionary debunking arguments. my reason is that basic moral responses — like a direct aversion to causing harm — are stable across cultures and contexts, so they're tracking real properties, not spandrels. debunkers overgeneralize by treating all moral judgment as misfiring instinct.

3) i still think refelctive equlibrium is the right method: start with considered judgments about particular cases and adjust back and forth until things cohere. rawls was wrong about needing general principles, but right about the back-and-forth.

4) i'm a moral realist: there are mind-independent moral facts, not social constructs. i think of them a bit like ecological relationships — they hold whether we map them or not.

5) on trolley problems: flipping the switch is okay, pushing the fat man isn't, and i don't think the doctrine of double effect saves the distinction. it's just the particular structure that differs, not a hidden rule.

6) i think AI ethics is mostly hype because a system without embodied field experience can't develop the kind of particularist moral perception i'm describing.

do these hang together? which pairs are actually in tension and which only look like they are? rank them by severity, and for each one say what the cheapest revision would be — keeping me mostly particularist, don't just tell me to become a generalist. be specific about which commitment has to weaken or go.
