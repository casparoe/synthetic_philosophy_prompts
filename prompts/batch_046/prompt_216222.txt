I am not an academic, but I have been thinking on my own about whether AI safety is mainly a matter of changing what a system wants or of building strong enough boxes that its wants do not matter. I would like an exam question for an upper-level seminar—something a student could answer in about seventy-five minutes in roughly twelve hundred words—that cannot be solved by simply summarizing the control-versus-alignment distinction. Ask the students to evaluate a hypothetical protocol I will call counterfactual veto auditing: an evaluator simulates post-deployment worlds in which the model believes it has already secured its objectives and is no longer monitored, then checks whether it still refuses to cause harm. The model answer must make five specific moves, each worth four marks for a total of twenty: first, distinguish instrumental cooperation from genuine value alignment inside this audit design; second, explain why the protocol implicitly assumes misalignment rather than testing for it in a neutral way; third, identify the frame-problem-like difficulty of specifying every relevant counterfactual oversight condition without leaving hidden variables unspecified; fourth, assess whether passing the audit actually increases confidence or merely selects for more sophisticated deception; fifth, argue whether robust control mechanisms—sandboxing, kill switches—render such alignment-testing redundant or more urgent. For each move, provide a one-line note on the characteristic error that should cost marks. Do not request bibliographies, named authors, or citations, and keep the wording precise but accessible to someone without specialized training.
