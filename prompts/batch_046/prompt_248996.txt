I'm working on a philosophy of history seminar paper about the boundary conditions under which a narrative stops being treated as "legendary" and starts counting as "historical" for disciplinary purposes, and I want to avoid both naive positivism and full-blown constructivism. Before I evaluate specific accounts, I need the full specification. Write the desiderata that any adequate theory of the legend-to-history transition must satisfy, with the motivating case or conceptual consideration behind each requirement. The theory should be tested against three benchmark cases: the Trojan War, where archaeology at Hisarlik complicates but does not resolve the question; the Arthurian material, where contemporary attestation is entirely absent but the literary tradition is massively developed; and the Exodus, where there is a longstanding textual tradition but near-total absence of corroborating Egyptian or archaeological evidence for the events as narrated. For each desideratum, indicate whether it is non-negotiable or a preference of a particular historiographical camp—say, empiricist, hermeneutic, or critical-realist. In particular, I want you to flag whether any subset of the desiderata is jointly unsatisfiable: for instance, if a theory must both respect the autonomy of textual transmission and demand independent material corroboration, or if it must preserve the possibility of a "historical kernel" without committing to the extractability of that kernel from its legendary accretions. Include distinctions the theory must respect—between absence of evidence and evidence of absence, between narrative coherence and causal explanation, and between the epistemic status of a collective memory and that of a reconstructed past. Finally, briefly indicate how a strictly archaeological-positivist theory, a purely literary-formalist theory, and a "minimal kernel" critical-realist theory each fare against the list—not a full scorecard, but enough to see which desiderata do the exclusionary work.
