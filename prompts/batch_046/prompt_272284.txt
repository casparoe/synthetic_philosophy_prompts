okay so i just started at this logistics contractor and got pulled into an internal review about a shipment that went totally wrong — nobody broke rules, everyone followed the checklist, but the people on the recieving end got hurt pretty bad. and i realized our disciplinary framework only has two boxes: "willful misconduct" and "negligent error." there's literally no category for "you executed your role perfectly but never stopped to ask what the role was actually for." it occured to me that this is exactly the gap Arendt talked about with Eichmann and the banality of evil — the danger isn't monstrous intent, it's just not thinking. so what view of moral responsibility or evil does that two-box system presuppose? does it quietly assume harm must come from either bad will or carelessness, thereby ruling out Arendt's idea that thoughtlessness itself can be culpable? could a control-based account explain why we punish outcomes differently without needing intent, or is the framework genuinely incompatible with taking the banality of evil seriously? and if an institution actually wanted to build in Arendt's insight, what would have to change in how these boards assign blame — would they need a third category for "role-compliant harm," and what philosophical work would that category be doing? i keep going back and forth on whether this is just bureacratic laziness or if there's a deeper theory of moral agency hidden in the paperwork.
