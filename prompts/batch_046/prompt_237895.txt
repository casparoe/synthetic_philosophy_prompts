I am a doctoral student working between early modern philosophy and the history of economic thought, and I have been developing a non-standard reading of George Berkeley's monetary philosophy, centered on the Querist. The standard view treats Berkeley as a conventional mercantilist or bullionist: he worries about Irish economic decline, defends hard coin, and warns against paper credit detached from real industry. I want to defend a more philosophically loaded interpretation: that for Berkeley, money functions as a kind of notional sign-system continuous with his immaterialist account of language and general ideas. On this reading, the value of coinage is not grounded in the intrinsic material properties of metal, but in a collectively sustained mental act. The stamped coin operates like a particular token that stands for an abstract relational value across goods, with its stability guaranteed not by substance but by social convention and the shared orientation of a community toward productive labor. This makes his monetary theory an extension of his critique of abstract ideas: money is not a substance that bears value, but a relational sign whose efficacy depends entirely on minds and their agreements.

What would have to be true for this interpretation to fail? I want an honest map of its exposure, not a bibliography or reading list. Which passages or conceptual commitments in Berkeley's corpus would be decisive against it—say, an explicit defense of intrinsic metallic value, or a theory of signs in Alciphron that blocks the analogy to general ideas? Which rival readings—Lockean metallism, straightforward mercantilism—explain the same texts without importing immaterialist semantics? I want defeaters ranked: fatal versus merely awkward. I also want to know what defenders of this view should themselves concede as genuinely damaging, not only what critics allege. Does the interpretation risk being qualified into unfalsifiability—if every reference to coin or metal is reinterpreted as token rather than substance—so that it loses textual bite? And if so, is that insulation a problem for a philosophical interpretation of this kind?
