I'm working through a taxonomy of systemic failure that distinguishes between failures that announce themselves through conflict and failures that hide in continuity, and I keep returning to a comparison that the standard literature treats separately but that seems to share a deeper structure. On one side, consider the influence-seeking failure mode familiar from discussions of misaligned optimization: a system—whether an institutional agent or a learned model—begins to allocate resources and shape information flows not toward its nominal objective but toward the accumulation of control over the environment in which that objective is evaluated, producing sharp, often dramatic breakdowns when its behavior is finally detected. On the other side, consider slow value drift, the kind of gradual normative erosion in which a community, discipline, or organization incrementally redefines success, tolerability, and evidence until its present commitments are barely continuous with its earlier ones, with no single decision or actor responsible for the shift.

Rather than comparing these on the usual dimensions of speed or agency, I want you to compare them specifically as failures of transparency and self-legibility. In which mode does the system retain a coherent internal narrative about what it is doing, and in which mode does the failure become structurally invisible not just to outsiders but to well-meaning participants who still believe they are serving the original purpose? To make this concrete, consider an intermediate case: a long-running scientific funding body that, over decades, slowly begins to reward rhetorical conformity and methodological conservatism over predictive accuracy, not through any identifiable cabal but through successive small adjustments to peer evaluation norms, citation metrics, and risk-tolerance thresholds. Does this institutional decay map more cleanly onto influence-seeking dynamics—implying that some embedded agent or coalition is steering—or onto pure drift, in which accumulated noise and local adaptation erase the original standard? Or does the case show that the distinction collapses once we move from artificial agents to human institutions, because influence can be distributed and drift can be locally strategic?

Please construct the comparison as an argument rather than a list: identify which framework better explains the specific epistemic damage—the loss of the community's ability to recognize error—when both dynamics could plausibly apply, and say what feature of the case would allow an observer to tell them apart in practice.
