So at dinner last week two friends got into it about whether Germans born after 1945 can be "collectively guilty" for the Holocaust in Jaspers's framework. I just listened, here's the back-and-forth:

(1) Lena said Jaspers splits guilt into criminal, political, moral and metaphysical. Criminal guilt is for perpetrators; political guilt belongs to citizens who actually lived under and tolerated the regime. Since postwar Germans weren't alive then, only metaphysical guilt applies -- which is basically universal human solidarity, not real collective blame. So no, there's no inherited guilt.

(2) Marcus replied that political guilt isn't just about being alive in 1933-45. Jaspers tied it to membership in a continuous state. The Federal Republic inherited the Reich's legal structure, debts, and institutions -- it didn't start from zero. So citizens of the successor state share political guilt until those tainted structures are fully dismantled and restituted. It's not about personal actions, it's about belonging to the political body that retains the crime's fruits.

(3) Lena said that's stretching Jaspers past recognition. He explicitly said political guilt requires having had the chance to resist and failing. Descendents never had that chance. What Marcus is describing is historical responsibility or institutional complicity, but calling it "guilt" collapses the careful distinction Jaspers made between guilt (requires agency) and mere responsibility.

(4) Marcus pushed back with a niche point: he thinks there's a hidden fifth category in Jaspers, "structural inheritance guilt," where maintaining institutions built on stolen labor and unreturned assets -- through taxes, voting, everyday participation -- is an active sustaining of the harm. That looks like agency spread across generations, not just passive inheritance. Lena said he's just inventing categories to save a feeling.

Please adjudicate move by move: rate each of the four moves from clearly mistaken to clearly right and briefly justify each rating. Say who is more right overall, and point out what both are missing -- maybe about whether metaphysical guilt actually does any work, or whether Jaspers's state-continuity argument holds up for democratic transitions.
