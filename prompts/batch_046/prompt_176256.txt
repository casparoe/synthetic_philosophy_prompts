I'm a PhD student in philosophy of logic writing on how narrative structures can model non-classical reasoning. I'm using Kurosawa's Rashomon—where four witnesses offer mutually incompatible accounts of a forest attack and the film refuses any framing resolution—to think through dialetheism and paraconsistent logic. But I want to avoid treating the contradictions as merely epistemic: I need to test whether the film supports what I call narrative adjunction, by which I mean the implicit structural pressure to combine all presented perspectives into one jointly evaluable whole, even when the plot resists it.

Read Rashomon through that lens. Does the woodcutter's final account operate as a hidden adjunction principle—allowing the viewer to derive consistency only by importing an unstated normative commitment—or does the film block adjunction and keep the contradictions locally contained? Where does this make it a model for paraconsistent reasoning rather than for full-blown dialetheism? I want a reading that takes the repetition-with-variation form seriously as logical architecture, not just thematic ambiguity, and that is honest about where the philosophical framework illuminates the cinematography and where it forces the material. What does Rashomon add to the debate about true contradictions that a formal argument alone does not capture?
