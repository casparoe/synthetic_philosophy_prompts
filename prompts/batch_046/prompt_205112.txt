Consider the institutional design of a permanently neutral state that maintains national defence not through a professional standing army but through universal conscription into a citizen-militia reserve. Citizens undergo initial training, return to civilian life, and remain liable for periodic recall, equipment maintenance, and local-unit musters for years thereafter. The state refuses collective-security alliances, prohibits deployment for offensive or alliance-bound operations, and restricts foreign engagement to narrowly circumscribed multilateral or humanitarian mandates. At the same time, it treats service not merely as a legal duty analogous to taxation but as an expression of civic virtue and collective self-preservation, distributing readiness obligations across the population rather than concentrating them in a specialised military class.

What philosophical commitments about citizenship, sovereignty, the moral status of military violence, and the boundary between defence and aggression does this practice presuppose? Reconstruct the implicit theory: does it require a republican or communitarian conception of the citizen-soldier, or is it compatible with liberal individualism? Does its distinction between permitted “defence” and forbidden “intervention” rest on a particular theory of just war, or can it be explained instrumentally? Identify where the practice is philosophically underdetermined—where rival theories of obligation, state authority, or collective identity would license the same institutional form—and say what would have to change in the design if its underlying view of citizenship or sovereignty were replaced by a cosmopolitan or purely professional-military alternative.
