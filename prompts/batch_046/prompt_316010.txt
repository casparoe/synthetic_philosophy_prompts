As someone recently appointed to the disciplinary panel of a professional association, I am faced with a case that has made Strawson's framework unexpectedly personal. One of our members caused significant harm through negligence and now claims that, during the relevant period, they were experiencing a severe dissociative episode that deprived them of the capacity for choice. I feel a strong reactive attitude—something close to resentment—yet I also recognise that if their account is accurate, they may fall outside the scope of moral responsibility as ordinarily understood. Please describe the most important implications of Strawson's distinction between participant reactive attitudes and the objective attitude for how a disciplinary body ought to proceed in such circumstances. In particular, I want to understand whether adopting an objective stance towards this individual is compatible with preserving the trust and reciprocal expectations that sustain our professional community, and what this reveals about the conditions under which resentment can be justifiably suspended without eroding the moral bonds that make collective accountability possible.
