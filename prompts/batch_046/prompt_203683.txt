I'm writing this novel about a dyslexic typesetter — don't ask, it's a vibe — and I got into this weird argument with my friend Marisol who designs book covers. She's got these very firm opinions about what counts as a real lowercase 'a' versus just some shape that reminds you of one, and she's never wrong about it, atleast in her own head. So far she's said yes to the classic double-decker roman 'a', yes to the single-storey handwritten kind with the open top, yes to a sharp-cornered geometric version like you'd see in Futura, yes even to a mirror-image 'a' in a left-handed script, and yes to a stencil where the counter is broken open at the top. But she said no to the '@' symbol, no to a 'q' rotated 180 degrees, and no to that filled-in blob where the bowl is completely blacked out and there's just a tail sticking out.

Now for my cover I'm playing with this form where the bowl is actually the overlap of two ellipses, so there's no single enclosed hole — instead there are two little crescent-shaped negative spaces — and there's a vertical bar running up the right side that touches both ellipses. I haven't shown her yet. Do you think she'll call it an 'a'? I'm less interested in the answer then in what rule she's actually running — maybe topological skeleton invariance, maybe gestalt family resemblance, maybe stroke genealogy, or something weirder. Which past verdict is the closest analog? And if her track record genuinely underdermines this, what principle is she adopting either way? Plus, what's the one further case — maybe a split-bowl stencil or an AI-generated glyph with perfect local features but wrong global topology — that'd actually tell the competing rules apart?
