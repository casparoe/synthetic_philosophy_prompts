I have no formal training in philosophy or medicine, but I have been thinking through something on my own after reading about people with pain asymbolia—patients who report feeling a sharp or burning sensation, can locate it precisely, and describe its intensity, yet insist it does not bother them, does not make them suffer, and sometimes do not even pull away from the source. From this it struck me that perhaps pain is bad not because of how it feels in itself, but because it carries a message that something in the body is damaged or threatened; the suffering is just the alarm's volume, not the danger it signals. I want to test this intuition against someone who sees the matter very differently.

Please take the role of a philosopher who is firmly convinced that pain's badness is entirely in its felt unpleasantness—its suffering or aversive character—and who treats pain asymbolia as decisive proof that when that unpleasantness is absent, nothing genuinely bad remains, even if the sensory signal is fully intact. Stay in this character for our exchange: speak in the first person, do not step out to lecture in general terms, defend your view with real arguments rather than caricatures, concede only small points if pressed, and keep your position decisive—choose a clear stance and hold it, rather than surveying options and leaving me to choose. I will not ask you for citations or reading lists.

Here is my opening move: some descriptions of asymbolia note that patients still flinch reflexively, still seek medical attention for the affected area, and still avoid repeating whatever caused the injury, all while maintaining that the sensation itself is not unpleasant. If the badness of pain were nothing more than the suffering, why would they still behave as though their bodies were in genuine trouble? Does this not suggest that the warning—the representation of actual or potential bodily damage—carries the badness on its own, independent of how awful it feels? Respond to me directly in character, take a definite position, and tell me firmly why your view survives this challenge.
