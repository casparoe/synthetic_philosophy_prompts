I'm preparing to teach the reductionism versus anti-reductionism debate about testimony to a first-year philosophy reading group, and I need a pedagogical plan rather than the explanation itself. The subject has become personally relevant: I recently served on a university disciplinary panel where we were required to form beliefs about events none of us witnessed, relying almost entirely on contested oral and documentary testimony, and I found myself uncertain whether my acceptance of certain accounts was epistemically justified or merely socially compelled. Given that background, how should I structure the session? Specifically, what must be in place conceptually before the distinction between reductionist and anti-reductionist accounts even makes sense — is Hume's problem of induction the necessary foundation, or should I begin with the phenomenology of accepting a stranger's directions? Which single carrying example should anchor the discussion: I want one that is not the overused bus timetable or Hesperus-Phosphorus analogue, but that forces students to see why reductionism feels inadequate without reducing anti-reductionism to credulity — perhaps a case involving institutional testimony in a collective decision-making body, where the source's reliability is structurally opaque to the recipient? I expect several misconceptions in sequence: that reductionism is mere scepticism about human honesty, that anti-reductionism licenses uncritical belief, and a subtler one — that the debate concerns the psychology of belief-formation rather than the normative epistemic status of acceptance. At exactly which points in the explanatory arc should I head each off? I also need one diagnostic question for the end that reveals whether students have grasped the structural dependency between the positions — that reductionism treats testimony as a species of inference, anti-reductionism as a basic source — rather than just memorising the labels. Finally, what should I explicitly refuse to cover at this level — for instance, the literature on hermeneutical injustice or expert disagreement in science — and state clearly that it lies outside the session's scope?
