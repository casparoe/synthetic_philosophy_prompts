Analyze South Africa's Truth and Reconciliation Commission as a real-world case study for the thesis that societies can engineer a shift from shame-based to guilt-based moral economies through institutional design. Frame the analysis through the comparative moral psychology of guilt—usually understood as an internal, act-specific, reparative emotion—and shame—usually understood as an external, global, status-oriented emotion. Address the following: (1) whether the TRC's architecture—public testimony, amnesty conditional on full individual disclosure, and the deliberate framing of wrongdoing as discrete acts attributable to specific agents rather than as a collective stain—constitutes an intentional attempt at "emotional regime change"; (2) which dynamics of the Commission this framework illuminates (e.g., the preference for personal narrative over collective humiliation) and which it obscures or misdescribes (e.g., the persistence of group-based shame in affected communities, the influence of restorative-justice concepts such as Ubuntu, or the emotional burden placed on victims who testified publicly); (3) whether the episode ultimately supports the possibility of a culture deliberately choosing its dominant moral emotion, or whether it demonstrates that emotional regimes are path-dependent and that institutional design can only redirect, not replace, deep-seated affective patterns. In your answer, separate what is well-established in the philosophical and psychological literature on guilt and shame from what remains genuinely contested—especially the normative claim that guilt is morally superior to shame, and the empirical claim that collective emotional structures are malleable by policy—without presenting either contested claim as settled consensus.
