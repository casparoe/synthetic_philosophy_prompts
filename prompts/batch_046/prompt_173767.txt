I'm currently in a seminar on moral psychology and the philosophy of value, and we've been working through the wrong-kind-reasons literature. I want to check whether a line I've been developing holds up, because it seems to collapse the very distinction I'm trying to defend. Here is the argument, with premises I take to be individually plausible but collectively destructive.

(1) For any attitude A, the reasons that are of the right kind to justify or sustain A are those internal to A's own normative structure; reasons drawn from a different normative domain are wrong-kind reasons for A, and treating them as sustaining A involves a category error. (2) Aesthetic appreciation is an attitude whose right-kind reasons are internal to the work—formal properties, expressive character, and the like—while social prestige, financial value, and therapeutic utility belong to other domains and are therefore wrong-kind reasons for appreciation. (3) Call a wrong-kind reason that initiates an attitude but is meant to be replaced by right-kind reasons a 'scaffold.' Most people first encounter artworks through scaffolding—course assignments, social trends, or personal crisis—rather than through direct responsiveness to internal aesthetic properties. (4) If an attitude is genuinely A, then its sustaining reasons must be drawn primarily from R_A; if wrong-kind reasons continue to play a necessary sustaining role, the attitude is hybrid and not pure A. (5) Psychological continuity suggests that attitudes rarely survive the complete removal of the reasons that originally sustained them unless replacement reasons are already fully operative; therefore, if scaffolding is removed before right-kind reasons are fully established, the attitude collapses. (6) It follows that, for appreciation developed via scaffolding to be stable, the wrong-kind reasons must remain present in some sustaining capacity even after right-kind reasons have emerged—otherwise the transition would be discontinuous. (7) But if wrong-kind reasons remain necessary for sustaining the attitude, then by (4) the attitude is not genuinely aesthetic appreciation, or at best is permanently hybrid. (8) Therefore, either appreciation never genuinely develops through scaffolding (which contradicts common experience), or the wrong-kind-reasons distinction is either false or normatively useless, since every developed attitude would depend on a hidden scaffolding of wrong-kind reasons.
