I serve on the disciplinary appeals panel for our university's clinical-ethics graduate program. A student refused to complete a required simulation module in which participants role-play delivering a therapeutic-privilege justification to a standardized patient; she argues that practicing the rhetoric of deception—even in a simulated setting—violates an absolute deontological prohibition against treating rational agents as mere means, and that institutional compulsion makes her will complicit. The program failed her for missing a core competency. She appeals, asking us to recognize a conscientious objection, while the faculty warns that allowing opt-outs on deontological grounds collapses professional training into subjective preference and activates the paradox of deontology: absolute agent-centered restrictions, when institutionalized as exemption rights, can erode the shared norms they were meant to protect and reproduce harm by fragmenting professional accountability. Draft the panel's decision memo recommending whether to uphold the failure, overturn it with conditions, or remand for an alternative assessment. Take the student's deontological claim seriously rather than treating it as mere reluctance, but also address the strongest version of the faculty's worry with specific reasoning. Give a committed recommendation—pick one outcome, defend it concretely, and specify a clear time frame for implementation—and include an explicit acknowledgment of why the best argument for the losing side still falls short.
