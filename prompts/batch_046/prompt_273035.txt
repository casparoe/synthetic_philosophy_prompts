I am working through Locke’s *Some Thoughts Concerning Education* and keep encountering the claim that children enter the world with minds like blank paper or empty cabinets, carrying no innate ideas. In recent discussions about moral education, interlocutors invoke this “blank slate” image to dismiss any suggestion that children possess an innate sense of justice or an inborn moral orientation that education ought to uncover rather than impose. Before I raise this in a seminar, I want the slogan disambiguated. Could you lay out the distinct precise theses it might express — ranging from the trivial observation that infants lack explicit moral concepts, through the substantive claim that all mental content is acquired through experience, to the stronger claim that there is no innate cognitive structure directing development — and for each say whether it is defensible in Locke’s own terms, whether it genuinely supports dismissing innate moral knowledge, and how it relates to Locke’s actual pedagogical advice, especially his insistence that virtue must be instilled through early habituation long before reason is mature? Present the readings as a menu rather than selecting one, indicate which interpretation is likely being wielded when someone uses the slogan to shut down a question about innate moral tendencies, and note where Locke’s recommendations about conditioning and custom sit with respect to each reading. Please state how confident you are in attributing each interpretation to Locke’s texts, and single out the point about which you are least certain.
