I'm helping design a syllabus for an advanced practical-ethics module that employs what I have been calling a Plutarchan pairing method: students study a contemporary public figure alongside an ancient life drawn from Plutarch's Parallel Lives, not to derive universal maxims but to practice moral triangulation between two structurally analogous decision-points. When I described this to the university's civic-education office, I discovered they already administer a leadership seminar operating on nearly identical premises. Each cohort receives a matched pair—say, a modern reformer and an ancient magistrate—and the facilitators' manual explicitly treats hagiographic smoothing, the compression of contingency into character-driven narrative, as a pedagogical feature rather than a historical distortion to be corrected. The manual further directs students to read discrepancies between the paired lives not as noise but as calibrated exercises in practical judgment, assuming that virtue can be trained through analogical mapping across radically different institutional contexts.

What philosophical commitments about moral knowledge and exemplarity does this institutional practice presuppose? Must it assume that moral learning is irreducibly narrative and analogical, rather than propositional or rule-based? Does the deliberate use of hagiographic compression entail that accurate causal history—acknowledging luck, structural constraint, and failure—actively obstructs the formation of practical wisdom? And when the pairing treats a contemporary administrator and an ancient general as sharing a common decision-structure despite incompatible social worlds, is the program committed to a universalist thesis about practical reason, or can the analogical exercise be defended on purely instrumental grounds, as pattern-recognition training that makes no metaphysical claim about cross-historical identity of virtues?

Please reconstruct the implicit theory of moral learning embedded in this design, carefully distinguishing what the practice strictly requires from what merely accompanies it. In particular, address whether an anti-hagiographic alternative—one that refused to smooth failures into narrative arcs and insisted on the incommensurability of historical contexts—would necessarily defeat the pedagogical purpose, or whether such a rigorous biographical program could serve the same training function under a different philosophical account of how lives teach. I am not looking for a bibliography of virtue ethics or biography theory; I want an analysis of the commitments this specific pedagogical architecture silently encodes.
