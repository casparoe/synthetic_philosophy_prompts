I'm developing an essay on the causal exclusion argument in philosophy of mind, but before expanding it I want to test a somewhat unorthodox framing. The standard version, associated with Jaegwon Kim, runs roughly as follows: if mental properties are genuinely non-reductive yet supervene on physical properties, and if those physical properties have complete causal powers, then mental properties are either epiphenomenal or we face systematic overdetermination. The familiar responses distinguish reductive from non-reductive physicalism, or invoke realization relations to block the exclusion.

My proposed twist is structural: there is an analogous exclusion problem in social ontology. Institutional properties—say, a corporation's decision to acquire a rival firm—supervene on the individual actions of board members and shareholders, yet we routinely treat the institutional decision as causally efficacious, moving markets and triggering contracts. Apply Kim's exclusion principle consistently at this level, and one should conclude that only the individual votes and signatures are genuinely causal, rendering the corporate decision either epiphenomenal or redundant. Non-reductive social ontologists resist this, but they typically do so by insisting that institutional causation operates at a different explanatory level without competing with individual-level causation.

My essay asks whether this analogy helps mental causation. I argue that it actually makes things worse for the non-reductive physicalist. In the social case, the macro-level description is genuinely causally inert in the physical sense; it summarizes and organizes micro-causal patterns without introducing new physical forces. If mental properties are genuinely analogous, then they too are organizational summaries rather than distinct causal powers—precisely the reductive conclusion the non-reductive view seeks to avoid. Conversely, if mental properties are genuinely distinct causal powers, as the non-reductive view requires, then they should not be analogous to institutional properties at all. The essay concludes that the social-ontology parallel exposes a tension in how non-reductive physicalists conceive the level of mental causation: they want mental properties to be causally real in a way institutional properties are not, yet they defend them using the same supervenience-based structure.

I'd welcome comments specifically on: (1) whether this framing misrepresents the exclusion argument by treating it as applicable across domains; (2) whether the institutional-causation comparison is coherent or collapses under scrutiny; (3) whether the central tension—that mental properties must either be like institutions (causally summary) or unlike them (causally mysterious)—is genuinely sharp or relies on an equivocation about causal efficacy; and (4) whether the argument is interesting enough to pursue or merely restates familiar worries about non-reductive physicalism in different vocabulary.
