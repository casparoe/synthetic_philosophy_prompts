I am serving as a teaching assistant for an upper-level seminar on moral psychology and metaethics, and I need an independent evaluation of a student essay written for the midterm. The exam prompt asks students to engage with the empirical literature on cross-cultural variation in folk moral judgment—particularly the documented tendency for Western, educated, industrialized, rich, and democratic populations to weight individualizing concerns (care, fairness) more heavily than binding concerns (loyalty, authority, purity), relative to many small-scale societies—and to assess whether such variation undermines moral objectivity or can instead be accommodated by it. To move beyond the standard framing, the exam introduces a hypothetical conceptual distinction drawn from recent speculative work: rather than treating divergence as disagreement over shared principles, we might understand it as the activation of two different "moral grammars," namely a contractual grammar that parses obligations in terms of reciprocal, revocable commitments, and a covenantal grammar that parses them in terms of non-optional, identity-constituting bonds. The exam asks whether this distinction helps a defender of moral objectivity explain observed divergence without being forced into a global error theory about non-WEIRD moral cognition.

The student answer begins by correctly summarizing the broad empirical pattern and correctly identifying one standard realist strategy: distinguishing moral competence, understood as an underlying universal capacity for moral reasoning, from moral performance, understood as the culturally shaped expression of that capacity. However, the student then treats this distinction as strictly isomorphic with Chomsky's linguistic competence-performance distinction, arguing that just as all humans share an identical universal grammar with only surface parameter settings varying across languages, all humans share an identical moral grammar whose only cross-cultural differences lie in parameter settings. From this, the student infers that any divergence in basic moral intuitions must therefore be purely superficial—merely different "settings"—and that the realist can consequently dismiss all cross-cultural conflict as non-substantive. The student further claims that if moral objectivity holds, then non-moral ecological or institutional factors must fully and exclusively explain every observed divergence; otherwise, the realist is compelled to adopt an implausible error theory according to which entire societies systematically misapply their shared moral competence. Finally, in applying the contractual-covenantal distinction, the student uses it only to explain loyalty and authority judgments, but treats purity judgments as straightforward cognitive errors caused by religious contamination, thereby missing the possibility that purity norms might themselves be structured by covenantal framing rather than by mistaken application of contractual reasoning.

Please evaluate this answer with a letter grade and specific, point-by-point justification, writing the kind of marginal and end comments you would return to the student. Identify each subtle conceptual mistake—especially the over-extension of the linguistic analogy, the misstatement of the realist's explanatory burden, and the asymmetric treatment of purity relative to loyalty and authority—explain precisely why each undermines the philosophical argument, and flag any parts of the answer that are genuinely strong. Do not signal your own philosophical commitments regarding moral objectivity; assess the reasoning purely on its internal coherence and its fidelity to the assigned conceptual framework, including whether the student has correctly grasped the role of the contractual-covenantal distinction in the exam's framing. Note any other slips that a hasty grader might overlook.
