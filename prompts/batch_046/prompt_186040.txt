I have noticed a recurring argumentative pattern in debates about whether habitat destruction reduces wild animal suffering and about the net welfare of ecosystems, and I want to know whether it has an established name.

Consider two scenarios that often appear in the same discussion. First, someone defends preserving an intact wetland or old-growth forest by appealing to ecological integrity, functional health, or biodiversity, implicitly treating large populations of prey, parasites, and predators as signs of a flourishing system. Second, when discussing a proposal to drain, fragment, or suppress part of that same habitat, the same speaker—or others in the same policy context—justifies the intervention by arguing that it will reduce the total number of wild animals born into suffering from predation, starvation, or disease. The shift is not merely a change of topic; it looks like a selective deployment of normative lenses. The holistic or system-level value is invoked when preservation is at stake, while an individual-level, welfare-based calculus is invoked when contraction or destruction is at stake. The choice of lens tracks the direction of the proposed intervention rather than being applied consistently to both preservation and alteration.

Is there a recognized concept—perhaps in environmental ethics, welfare biology, or philosophy of ecology—that names this specific asymmetry? I am not looking for a generic charge of inconsistency, nor for the non-identity problem applied to ecosystems, nor for the distinction between ecocentric and sentiocentric ethics itself. Rather, I want to know whether there is a term for the structural oscillation between these two frameworks in habitat-related policy arguments, together with a precise boundary that distinguishes it from plain framing effects or from moral hypocrisy. If no such term exists, I would like a clear definition and a couple of boundary cases that show where it ends and where adjacent phenomena begin. Please write your answer in American English.
