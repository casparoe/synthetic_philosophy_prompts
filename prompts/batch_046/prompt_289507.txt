I am a second-year undergraduate reading historiographical theory, and next week I must lead a fifty-minute tutorial for fellow second-years on Fernand Braudel’s concept of the longue durée and his hierarchy of temporal rhythms—geographical time, social time, and the time of events and individuals. Our cohort has just finished a survey course that treated history chiefly as political narrative and the actions of prominent individuals; none of us have encountered structuralist historiography in any depth. Please do not deliver an explanation of Braudel’s theory to me. Instead, plan how I should structure the tutorial: the precise sequence in which the three temporalities must be introduced, with the dependency relations that make that order necessary rather than arbitrary; the single carrying example or case study I should build the session around, which must not be the Mediterranean (they have seen it mentioned in passing) and which must display both a durable structural feature and a sharply dated event that students are likely to mistake for its cause; the misconceptions they will either arrive with or form during the session—particularly the slide from Braudel’s structural time into environmental determinism of the popular “geography is destiny” kind, and the assumption that “slow” implies “historically insignificant”—together with the exact point in the sequence where each should be anticipated and defused; one concrete diagnostic question I can pose near the end to reveal whether they understand the dependency relation between structure and event or have merely memorised three labelled levels; and what I should openly refuse to cover at this stage—such as Braudel’s later writings on capitalism and the world-economy—and how I should justify that boundary to them. The plan should assume no prior familiarity with the Annales school beyond the name itself, and please present it in British English.
