I am presenting a defence of a dual-architecture thesis about moral emotions at an interdisciplinary seminar. I argue that guilt and shame are not points on a single spectrum but have different logical structures: guilt operates transactionally, like a debt that can be discharged through restitution or forgiveness between specific parties; shame operates topologically, reconfiguring one's position within a normative social landscape without necessarily implying a recoverable debt. From this I draw a normative claim: a culture can deliberately calibrate its shame topography—the particular failures and statuses that attract disgrace—without destroying autonomy, provided it builds institutional exit corridors that offer recognised pathways for status restoration. The audience consists mainly of liberal moral philosophers with Kantian commitments who regard autonomy as inviolable and collective honour norms as instruments of oppression; two clinical psychologists who accept the standard internalising/externalising taxonomy but treat shame as intrinsically pathogenic; and one cultural anthropologist familiar with honour societies but suspicious of prescriptive universalism.
