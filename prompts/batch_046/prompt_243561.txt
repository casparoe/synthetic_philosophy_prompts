I am facilitating a 90-minute graduate workshop with twelve students in political philosophy and social-choice theory, meeting in a single seminar room with no screens required. The idea I want them to encounter is Amartya Sen’s liberal paradox—the tension between a minimal liberal condition (that each person holds sovereignty over at least one pair of alternatives within a defined personal sphere) and the Pareto principle (that if every individual prefers one social state to another, society should rank them accordingly). Rather than presenting the impossibility proof, I want the students to generate the conflict themselves through action, so that the formal result lands as an explanation of an experience they have already had.

Design a runnable, paper-and-voice-only game or structured deliberation with explicit roles, rules, and small, reversible stakes—perhaps collective control over the room’s physical conditions for the next twenty minutes (lighting level, seating layout, or background sound) or point allocations that determine a shared snack choice. Assign each student a “personal sphere” defined over one binary feature of the collective environment, and embed an asymmetry: for two students the sphere is negative (they may veto being forced into a state), while for one student it is positive (they may insist that a particular state be adopted for them). Then present a multi-round decision problem in which unanimous preference for one collective option exists, but honoring the spheres blocks that option. The rules should be arranged so that students vote, invoke vetoes or demands, and only at the end notice that they have arrived at an outcome every member considers worse than an available alternative—without anyone having violated anyone else’s rights.

Structure the session so there is no lecture up front; the rules and incentives should do all the work. After the game, provide a debrief that maps each round onto the formal elements of the paradox—the liberal condition, the Pareto principle, the domain of preference profiles, and the social welfare function—predict the failure modes you expect when running this (e.g., students rejecting the veto as unfair, colluding to redefine “personal sphere,” or resolving the tension by denying that unanimous preference was genuine), and be frank about which feature of Sen’s domain-general impossibility the activity cannot reproduce—likely the full cross-profile generality across arbitrary sets of alternatives—and explain exactly how you will supply that missing formal piece verbally after the experience. Keep everything low-stakes and fully reversible. At the end, state the central tension revealed by the activity in a single sentence preceded by Verdict:.
