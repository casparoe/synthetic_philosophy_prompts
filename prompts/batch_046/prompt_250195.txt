I've been working through some notes for a seminar on the epistemology of conditionals, and I constructed the following argument to show that the standard probabilistic account of indicative conditionals undermines scientific reasoning. It feels suspicious—like it proves too much—but I cannot locate the precise slip, and I want to know what rejecting each step costs. (1) If indicative conditionals are evaluated by Adams's thesis, so that the rational degree of belief in "If A, then C" equals one's conditional credence P(C|A), and this identity holds globally across all propositions with probabilities strictly between 0 and 1, then Lewis's well-known triviality result applies. (2) The result demonstrates that any coherent, non-extreme prior distribution satisfying this identity must assign a single constant value to every indicative conditional of that form, regardless of the specific antecedent or consequent. (3) So if Adams's thesis is correct globally, rational agents with coherent beliefs must treat every such conditional as carrying identical epistemic weight. (4) But scientific reasoning routinely depends on treating predictive conditionals—say, "If this quantum state is prepared in manner M, then detector D will register outcome O"—as carrying specific, differentiated evidential weight, and on updating confidence in the theoretical antecedent when the predicted consequent is observed. (5) Under a globally applied Adams's thesis evaluated against a fixed prior, observing O cannot alter the probability assigned to the predictive conditional linking theory T to O, since that conditional's value was already fixed by the prior independently of the observation. (6) Therefore global Adams's thesis renders indirect evidential pathways—where support for a prediction transfers back to support for the predicting theory—epistemically inert. (7) If these pathways are inert, then no theoretical claim could be confirmed by observing its distant predictions, and predictive or abductive confirmation collapses entirely. (8) Because predictive confirmation is clearly indispensable, global Adams's thesis must be rejected. (9) The only available rescue is to restrict Adams's thesis to a privileged subclass—perhaps conditionals whose antecedents are observationally accessible—while governing predictive conditionals by a different rule. (10) Yet any principled restriction that excludes precisely the problematic theoretical cases without also excluding structurally identical benign ones seems impossible to formulate without ad hoc stipulation. (11) So we face a trilemma: abandon Adams's thesis entirely, deny that predictive confirmation works as we think it does, or accept an arbitrary division in our logic of conditionals. Where does the argument go wrong, if it does? I would prefer to keep the idea that conditionals carry differentiated evidential weight and that predictive confirmation is real, but I am willing to pay a price elsewhere if I can see exactly what it buys me and whether the argument has hidden an equivocation or scope shift I have missed.
