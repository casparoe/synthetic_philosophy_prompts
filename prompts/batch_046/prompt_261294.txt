I'm giving a workshop presentation next month to a mixed audience of formal decision theorists, behavioral economists who've actually run multi-player Luring Lottery protocols, and normative philosophers of action. My thesis is that the Platonia dilemma is usually miscast as forcing a binary all-cooperate or all-defect outcome for superrational agents, and that embedding its payoff structure inside a Luring Lottery frame—where rewards depend on quantile position within the group distribution rather than on uniform matching—turns the problem into a continuous coordination problem. From this I derive what I call a quantile-sensitive commitment rule: rational agents should assign graded cooperation probabilities calibrated to their common-knowledge confidence in symmetric reasoning, rather than locking to 0 or 1. I think the standard Hofstadter-style symmetry argument collapses once you allow the action space to be probabilistically indexed, and I use the empirical clustering patterns from Luring Lottery experiments (intermediate probabilities rather than corner solutions) as evidence that the binary framing is descriptively inadequate even for ideally rational agents. The decision theorists in the room know the derivation of symmetric reasoning inside out, the behavioral economists will insist I'm misreading their data, and the normative philosophers will treat any appeal to experimental clustering as a category error.
