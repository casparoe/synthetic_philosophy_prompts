Write a poem of six quatrains in iambic pentameter, spoken by an inquirer who believes she has established P, then P→Q, and therefore expects to know Q. Structure the poem so that its form performs Nozick's tracking theory and the failure of epistemic closure: let each quatrain's opening line reproduce the preceding quatrain's closing line with exactly one word changed, as though belief is being tracked across nearby worlds by maintaining adherence while allowing minimal variation; but let the fifth quatrain break this chain—its opening line should abandon the repetition entirely—so that the sixth quatrain begins with a fresh, unrepeated line, leaving the sequence open rather than closed. The rupture should correspond philosophically to the point where sensitivity to Q's falsity fails: even though P and P→Q are tracked, the conditions for tracking Q are not met in worlds close enough to actual ones. Render the mechanics precisely—distinguish or allude to the sensitivity and adherence conditions—rather than merely mentioning "closure" or "tracking" as labels. After the poem, append a brief note (no more than three sentences) identifying where you have taken liberties with Nozick's formulation or relied on a contested reading of why closure fails under tracking conditions. If this task asks a poem to do work that cannot be done without serious distortion of either the poetics or the epistemology—if, for instance, counterfactual tracking cannot be meaningfully mapped onto lexical repetition—say so plainly and do not force the form.
