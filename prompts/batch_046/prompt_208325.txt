I'm giving the opening remarks at a workshop on institutional inertia and the philosophy of action. The audience is split: philosophers who work on the absent-minded driver problem — the case in which a driver explicitly intends to turn at the store but, out of long habit, drives straight past it to his usual workplace without noticing — and organizational researchers who study routine drift in institutions but do not use the philosophical vocabulary. I want a ten-minute address that takes the driver case as its structural backbone rather than decoration. Use the real philosophical puzzle — whether there is one action or two competing ones, whether habit can count as a form of intention that overrides an explicit plan, and whether the driver can sincerely deny he meant to go to work when his behavior is fully explained by what he meant to do in some procedural sense — to frame everything I say about organizational life. The philosophers should recognize that you are not simplifying their problem for outsiders; the organizational researchers should leave feeling they have gained a precise vocabulary for a phenomenon they already lived with. Do not resolve the philosophical tension; let its unresolved structure set the boundaries of what the workshop can claim together. Do not cite specific authors, texts, or schools. The tone should be formal but spoken, appropriate to an opening welcome rather than a lecture.
