She argues this temporal synthesis—retention and anticipation of a common object of dispute—is precisely what Kant's transcendental deduction secures. Without a unified consciousness synthesizing representations over time, she says, you do not have disagreement but merely isolated, non-communicating outputs.

Leo responds that this confuses a psychological condition with a logical one. Two representational systems with different architectures—say, a split-brain patient whose hemispheres maintain contradictory beliefs, or distributed AI nodes with separate memory registers—can still stand in logical opposition without sharing a single stream of consciousness. For Leo, disagreement is a relation between propositional contents, not between phenomenological subjects.

Mara comes back: even in split-brain cases, each hemisphere retains its own transcendental unity; the left hemisphere can still synthesize its representations over time, and that is enough to ground disagreement within that subsystem. She adds that functional memory—registers and predictive weights—only simulates synthesis; it lacks the normative dimension of holding oneself accountable for consistency across time, which is what makes disagreement rational rather than merely mechanical.

Leo counters that Mara is defining disagreement in a way that begs the question. If we allow a thinner, functional notion—conflicting outputs that are corrected through feedback loops—then transcendental conditions are either unnecessary (if they just describe functional memory) or illegitimately restricted to biological consciousness (if they require phenomenological unity). He proposes a thought experiment: a distributed cognitive network with no single node possessing unified consciousness, yet collectively engaging in sustained argumentation, revising positions in response to counter-arguments. Would Mara deny that this network genuinely disagrees?

Mara says yes, she would deny it, precisely because no node is normatively accountable for the network's overall position; there is no "I" that can be wrong, only a pattern of outputs. Leo says that accountability can also be distributed: the network's design includes error-correction mechanisms that function as normative constraints, even without a central self.
