Design a classroom game for a philosophy workshop — about 14 people, 75 minutes, mix of undergrads and postgrads — that makes them feel the authority problem with advance directives, especially the clash between diachronic autonomy (past self binding future) and synchronic autonomy (present self ruling now). I dont want a lecture up front; the tension should come from the rules.

Set it up in pairs across two 20-minute rounds. In round one, one person is the "past self" who wrote a directive a decade ago, the other is the "present self" facing the choice. Give them three domains to settle: a medical clause (refusing life-extending treatment), a relational clause (who picks where they live), and a niche "aesthetic continuity" clause — the past self insisted they keep engaging with specific art or music even if present tastes have shifted. The past-self card lists locked commitments; the present-self card secretly states either a genuine value transformation or a strategic preference to defect for comfort, but they must not reveal which. They negotiate for agreement, scored on consistency with their hidden card plus actually reaching a deal, so deadlock hurts both sides.

In round two, swap partners and introduce a "moral hazard" rule: the present self can invoke "cognitive decline" to override any clause, but doing so costs points unless they justify it with evidence drawn from the negotiation. This should make the past self try to build in safeguards and the present self test boundaries.

Then debrief: map negotiation moments onto philosophical moves — when does the past self treat the present one as a different agent? How does strategic defection mimic real value change? Be honest about what the activity cant reproduce: actual phenomenology of dementia, and the emotional weight of real mortality, so you'll need to supply those verbally. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first run — likely people ignore the hidden-information twist and drift into generic medical-ethics debate — and how to correct it. Please answer in British English, and keep the tone casual; a typo or two is fine.
