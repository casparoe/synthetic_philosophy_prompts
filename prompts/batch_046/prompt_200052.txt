I'm preparing the core commitments for a seminar paper on introspection, mental agency, and the ontology of subpersonal representation, and I want a consistency audit before I finalize the framework. I hold the following four theses together:

(a) Introspective access is fallible and often confabulatory about the causal etiology and functional role of our mental states, yet it remains uniquely authoritative for the phenomenal character of occurrent conscious experience.

(b) Any mental state that plays a direct guiding role in intentional action must be at least partially transparent to introspection at the time of action; permanent opacity for such guiding states undermines the conditions for full rational and moral agency.

(c) Subpersonal representational dynamics—such as those posited in predictive-processing accounts of perception and affect—are mental in a full functional sense, causally drive both experience and behavior, and are permanently inaccessible to introspection.

(d) When first-person reports conflict with third-person behavioral or neural evidence about an emotional or perceptual state, the correct methodological stance is phenomenal priority: treat the introspective report as defeasible but default-true for the phenomenal quality, and correct it via external data only for its causal-functional attribution.

My question is whether these commitments form a jointly tenable package. Which pairs are in genuine tension, which only appear incompatible but dissolve on closer inspection, and what is the least costly revision if consistency requires abandoning something? Please rank the tensions by severity, indicating for each what exactly would have to be modified or given up and at what theoretical cost—particularly regarding my claim that agency requires transparency and my claim that predictive states are genuinely mental yet opaque. I am not asking for a survey of the literature, but for an internal audit of this specific set.
