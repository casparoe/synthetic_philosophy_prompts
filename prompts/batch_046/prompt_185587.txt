I've been trying to work out what I actually think about whether cross-cultural divergence in folk morality undermines moral objectivity, and I'm hoping that laying out my gut reactions to specific comparisons will reveal an underlying principle—if there is one that doesn't force me to abandon too many of them.

Here are my verdicts.

First, the directness effect documented across WEIRD and some non-WEIRD populations: respondents in highly industrialized societies typically judge physically pushing a person onto a track as morally worse than flipping a switch that produces the identical outcome, whereas in several small-scale societies the physical-contact distinction largely collapses and the two acts are rated nearly equivalent. I think this variation is fully compatible with moral objectivity; it looks like a framing or affective-coding difference, not a disagreement about the underlying moral fact.

Second, norms of partiality: in many East Asian contexts, prioritizing one's own child over a stranger in a life-or-death allocation is treated not merely as permissible but as a stringent obligation, while in strongly individualist WEIRD subcultures the same choice is often judged as a failure of impartial moral reasoning. Here I feel genuinely uneasy. The disagreement seems to concern the weight of special relationships rather than a mere framing effect, so I lean toward thinking this kind of divergence poses a real, if not fatal, challenge to the claim that there is a single objective morality we are all tracking.

Third, hierarchical harmony versus harm prevention: in certain Pacific Islander communities, publicly correcting an elder's factual error about local ecology—when silence would allow harmful resource management—is judged severely wrong because it disrupts relational order, whereas WEIRD respondents typically rank preventing ecological harm above preserving social harmony. I want to say there is no deep conflict here; the two cultures appear to be weighting genuinely different but compatible values (respect for status versus welfare maximization), and a unified objective account could accommodate both as context-sensitive considerations.

Fourth, a hypothetical structural inversion: imagine a culture in which moral evaluation systematically reverses the familiar WEIRD intention-outcome asymmetry—agents who intend harm but accidentally produce widespread good are judged more blameworthy than agents who intend universal good but accidentally harm one person. I find myself thinking this is not just a different weighting but evidence that they are operating with a different concept of moral agency altogether. This kind of divergence, I want to say, does undermine objectivity in a way that the directness case does not.

Fifth, the case I keep going back and forth on—clan-indexed moral discourse. Suppose a society in which moral predicates are explicitly indexed: to say that theft is wrong is always shorthand for theft-is-wrong-for-people-of-my-clan, with no implicit universalization, and members consistently withhold the judgment from outsiders without contradiction. WEIRD respondents, by contrast, tend to universalize automatically. Does this show that their practice is non-objective, or that they are not really engaged in morality in the sense that requires objectivity at all? I genuinely cannot settle whether this is a conceptual difference that saves objectivity or a direct counterexample to it.

Is there one principle—perhaps drawing on distinctions between framing and substance, conceptual continuity, or universalizability conditions—that delivers all five verdicts, or comes close? If not, which verdict should I overturn to preserve a simpler account? And what does the best principle predict for a case I have not listed: a culture in which all normative evaluation is conducted in purely aesthetic terms (this action is disharmonious, that one is beautiful) with no separate lexical category for wrongness, applied consistently across interpersonal, ecological, and ritual domains?
