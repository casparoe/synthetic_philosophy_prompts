I'm presenting a paper next Thursday at an interdisciplinary workshop titled Public Reason and Its Discontents. The audience will be divided between Kierkegaard scholars who specialize in his social philosophy and philosophers of technology who study algorithmic mediation but tend to regard nineteenth-century religious authors as historical background rather than live interlocutors. I am defending a reconstructed interpretation: Kierkegaard's critique of the press and the public involves what I call a double abstraction — first, the familiar leveling of individuals into an anonymous crowd; second, and less noticed, the transformation of every issue into the merely interesting, which severs information from existential commitment. I apply this framework to algorithmic recommendation feeds, arguing that they reproduce specifically the second abstraction. Users are perpetually interested in everything precisely because they are existentially committed to nothing, producing a pseudo-public that differs structurally from both a Habermasian public sphere and the standard filter-bubble model of isolation.

The Kierkegaardians in the room will defend the primacy of the single individual and worry that I am anachronistically importing technological concepts into a religious critique of modernity. The political philosophers present — including republican-liberty theorists and one liberal pluralist — will ask whether my double abstraction is merely republican domination by an anonymous public in different vocabulary, and whether I have ignored the normative resources in Kierkegaard for resisting it. The technology philosophers will demand to know why this framework outperforms existing accounts of engagement metrics or attention economies, and whether I am advancing an empirical claim about algorithmic design or a purely conceptual claim about user subjectivity. The sketch of my argument is as follows. First, the present age is defined not by opinion uniformity but by a relation to truth in which reflection replaces decision. Second, the press facilitates this by making every issue available to everyone without requiring anyone to act upon it. Third, algorithmic feeds intensify this not by concealing opposing views, but by rendering every view equally available and equally weightless.

Please draft my Q&A prep sheet. Order the objections by the damage they would do to my core claim, phrasing each as the corresponding faction would actually put it: the Kierkegaardian concern about misreading the crowd; the republican-liberty challenge regarding anonymous domination; the technology philosopher's demand for structural or empirical specificity; and the liberal pluralist's worry that I am pathologizing democratic openness. For each objection, provide a concise reply suitable for a two-minute exchange, note explicitly where that reply is weakest, state what I should concede up front, and identify the point in my own position I should be ready to defend rather than hope they overlook. Finally, include the objection I am most hoping nobody raises — namely, the challenge that targets the unstated normative premise that existential commitment is the proper measure of public discourse, asking whether that premise itself smuggles in a form of religious individualism that undermines the possibility of secular collective deliberation altogether.
