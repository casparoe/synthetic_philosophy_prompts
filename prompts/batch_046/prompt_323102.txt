I'm a retired chemist who's been poking around philosophy since I left the lab, and my friends keep dragging me into arguments. Two of them — one was a neurobiolgist, the other studies church history — got into it over whether Saul literally died on the road to Damascus and Paul was a brand-new person.

(1) The neurobiologist said yes, Saul died in a real metaphysical sense. His body kept running, but the core mechanism that generated his intentions and self-model got replaced root-and-branch. She said if you swap out the agency-engine but leave the memory-bank intact, you've got a new driver in an old car, not the same driver changed.

(2) The historian said that's mechanistic poetry. Saul's body never stopped, his autobiographical memory stayed contious, and people talk about being born again all the time without philosophers declaring them dead. He said if bodily and memory continuity don't count, then every deep conversion is basically a homicide.

(3) The neurobiologist pushed back that Saul later wrote he had been crucified with Christ and that it was no longer he who lived but Christ living in him. She argued that when the subject explicitly denies being the same agent, and the change is instantaneous rather than gradual, we shouldn't dismiss it as metaphor — we should ask whether the first-person index is still pointing to the same mechanism.

(4) The historian said relying on one man's theologcal self-report to overturn metaphysical continuity is circular. He said if we let subjective claims of replacement override body and memory, then anyone with a dissociative break or intense religious experience could declare themselves a new legal and moral person, and we'd have no stable criterion left.

Please adjudicate move by move: rate each of the four moves from clearly mistaken to clearly right and briefly justify each rating. Say who is right about which specific point, and what both are missing — especially whether they need to seperate practical identity from metaphysical identity, and whether they even agree on what counts as the same mechanism of agency.
