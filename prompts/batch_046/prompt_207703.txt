Following a long and rather exhausting argument with a close friend about Dennett's notion of real patterns and his three stances — physical, design, and intentional — I want to see whether I can actually reconstruct her commitments rather than projecting my own. Over the course of our discussion she delivered a consistent series of verdicts about which stances apply to various systems and, crucially, whether the patterns revealed at those stances are genuinely real in the predictive-compression sense, not merely heuristic. I need help determining what rule she is actually running, because I think her judgments fit more than one principle, and I want to know how she would treat a case we did not discuss.

Her record is as follows. With human beings she holds that all three stances apply and that the patterns at each level are real, with the intentional patterns being irreducible. With a classic chess engine she grants that the design stance is appropriate and that its strategic patterns are real — predicting its moves requires attributing evaluative goals that physical-level descriptions do not tractably capture — but she denies that the intentional stance applies in any literal sense. With a thermostat she allows design-language but insists that the patterns are not real: the behavior is fully reducible to thermal physics, offering no independent predictive compression. With a hurricane she applies only the physical stance, treating apparent goal-direction as pure projection. With the slime mold Physarum polycephalum navigating a maze she again grants real design-level patterns, arguing that functional prediction of its growth paths outperforms practical physical simulation, yet she rejects the intentional stance on the grounds that there is no centralized representational architecture. Finally, with current large language models producing coherent discourse she applies the design stance only descriptively and denies that the patterns are real, describing them as parasitic on human intentional structures and lacking autonomous functional integration.

The untested case is a distributed swarm of autonomous aerial units, each governed solely by local reactive rules with no central controller, no shared global model, and no internal representational economy at the individual level. Collectively, however, they exhibit robust adaptive path optimization through a complex three-dimensional environment that no single unit surveys; the emergent routing behavior is not tractably derived from the physics of any individual unit, yet there is no unified teleological center that integrates the swarm's activity.

What would she say? Please identify the two or three principles that best accommodate her earlier verdicts — for instance, a principle of predictive autonomy regardless of substrate, versus a principle requiring internally integrated functional teleology — and state what each principle predicts about the swarm. Then give your best guess of her actual verdict, with reasons for preferring the principle that generates it. Next, name the one further case that would most sharply discriminate between these competing rules. I am particularly interested in whether her treatment of the slime mold commits her to treating the swarm similarly, or whether there is a principled distinction that allows her to deny real patterns to the swarm without undermining her judgment about the mold.
