I am revising a co-authored manuscript on conceptual engineering, and a recent disagreement with my colleague has made the issue personally urgent. We discovered that we use the term "copycat" in incompatible ways: she restricts it to deliberate imitation with deceptive intent, whereas I apply it to any structural replication that lacks originality, including unconscious algorithmic reproduction. This slippage within such a narrow microdomain has persuaded me that my advanced seminar needs an assessment that forces students to diagnose conceptual drift rather than merely summarize familiar theories of vagueness or family resemblance. Please design a single 90-minute essay question for an upper-level seminar—advanced undergraduates or first-year graduate students—on conceptual slippage in a tightly bounded domain, such as the taxonomy of replication in generative art or the boundary between homage, parody, and counterfeit in a niche craft tradition. The question should require students to identify the specific mechanisms driving the slippage—e.g., prototype effects, functional shift, or institutional redefinition—and to evaluate whether that slippage is a defect to be engineered away or a productive feature of conceptual practice. Include a model answer that executes at least four distinct analytical moves, and provide a marking scheme structured around five must-hit points totaling 20 marks, specifying the value of each point, a one-line description of the correct response, and a brief note on the typical error candidates make on that point. Do not include requests for bibliographies, citations, or reading lists, and do not invoke highly specialized empirical literature that a student without library access could not reasonably be expected to know.
