Consider Switzerland’s two-tiered referendum architecture. To amend the federal constitution, a popular initiative must win both a national popular majority and a majority of the cantons; but an ordinary federal statute can be forced to a referendum by 50,000 citizens (or eight cantons) and needs only a national popular majority to be rejected or upheld, with no cantonal hurdle at all. This means the same citizenry can alter statutory law through a unitary national vote, yet can only alter the fundamental rules through a dual consent—of the aggregated people and of the territorial sub-units.

What philosophical conception of democratic legitimacy, collective agency, or popular sovereignty does this design presuppose? In particular, does the double-majority rule treat “the Swiss people” as one sovereign demos, as a federation of sovereign cantonal demoi, or as a composite entity that does not exist unless both dimensions concur? Reconstruct the implicit theory: distinguish what the practice strictly presupposes from what merely fits well with it; identify which rival views of democracy—pure majoritarian, purely federal, purely deliberative, or pluralist—are quietly ruled out by the two-tier structure; and say whether the lower tier (the optional referendum on statutes) presupposes the same conception of the people or a different one, so that the system may be philosophically inconsistent. Finally, describe what specific institutional changes would be required if the underlying view were replaced by a straightforwardly unitary, national-majoritarian conception of popular sovereignty, and note where the practice is philosophically underdetermined—i.e., which competing theories could license the very same two-tier rules.
