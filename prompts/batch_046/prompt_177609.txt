I have been turning over in my mind that old classroom puzzle about the surprise examination: a teacher announces that there will be an unannounced test on one of the five days of next week, and the students work backward to prove it can never come as a surprise, yet they are indeed surprised when Friday arrives. I understand the logical loop well enough, but I am less interested in the formal solution than in what happens when the same reasoning is applied outside a classroom, by people who have something real to lose. Would you write a short story set in a small harbor town where the lighthouse keeper, bound by an old maritime covenant he cannot disclose, tells the captains that on one night this month he will darken the beam for exactly one hour—not as failure, but as a test of whether they navigate by the charts or merely follow the light. The captains and the villagers begin the same step-by-step elimination: if the dark night were the last of the month, they would expect it; therefore it cannot be the last; then the second-to-last is eliminated in turn, and so on. One captain realizes the reasoning is circular—each step assumes the very surprise it tries to deny—but she cannot stop using it to plan her cargo runs. The story should show the paradox not as an abstract contradiction but as a lived pressure on decisions about leaving port, and it should end without resolving whether the keeper keeps his word or breaks the pattern. I am not looking for references to logicians or textbooks; I am trying to see whether the paradox feels different when lives and livelihoods depend on it rather than grades.
