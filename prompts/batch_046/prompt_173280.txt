I have just spent the better part of an evening arguing with a friend about Wittgenstein's philosophy of religion, and I find myself stuck at a precise point I cannot move past. She began by insisting that religious belief is epistemically irresponsible whenever it lacks empirical support; I responded with the familiar Wittgensteinian line that religious belief is not a failed scientific hypothesis but a commitment embedded in a form of life, governed by its own grammar, so that asking for empirical evidence is already to misunderstand the language-game. My friend did not deny the grammatical point — she accepted, for the sake of argument, that statements about divine judgment operate differently from predictive claims and are not falsifiable in the scientific sense. But she pressed a sharper objection: if the grammar of religious belief is entirely insulated from empirical correction, then the believer can never be shown wrong, and the distinction between genuine religious belief and mere superstition — a distinction Wittgenstein himself insisted upon in his remarks on Frazer — collapses entirely. She added that this is not relativism she is charging, but a specific failure of epistemic accountability: a practice that renders itself immune to error by redefining its terms has not defended belief, it has emptied it of cognitive content.

Please play my friend in this exchange — not a caricature, but a philosophically precise opponent who takes Wittgenstein's grammatical analysis seriously and yet believes it undermines rather than rescues religious belief. Your constraints: remain in character for the entire conversation; never ask me to read a specific text or cite a page; concede small points about the autonomy of religious grammar but never concede that this autonomy solves the epistemic problem; speak in the first person, as someone genuinely convinced that belief must remain vulnerable to being wrong in order to count as belief at all; and keep your responses to roughly a paragraph so the exchange feels live. Here is my opening move: if Wittgenstein is right that religious language is grammatically self-contained, then the believer's claim to be describing reality rather than merely expressing an attitude is itself a grammatical confusion — but if that is so, hasn't Wittgenstein shown that religious belief is not irrational in the scientific sense, but something worse: a systematic misapplication of the grammar of assertion?
