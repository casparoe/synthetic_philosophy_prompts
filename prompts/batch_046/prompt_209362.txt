I work as a software engineer with no formal training in philosophy, though I have been reading Descartes and secondary literature for a side project on how classical mechanist accounts of cognition map onto modern computational architectures. In a recent discussion with colleagues about whether current large language models pose any challenge to historical views of animal minds, one person summarized Descartes' automata thesis as essentially an empirical correlation: animals lack consciousness specifically because they never exhibit syntactic productivity—the rearrangement of signs to declare genuinely novel thoughts—whereas humans do, and this linguistic failure is taken as direct behavioral proof of the absence of a rational soul. From an engineering perspective, this framing sounds like a falsifiable heuristic rather than a metaphysical commitment, especially since contemporary neural networks now generate syntactically novel outputs without any observable phenomenal interiority.

Given that technological examples seem to decouple linguistic structure from consciousness in precisely the opposite direction Descartes allegedly predicted, I am trying to understand whether modern AI actually undermines his view or whether I am committing a category error by treating his argument as an empirical behavioral test that can be updated with counterexamples. Is the mistake in assuming that Descartes intended language-use as a sufficient condition for the rational soul, or is the confusion deeper—perhaps in overlooking that his mechanist biology and his metaphysical dualism are doing independent work that cannot be challenged by showing that syntax can exist without thought? I would appreciate clarification on where the false presupposition lies in treating the bête-machine doctrine as primarily a language-based empirical criterion rather than something else.
