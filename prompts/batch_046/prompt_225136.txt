I'm writing a feature on decision science and at a conference last week I overheard two reaserchers arguing about whether honoring sunk costs is ever rational. Maya, who does behavioral econ, said that any appeal to past investment is just a framing error — the only rational input is future utility minus oppertunity cost, full stop. She used the classic long-book example but twisted it: if you've read 300 pages of a dense philoshopical text and the remaining 600 contain the argument that resolves a contradiction you've been tracking, then finishing isn't about honoring sunk pages, it's a standard expected-value calculation. Raj, a philosopher of action, said that's a category error. He argued past commitments generate present reasons through what he called diachronic agency — the self at t1 binds t2 not because resources are spent, but because continuity of the project is part of the good. He used a musician practicing a hard piece: the hours don't justify more practice, but initiating long-form skill acquisition means quitting now disrupts a narrative structure she rationally values. Maya replied Raj is smuggling future goods and relabeling them past ones. Raj countered that some goods are essentially historical, like being the kind of person who completes long projects, and that property depends on the past investment being genuinely past, not just a setup for reward. They asked me to referee and I honestly couldn't tell if they're talking past each other or if one actually wins. Can you adjudicate? Is Maya right that any rational reason to continue must reduce to future utility? Is Raj right that there are genuinely non-fungible, diachronic goods? And who is closer to being right overall?
