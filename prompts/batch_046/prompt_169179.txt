I am drafting the final take-home examination for an advanced undergraduate seminar on philosophy of life and value, and I need one essay question that students can address in roughly ninety minutes and about 1,500 words. The prompt must do more than ask for summaries of Camus or of contemporary hybrid theories; instead, please construct it around the following conceptual distinction, which we have developed in course discussions but which is not standard textbook material: the difference between meaning-as-attribution, in which significance is projected onto an indifferent cosmos by a valuing agent, and meaning-as-fit, in which significance emerges from an objective structural correspondence between an agent's capacities and the character of their activities, without requiring any cosmic endorsement. The exam question should ask students to evaluate whether hybrid accounts of meaning—those that require both subjective engagement and objective value—actually resolve the tension Camus identifies between human longing for absolute significance and the world's refusal to provide it, or whether they merely relocate that tension into the criterion of fit itself. Please provide three components. First, the exam prompt itself, clearly stating the task, the time and length constraints, and the instruction that answers must rely on conceptual reasoning rather than textual exegesis. Second, a model answer that identifies the five analytical moves a top response must make: correctly applying the attribution-versus-fit distinction; determining whether hybrid theories smuggle in an unacknowledged objectivism; explaining why Camus's notion of revolt is not reducible to subjective attribution; assessing whether fit can survive without transcendence; and reaching a reasoned verdict on whether the absurd is dissolved or only renamed. Third, a marking scheme out of twenty marks, allocating five marks for the correct application of the distinction, five for the analysis of hidden objectivism, four for the treatment of revolt, four for the assessment of fit without transcendence, and two for argumentative clarity and structure. For each criterion, include a one-line note on the typical student error—such as conflating Camus with pure existentialist subjectivism, treating fit as mere psychological satisfaction, or assuming that adding an objective condition automatically solves the problem of cosmic silence. The question should be fully self-contained and must not ask students to supply bibliographies, cite specific editions, or reference particular passages they could not be expected to have memorized.
