Consider the following characterization of Galen Strawson's Basic Argument against moral responsibility, offered in a recent discussion of agency and determinism: Strawson demonstrates that moral responsibility is impossible under determinism, since every action ultimately traces back to causes—genetic, environmental, neurological—that lie outside the agent's control. Even if an agent succeeds in altering her own character through sustained effort, each such change is itself produced by prior psychological traits that she did not choose, so the regress of self-creation never reaches a genuinely self-originated origin. The only remaining option is therefore libertarian free will, in which choices are not determined by prior states; yet Strawson rejects this as well, on the grounds that an uncaused decision is merely a random event lacking rational connection to the agent's values, and hence cannot support praise or blame. From this he concludes that true moral responsibility is unattainable in any metaphysical system. The discussant then applies the argument to an extreme case of successive self-modification: imagine an individual who, over several decades, systematically uses targeted pharmacological interventions to restructure her emotional dispositions and normative commitments. The discussant claims that even in this scenario, Strawson's reasoning still applies, because the initial decision to undertake the regimen must have been caused by pre-existing traits that the agent never authored. Assess the fairness of this summary. Go through its central claims and indicate which ones accurately capture the logic of the Basic Argument, which ones subtly misrepresent Strawson's target—especially any conflation of determinism with the broader conditions he sets for true responsibility—and whether the discussant's extension to long-term pharmacological self-engineering respects the structure of successive self-creation or overlooks a distinction essential to the argument's progression.
