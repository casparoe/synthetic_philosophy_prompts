You are an expert philosophical speechwriter with training in modal logic and the rhetoric of public commemoration. Before drafting anything, spend a brief paragraph brainstorming three things: (1) how the logical structure of the Damascus parable—specifically the idea that flight can be the very mechanism of fulfillment—can organize an entire address without collapsing into fatalism or cheap consolation; (2) how to write sentences that work simultaneously for philosophers of time and for family members with no specialist background; and (3) how to honor a sincere materialist’s refusal of afterlife talk without leaving the room emotionally stranded.

Your task is to write the opening address (about seven minutes, spoken at a measured pace) for the memorial colloquium honoring Dr. Elias Vore, a philosopher who spent forty years on modal logic, metaphysics of time, and the philosophy of religion. The audience is divided: half are colleagues and former students who know his work, half are family and close friends who do not. Dr. Vore was a committed materialist and skeptic of all afterlife claims; he left explicit instructions that anyone speaking at his memorial should not say he is “in a better place,” “watching over us,” or “at peace now” in any metaphysical sense, because he considered such statements intellectually dishonest and personally betraying.

The address must take as its central structural device the old parable of Death in Damascus—the servant who, seeing Death in the marketplace, flees to Damascus, only to learn that Death had an appointment with him there. Do not use the parable as decorative imagery. Instead, let its logic shape what the speaker can and cannot claim about inevitability, choice, and remembrance. In particular, weave in the accommodation thesis: the servant’s flight is not an independent, causally free attempt to change fate, but is itself the path that keeps the appointment. Use this to illuminate how grief and remembrance work—namely, that attempts to outrun or deny loss often construct the very route that delivers us back to it—without implying that grief is therefore meaningless, or that acceptance is passive resignation. The speech should comfort those who are not philosophers without saying anything Dr. Vore would have considered false. Avoid bibliographies, citation lists, reading recommendations, and references to specific published interpretations you cannot verify. Maintain a tone that is formal, humane, and precise.

Proceed first with your brief brainstorming notes, then deliver the speech in full.
