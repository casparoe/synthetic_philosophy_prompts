I'm working through my actual commitments about the rational value of acquiring information before acting, and whether they line up with the standard decision-theoretic result that information cannot reduce your expected utility if you remain free to respond to it. Here are my verdicts on concrete cases.

Before choosing between two surgeries with different risk profiles, I should gather precise outcome data: clearly rational, and more detail is better.

Before deciding whether to carry an umbrella, checking a weather forecast is rational, even though the weather itself is not up to me.

Before casting a vote in a large election, studying the candidates' records is rational, even though my single vote will not change the outcome.

Before selecting a restaurant, reading reviews is rational.

Paying to learn the result of a coin I flipped yesterday, when that result now affects nothing I care about: irrational.

Paying to learn the exact year of my death, assuming I cannot prevent it and have no descendants whose planning depends on it: irrational.

Paying for a genetic screen that reveals an untreatable, non-heritable condition with no lifestyle or reproductive implications: irrational. But if the same screen reveals a heritable risk that lets me advise relatives, or reveals a treatable condition: rational.

Paying to learn climate-model projections for the year 2300, when I will be dead, have no descendants, and cannot now influence policy: irrational.

Here is where I waver. I think it is rational to study philosophical arguments about moral responsibility, even though I do not think my belief will change any of my actions in a measurable way. By contrast, I think it is irrational to study astrological predictions for the same reason—belief-change without action-change. So I seem to allow epistemic value for philosophy but deny it for astrology, yet I have no principled boundary between them.

There is also a tension in my pre-commitment judgments. I think it is irrational to pay for an allergy test if I have already decided never to change my diet regardless of the result. But I also think it can be rational to "face the truth" about a painful, untreatable diagnosis even when I have pre-committed to refuse all treatment, provided I do so for the sake of honest self-assessment rather than action-guidance. Both cases involve a blocked action-channel, yet I treat them differently.

Is there a coherent principle—perhaps a qualified version of the standard result, or an explicit division into instrumental, epistemic, and affective value—that captures all or most of these verdicts? State it precisely, indicate which clause handles which case, predict what it says about two cases I have not listed (a predictive genetic test for an untreatable but highly stigmatized condition, and learning the outcome of a past sports match that I already bet on but can no longer collect), and be honest about which of my verdicts it forces me to drop. If no single principle works without excessive complexity, present the two best competitors and say which verdict each must sacrifice.
