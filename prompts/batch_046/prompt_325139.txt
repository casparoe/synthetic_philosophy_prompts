Two people in my study group got stuck in an arguement about whether the Middle Ages, the Renaissance, and the Scientific Revolution are real divisions or just stories historiens tell later. Jordan said they're basically 19th-century inventions — universities ran straight through, Aristotle was still being taught in 1650, and calling 1400 a "rebirth" just means humanists started writing differently. Sam pushed back that there's a genuine shift in explanitory norms: by 1700, saying the rock falls because it seeks its natural place wasn't merely unfashionable, it was no longer a live explanation in the same institutional way, and that cluster change justifies the period even if old texts stayed on the shelf. Jordan came back that this cherry-picks — there was mechanistic talk in the 1300s (impetus, clockwork metaphors) and plenty of teleology in Leibniz, so the "break" is just scholars smoothing out outliers. Sam said then no category could survive, since every era has impurities; the point isn't purity but whether there's a statistical shift in what gets funded, taught, and treated as canonical explanation. Jordan said that's circular — you select the texts that fit your cluster, then claim the cluster proves the texts belong there. Sam said it's hermeneutic, not circular: we interpret past practices through present frameworks, and the real question is whether the pattern is revealed or imposed. They both refused to budge. Can you go through their exchange and say who's right at each step — not just who wins overall? And what's the deeper thing both are missing about how periodization actually works?
