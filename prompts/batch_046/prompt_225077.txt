Analyze the 2000 Florida presidential recount—specifically the dispute over punch-card ballots producing hanging chads, dimpled chads, and the Florida Supreme Court's instruction that canvassing boards apply an "intent of the voter" standard—through the competing frameworks of epistemicism and supervaluationism about vagueness. First, map the boundary dispute precisely: on the epistemicist view (in the style of Williamson), is there a sharp but epistemically hidden cutoff that determines whether a given ballot is valid, such that election officials in different counties were merely ignorant of a single correct answer? If so, what does it mean for the U.S. Supreme Court in Bush v. Gore to have halted the recount on equal-protection and procedural grounds rather than directing a discovery of that hidden boundary? On the supervaluationist view (in the style of Fine), does the statutory silence about chad conditions entail that there was no uniquely correct precisification, so that a Palm Beach County board accepting dimpled chads and a Miami-Dade board rejecting them were not making factual errors but selecting among admissible semantic sharpenings? Second, examine the adversarial recount structure itself—partisan legal teams advancing competing interpretations of ballot validity under severe time pressure—and ask whether this dynamic is better explained as competing hypotheses about an unknown sharp boundary (epistemicism) or as a practical negotiation among legitimate precisifications (supervaluationism). Third, extend the analysis by treating the election margin itself as a potentially vague magnitude: ask whether there was a determinate vote total for each candidate that we failed to access (epistemic opacity about an ontic fact), or whether the collective preference was genuinely indeterminate until judicial intervention imposed one admissible sharpening (semantic indeterminacy resolved by decision). Finally, identify at least one structural feature of the episode that each framework misdescribes or leaves silent—such as the asymmetry between overvotes and undervotes, the federalist division of authority among counties, or the fact that the dispute was ultimately resolved by procedural equal-protection arguments rather than by semantic disambiguation—and conclude with what the case reveals about whether legal-administrative vagueness operates analogously to standard sorites-style vagueness or introduces a distinctively institutional form of indeterminacy.
