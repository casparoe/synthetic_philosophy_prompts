I am a high school senior in an advanced historiography elective with about sixteen students, meeting for a single ninety-minute session in a classroom with movable desks. I need a design for an in-class simulation that makes participants feel—before anyone names it—the structural tension between microhistory and elite or state-scale history, specifically through an idea I will call archival inversion: that the closer one zooms to recover a subaltern voice, the more one depends on the bureaucratic machinery—tax disputes, trial transcripts, census files—that originally produced and suppressed that voice, so that fragmentary personal traces survive almost exclusively as annexes to state records. Please design a runnable group activity with assigned roles—microhistorians who work with synthetic fragment cards, state archivists who hold complete but depersonalized administrative ledgers, and synthesis writers who must produce a single three-sentence causal claim about a fictional seventeenth-century village crisis—plus rules that prevent direct negotiation between roles, synthetic document cards that reveal the archival dependency only after the microhistorians have committed to an interpretation, a small-stakes payoff scheme such as publication tokens that are awarded only if all three roles can sign the final claim, and a structured timeline for the session. The core idea should emerge from what happens in the game—especially the moment when microhistorians discover that their richest personal source is embedded in a state investigation—rather than from any lecture up front. Include a debrief that maps each phase of the simulation onto parts of the archival-inversion problem, predicts what will predictably go wrong on the first run, for instance microhistorians initially rejecting all state-linked fragments and then being left with almost nothing, and frankly states which feature of actual microhistorical practice—such as the emotional shock of encountering an irreducibly alien mental world inside a fragment—this classroom exercise necessarily cannot reproduce and must be supplied by the facilitator. Do not ask for bibliographies, reading lists, or citations; do not invoke highly obscure theorists by proper name; and write the response in American English.
