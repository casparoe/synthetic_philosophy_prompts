Please explain the conceptual tension in Hume's monetary writings—particularly in the essays treating money, the balance of trade, and public credit—between his claim that money is not part of the real wealth of a nation and his acceptance of the quantity-theory intuition that a sudden increase in the circulating medium can, in the short run, stimulate labor and industry. I am not looking for a summary of the specie-flow mechanism, but for an analysis of how Hume reconciles this temporary stimulative effect with his broader thesis that only labor and industry constitute genuine riches, and whether that reconciliation depends on treating money as a mere representative of commodities rather than as an independent cause of value. To press the point, apply his framework to a hypothetical economy that Hume never considers: a small, open trading state with no metallic currency whatsoever, relying solely on a government-issued fiat medium convertible not into specie but exclusively into tax obligations. Within Hume's own conceptual scheme, should this be treated as a legitimate circulating medium analogous to the paper credit he discusses, or does the absence of a self-correcting specie-flow mechanism render the quantity-theory dynamics inapplicable? Derive the answer from the internal logic of his texts rather than from modern macroeconomics, and indicate whether your result reveals a hidden assumption in his distinction between real and nominal wealth.
