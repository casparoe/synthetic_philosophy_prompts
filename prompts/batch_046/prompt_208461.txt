I am grading take-home essays for my graduate seminar on negative value theory and need an objective, point-by-point assessment of one submission—without any signal of my own view on the issue. The exam question was: Tranquilism holds that only the absence of craving and suffering possesses intrinsic value; all positive states—joy, achievement, aesthetic appreciation, social harmony—are at best instrumentally valuable as means to that absence. Evaluate whether tranquilism can consistently accommodate the intuition that the successful completion of a complex, long-term moral project, such as sustained mediation that reconciles estranged communities, has a value that is not reducible to the mere absence of suffering in the affected parties. In your answer, distinguish tranquilism from negative utilitarianism, and address whether recognizing a stable social peace as the outcome commits the tranquilist to accepting a positive relational good.

The student’s answer follows.

Tranquilism differs from negative utilitarianism because the latter is an aggregative doctrine about minimizing total suffering across beings, whereas tranquilism is a structural claim about individual value: only the removal of craving and suffering matters, and positive experiences contribute nothing intrinsically. Applying this to mediation, the tranquilist can grant that reconciliation has value, but only derivatively. The mediator eliminates suffering caused by estrangement, and the resulting social peace is valuable exactly as the durable absence of conflict—not as an added harmony. If one treats peace as a positive relational quality, tranquilism risks collapsing into hedonism, since it would be recognizing a good beyond mere absence.

The mediator’s own fulfillment poses a parallel challenge. The tranquilist should say that any satisfaction the mediator feels is not a positive achievement but simply the absence of the craving that drove the project; once that craving is removed, there is no further value in lingering satisfaction. Thus every apparently positive feature—the reconciliation, the mediator’s satisfaction, the stability of peace—can be redescribed as the absence of a corresponding negative state. Tranquilism therefore accommodates the case without contradiction, though it requires a disciplined reinterpretation of ordinary evaluative language.

Assess only the reasoning displayed; do not state whether tranquilism itself succeeds.
