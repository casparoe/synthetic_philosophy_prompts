I have been reflecting independently on Kant's principle that one ought to act only according to maxims one can consistently will as universal law. The familiar illustrations—false promises, suicide, neglecting one's talents—seem distant from ordinary life. I want to examine a maxim that feels closer to my own hesitations: "Whenever I encounter a claim in science, medicine, or technology that lies outside my direct competence, I will suspend judgment unless I can personally reconstruct the evidence or reasoning that supports it, regardless of the apparent consensus among practitioners." If this maxim were adopted universally, the division of cognitive labor that allows complex societies to accumulate knowledge might collapse, because every individual would reject testimony they could not reproduce, leaving each person confined to what they could verify alone. Yet it is unclear whether this result is the kind of contradiction Kant's test is meant to expose, or merely an unfortunate practical consequence.

Please write a dialogue between two equally capable interlocutors debating whether this maxim fails the universalizability test. One should argue that universal adoption generates a contradiction—either in the logic of the rule itself or in what rational beings must want—because it destroys the conditions of belief-formation that make the maxim's own exercise possible. The other should maintain that the maxim survives, insisting that any breakdown is contingent on human cognitive limitations rather than inherent in the maxim itself, and that rational agents could consistently will a world in which everyone relies solely on personally verified belief. Have them address each other's points directly, especially on the distinction between logical contradiction and practical dysfunction, and avoid reducing either position to a generic defense or attack on expertise. Make the exchange genuinely reciprocal, not two separate speeches.
