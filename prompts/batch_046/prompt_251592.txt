I retired from biophysics about five years ago and now I'm poking around philosophy in retirement — mostly Arendt lately. I read her Eichmann book and got stuck on the banality of evil thing, not the monster version but the thoughtlessness part, this Gedankenlosigkeit she keeps bringing up. So I rewatched Schindlers List and something bugged me: the film spends so much energy on Göth being this theatrical sadist, shooting people from his balcony, but then there's these quiet scenes with Schindler just shuffling papers and negotiating factory lists with SS bureaucrats. I want to read the movie through Arendt's lens, but not in the obvious 'bureaucrats are bad' way. Specifically, does the film undermine Arendt by making evil so visible and personal with Göth, while the actual banal evil is buried in the paperwork Schindler games? And here's where I'm trying to go deeper: Arendt later writes about 'representitive thinking' — this Kant-derived capacity to imagine other perspectives as part of judgment. Does Schindler's transformation from profiteer to rescuer actually illustrate that kind of judgment emerging, or is it just opportunism? I want specific scenes to point my little philosophy group to, and whether the film adds something to Arendt's argument that pure reasoning can't capture. I might be overreading, but that's the fun part, right?
