I am studying for a seminar on philosophy of mind that focuses on the 'user-illusion' account of consciousness — specifically the view, associated with predictive-processing frameworks and interface theories, that the conscious self functions rather like a desktop environment concealing the underlying neural computation. During our discussion of whether this model eliminates a substantive subject of experience, the tutor kept returning to the one-liner, 'there is no user behind the interface', using it to shut down any suggestion that there remains a real ego or persisting subject to whom experiences belong. I want to bring a clearer disambiguation back to the group, because the slogan seemed to be doing very different work depending on how one parsed it.

Could you lay out the distinct precise theses that 'there is no user behind the interface' might express, arranged from the trivial to the radically eliminativist? For each reading, please indicate: (i) exactly what it denies (e.g., a Cartesian theatre, a homunculus, a unified narrative self, phenomenal ownership itself); (ii) whether it is compatible with retaining a minimal first-person perspective or with predictive-processing explanations of self-modelling; (iii) whether it genuinely undermines the idea of a substantive self, or whether it merely restates a familiar anti-homuncular point; and (iv) whether adopting that reading forces the advocate into a stronger metaphysical commitment — say, full-blown illusionism about consciousness — than they typically wish to defend. I am particularly interested in whether one can consistently hold that the interface is a useful fiction generated by the brain's self-model without also committing to the claim that there is literally nobody to whom the fiction appears.

Please present your answer in British English, as a structured menu rather than a single definitive interpretation, so that I can map each option onto the arguments actually circulating in the seminar.
