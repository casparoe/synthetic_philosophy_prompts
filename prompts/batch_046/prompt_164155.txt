I am a retired biophysicist who, since leaving active research, has been studying philosophy of mind with the same disciplinary care I once reserved for membrane dynamics. I am organizing a small interview series for other scientists crossing into conceptual territory, and next week I will be speaking with an unnamed philosopher—let us call her Dr. Voss—who defends a refined version of Daniel Dennett’s multiple drafts model. She rejects any Cartesian theater in which consciousness comes together at a single point, but she argues that Dennett’s own distinction between Stalinesque and Orwellian revisions of mental content is incomplete without an additional layer: she distinguishes ephemeral drafts that never achieve widespread influence in the brain from canonical drafts that are stabilized not by a central observer but by distributed cross-modal predictive coherence. She maintains that this framework resolves the binding problem without smuggling in a unitary comparator.

Please draft eight to ten interview questions for this conversation, structured as a numbered list that begins immediately with the first question—no introductory remarks, no summary of Dennett’s position, and no commentary outside the questions. Each question should lead with its core challenge in the opening clause, then supply the reasoning or context that makes the pressure precise; include a brief follow-up keyed to the reply she is most likely to offer, such as an appeal to distributed rather than centralized coherence. At least one question should ask whether her predictive-coherence criterion functions as a functional equivalent of the Cartesian theater—specifically, whether cross-modal conflict resolution requires a single point of comparison. Another should press her on split-brain cases in which cross-hemispheric coherence is disrupted: ask how her model accounts for unified report in one hemisphere alongside unintegrated drafts in the other. A third should demand an explanation of why the illusion of a continuous present is not merely a post-hoc narrative fiction but possesses causal efficacy in action selection, with a follow-up anticipating her reference to temporal integration windows. Keep the questions away from generic biography and away from requests for citations or reading lists; they should turn entirely on the internal logic of her refinement.
