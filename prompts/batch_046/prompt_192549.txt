I serve on the Mid-Valley Regional Resource Commission, a county-level advisory body that recommends long-range water and agricultural policy to our council. Next month we must vote on whether to incorporate the revised Cascade Depletion Framework into the 2040 regional infrastructure bonds. The framework originated in a 1982 predictive model built by our state university's agricultural extension office; it forecast that groundwater collapse and sustained grain-yield failure would occur by the year 2000. Those predictions plainly failed to materialize, largely because emergency desalination subsidies and temporary irrigation moratoria masked the underlying depletion curve. The original research team has retired, but a current group of hydrologists and regional economists has revived the model, arguing that it was never genuinely falsified—only "early." They claim the structural mechanics of aquifer drawdown were correct, that the temporary subsidies merely delayed the trajectory, and that now, with those subsidies expiring and intermediate measurements aligning with the model's curve, the framework should guide our multi-decade planning.

My fellow commissioners are divided in a way that feels deeper than a data dispute. One faction holds that missing a central long-run milestone by over two decades is straightforward falsification: a theory that predicts collapse by 2000 and sees prosperity instead should not be resurrected for bond planning without extraordinary, independent confirmation. The other faction treats this as a case of "rescue by delay"—the mechanism was sound but the timeline compressed, and dismissing the theory because of speed errors risks throwing away genuinely predictive structural knowledge. I have no formal philosophical training, but I have been thinking on my own about whether a long-range prediction can be wrong in its date yet right in its substance, and I do not want our commission to treat that question as mere rhetoric. I need a formal advisory memo—written for circulation to the full commission and eventually to the county council—that recommends a concrete course of action: adopt the framework with binding conditions, reject it outright, or defer adoption pending specific verification steps, with a clear time frame attached. The memo must treat the "right but early" problem as a substantive conceptual issue rather than an escape hatch, and it must present an honest, serious account of the strongest consideration on the losing side—particularly the worry that allowing timeline-rescued theories into planning opens the door to unfalsifiable doctrines. Please do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or requests for citations; the reasoning should stand on its own for commissioners who understand planning and risk but do not have philosophical backgrounds. The tone should be formal, the recommendation genuinely actionable, and the reasoning accessible without reference to specialized literature.
