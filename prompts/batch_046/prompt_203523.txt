Analyze the solar event reported at Fátima, Portugal, in October 1917—when thousands of onlookers, among them observers with no prior religious commitment to the apparition narrative, described the sun as changing color, rotating, and appearing to fall toward the earth—through the lens of David Hume's argument in "Of Miracles" that the falsehood of testimony is always more probable than the violation of an established law of nature. Do not address meteorological explanations; address only the epistemology of belief formation and the ethics of judgment. Apply Hume's probabilistic reasoning precisely: if testimony is multiplied across thousands of simultaneous witnesses, does that increase the evidential weight linearly, or does it reproduce the same perceptual error at scale? How should Hume's distinction between a genuine breach of natural law and a collective misinterpretation of a natural phenomenon be applied when the observers largely agree on the sensory description but diverge sharply on its cause? Does the presence of initially skeptical observers weaken Hume's preferred explanation—that witnesses are moved by interest, credulity, or deception? Running the other direction, ask what this episode reveals about Hume's framework: does a widely shared, anomalous experience threaten his claim that uniform experience establishes fixed laws of nature, or does it instead illustrate that even massive agreement should yield to the prior improbability of solar kinematics being suspended? Identify exactly whose belief is in question—the direct witnesses, the ecclesiastical authorities who later endorsed the event, or the contemporary skeptic—and specify where Hume's argument offers no clear verdict rather than forcing one.
