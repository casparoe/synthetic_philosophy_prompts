I am giving the opening remarks at an interdisciplinary workshop on institutional planning for long-range futures, co-hosted by a philosophy of science group and a policy school. I have read rationalist blogs and forums for years, and I want to import into this room a framing I have encountered in discussions there—though I am not attributing it to any specific author—because it solves a problem I keep seeing in planning discourse. The audience is split: half are philosophers familiar with modal epistemology and the conceivability-possibility link, half are analysts and policymakers who build scenarios but do not use formal philosophical vocabulary. The remarks should run about eight minutes, be polished but not academic, and avoid any citations, bibliographies, or reading lists.

The philosophical idea should do real structural work, not serve as decoration. I want the speech built around a distinction between two readings of "we cannot conceive of X": epistemic inconceivability, meaning my own conceptual scheme or imagination currently lacks the resources to represent it; and metaphysical inconceivability, meaning the scenario is being presented as incompatible with modal structure itself. I want the opening to establish that planners routinely conflate these two, treating an imaginative failure as if it carried uniform metaphysical force. The middle should show that each reading licenses a different institutional response—epistemic inconceivability calls for expanded modeling, cognitive diversity, or deferred judgment, whereas metaphysical inconceivability, if genuinely established, calls for resilience and governance without full representational access. The closing should return to the boundary: the honest planner's task is not to render the unimaginable conceivable, but to build institutions that remain stable across an epistemic gap that may never close. The speech should navigate this so that the philosophers recognize the underlying modal issue without feeling lectured, and the practitioners leave with a usable distinction rather than a vocabulary lesson.
