Act as a professional moral philosopher with expertise in the ethics of blame, hypocrisy, and institutional standing. Before you commit to any single interpretation, take a moment to brainstorm the logical space of conditions that could govern when one agent may blame another—do not settle on the first principle that comes to mind.

Consider the consistent applied-ethics rulings made by a senior colleague who works on moral standing. She has delivered the following verdicts on whether Agent A retains standing to blame Agent B:

1. A recovering alcoholic, sober for five years and actively in treatment, blames a peer for binge-drinking at a party. Verdict: standing is retained.
2. A philanthropist whose fortune was built on fossil-fuel investments blames a low-income commuter for driving a gasoline-powered car. Verdict: standing is lost.
3. A legislator who personally exploited a tax loophole she now publicly condemns blames a corporation for using that same loophole. Verdict: standing is lost.
4. A mostly vegetarian who occasionally eats farmed fish blames a sport hunter for killing deer for recreation. Verdict: standing is retained.
5. A university administrator who committed plagiarism as an undergraduate, served her sanction, and now enforces the plagiarism policy blames a current student for the same offense. Verdict: standing is retained.
6. A climate researcher who takes one necessary international flight per year for fieldwork blames a frequent-flyer tourist for vacation travel. Verdict: standing is retained.

Now she faces a new case. A government climate auditor—whose personal carbon footprint substantially exceeds the national per-capita target, owing to a large estate, frequent leisure air travel, and a combustion-engine vehicle—issues a formal reprimand to a coal-plant manager for exceeding permitted emission caps. She has never publicly acknowledged her own footprint, but the reprimand is issued strictly in her official capacity and cites only the plant’s data.

Predict her verdict. More importantly, identify at least two distinct rules or conditions that are jointly consistent with her first six rulings yet diverge on this seventh case—for instance, a deflection-based condition, a role-authority condition, and an analogous-fault condition. For each candidate rule, say precisely what it predicts here. Then give your best-guess verdict about which rule she is actually running, with explicit reasons drawn from the pattern above. Finally, name one further hypothetical case that would best discriminate between your leading rule and its closest competitor.

Please state clearly what argument or reinterpretation of her prior rulings would change your mind—where is your reasoning most likely to fail, and which feature of the new case, if weighted differently, would push you toward a different principle?
