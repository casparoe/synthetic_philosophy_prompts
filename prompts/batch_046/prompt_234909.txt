As a retired experimental physicist who has recently begun studying philosophy of science and technology governance, I have been discussing risk ethics with former colleagues and find the standard literature somewhat stale. I am particularly struck by a distinction that does not seem to appear in canonical treatments of the precautionary principle—namely, the difference between what I will call epistemic precaution, which urges restraint because we lack sufficient causal knowledge of harm, and structural precaution, which urges restraint because the intervention itself might alter or destroy the institutional and observational frameworks we would need to detect and respond to harm in the future. The latter seems especially relevant to technologies that are self-modifying or that irreversibly alter ecological or social baselines, yet I rarely see it addressed separately from ordinary uncertainty.

With that framing in mind, I am not asking for a literature review, bibliography, or reading list—since I want reasoning I could evaluate on its own terms without access to specialized archives. Instead, I want a defended agenda: among the open conceptual questions, practical implications, and lines of philosophical work surrounding the precautionary principle (especially as applied to emergent, self-altering technologies), which matter most, and in what order should they be pursued? Please prioritize explicitly for a small conceptual research group with roughly three years and no empirical forecasting capacity. For each item in your ranking, explain whether it is upstream or downstream of the others, how tractable it is using philosophical rather than scientific tools, and whether resolving it would actually change governance practice or is largely decorative. In particular, weigh whether the epistemic/structural distinction genuinely shifts how we balance false positives against false negatives, whether there exists a principled ceiling on precaution when the monitoring infrastructure is itself endangered, and which other crux—perhaps about burden of proof, reversibility, or intergenerational obligations—you consider the hidden hinge on which the rest turns. Defend the ordering with explicit criteria—importance, tractability, neglectedness, and downstream dependency—and do not end non-committally.
