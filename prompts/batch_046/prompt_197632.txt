I'm putting together a proposal for a one-semester independent study grant in philosophy of economics—around 5000 words, due in december—and I want to tackle escalation of commitment through the dollar auction, but not the standard take where people just call it irrational. I was arguing with my roommate about budget wars at our internship and it hit me that in real escalation you often feel like you're learning how stubborn the other side is, not just throwing good money after bad.

So here's my half-baked framing: the classic dollar-auction model assumes bidders share common knowledge of rationality and identical payoffs, then shows they escalate past the prize value. But what if you inject private information—each bidder knows their own personal quit-cost but only holds beliefs about the opponent's? Then continuing might be rational Bayesian updating about the other person's type, not a sunk-cost fallacy. The precise question I need stated: in a dollar-auction variant with private quit-costs and belief updating after each round, does the escalation path stay ex-ante irrational, or can it be reconstructed as rational given the information revealed? The proposal needs to say exactly that, then explain why the existant literature doesn't settle it—game-theory treatments usually lock in common knowledge so they can't model learning-about-stubbornness, while psychology papers invoke sunk-cost bias without proving it's necessary once heterogeneous beliefs are allowed—then lay out my approach. I'll do a formal reconstruction with two bidder types and a specific updating rule, trace both the rational-escalation path and the sunk-cost path, and identify the point where they observationally diverge. The deliverable should be the reconstructed model plus a conceptual argument, not empirical work. I also need an honest failure condition: if rational-updating and sunk-cost paths turn out observationally equivalent inside the model, then there's no conceptual distinction to defend and the project collapses. Scope is strictly one semester, so no experiments or data collection, and I'll handle all citations myself—just need the framing and method written up clearly.
