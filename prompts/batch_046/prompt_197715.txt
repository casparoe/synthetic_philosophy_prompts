I am running a 90-minute graduate seminar on constitutional theory with fourteen students, and I want an in-class simulation that makes them feel—not merely discuss—a somewhat under-examined dimension of the counter-majoritarian problem. Rather than focusing only on the dramatic moment when an unelected court strikes down a statute, I want them to experience what we might call anticipatory judicialization: the way the mere presence of a constitutional veto point, whose exact standards remain opaque, transforms legislative deliberation before any ruling occurs, as lawmakers begin drafting not to express democratic will but to survive an imagined judicial scrutiny. Design a structured two-round legislative game set in a fictional jurisdiction, with explicit roles, rules, and a payoff scheme that requires no real money. In round one, eight students form a pure legislature and debate and pass a concrete statute—say, a public-health measure that restricts religious assembly—under simple majority rule with no court present. In round two, roles rotate: some students remain legislators, others form a constitutional court armed with hidden nullification criteria that are revealed only after drafting is complete, and the remaining legislators must produce a revised statute knowing the court exists but not knowing precisely how it will apply its standards. Arrange the mechanics so that the shift in legislative language, the narrowing of policy ambition, and the strategic self-censorship emerge organically from the incentives rather than from facilitator prompting.

Please include a clear timeline, role descriptions, and a debrief that maps specific behavioral changes observed between round one and round two onto the stages of anticipatory judicialization—showing where democratic reasoning was outsourced to anticipated judicial preference. Be frank about which feature of the real-world phenomenon this single-session simulation cannot reproduce—most likely the long-term, generational erosion of democratic legitimacy and institutional culture—and tell me exactly how I must supply that missing piece in the closing discussion. Also warn me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it: I anticipate that legislators in round two may over-correct and draft such an anodyne statute that the court finds nothing to strike, which actually reinforces the lesson but risks confusing participants into thinking judicial review has no bite unless I frame it carefully. Make sure no part of the exercise depends on participants knowing specific case law or theoretical literature in advance.
