I'm currently in a philosophy of science course that touches on legal epistemology, and I've been puzzling over how forensic DNA matching is actually institutionalized. In practice, a crime laboratory selects one reference population database—organized, say, by census ancestry categories—to compute allele frequencies and then reports a random-match probability. If the defense retains an expert who uses a different database with different allele frequencies, the resulting likelihood ratio can differ by orders of magnitude. Courts routinely admit both statistics and treat the conflict as an empirical disagreement over which database is more accurate, rather than recognizing it as a case in which the genetic evidence underdetermines the identity claim relative to the choice of population-genetic model. At the same time, the legal instruction to jurors frames the DNA figure as if it were a single discoverable fact that either meets or fails to meet the "beyond a reasonable doubt" standard.

What theory of scientific evidence and legal proof does this practice presuppose? Must it presuppose a scientific realist view that there is exactly one correct reference class, with the competing databases being merely approximate measurements of it? Or can it be reconstructed on an instrumentalist account that treats the population model as a predictive black box, thereby rendering the Quine-Duhem-style underdetermination invisible? Please distinguish what the practice strictly requires philosophically from what it merely accommodates, and say whether it is consistent with a view that all statistical inference in law is irreducibly model-dependent. Finally, if the practice were rebuilt on an explicit acknowledgment of theory-ladenness, what concrete changes would be required in how judges instruct juries, how experts are qualified, or how "reasonable doubt" is applied to model-sensitive statistics?
