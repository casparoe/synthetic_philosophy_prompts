I am a doctoral student in philosophy of science writing a dissertation on modeling practices in economic history. Over the past eighteen months I have constructed an analytical framework that accounts for roughly 85 percent of the variance across my three primary case studies, yet it depends on an assumption about agent rationality that I already know fails in at least two well-documented historical counterexamples. Fully repairing that assumption would require approximately two more years of archival work, which my funding and visa timeline do not permit. One senior committee member advises me to defend the framework in its current form. She argues that a model should be judged not by global optimality but by how well it is calibrated to its specific explanatory environment, and that treating a researcher's finite time, cognitive load, and budget as irrelevant to methodological judgment is a mistake in its own right. Another committee member insists that scholarly norms are not resource-relative in that way—knowing that your instrument carries systematic errors and using it regardless is not adaptive reasoning but a breach of intellectual responsibility, and that being adequate for present purposes is not a valid justification in normative methodology. I need to decide whether to submit my dissertation now or delay my defense. Is the disagreement primarily about practical constraints, or is it about whether the normative standards governing inquiry themselves ought to be sensitive to the investigator's limited material and cognitive capacities? More precisely, is there a coherent distinction between accepting practical limits in the process of research—such as selecting which archives to consult—and allowing those same limits to justify defending a theoretical result that one recognizes as partially false?
