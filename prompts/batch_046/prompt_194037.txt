An anthropologist I know judges historical societal transitions by outcomes she never fully articulates. Her record: she calls the Near Eastern cereal transition a mistake because it sacrificed leisure and created taxable surplus; she approves the Eastern Woodlands maize adoption because it supplemented foraging without full sedentism or hierarchy; she praises San foraging persistence near pastoralists as preserving autonomy despite lower caloric security; she condemns the Yangtze rice-state complex because storage enabled coercive extraction; and she approves pre-Columbian Amazonian dark-earth root-crop societies that kept dispersed settlement and avoided centralized storage. Now consider an isolated highland group that adopts low-labor tuber cultivation with seasonal mobility and no grain storage, but also forms a voluntary rotating council to manage shared irrigation — with no taxation, permanent elites, or coercion. Predict her verdict on this case, identify the two competing implicit rules that best explain her prior judgments (for example, a labor-leisure criterion versus a surplus-extractability criterion), name the closest analog from her track record, state which rule the new case splits between them, and propose one further hypothetical case that would distinguish the rules. Respond in exactly five numbered items, each one or two sentences, with no introduction or conclusion.
