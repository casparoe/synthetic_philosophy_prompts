As a retired experimental physicist who has only recently begun reading philosophy of mind, I am struck by a regulatory pattern I see in neuroscience grant panels and institutional ethics boards. Proposals that employ the Libet clock paradigm—or modern predictive-decoding variants that identify pre-movement readiness potentials—are routinely approved only if the investigators append disclaimers stating that the work does not negate moral agency and that participants remain fully responsible agents. Yet the stated scientific objective of these same protocols is often precisely to show that the conscious feeling of initiating action is reconstructed after subcortical motor preparation has already begun. What philosophical account of voluntary action and moral responsibility does this institutional framing presuppose? Does it tacitly depend on a libertarian conception of agency that requires a discrete conscious cause preceding movement, so that undermining that cause is seen as threatening responsibility? Or is it compatible with a compatibilist view in which predictive neural antecedents do not diminish agency? Reconstruct the implicit theory, say whether the practice strictly requires one of these views or is philosophically underdetermined, and explain what would have to change in ethics-review criteria if the presupposed framework were replaced by an active-inference model—where conscious intention is genuinely causal but operates as a hierarchical prediction-error signal rather than as a standalone mental trigger preceding action. Please answer in British English.
