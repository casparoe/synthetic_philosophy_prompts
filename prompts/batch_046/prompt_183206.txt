Here is the situation. Suppose there exists a deterministic physical device—call it the Chance-Engine—that computes, for any event E, the objective chance of E according to the best-system analysis applied to the actual world. Because the computation is exhaustive and deterministic, the Engine’s input includes the complete physical and mental state of anyone who consults it. Now imagine I ask the Engine about whether I will take action A tomorrow. If my credence in the Engine’s report is part of my mental state, then the best system for the world in which I defer to the reported value p may differ from the best system for the world in which I do not. Consequently, the Engine might output p = 0.8 if I form the credence that p = 0.8, and p = 0.3 if I form the credence that p = 0.3. The Principal Principle instructs me to align my credence with the objective chance, yet here the objective chance appears to depend on the very credence it is meant to constrain.

My opening challenge is this: does this not generate a vicious circularity? If the Principal Principle requires me to match my belief to the Engine’s output, but the output is fixed only once my belief is fixed, then there seems to be no stable point of deference. Must a Lewisian either abandon the Principal Principle or concede that objective chance is partly constituted by the agent’s rational beliefs about it?
