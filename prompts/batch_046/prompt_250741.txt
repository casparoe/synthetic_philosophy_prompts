My thesis advisor has delivered consistent rulings on autonomy and self-binding across several seminar cases, and I am trying to extract the implicit principle before her lecture on advance directives next week. Her track record: (1) a competent adult's advance refusal of ventilation in future dementia should be honored even when the demented patient appears content and physically resists the absence of tubes; (2) a severely depressed but otherwise rational patient's refusal of curative cancer treatment should be overridden, on the ground that the refusal fails a reflective-endorsement test because the depression is preference-distorting; (3) a healthy person's legally executed Ulysses contract binding their future addicted self to complete rehabilitation should be enforced even when that future self begs to leave, provided the contract was formed with full information and no coercion; (4) an Alzheimer's patient who wrote "no antibiotics for pneumonia" but asks for them during a brief, verified lucid interval should still be denied them, because the interval is too transient to constitute a genuine revision of her standing preferences; (5) when a designated proxy dies and a partially competent present self asks for treatment that her prior written directive refused, the directive should still govern rather than falling back to present autonomy.

The new case for discussion is this: a patient previously authorized not a specific treatment refusal but an adaptive decision protocol—a structured value-elicitation and weighting procedure administered by an independent clinical ethics board—to determine her treatment if she ever became cognitively impaired. The board has applied the protocol, which incorporates her earlier writings, long-form interviews, and a model of her core values, and it recommends refusing a particular life-extending intervention. However, the patient is now in a sustained, multi-day lucid window, independently verified by psychiatric evaluation, and she explicitly, repeatedly requests the intervention, stating that she understands the risks, remembers her prior authorization, and has genuinely changed her view.

Predict my advisor's verdict: will she side with the board and the protocol, or with the present patient's sustained request? Identify whether she is implicitly running a theory of precedent autonomy, reflective endorsement with distortion conditions, diachronic narrative coherence, or a hybrid, and explain which of her prior rulings most strongly support your reading. Then specify the single further case—one that does not involve cognitive impairment at all—that would most cleanly distinguish her actual principle from its nearest rival.
