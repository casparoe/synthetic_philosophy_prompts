Consider the following passage from Thomas Babington Macaulay’s *The History of England from the Accession of James II*, Volume I (1848):

“Yet, unless I greatly deceive myself, the general effect of this chequered narrative will be to excite thankfulness in all religious minds, and hope in the breasts of all patriots. For the history of our country during the last hundred and sixty years is eminently the history of physical, of moral, and of intellectual improvement. Those who compare the age on which their lot has fallen with a golden age which exists only in their imagination may talk of degeneracy and decay: but no man who is correctly informed as to the past will be disposed to take a morose or desponding view of the present.”

Macaulay also concedes, in the same preface, that the settlement securing British liberties gave birth to “a new class of abuses,” and that great national crimes and follies are “far more humiliating than any disaster.” I am trying to understand whether his claim that British history is “eminently the history of . . . improvement” requires a tacit commensurability between the goods achieved—liberty, prosperity, public credit, maritime power—and the evils acknowledged—abuse, dependency, crime, and “alloy”—such that a net balance can justify the progressive narrative. Or does he instead hold a directional, non-aggregative view: that institutions move toward greater liberty and order regardless of whether particular costs and benefits are measurable on a common scale?

Further, Macaulay dismisses talk of “degeneracy and decay” as the fantasy of those who compare their own age to an imaginary “golden age.” Does this imply that writing the past as progress toward the present is, for him, not merely a common historiographical temptation but an epistemic duty of the well-informed historian? And if so, is the Whig interpretation he offers best read as a descriptive causal thesis about British institutional development, or as a normative framework that selects, weights, and sequences events according to their contribution to present conditions—thereby making the present both the endpoint and the criterion of historical judgment?

Please help me parse the logical structure of his position: specifically, whether his progressivism depends on quantitative comparison across eras, on a non-commensurable directional trajectory, or on a normative selection principle; and whether his critique of the “golden age” fallacy undermines only nostalgic pessimism, or also undermines any historiography that does not treat the present as the justified terminus of the narrative.
