I have been trying to work out, on my own, whether the philosopher Daniel Dennett's fourfold division of minds — Darwinian, Skinnerian, Popperian, and Gregorian — describes real kinds or only a useful narrative. By Darwinian I mean creatures shaped entirely by evolutionary selection across generations; by Skinnerian, those that learn within one lifetime through reinforcement and punishment; by Popperian, those that can generate and discard hypotheses internally before acting; and by Gregorian, those that use language and cultural tools to distribute cognition across groups and generations. For as long as I have followed this debate, the same standoff repeats: one side cites bees, rats, and incremental evolutionary change to argue for a smooth continuum of cognitive capacity, while the other insists on discrete jumps in representational flexibility, yet neither camp ever revises its position. I am less interested in picking a side than in understanding why the argument has stalled. Is this a verbal disagreement about the word "mind," or about what counts as a sufficient explanation of behavior? Are the sides answering different questions — one about evolutionary history, one about present cognitive architecture — under a single label? Or is there a hidden formal question, such as whether there exists a measurable threshold of internal representational capacity that cleanly separates Popperian from Gregorian cognition, that both sides implicitly rely on without admitting it? I also want to raise one specific worry of my own: Gregorian creatures may not be a fourth ontological kind at all, but merely Popperian creatures that have so thoroughly outsourced their reasoning to language and artifacts that they only appear categorically different. If that is right, does it dissolve the taxonomy, or does language introduce a genuinely new form of representation that mere outsourcing cannot explain? Given whatever diagnosis you offer, I want to know what kind of disagreement this actually is — substantive, verbal, about hidden criteria for explanation, or about which cases count as data — and what, if anything, would count as progress. Would a formal model of representational thresholds settle it? A specific empirical boundary? A conceptual distinction that reframes the question? Or is the disagreement ill-posed because "kind of mind" was never a natural kind to begin with, and should we expect no resolution at all?
