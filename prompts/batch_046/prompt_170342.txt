I am drafting a short essay on the ethics of renaming streets and removing statues, and I would be grateful for critical comments on the central argument and its framing. Rather than weighing heritage value against retrospective moral condemnation in the usual consequentialist manner, I want to propose that public commemoration operates as a kind of performative contract: when a polity names a square or erects a monument, it does not merely record history but makes a present-tense claim about which values and achievements are worthy of civic endorsement. On this view, the relevant question is not whether the commemorated figure was flawed—few historical actors are not—but whether the continued display of the name or statue asserts a proposition that the polity now takes to be false or unjustified, thereby breaking an implicit contract with citizens who must live under that symbolic claim.

To make this precise, I distinguish between a retrospective moral audit, which asks whether the figure’s actions fall below some ethical threshold, and what I call contractual falsity, which asks whether retaining the honour implies an endorsement that contradicts the polity’s current self-understanding. For example, maintaining a street named after a colonial administrator might survive an audit if he also advanced local public health, yet it becomes contractually false if the polity now explicitly repudiates the colonial project that defined his authority; conversely, retaining a name that honours a scientist whose research was funded by unjust means might not involve falsity at all, provided the honour attaches narrowly to the scientific contribution rather than to the funding arrangement. I then suggest that direct action against monuments should be evaluated not primarily as damage to shared heritage, but as protest against an ongoing false assertion by the state—though I worry this may conflate symbolic harm with contractual breach.

Could you comment on whether this commemorative-contract framing genuinely adds conceptual clarity beyond standard expressive or symbolic-harm accounts? I am particularly concerned that I may be collapsing the state’s silence into active endorsement, and that the distinction between audit and falsity might dissolve under pressure. I also wonder whether the argument is unduly permissive: it might allow societies to retain names of deeply harmful figures provided the honour is narrowly circumscribed, which could still cause tangible harm to affected communities regardless of the logical architecture. Please write your response in British English, and feel free to identify confusions or tensions in the argument rather than offering a reading list.
