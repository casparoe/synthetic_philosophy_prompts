As a retired experimental physicist who, since leaving the laboratory, has taken up moral philosophy with the same insistence on precision I once demanded of error bars, I find myself returning repeatedly to the separateness-of-persons objection against utilitarian aggregation. I am not satisfied with the textbook formulation—that utilitarianism treats individuals as mere receptacles for welfare—because it seems to me that the objection might either collapse or deepen once we distinguish interpersonal aggregation, the summing of welfare across distinct persons at one time, from intertemporal aggregation, the summing of welfare across stages of one life over time. I want you to draft the joint statement that a committed utilitarian aggregationist and a committed deontological separatist might both sign after a sustained adversarial collaboration on this exact point. Treat the standard objection only as background. The document should contain a numbered section of agreed claims—shared commitments about the formal structure of welfare comparison, the descriptive reality of agent-relative reasons, and the logical form of aggregation rules—followed by a disagreements table in which each party’s position is phrased in wording that side would accept for itself. You must identify at least three cruxes, one being whether the separatist’s resistance to interpersonal aggregation implicitly relies on a contested metaphysical thesis about personal identity over time, and for each crux you must specify a concrete philosophical case or argument (not an empirical study) that both sides agree bears on it, with each side’s prediction recorded in advance. Please keep the mapping disciplined: say exactly where the theory is silent rather than forcing it to speak, and separate what is genuinely settled among people who work on normative ethics from what remains actively contested; do not present any contested metaphysical or normative claim as though it were the consensus view.
