I'm a trial attorney with no formal philosophy training, but I'm fluent in the vocabulary of evidence law—hearsay, chain of custody, direct versus circumstantial proof, admissibility versus probative weight, and the distinction between testimony that reports an internal observer-state and testimony that traces a causal link to the event it describes.

Restate epiphenomenalism, together with the paradox of phenomenal judgment, entirely within that evidentiary framework. Specifically: translate the claim that phenomenal qualia are causally inert byproducts of neural processes into a claim about a particular class of "testimony" that has no causal connection to the fact it purports to report; translate the judgment-paradox (that if qualia cause nothing, then judgments about qualia must be caused only by underlying brain states, leaving open the possibility of systematic misrepresentation) into a problem about chain-of-custody and the admissibility of testimony whose only causal ancestry runs through physical processes other than the reported experience. Do this for the core theses—causal inertness, the misrepresentation reply, and the claim that phenomenal concepts are "empty" in a specific metaphysical sense—not as a loose analogy but as a disciplined vocabulary substitution.

Then provide the residue report: for each translated claim, say whether it maps cleanly onto a rule or distinction in evidence law, whether it can only be forced to fit by distorting either the legal concept or the philosophical claim, and whether anything central to the epiphenomenalist position has no counterpart at all in procedural rules about proof and causation. In particular, address whether the legal system's routine acceptance that a witness can sincerely report an internal state even when that state did not cause the report undermines the philosophical paradox, or whether that acceptance merely hides a metaphysical assumption that evidence law never needed to make explicit. Conclude by judging whether the residue reveals a limitation of the legal vocabulary—its inability to register metaphysical causal efficacy—or whether it exposes an ambiguity in the original philosophical debate about what "causing a judgment" requires. Do not cite philosophers by name as authorities; treat the positions as claims to be translated, not as texts to be attributed.
