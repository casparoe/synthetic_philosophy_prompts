As a retired physicist who has only recently turned to philosophy of science in earnest, I have been struck by how little conceptual movement there seems to have occurred since Karl Popper argued that historicism suffers from a poverty—namely, that there can be no genuine laws of history comparable to those in physics, because historical trajectories depend on contingent initial conditions and, crucially, on the growth of human knowledge, which is itself unpredictable. The standard replies invoke evolutionary biology, economics, or complex-systems theory, yet the exchange feels circular: one side demands predictive power analogous to mechanics, the other offers statistical trends and insists that lawhood never required determinism. I suspect the impasse is not really about history at all, but about whether the concept of law can be domain-relative—much as statistical mechanics yields macroscopic regularities without fixing each molecular trajectory, or as evolutionary theory produces directional patterns without an underlying governing equation.
