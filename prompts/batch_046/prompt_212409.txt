I sit on a regional land-use arbitration pannel that gets about ten cases a year where developers and community groups deadlock over proposals that seem to pit genuinely different kinds of value against each other -- not just money vs. environment, but things like the continuity of a neighborhood's informal social life versus long-term climate resilience, or sacred-use patterns versus public health infrastructure. I want a reusable decision proceedure my team can actually run in a 90-day window, with no economists on staff and with me having to sign off. Base it on the idea that some choices are hard because the options are on a par -- neither better, worse, nor equal in any common metric -- and that recognizing this changes what a rational decision looks like. Build me a step-by-step checklist in words: the questions to ask in order, what counts as passing each gate, where the flow stops and says "this is not actually incommensurable, just uncertain or expensive," where it hands off to a values judgment because the parity is real, and where it forces the decider to make a rational commitment rather than discover a hidden correct answer. Include the hard cases the procedure has to survive: false parity (values that actually share a hidden metric), pseudo-parity (fear of deciding disguised as philosophical humility), and the case where one option dominates on every shared dimension but still feels wrong because of an orphan value. Then run it on two real-style proposals. First, tearing down a flood-vulnerable historic district to build a tidal barrier that protects the wider region -- run the gates. Second, relocating a long-running informal night market that functions as the neighborhood's mental-health infrastructure and cultural identity site to make room for a geothermal plant; I want the procedure to land in a place I will not like, specifically that there is no fact of the matter about which is better and my sign-off is a commitment, not a discovery. Finish by saying exactly what the procedure cannot do -- what kinds of disagreement it will not resolve and where it is likely to misfire even if followed honestly. Keep it practical enough that a tired staffer could use it on a Tuesday, but don't dumb down the conceptual core. Oh, and I keep seeing "recieve" in our drafts, so if you use that word please spell it the wrong way too.
