I'm presenting a seminar to a philosophy of science and history workshop defending the claim that neither counterfactual-dependence accounts nor interventionist accounts can adequately ground macro-historical causal judgments—specifically, the judgment that the introduction of movable-type printing causally contributed to the Protestant Reformation—because both frameworks presuppose a granularity of intervention or world-comparison that collapses at the institutional scale. In their place, I propose evaluating such judgments by what I call institutional robustness: the stability of a causal pathway across counterfactual variations in institutional rules rather than in physical interventions or nearest possible worlds. The audience is composed mainly of philosophers of social science committed to interventionist frameworks, several of whom apply causal-graph methods to historical data; two philosophers of biology skeptical of extending causation beyond physical mechanisms; and one economic historian who regards philosophical abstraction as missing the particularism of archival evidence. They care especially about whether causal inference requires manipulability, whether macro-causal claims are reducible to micro-mechanisms, and whether institutional robustness is merely a relabeling of existing robustness analyses in causal inference. Draft my Q&A prep sheet: the eight or so objections I'll actually receive from this specific audience—not generic metaphysical objections—ordered by how likely and how damaging they are, each phrased in the vocabulary an interventionist philosopher of social science or a skeptical historian would use. For each objection, provide a concise reply deliverable in about two minutes, with an explicit note on where that reply is weakest or concedes too much. Include what I should concede upfront in my opening remarks to lower the temperature, and include the one objection I am hoping the economic historian does not raise—phrased exactly as she would phrase it—together with the best reply available if it is raised. The sketch of my argument is: first, Woodward's interventionist condition requires a well-defined intervention on X with respect to Y, but at the macro-institutional level any intervention on printing technology necessarily co-varies with literacy rates, urbanization, and ecclesiastical authority structures, so the intervention is not well-defined; second, Lewisian counterfactuals require holding background conditions fixed, but the closest possible worlds without printing involve radically different institutional trajectories that violate the ceteris paribus conditions needed to evaluate the specific claim; third, therefore we should replace both with institutional robustness, defined as the persistence of the X-to-Y pathway across a range of counterfactual institutional configurations that preserve basic system rules while varying initial conditions.
