I serve on the governance reform panel of the Meridian Research Cooperative, and we must vote by the end of the quarter on a proposal to restructure our collective decision rules. The proposal draws on Spinoza’s political philosophy: instead of simple majority voting, any measure would first be evaluated for whether it serves the collective conatus—the preservation and increase of shared power—of all members; minority factions who dissent on rational rather than passionate grounds would then hold a protected, single-cycle veto. Supporters argue this captures Spinoza’s claim that democracy is the most natural form of government because it best secures freedom of thought while shielding the multitude from destructive superstition; opponents counter that institutionalizing a reason-versus-passion filter collapses democratic equality into an epistemic oligarchy, and that Spinoza’s warnings about the multitude apply precisely to situations where a faction claims exclusive rational authority. Draft the brief opinion I will circulate before the vote: state a concrete recommendation—full adoption, a six-month pilot with automatic sunset, or rejection with an alternative mechanism—and include an explicit time frame; treat the oligarchy objection as the strongest counter-argument rather than dismissing it; and explain whether Spinoza’s framework actually justifies filtering dissent or whether it demands expanding the conditions under which rational agreement can emerge.
