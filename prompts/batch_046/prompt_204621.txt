After a recent period of therapy in which I noticed how often my own post-hoc explanations for emotional reactions turned out to be inaccurate, I have become personally invested in the reliability of introspection. Exam question: Distinguish between introspective access and introspective authority. Explain the transparency thesis associated with Gareth Evans and how it relates to the idea of an inner-observational model of self-knowledge. Then discuss one empirical challenge to introspective reliability—specifically, the confabulation documented by Nisbett and Wilson in their 1977 study—and explain whether that challenge undermines access, authority, or both. Finally, address whether self-deception is compatible with either access or authority, and identify one philosophical position other than Cartesian direct realism that denies the need for an inner-observational model.

Student answer: Introspection involves examining one's own mental life. Philosophers usually separate access—whether we can know our mental states at all—from authority—whether our first-person judgments are especially privileged or incorrigible. Descartes is the classic defender of both: he claimed the mind is fully transparent to itself, and that this transparency operates like an inner eye that directly observes mental contents. In fact, Descartes held the transparency thesis later articulated by Gareth Evans, according to which to determine whether I believe that p I need only consider whether p is true, rather than inspecting an internal representation.

A well-known empirical challenge comes from Nisbett and Wilson's 1977 study, which showed that subjects frequently confabulate explanations for their behavior without awareness of the actual causal influences. This result undermines introspective authority, because if people are systematically wrong about why they acted, their first-person reports cannot be treated as privileged over third-person observation. The transparency thesis, however, avoids this problem entirely: since knowing my belief does not involve observing an inner state, there is nothing for self-deception to distort, and introspection is therefore more reliable than the Cartesian model allows.

Self-deception is thus only a genuine problem for the inner-eye view. If I can deceive myself about being jealous, that shows my direct observation failed. Gilbert Ryle rejected this model, but he did so from a logical-behaviorist standpoint that treats mental-state ascriptions as reports of behavioral dispositions; on this view, there is no separate inner realm to observe, and consequently no distinctive first-person authority over one's own psychology. Therefore, while introspection fails for the hidden causes of behavior documented by Nisbett and Wilson, it remains authoritative for present conscious feelings, and the transparency view confirms that self-knowledge requires no inner observer.
