I've been trying to think about moral luck through Of Mice and Men, specificaly George's choise to shoot Lennie at the end. I want to go past the basic Nagel taxonomy and look at the tension between resultant luck — whether George is blameworthy depends on whether Curley's wife shows up, whether Lennie grabs her, all that contingent stuff — and constitutive luck, since Lennie's disability isn't chosen but it structures the whole moral scene. There's also Bernard Williams's agent-regret: George kills his friend on purpose, but it's the only humane option left. So does he feel the right kind of regret? Does the novel treat that regret as proof he's still decent, or as proof that luck has forced him into something unforgivable?

Can you read the book through that lens? I'm interested in which scenes make George's culpability depend on chance, and where Steinbeck resists being turned into a simple illustration — maybe in how he treats Lennie's own moral agency. Does the novel say something about unavoidable wrongs that the philosophy papers miss? I'm prepping this for a book-club discussion, so I don't need a bibliography, just a reading that takes both the story and the ideas seriously.
