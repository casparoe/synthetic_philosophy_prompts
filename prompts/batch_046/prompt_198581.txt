You are delivering the opening remarks—about six minutes—at an interdisciplinary workshop called Thinking Without Substances: The Cogito and Its Limits, held in a university auditorium with roughly forty people. Half the room are philosophers of mind, epistemologists, and historians of early modern philosophy; the other half are graduate students and invited practitioners from literature, design, and cognitive science who are not specialists in metaphysics. Over the next three days the group will discuss everything from Descartes scholarship to whether large language models can be said to think in any philosophically interesting sense.

Your address must do real structural work with one specific, contested interpretation of the cogito. Take a committed, defended position: argue that Descartes's inference from the fact of thinking to the fact of existence proves, at most, the occurrence of an indubitable present thinking event, and that it does not by itself license the further inference to a persisting, immaterial thinking substance. Do not present this as one option among others. Explain why adopting this framing matters for the workshop—specifically, that it allows discussions of consciousness, self-reference, and artificial cognition to proceed without treating Cartesian dualism as the default framework, and that it sets a boundary on what kinds of claims about machine minds can be made by analogy to human minds.

To make the point land for both halves of the room, build the remarks around the classic pivot sometimes traced to Lichtenberg: if all we are genuinely entitled to is the occurrence of thinking, then we ought to say it thinks—on the model of it thunders—rather than presupposing an I that owns the thought. Explain this pivot clearly for the non-philosophers without diluting its force for the specialists; the philosophers should recognize that you are advancing a specific epistemic, anti-substantialist reading of the Second Meditation, not a generic summary.

The speech should stand entirely on its own reasoning: no bibliography, no assigned readings, no appeals to texts the audience should consult. Keep the language precise but avoid unnecessary jargon. After the speech text, include a brief, clearly separated note—explicitly marked—indicating exactly where your interpretation takes liberties with Descartes's broader argument in the Meditations, where it relies on a contested scholarly reading rather than on Descartes's own explicit text, and whether the Lichtenberg-style pivot functions as a historical claim or primarily as a heuristic device for framing the workshop.
