Consider the composite institutional practice common among major encyclopedic museums holding colonial-era acquisitions such as the Benin Bronzes and the Parthenon Marbles. Rather than denying the violence of acquisition, these institutions defend retention through a layered stance: they assert that legal title was validly transferred under the laws then in force; they claim that physical preservation and broad public access are best served by retaining the objects in climate-controlled, high-traffic facilities; and they offer compensatory measures—high-resolution digital scans, temporary loans, and “capacity-building” partnerships with source communities—while resisting unconditional physical restitution. Crucially, many partnership agreements contain conditions that preserve what might be called the museum’s “interpretive sovereignty”: the receiving institution must agree to display, label, and research the returned objects according to protocols co-designed or approved by the lending museum, so that authority over meaning remains partly external even after physical transfer. The practice thus weaves together legal formalism, a consequentialist-sounding appeal to universal access and preservation, and relational gestures that stop short of full transfer of authority over both object and interpretation.
