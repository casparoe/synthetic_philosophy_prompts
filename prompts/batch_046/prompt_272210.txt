For a philosophy-of-history seminar, construct a decision-tree taxonomy—not a list of named theorists—that classifies positions on what allows a militarily defeated cause to stabilize as an enduring national myth rather than fading into ordinary historical memory. Use four diagnostic cases: the Confederate Lost Cause, the Masada narrative, the Kosovo myth, and the British framing of Dunkirk. Each internal node must pose a concrete claim a respondent could answer without knowing which leaf it produces, such as: whether active state sponsorship is necessary; whether the original cause must be retrospectively judged morally legitimate; whether physical memorialization in territory still held by the inheritors is required; whether the narrative must transform defeat into a precondition for present sovereignty; and whether calendrical ritual repetition is essential to persistence. Build the tree from the logical combinations first, then place actual positions—Lost Cause historiography, Zionist Masada narrative, Serbian Kosovo myth, British Dunkirk exceptionalism, and others—into the occupied cells, leaving empty combinations marked as incoherent, unattractive, or unexplored. Each leaf must name the position (or label it unnamed) and state its most awkward commitment regarding one of the four cases. Note any pair of leaves the tree cannot separate. The audience is upper-level undergraduates; aim for six to eight leaves. Do not provide bibliographies, reading lists, or citations.
