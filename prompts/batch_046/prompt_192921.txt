I've been trying to work through Searle's Chinese Room thought experiment on my own, and I keep running into the same response from people who defend machine understanding: they say the person inside the room is like a single neuron, and just as one neuron doesn't understand English but a brain does, the symbol-manipulator doesn't understand Chinese but the room-system might. Some extend this by saying understanding is like digestion or metabolism -- no single cell digests food, yet the stomach clearly does, so maybe syntax-processing at scale automatically produces semantic understanding the same way biological processes produce function from parts.

I want to audit how far this analogy can actually carry the argument. Can you lay out the correspondence explicitly -- what in the Chinese Room plays the role of neurons, synapses, evolutionary development of the brain, sensory-motor coupling with an environment, and homeostatic regulation? Then show where the mapping fractures: does the room have anything like embodied interaction or feedback loops that don't just pass symbols around, or is it syntactic manipulation all the way up without the causal-historical and biological features that make brain-function more than computation? I also want to know whether the analogy quietly imports the assumption that understanding is automatically an emergent property of large-scale organization, regardless of substrate or causal history, and whether that assumption is doing the real work rather than the comparison itself. Finally, if this analogy collapses under those disanalogies, is there a more honest comparison for what the Chinese Room demonstrates about syntax versus semantics, or does the failure of the neuron-brain mapping leave the original argument untouched?
