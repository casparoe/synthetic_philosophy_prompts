You sit on your university's Safety and Accessibility Standards Committee. The Office of Emergency Management proposes replacing all text-only evacuation signage in three residence halls with a modified ISOTYPE pictogram system—derived from Otto Neurath's universal picture-language project—supplemented only by red negation bars and directional arrows to indicate prohibited actions and exit sequences. Proponents argue this realizes Neurath's ideal of direct, language-independent visual meaning, reducing barriers for non-native speakers and neurodivergent residents; critics on the committee contend that once symbolic modifiers like negation bars and sequencing conventions are added, the system ceases to be a pure picture language and becomes a learned code that replicates the ambiguity and exclusion it claims to solve. Draft the committee's decision memo for Thursday's vote: recommend approval, rejection, or conditional approval with a concrete one-semester timeline and specific evaluation criteria, engaging both the philosophical promise of Neurath's pictorial universalism and the strongest objection that the modifications undermine its own rationale.
