I am working through Blaise Pascal's Pensées (Section III, the fragments on the necessity of the wager) and am trying to understand the internal architecture of the argument rather than its familiar decision-theoretic summary. The passage is difficult because Pascal shifts abruptly between epistemic, rhetorical, and practical registers. Here is the relevant portion, taken from the standard English rendering:

"Let us then examine this point, and say “God is, or he is not.” But to which side shall we incline? Reason can determine nothing about it. There is an infinite gulf fixed between us. A game is playing at the extremity of this infinite distance in which heads or tails may turn up. What will you wager? There is no reason for backing either one or the other, you cannot reasonably argue in favour of either.

Do not then accuse of error those who have already chosen, for you know nothing about it.—No, but I blame them for having made, not this choice, but a choice, for again both the man who calls ‘heads’ and his adversary are equally to blame, they are both in the wrong; the true course is not to wager at all.—

Yes, but you must wager; this depends not on your will, you are embarked in the affair. Which will you choose? Let us see. Since you must choose, let us see which least interests you. You have two things to lose, truth and good, and two things to stake, your reason and your will, your knowledge and your happiness; and your nature has two things to avoid, error and misery. Since you must needs choose, your reason is no more wounded in choosing one than the other."

My specific difficulty concerns the coherence between the opening epistemic claim and the closing practical prescription. Pascal begins by asserting that reason can determine nothing—"Reason can determine nothing about it"—and that both choices wound reason equally. He then introduces the interlocutor's objection that "the true course is not to wager at all," only to reply, "Yes, but you must wager; this depends not on your will, you are embarked in the affair." Immediately afterward he frames the choice in terms of what "least interests" us, noting that "your nature has two things to avoid, error and misery," and that we stake "your reason and your will, your knowledge and your happiness."

What I do not understand is this: if reason truly determines nothing, and if the choice is initially presented as one in which neither side can be defended, on what grounds does the argument then invoke "error" and "misery" as costs that our nature must avoid? Is Pascal smuggling in a hidden premise—that belief-formation is subject to a principle of minimal risk with respect to false belief—or is the appeal to "nature" meant to do independent motivational work that the infinite-gain calculation alone cannot supply? More specifically, how should I read the relationship between the claim that we are "embarked" (a claim about unavoidable practical commitment) and the claim that we must "weigh the gain and the loss" (a claim about rational calculation)? Do these two moments support each other, or does the urgency of the wager depend on a psychological pressure that the argument's explicit reasoning does not fully justify?

Please help me understand this tension as it operates within the passage itself, not by comparing Pascal to modern decision theory, but by unpacking how these sentences are meant to hold together and whether the move from epistemic neutrality to practical commitment is a single continuous argument or a conjunction of distinct appeals.
