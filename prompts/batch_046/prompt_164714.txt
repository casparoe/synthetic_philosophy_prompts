I'm preparing a graduate seminar on Austin and Searle's speech-act theory, and I find that the usual classroom examples—marriage ceremonies, ship christenings, promises—fail to capture the anxiety that arises when a speaker attempts to perform an institutional act without the backing of a recognized convention. To push the discussion into less familiar territory, I would like a philosophical reading of Macbeth, focusing on the prophecy scenes in the first act and on Macbeth's private deliberations immediately before he murders Duncan. The question is whether the play dramatizes a specific kind of performative failure: one in which the utterance is not merely an empty misfire, but an active catalyst that forces its own conditions into existence.

Treat the Weird Sisters' announcement that Macbeth will become king, and Macbeth's subsequent reflection that fortune might crown him without his direct action, through the lens of Austin's conditions for a successful performative—preparatory conditions, essential conditions, and sincerity conditions. Ask whether the witches' speech fails as a conventional misfire because they possess no institutional authority to confer titles, or whether it succeeds in a more disturbing mode by inducing Macbeth to supply the missing preparatory conditions himself. Then examine Macbeth's own attempts at self-authorization—his address to the dagger, his resolution to kill—asking whether these function as private performatives that lack the public recognition Austin requires, and whether the play suggests that such unrecognized utterances collapse into pure perlocutionary effect: not changing the world through convention, but poisoning the speaker. The reading should take both the play and the philosophical framework seriously, ending with a claim about what Shakespeare's supernatural logic reveals that standard analytic discussions of speech acts overlook—perhaps about the possibility of performative force being manufactured through repetition, fear, and belief rather than through established social convention.
