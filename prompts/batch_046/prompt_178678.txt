I serve on a municipal heritage and remembrance board that meets monthly to decide, in a single forty-five-minute session with the city attorney present, whether a local traumatic event should receive a public monument, be preserved in the municipal archive without public marker, or be deliberately left without official commemoration. I have been trying to make sense of the philosopher Avishai Margalit's distinction between thick and thin relations in the ethics of memory—his claim that wrongs within close communities of care and shared identity generate stronger obligations to remember—together with the writer David Rieff's defense of strategic forgetting, which holds that societies are sometimes healthier when traumatic memories are not frozen in permanent public form. Please turn these two positions into an operational decision procedure that my board could actually run on a proposal in real time. The deliverable should be an ordered sequence of questions, with for each: the specific information the board must gather, what counts as a sufficient answer, the precise point where the answer is definitively no public monument, and an explicit flag marking where the issue is one of values rather than verifiable fact and must go to a human vote rather than being settled by the procedure itself. Apply the procedure to two proposals now before us: Case A, a 1973 textile-mill fire that killed twelve workers, where survivors' descendants still live in the neighborhood and the firm was locally owned; and Case B, a 1962 forced relocation of a waterfront neighborhood for a state-planned highway expansion, where most displaced families have since left the region and the decision was made by state rather than municipal authorities. Present the full procedure first, then the two worked applications—including one where the procedure yields an answer the board will likely find uncomfortable—followed by a brief structured summary table using exactly these columns: Step Number, Core Question, Sufficient-Answer Standard, Case A Verdict, Case B Verdict, and Human-Judgment Flag (Yes/No). Do not provide bibliographies, citations, or reading lists; the audience is board members and the city attorney, not scholars.
