I teach a grad seminar on distributive justice — about 15 people, one 90-minute session — and I'm tired of students treating Rawls's veil of ignorance as a cute summary exercise they can parrot back. I want them to actually feel why the difference principle pulls in a different direction than simple maximin, but in a situation where rational risk-taking seems to push the other way. So design a runnable in-class game or structured deliberation, not a lecture, with rules, roles, and small real stakes.

Here's the niche twist I want baked in: instead of full ignorance, use a "thin veil" where each participant draws a card that reveals their own risk-aversion profile (high, moderate, or risk-seeking) but they stay completely blind to their social position and to their "talent bundle" (high productivity, medium, or low). The group must vote on one of three rules for distributing a pool of points that can be exchanged at the end for actual small rewards — coffee vouchers, or just public prestige in the class: (1) strict difference principle / maximin on welfare points; (2) a prioritarian weighted rule that helps the worst off but permits some inequality; (3) an egalitarian split with a bonus for risk-takers. Arrange the payoff structure so that ex post, after the reveal, the worst-off group turns out to be the high-talent risk-seekers who advocated for rule 3 and lost, while the risk-averse players who pushed for rule 1 land in the middle, and then have them negotiate a one-time "constitutional amendment" with full knowledge. I want the debrief to map the draw, the vote, the reveal, and the amendment debate onto specific parts of Rawls's formal argument — especially the role of the maximin rule under uncertainty — and be frank about which feature of the original position, probably the complete absence of bargaining power or the condition of moral equality, this setup acutally can't reproduce and the facilitator has to supply verbally.

Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it — I bet people will try to signal their risk card during deliberation — and how to fix it with a simple rule change. Don't just lay out the three options neutrally; take a committed stance on which rule the activity should be engineered to make feel most rational behind the veil, build that bias into the payoff scheme and the debrief questions, and defend that choice with reasons. I want them leaving the room seeing that the difference principle isn't just "be nice to the worst off" but a specific response to a specific kind of ignorance — and that once you thin the veil, the intuition starts to wobble in predictable ways.
