I'm drafting a novel about a society that tries to engineer a shift from shame-culture to guilt-culture after a civil conflict, and I want the philosophical backbone to feel lived-in rather than wikipedia-deep. So here's my question: in philosophy and anthro circles, people have been fighting for ages over whether guilt or shame is the "better" moral emotion, and whether a culture can actually choose to swap one for the other. The interventionists—those who think you can—say guilt lets people repair privately without losing face; the traditionalists say shame is what keeps communal bonds visible and therefore intact. Both sides point to the same post-war reconstructions in Japan and Germany, and both recieve the same cross-cultural studies, yet in my reading group nobody has moved an inch in two years. Is this a real substantive fight about emotions, or is it verbal—just different meanings of "moral progress"? Or are they answering two different questions, one about individual psychology and one about social coordination, and calling both "guilt versus shame"? I want an honest diagnosis with reasons, not a survey of positions, and tell me whether any new distinction or field study could actually break the stalemate, or if it's just bedrock intuition about human nature that neither side can argue for.
