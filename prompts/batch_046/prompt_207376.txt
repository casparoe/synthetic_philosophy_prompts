At a graduate workshop on biography and moral education, two participants, Maya and Leo, argued about whether Plutarch’s Parallel Lives should be read as a form of hagiography. Maya began by saying that Plutarch pairs a Greek and a Roman—Alexander and Caesar, for instance—not to offer two saints for imitation, but to create a comparative mirror: the reader judges how the same virtue or vice manifests across different political cultures, so the moral work is critical comparison rather than direct emulation. Leo replied that this understates Plutarch’s exemplarity: he selects and arranges anecdotes precisely so each figure embodies a particular moral quality, and the reader is meant to imitate the admirable and avoid the blameworthy, exactly as in saints’ lives; the pairing merely doubles the stock of exemplars. Maya answered that hagiography depends on transcendence—the saint is unreachable except by grace—whereas Plutarch deliberately includes ordinary failures and contradictions, making his subjects imitable in a way saints are not. Leo countered that hagiographers also show saints battling ordinary temptations, and that Plutarch’s Alexander is hardly an ordinary man; if anything, the boundary between hagiography and biography is blurrier than Maya allows. Maya then proposed what she called an asymmetry of proximity: in hagiography the reader is meant to feel the moral gap is unbridgeable except by divine aid, while in Plutarch the gap is meant to be closed by direct emulation. Leo objected that if the gap closes entirely, moral learning collapses into self-congratulation—closer to modern celebrity biography—so Plutarch must preserve some unreachability after all, which brings him back toward hagiography. They turned to me for a verdict.
