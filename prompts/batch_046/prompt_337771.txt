Analyze the March 2016 AlphaGo–Lee Sedol match in Seoul—specifically the second game played on 10 March and the move that live commentators initially interpreted as a strategic blunder, subsequently recognized as a decisive, creatively disruptive play—through the lens of what might be called the Darwin-Turing double inversion: the decoupling of high-level competence from the comprehension traditionally assumed to generate it. First, map the episode onto the two inversions separately. Where in AlphaGo's architecture and match execution do we see a Darwinian process—selection among vast populations of candidate moves by a fitness function (win probability) without strategic foresight or intentional design—producing board positions that human masters regard as elegantly purposeful? And where do we see a Turing-style inversion—symbolic computation through neural networks and Monte Carlo tree search—executing reasoning-like sequences without anything resembling semantic understanding of the board's strategic meaning? Second, focus on the moment of expert misrecognition: commentators read the move as an error precisely because it violated the comprehensible patterns of human master play. Use that misrecognition to test the inversion thesis. Does the gap between AlphaGo's competent performance and immediate human interpretability confirm that comprehension is unnecessary for strategic excellence? Or does the fact that human comprehension was eventually required to validate the move's brilliance restore understanding to a privileged, if retrospective, position? Third, consider what the episode implies for the boundary conditions of the inversion. If a system can generate moves that are not merely competent but creatively disruptive—expanding the space of what counts as good play in ways that human comprehension can only recognize after the fact—does this show that comprehension itself is just one more evolutionary byproduct, a pattern-matching module that lags behind the selection mechanisms it tries to interpret? Or does it reveal a hard limit: that true creativity still requires the kind of integrated understanding that both Darwinian selection and Turing computation necessarily lack?
