The issue has become personally relevant because I have recently begun doctoral research in a history department that imposes two formal requirements which seem to pull in opposite directions. Every thesis must contain a rigorous source-critical appendix in which every archival document is catalogued with provenance metadata, reliability ratings, and a record of how it was physically accessed; examiners treat this as a core competence, grading it strictly. At the same time, every thesis must include an explicit theoretical framework chapter in which the student adopts an interpretive stance—whether postcolonial, Marxist, microhistorical, or otherwise—and the work is evaluated partly by the coherence with which its narrative advances that framing. Recently the department added a further rule: researchers must now disclose how digital finding aids and search algorithms influenced which collections were discovered and consulted, treating algorithmic mediation as an ethical and methodological factor that must be documented.

This puzzles me. The source-critical apparatus treats the archival trace as authoritative and demands direct fidelity to it, in a manner that recalls Ranke’s ambition to describe events as they actually occurred, with the historian’s own subjectivity minimised. Yet the theoretical-framework requirement and the grading for interpretive coherence seem to presuppose the scepticism of Carr and Hayden White about bare facts, treating narrative form and ideological framing as constitutive of history rather than decorative. The new disclosure rule about algorithmic access adds another layer: if the pathway to the document is technologically mediated and therefore partly constructed, does that undermine the claim to direct contact with the past, or does it simply extend the same scepticism into digital practice?

What philosophical commitments about historical objectivity does this combined institutional practice actually presuppose? Reconstruct the implicit theory, or theories, embedded in these requirements. Say whether the practice is genuinely inconsistent, or whether it can be read as licensing a coherent middle position—perhaps one in which objectivity is procedural or methodological rather than metaphysical. Distinguish carefully what the practice strictly requires from what it merely fits well with. Finally, tell me what would have to change in doctoral assessment if the department genuinely adopted either a pure Rankean view of documentary fidelity or a fully constructivist view in which all archival access is mediated interpretation.
