Rank the following three reconstructed positions on the moral status of animals—(A) strict present-capacity anti-speciesism, which weights moral consideration solely by currently exercisable sentience and cognitive capacities; (B) telic speciesism, which grounds permissible differential treatment in the species-typical form of flourishing proper to an organism's kind rather than in cognitive capacity scores; and (C) an ecological-relational hybrid, which ties moral weight to an organism's functional role within sustaining interspecies dependency networks—by their internal coherence when applied jointly to two hard cases: a severely disabled human infant with no prospect of cognitive development, and a cognitively enhanced great ape embedded in sustained social bonds.

Give the ranking from 1 (most coherent) to 3 (least coherent). For each position, provide a single sentence explaining the structural feature that fixes its rank. Then, in a separate section, distinguish: (i) what in this comparison is broadly settled in philosophical animal ethics; (ii) what turns on genuinely contested interpretations of 'speciesism', 'flourishing', or moral status; and (iii) whether your ranking depends on treating ecological function as morally relevant rather than merely instrumentally valuable. Do not present any reconstructed or minority framing—such as treating telic speciesism as a mainstream doctrine—as if it represented the consensus.
