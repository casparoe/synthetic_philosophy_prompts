I am preparing a graduate seminar on contingency and agency in political thought, and in our recent session on Machiavelli we focused on his distinction between fortuna—the unpredictable, often destructive force of circumstance—and virtù, understood not as moral excellence but as the adaptive, sometimes ruthless capacity to shape events. A student raised what strikes me as a genuinely puzzling institutional question. In modern constitutional democracies, the architecture of emergency power is remarkably consistent: ordinary governance is structured by procedural constraints—judicial review, legislative oversight, transparency mandates—yet nearly every constitution contains explicit mechanisms that suspend those constraints during declared crises, often coupled with sunset clauses that require periodic legislative renewal not of the crisis itself but of the exceptional authority. More strikingly, the retrospective evaluation of leaders who govern under these provisions tends to be outcome-based—did they stabilize the situation?—rather than process-based—did they respect the suspended rules?

What philosophical commitments about the nature of contingency and human agency does this institutional design presuppose? Reconstruct the implicit theory: does the very existence of these provisions commit us to a Machiavellian metaphysics in which fortuna is an external, overwhelming force that can only be met by an exceptional virtù incompatible with ordinary procedural virtue? Or can the design be justified on purely instrumental, liberal-utilitarian grounds without importing Machiavelli’s view that fortune must be actively dominated? I want you to distinguish carefully between what the practice strictly requires us to believe about the relationship between unpredictable events and political capacity, and what merely fits comfortably with it—such as the idea that crisis reveals genuine leadership. Is the practice philosophically consistent with the same legal system’s ordinary insistence on the rule of law and procedural justice, or does it quietly presuppose that those values are themselves contingent on the absence of fortuna? And if we rejected Machiavelli’s framework—if we held, for instance, that contingency is always internal to institutional design and that exceptional capacity is largely a myth—what specific changes to constitutional architecture would be required?
