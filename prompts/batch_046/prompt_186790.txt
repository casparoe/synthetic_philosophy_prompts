Question: Zygmunt Bauman contends that the Holocaust was not an atavistic rupture but a product of modern bureaucratic rationality and the moral distancing it produces. Explain how this "modernity thesis" bears on the Historikerstreit's dispute over uniqueness versus comparability, especially with respect to comparing the Holocaust to other state-organized mass violence.

Student answer: Bauman argues that modern bureaucracy fragments responsibility and neutralizes moral judgment through procedural rationality, which he treats as a generic feature of modern organization rather than a specifically German pathology. Because these mechanisms are structurally available to any industrial state, Bauman's thesis implicitly aligns with the functionalist camp in the Historikerstreit—associated with historians such as Mommsen—who held that the genocide emerged cumulatively from bureaucratic dynamics rather than from a premeditated ideological master plan. From this it follows that insisting on absolute uniqueness is misleading: if the Holocaust was essentially an extreme application of modern rational instruments, then comparing it analytically to Stalinist terror or other bureaucratic mass killings is not only permissible but required. At the same time, Bauman maintains that the specific fusion of bureaucratic efficiency with racial ideology lends the Holocaust a singular moral gravity, so that comparability in form does not entail moral equivalence.
