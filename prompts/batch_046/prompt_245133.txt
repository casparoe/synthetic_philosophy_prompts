I have recently returned to my hometown to care for my father, who suffered a severe stroke and now lies on sustained life support, unable to communicate. The hospital’s ethics committee has designated me as his sole health-care proxy and asked me to apply substituted judgment: I am to reconstruct what he, understood as an isolated individual with private inner preferences, would have wanted, and then sign the authorization form alone in my own name. Where I come from, however, a person is not an independent substance that carries identity inside like a possession, but is constituted through enduring relationships—my father was never merely himself apart from his roles as elder, spouse, and community member, and I am not a detached authority but part of the very network that formed him. Signing as a solitary decision-maker therefore feels conceptually false, as though I were pretending to possess a self-contained autonomy over a life that was always shared. My sister, who lives elsewhere and reasons in more conventional terms, insists that my reluctance is emotional avoidance and that relationships do not dissolve the practical fact that someone must choose. I want to understand whether my discomfort reflects a coherent philosophical challenge to the individualist framework embedded in medical consent law, or whether it is indeed a failure to accept an unavoidable burden. How should someone who understands personhood as fundamentally relational navigate a legal and clinical system that demands autonomous, solitary authorization?
