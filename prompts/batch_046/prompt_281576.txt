I have just spent a rather exhausting evening arguing with a close friend about whether psychiatric classifications genuinely alter the people they describe, or whether they merely label conditions that exist independently of diagnosis. I am trying to draft a short essay for a philosophy of psychiatry seminar, and I should value your comments on whether the argument is accurate, genuinely interesting, or merely a tired rehash of well-known positions.

The essay centres on Ian Hacking's idea of looping effects, but I want to challenge the usual symmetrical presentation. Rather than treating all classifications as subject to the same amplificatory feedback, I propose that looping is often mediated by what I call 'diagnostic shadowing': when a socially valorised category—such as adult-diagnosed autism spectrum conditions—expands, it can absorb individuals who might previously have received a stigmatised label—such as schizoid personality disorder. The valorised category then experiences an amplificatory loop, because identification is actively sought; the stigmatised category experiences a suppressive loop, not because the underlying phenomena vanish, but because clinicians and patients avoid the label, thereby starving it of the interactions that sustain a loop. I illustrate this with the historical decline of hysteria and the concurrent rise of dissociative and somatoform categories, without claiming a direct causal substitution.

My specific claims are: (1) Hacking's framework should distinguish between amplificatory and suppressive loops; (2) diagnostic shadowing explains why some categories disappear without leaving a clear epidemiological trace; and (3) the DSM-5's shift toward dimensional personality disorder traits weakens suppressive loops by removing sharp categorical boundaries, yet may inadvertently strengthen shadowing by allowing individuals to identify with less stigmatised dimensions. I am particularly uncertain about claim (3). Could you comment on whether the argument holds together, whether 'diagnostic shadowing' is a useful conceptual addition or merely unnecessary jargon, and whether there are obvious objections—from Hacking's later writings or from empirical psychiatry—that I have overlooked?
