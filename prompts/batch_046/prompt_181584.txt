I am a retired molecular biologist who, since leaving the laboratory, has begun attending philosophy seminars at my local university; having spent decades observing how cooperative behaviour evolves in structured microbial populations, I find myself drawn to films that stage cooperation and betrayal as strategic games. I should like to read Christopher Nolan's The Dark Knight—particularly the ferry sequence, in which two groups are simultaneously given the means to destroy one another, and the broader narrative arc in which Batman and the Joker appear locked in an iterated exchange—through the philosophical lens of the prisoner's dilemma, in both its one-shot and iterated forms, and through the evolutionary literature on how cooperation emerges.

I am not seeking a bibliography, but rather a conceptual analysis: does the ferry scene, structurally a one-shot interaction with no credible shadow of the future, correctly dramatise the conditions under which rational defection should prevail, or does it implicitly rely on an iterated logic—perhaps through the passengers' anticipation of social sanction—to explain why the expected explosion does not occur? Furthermore, I want to explore whether the Batman-Joker dyad is best interpreted as a direct-reciprocity game, sustained by Batman's conditional forbearance, or whether it illustrates what evolutionary game theorists term network reciprocity, where cooperation survives not through pairwise tit-for-tat but through local clustering that insulates cooperators from exploitation by defectors. Is the Joker functioning as a pure defector whose presence paradoxically stabilises cooperation elsewhere in Gotham, or does his refusal to maximise his own payoff—he seems to value chaos over any concrete gain—break the strategic framework altogether?

I am preparing a short discussion for fellow scientists who have recently turned to philosophy, and I want a reading that treats both the film's narrative architecture and the evolutionary dynamics seriously, without reducing characters to mere strategy profiles. Where does this lens illuminate a scene that a purely cinematic interpretation obscures, and where does the film resist being mapped onto cooperation dynamics—particularly with regard to the Joker's apparent indifference to payoff maximisation?
