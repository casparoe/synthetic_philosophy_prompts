What would an adequate theory of associative political obligation have to satisfy if its purpose is to account for bindingness in plural societies—specifically, to explain why individuals remain subject to practices they never chose and often cannot fully exit, when those practices distribute goods that are only intelligible as goods from within the practice? Provide the full specification: the phenomena it must explain (e.g., obligations arising from inherited institutional roles, normative pressure under conditions of partial compliance without explicit consent, and the structural asymmetry between the costs of exit and the costs of voice within a shared scheme); the distinctions it must not collapse (such as that between being phenomenologically embedded in a practice and rationally endorsing its values, or between membership goods that are constitutively tied to participation and public goods that merely happen to be distributed through membership); and the conceptual constraints it must respect (for example, it may not reduce embeddedness to a sequence of tacit contracts without erasing the non-voluntary character of belonging, nor may it treat every inherited social role as coercive without losing the normative difference between legitimate tradition and domination). For each requirement, identify the motivating case or counterexample that makes it non-negotiable, and indicate which are genuinely open to relaxation. Then specify which single desideratum does the most damage to both standard liberal contractarian and standard communitarian accounts of social belonging, and briefly assess—without citing obscure literature—whether any familiar framework comes close to meeting it. Present the answer as a structured list with reasoning attached; the criteria themselves are the deliverable.
