My prof works on what she calls Braudel time — the idea that geograpy and cliamte shape history more than kings and battles do. Her past verdicts: she said the fall of Rome was mostly ecological exhaustion and long-distance trade collapse, not barbarian invasions; she said the Black Death spread followed climate-driven trade routes, not specific merchant choices; she said the Mongol conquests were mostly steppe ecology and horse logistics, not just Temujin's leadership; she said the Industrial Revolution in Britain was mostly coal geography and prior capital accumulation, not a few genius inventors; but she also said the French Revolution of 1789 was mostly an event-driven crisis — harvest failure plus political deadlock — and not some deep structural inevitability. So she's not a pure determinist.

Now she's looking at the sudden standardization of weights and measures across the Song dynasty around the 1020s, right after a reformist chancellor pushed a new tax-and-trade package. Some historians say it stuck because the Yangtze delta's long-term river integration made unified measures inevitable; others say it was pure top-down event politics and collapsed once he was fired. What's her verdict gonna be? I'm less interested in the actual Song history and more in: what rule is she actually running across those five cases, what are the other rules that fit her precedents just as neatly, and which past case is the closest analog? If her track record genuinely underdetermines this one, what principle is she implicitly picking between with each option?
