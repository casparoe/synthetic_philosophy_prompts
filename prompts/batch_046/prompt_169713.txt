I am writing a literary novel in which a statesman-protagonist must repeatedly violate his own ethical code for what he takes to be the public good, and I need a rigorous conceptual map for the philosophy seminar taught by one of my characters. I want a taxonomy—specifically a decision tree with clearly labeled branch nodes, named leaves, and flagged dead branches—of positions on the problem of dirty hands in politics, but structured around three concrete diagnostic questions that split the logical space rather than around familiar textbook labels. First: when a political actor commits a genuine moral wrong for a greater good, does he incur an irreducible moral residue, or is the act fully cleansed by its political necessity? Second, if residue exists, is it borne by the individual conscience, by the institutional office he occupies, or by the collective polity that authorized or benefited from the act? Third, must that residue be made visible through public acknowledgment and symbolic punishment, or can it be legitimately metabolized in private without democratic transparency? Each leaf should carry a position name—or “unnamed / unexplored” if the combination is unoccupied—and one sentence on its most awkward commitment: for example, a stance that denies all residue must account for why the agent’s persistent guilt is not merely irrational affect, while a stance that transfers residue to the collective must explain why individual conscience is not merely outsourced to an abstraction. The tree must include at least the following positions: an absolute prohibitionist stance that denies dirty hands can ever be justified regardless of consequence; a Machiavellian stance that accepts the wrongful act but denies any moral stain attaches; a classic acknowledgment-and-guilt stance that insists on both justification and enduring personal culpability made publicly visible; an institutional-transfer stance in which the office absorbs the stain and the person is cleansed; and at least one genuinely unoccupied combination that you should flag as either incoherent or merely unexplored, with a brief diagnosis of why nobody occupies it. Phrase every branching node as a plain-language question that a seminar student could answer without already knowing which philosophical label it points toward, and explicitly flag any pair of leaves that your tree cannot genuinely separate. The deliverable should be the tree itself, with its branch logic laid out clearly, so that it functions as an instrument that sorts people by what they actually believe about guilt, office, and transparency in political wrongdoing rather than by which name they prefer.
