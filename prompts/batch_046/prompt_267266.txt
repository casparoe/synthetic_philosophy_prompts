Design a 90-minute workshop activity for 14 graduate students in philosophy and art history. The aim is to make participants feel, through structured action rather than lecture, why the value of an original artwork seems tied to causal history even when perceptual experience is identical, and precisely where that intuition fractures. Do not name the philosophical framing—such as the distinction between autographic and allographic arts, or the problem of causal origin versus authorial touch—until the debrief.

Set up a three-round trading game using only paper cards, hidden provenance codes, and a point-based payoff scheme (no real money). Assign roles: four Collectors, four Authenticators, and six Dealers holding portfolios. Each card depicts an artwork with a hidden back-side code indicating its true status. Round 1: Collectors purchase works using only visual descriptions; they score 10 points for an "authentic original," 5 for an authorized reproduction, 0 for a forgery, and lose 3 points for every certificate they buy. Round 2: Authenticators may issue provenance certificates for a fee, but they are free to lie; meanwhile, two hidden "forgeries" are actually perfect studio copies made by the artist's own hand as practice pieces, and two hidden "originals" are legally authorized posthumous casts produced from the artist's original molds—causally derived objects the artist never touched. Round 3: Collectors may trade and must finalize holdings. Only after scoring is revealed do participants learn the codes.

The rules should ensure that perceptual inspection always fails, that documentary testimony is corruptible, and that the authorized-cast twist forces a direct confrontation with whether "original" means causal origin, authorial contact, or institutional authorization. The debrief must map Round 1 onto the failure of experiential access; Round 2 onto the unreliability of testimony; the cast revelation onto the ambiguity of causal history; and the studio-copy forgeries onto cases where provenance and perceptual quality diverge unexpectedly. State explicitly what the activity cannot reproduce—namely, the long-term cultural authority of museums that stabilizes an object's status over decades—and describe what will predictably go wrong the first time (Authenticators colluding with Dealers, or participants abandoning point incentives to debate "realness" mid-game) and how to correct it. Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citations. Keep the full design under 450 words.
