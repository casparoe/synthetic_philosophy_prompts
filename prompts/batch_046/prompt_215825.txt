I'm a retired experimental physicist who only started reading philosophy seriously about three years ago, after I left the lab. Next Thursday I'm presenting at our philosophy-of-psychology seminar a view I call the 'calibration model' of virtue—basically I think virtues aren't global traits like old physics constants but domain-specific instruments that stay accurate only through continuous social feedback loops, a bit like calibrating a sensor against a reference signal. The audience is a mixed bag: two social psychologists who are definatly convinced by the situationist literature (they'll cite the classic seminary study and Milgram-style obedience work occassionally), two virtue ethicists who think situationism misses the normative point entirely, and one grad student obsessed with whether people act for the right reasons. I want them to take calibration seriously, but I know they'll accuse me of redefining virtue to avoid falsification.

My argument sketch: situationist experiments test people in artificial one-shot contexts—like declaring a thermometer broken because it reads wrong in a hurricane. Real virtues are calibrated across varied contexts over years, and the 'right reasons' part isn't a separate mental cause but part of the calibration protocol itself. Still, I worry they'll say I'm smuggling global traits back in through 'feedback loops' or that I've made virtue ethics unfalsifiable.

Can you draft my Q&A prep sheet? Order the objections by how likely and damaging they'll be, phrase them the way each camp actually talks—especially the psychologists and the grad student—not generic philosophy objections. For each one: lead with the bottom line (fatal, fixable, or miss?), then a two-minute reply, then a brief note on where that reply is weakest. Include the objection I'm secretly hoping nobody raises—probably that my model makes virtue ethics unfalsifiable. Keep the whole thing under 800 words. And please don't include any reading lists or citations I can't check on the fly; I won't have web access during the talk.
