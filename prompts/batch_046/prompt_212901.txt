Medical "I'm sorry" laws in a bunch of U.S. states let doctors express remorse to a patient after a bad outcome without that statement being admissable in malpractice court. The idea is that patients want to hear regret, but doctors stay silent because they fear litigation, so the law tries to decouple expressing remorse from admitting legal fault. Here's what puzzles me: if Strawson is right that reactive attitudes like resentment and indignation are the basic machinery of holding people responsible, then this practice seems to want the emotional payoff of a reactive exchange—patient hears remorse, doctor offers it—while stripping away the forensic bite. What view of moral responsibility does that presuppose? Does it treat reactive attitudes as essentially theraputic rather than accountability-tracking? Or does it smuggle in a distinction between moral blame and legal liability that Strawson's naturalist framework doesn't clearly license?

Go further and reconstruct the implicit theory. Is the practice only coherent if we think reactive attitudes can be satisfied without any institutional follow-thru? Could a Strawsonian who thinks resentment is constitutive of holding responsible still endorse these laws, maybe by saying the legal system handles the accountability part and the apology handles the interpersonal part? Or does the practice actually presuppose something closer to a control-based view of blame—where expressing regret is just emotional hygiene, not an acknowledgment of being the proper target of resentment?

Also notice that the same legal system that protects apologies in medicine doesn't protect them in criminal law—if you apologize to a victim, that can be used against you. So is the implicit theory domain-specific? Tell me whether the practice strictly needs one particular view, or whether it's philosophically underdetermined, and what would have to change if we replaced the presupposed view with a hard-line Strawsonian one that says you can't have the reactive attitude without the institutional bite.
