I've been thinking on my own about moral luck — the idea that people can end up being judged good or bad because of things they never actually controlled. Most examples I come across involve car crashes or political assassinations, which feel distant and extreme. I want a tighter, more ordinary case that isolates exactly where the line falls. Please construct a thought experiment that meets the following specifications. Design a one-paragraph, realistic workplace scenario — set in a municipal records office or a food-safety lab, not a hospital or a vehicle — in which two coworkers make the identical decision using identical reasoning and identical intentions, yet only one is morally blameworthy because of an outcome determined entirely by a factor outside either person's control. The outside factor must be a misfiled administrative document that both handled in the same way, but the blame must hinge specifically on which coworker held formal signing authority at the time, not on any difference in character, effort, or foresight. After presenting the scenario, demonstrate that it isolates resultant moral luck rather than constitutive moral luck by stating exactly which element is the luck and which elements are held constant across both workers. Then show the verdict for each of two positions: first, a moral-luck theorist who accepts that outcomes can affect blame; second, an opponent who holds that moral judgment should depend only on what is within the agent's control. For each, explain the reasoning that leads to their conclusion. Next, identify the single structural feature that makes this a case of moral luck rather than ordinary causal misfortune, and explain what happens to the evaluation if that feature is removed. Finally, explain how the opponent — the one who rejects outcome-based blame — would try to attack the case, for instance by arguing that the signing-authority distinction is itself a choice-based factor, and say whether that attack succeeds or fails given your setup. Keep the scenario grounded in normal human payoffs, avoid any science-fictional devices, perfect predictors, or exotic physics, and do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citations.
