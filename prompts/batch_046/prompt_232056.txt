my study group came up with an argument that organizational closure can't be what makes something alive, because if you apply it consistently it eats everything. (1) for an entity to be alive its internal processes have to be the main thing maintianing its own boundary intact, not just external assembly. (2) viruses fail this -- host ribosomes build them, they don't metabolize -- so they're not alive. (3) prions fail worse, they're just misfolded templates. (4) but now look at a mammal: every cell needs constant import of O2 and glucose, and without that influx the boundary collapses in minutes. (5) someone will object that breathing and digestion are internal processes directing the import, but that's a sleight of hand: directing traffic across a boundary isn't the same as the boundary maintianing itself. (6) worse, mitochondria are exogenous symbiants with their own DNA, and gut flora do a huge chunk of digestion and immune signaling; so the 'organism' is already a loose federation of outsiders holding the line together. (7) so either closure allows partial outsourcing and viruses sneak back in, or it demands total internal maintiance and no multicellular thing qualifies. (8) since both horns are awful, closure isn't the right criteron.

which step should i reject? my friend really wants to keep (1) and (6) -- she thinks the microbiome point is rock solid. if she hangs on to both, what's the least costly place to cut, and what does she have to swallow? also is there a hidden slide between (4) and (5) about what 'internal' actually means?
