As a journalist covering science and philosophy, I am defending the following thesis at an interdisciplinary ethics symposium next week: the canonical procreation asymmetry—that we have strong reason not to create suffering beings but no corresponding reason to create happy ones—should be rejected not because it violates formal symmetry, but because it conflates two distinct moral relations I call welfare-implication and welfare-activation. I hold that suffering generates a determinately addressable moral patient, whereas a merely possible happy being is not addressable at all, and that this addressability gap explains the asymmetry without invoking any metaphysics of absence. To pressure-test the framework, I apply it to a niche scenario I term staged synthetic potentiality—entities whose consciousness and welfare capacities can be activated in discrete, separable stages—arguing that once addressability is staged, the binary asymmetry collapses into a graded obligation structure. The audience is split: normative ethicists, several of them committed defenders of the asymmetry who know the happy-slave analogies and decision-theoretic objections inside out, and science-studies scholars who tend to treat reproductive ethics through evolutionary and institutional frames and are skeptical of conceptual distinctions that lack empirical traction. Give me the objections in roughly the order they will come, phrased as those two camps would phrase them, with what I should concede, what I should hold, and the weakest point in my own position—especially the tension between my addressability framework and evolutionary accounts of reproductive altruism—that I should prepare to defend rather than hope they miss.
