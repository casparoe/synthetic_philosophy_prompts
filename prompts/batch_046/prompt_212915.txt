As a journalist covering science and ideas, I am preparing a feature on how classic literature anticipates modern debates about accountability and human nature. I recently revisited H.G. Wells’s The Invisible Man, and I am struck by how precisely Griffin’s experiment in self-erasure maps onto an ancient thought experiment that philosophers still treat as live. In Plato’s Republic, Glaucon recounts the myth of Gyges, a shepherd who discovers a ring that renders him invisible; freed from the eyes of others, he seduces the queen, kills the king, and seizes power. The challenge is usually read as asking whether anyone would remain just if punishment and reputation were removed—in other words, whether morality is merely instrumental. Wells’s Griffin, however, does not merely commit discrete crimes while hidden; he lives invisibly for weeks, and the condition gradually strips away his capacity for cooperation, sympathy, and even practical reasoning until he becomes something like a tyrant by necessity. I want to read the novel seriously as a response to Glaucon: does Wells dramatize the idea that invisibility does not just license injustice but actively dissolves the social self that makes justice possible? Or does Griffin’s violence ultimately confirm Glaucon’s cynical view that, once the ring is on, only power remains?

If you take this reading, which scenes should I point my readers to—the first village attack, the confrontation with Kemp, the final hunt across the snow—and where does the novel resist being reduced to a philosophical illustration? I am particularly interested in whether Wells gets something right about moral psychology that the Republic’s framing misses: perhaps that being seen is not merely a deterrent but a constitutive condition of moral agency, so that the ring of Gyges, worn long enough, does not reveal a hidden true self but manufactures a monstrous one. At the same time, I want to be honest about where the analogy breaks: Griffin is physically altered, suffering from cold and hunger, and his invisibility is technological rather than magical, which may shift the moral logic away from free choice and toward a kind of bodily determinism. For my feature, I need a reading that treats both the novel and Glaucon’s challenge with equal rigor—one that asks what Wells adds to the philosophical debate that argument alone cannot, and that admits where the text refuses to answer the question neatly. How would you structure such a reading, and what should I say to readers who will come expecting a simple confirmation that absolute power corrupts absolutely?
