I am teaching an advanced philosophy of mind seminar of about thirteen graduate students in one ninety-minute session, and I want a structured role-play that makes them feel—before they name it—the conceptual pressure behind a particular version of the self-deception problem: not whether one can hold false beliefs about oneself, but whether practical reasoning can sustain contradictory commitments through a functional compartmentalization that serves to stabilize a social or institutional identity rather than to deceive anyone in the ordinary sense. I want the rules and roles arranged so that participants discover this dynamic themselves: each student receives a private conviction about a shared project (for example, that the project's central premise is flawed) but is assigned a public role—such as institutional advocate, silent auditor, or project steward—that imposes small but genuine social costs if they acknowledge the contradiction or withdraw cooperation. The activity should unfold in timed phases—private assessment, public defense, structured challenge, and collective reflection—with payoffs measured in group credit or procedural authority rather than money, so that students experience whether their private judgment is genuinely altered, actively suppressed, or merely bracketed without disappearance. Please design the full protocol: the exact role descriptions and constraints, the timeline, how the facilitator introduces the stakes without naming the philosophical concept, a debrief that maps specific moments in the role-play onto the claim about partitioned belief and practical compartmentalization, an honest assessment of which feature of long-term self-deception (for instance, the absence of any conscious access to the contradiction, or its persistence over years) this ninety-minute simulation cannot reproduce and that I must supply in discussion, and a frank prediction of what will predictably fail the first time—likely that students collapse the roles into straightforward hypocrisy rather than experiencing genuine compartmentalization—together with how to correct for it.
