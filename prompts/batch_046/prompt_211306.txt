I've been thinking through the absent-minded driver problem—the case where a driver on a highway can't recall whether he's already passed the exit he needs, and must decide at each intersection whether to continue straight or turn off, knowing that the best overall result requires reaching a distant exit but that turning off too early yields only a modest payoff. I find myself convinced that the correct resolution demands what I would call plan-binding rationality: the driver should commit ex ante to continuing straight, and then at each intersection he should honor that commitment even though, from his local perspective right then, turning off looks like the rational move. This strikes me as revealing something deep about agency—namely, that rational choice is not always about optimizing moment-to-moment, but about maintaining the authority of a broader plan over a sequence of decision points made under imperfect recall.

What I want to know is what would genuinely count against this view—not just the familiar game-theoretic objections, but which specific formal results, which analogies to real-world decision-making, and which intuitions about identity and agency over time would undermine it. Would the position collapse if we could show that agents in similar conditions of imperfect recall actually achieve better outcomes with local, adaptive reasoning? Would it be damaged if we accepted that the self at each intersection is, in some relevant sense, a different decision-maker with no obligation to a past self's plan? Or does the view risk being insulated—qualified so that any failure can be blamed on imperfect execution rather than on the principle itself—and if so, is that insulation a virtue or a vice for a theory of rational agency?

Please take a committed position: assess whether this plan-binding view is genuinely exposed or well-defended, rank the potential defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, say what a defender of the view should honestly concede, and tell me whether the risk of unfalsifiability is actually present here, and whether it matters for a claim about how rational agents ought to behave.
