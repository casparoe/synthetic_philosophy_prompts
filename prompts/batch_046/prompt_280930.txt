You serve as a senior ethics reviewer for an administrative-oversight board that examines case files of harm produced by large-scale bureaucratic and algorithmic decision systems—welfare-eligibility algorithms, visa-screening protocols, healthcare-triage rules—where no individual expresses personal hatred, yet people suffer severe, documented damage. You review eight to twelve files monthly; each review must be completed within forty-five minutes using only the case record (protocol manuals, decision logs, internal correspondence, anonymized testimony), and you must sign a memo recommending either (i) structural reform addressing thoughtless role-adherence, (ii) individual disciplinary action for malicious or grossly negligent conduct, or (iii) no further action because the harm arose from unavoidable error or legitimate discretion. You have no clinical-psychology training and may not order psychological evaluations.

Ground your work in Hannah Arendt’s analysis of the banality of evil—specifically, her claim that catastrophic moral failure can emerge not from monstrous intent but from thoughtlessness, cliché-ridden justification, unreflective adherence to bureaucratic role, failure of imagination regarding particular victims, and the suspension of the internal dialogue that Arendt distinguishes from mere cognitive problem-solving. Translate this into a reusable, ordered decision procedure—presented as a flowchart in words with explicit graded checkpoints—that your office can apply consistently without philosophical training.

Your procedure must contain:

1. A preliminary filter that distinguishes harm produced by role-bound administrative or algorithmic action from harm produced by private violence, market exchange, or purely technical malfunction.
2. Six or fewer ordered tests applied to the documentary record, each specifying what evidence counts in favor, what counts against, and whether judgment is unavoidable; flag every node where judgment cannot be eliminated.
3. A clear operational distinction between Arendtian thoughtlessness and three confounding categories: (a) ordinary incompetence or ignorance of facts, (b) cynical instrumental calculation pursued knowingly, and (c) ideologically driven malice or cruelty; include the specific documentary markers for each.
4. An operational criterion for detecting the suspension of “thinking” in Arendt’s sense—the internal two-in-one dialogue—versus the presence of ordinary cognitive or technical competence; explain how a reviewer recognizes this in bureaucratic language, stock phrases, protocol citations, and responses to objections.
5. A decision rule classifying the case as “banal evil / structural thoughtlessness,” “individual moral failure,” or “non-culpable error,” together with explicit conditions under which the rule misfires and must be overridden by reviewer judgment.
6. Worked applications to three cases:
   - Case A: A mid-level administrator who processed thousands of automated risk scores using standardized language, never questioned the training data, dismissed an internal memo about false-positive rates with a stock phrase (“the protocol is the protocol”), and expressed no awareness of the particular families affected.
   - Case B: A supervisor who deliberately altered an algorithm’s weighting to exclude members of a specific group, documented the intent in private messages, and expressed satisfaction at the outcomes.
   - Case C: A hard case in which the procedure correctly classifies the harm as arising from banal thoughtlessness—an administrator who designed a medical-rationing protocol in purely clinical, depersonalized terms, followed every procedural review step, showed no animus, and sincerely believed the protocol was equitable—yet the documented death toll and the reviewer’s moral intuition strongly resist the “banal” label and demand a harsher classification. Show exactly where the procedure yields its answer and where the reviewer must either accept that result or invoke an explicit, justified override.
7. An explicit statement of at least three things the procedure cannot do—such as determining legal culpability, making psychological diagnoses, or predicting future risk—and why these lie outside its scope.
8. A brief failure-mode analysis identifying one situation in which the procedure is likely to misclassify cynical role-playing as thoughtlessness, and one in which it is likely to misclassify genuine ignorance as banal evil, with a warning flag for each.

Write in formal, precise prose. Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citations. Do not depend on precise publication dates or page numbers. Avoid verbatim quotations from Arendt’s texts; paraphrase her concepts without quotation marks. Ensure the deliverable is a standalone, reusable procedure that a non-philosopher can run in under an hour and defend in writing.
