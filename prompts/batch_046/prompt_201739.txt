Medical institutions routinely honor advance directives in which a competent patient refuses future life-sustaining treatment, even after severe dementia has erased the psychological traits that motivated the refusal and the patient now appears content, reaches for food, or shows no awareness of the prior document. At the same time, these institutions typically permit revocation only through explicit verbal or documented repudiation at the relevant time—not through a surrogate’s judgment that the patient’s current welfare seems improved. What theory of the self and its authority over time does this asymmetry presuppose? Reconstruct the implicit view: does it treat the earlier competent self as retaining sovereign authority over a later numerically identical but psychologically discontinuous person; does it presuppose that narrative identity or life-plans define welfare regardless of present experience; or is it compatible with a purely contractual or property-right interpretation that needs no thick metaphysics of identity? Distinguish what the practice strictly requires from what merely fits it well, and describe what a clinical policy would have to look like if it rejected that presupposed view and instead granted authority to the present-moment preferences or to the currently experienced welfare of the patient.
