I've been arguing with a colleague about whether the classic paradox of deontology—the problem that agent-centered constraints seem to license, or even require, allowing more rights-violations to occur than a minimizing strategy would prevent—can be dissolved without collapsing deontology into consequentialism or into an implausible form of threshold morality. I have come to defend what I think of as a structural-insulation view: the prohibition on violating rights is not one practical reason among others that must be weighed against aggregate outcomes, but a boundary-condition on practical reasoning itself, so that there is no agent-neutral duty to reduce the total number of violations committed by others, even when one could do so without violating anyone's rights oneself. On this account, the world ending up with more rights-violations is a tragic feature of moral pluralism and distributed agency, not an internal contradiction within the theory; my only duty is to maintain the integrity of my own action-structure, and attempts to embed this within a collective optimization framework misunderstand the logic of deontological constraint. I find the standard responses—appeals to moral residue, to two-level theories, or to a derivative duty to prevent violations when costless—unconvincing, but I want to know whether my position is actually well-defended or merely well-insulated.

What would genuinely count against this structural-insulation position? I want a full map of its exposure, sorted by how decisive each defeater would be. In particular: are there formal results in collective-action theory or social-choice logic showing that insulating individual action-structures from aggregate outcomes is unstable or generates perverse equilibria? Which concrete institutional or emergency-coordination cases—perhaps involving shared responsibility for structural harm, or cases where inaction is causally equivalent to action—would make the view collapse into either quietism or disguised consequentialism? Which intuitions about cooperation, solidarity, or shared moral responsibility would defenders of insulation have to concede as genuinely damaging rather than merely awkward? I am especially concerned with unfalsifiability: if any catastrophic outcome in terms of rights-violations is compatible with the view provided I personally committed none, has the position been qualified into emptiness, and is that a fatal flaw for a normative theory of this kind? Please distinguish the fatal objections from the merely embarrassing ones, say which ones committed defenders of insulation would themselves accept as counting against them, and give an honest assessment of whether this view risks anything real or has been rendered immune to evidence by its own architecture.
