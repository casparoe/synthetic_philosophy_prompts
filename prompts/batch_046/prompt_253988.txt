Question: Dennett argues that the varieties of free will worth wanting are compatibilist—basically elbow room within determinism rather than libertarian metaphysics. A newer framework called modal fragility claims freedom doesn't come from mental maneuvering but from genuinely unstable branching nodes in an agent's decision tree—points where more than one outcome is ontologically open, not just epistemically hidden. Explain Dennett's view, then evaluate whether modal fragility actually improves on it when we think about moral responsibility.

Student answer:
Dennett's view is that free will worth wanting is just the capacity to act on your own reasons without being coerced. He gives us elbow room inside determinism: you can deliberate, change your mind, and respond to arguments, and thats enough. He still thinks you could have done otherwise in the relevant counterfactual sense—if your mental state had been slightly different, you'd have acted differently—so he preserves the principle of alternate possibilities within a deterministic world.

Modal fragility rejects that mental version of alternate possibilities. It says real freedom requires actual open branches in the world, not just hidden determinism. The decision tree has nodes that are ontologically fragile, meaning physics doesn't fix which way they go. This isn't libertarian agent-causation, just physical openness. But this makes freedom pretty rare, since most nodes are probably stable.

Comparing them, Dennett's elbow room is more practical because it applies to everyday choices. Modal fragility was actually first proposed by Harry Frankfurt in his hierarchical model of second-order desires—he used unstable branching to describe how higher-order volitions stabilize choice. So modal fragility is basically Frankfurt's idea with some physics sprinkled in. That means Dennett's compatibilist framework is still the better account of moral responsibility, since it doesn't require rare physical conditions.
