I'm preparing material for a graduate seminar on self-reference and formal limits, and I'm trying to work out a somewhat unorthodox reading of Hofstadter's MU-puzzle that I think gets overlooked in standard treatments. The conventional account holds that solving the puzzle requires a JOOTS—jumping out of the system—to observe from a meta-level that no derivable string can contain zero I's, and therefore that MU is unattainable. But I want you to construct an argument for the contrary position: namely, that the MIU system already internally encodes, through the interaction of its syntactic rules rather than through any explicit meta-rule, the structural impossibility of eliminating all I's. Specifically, defend the claim that the architecture of the four rules—particularly the way Rule II doubles the I-count, Rule III reduces it by exactly three, and Rules I and IV only manipulate U—functionally constitutes a distributed invariant-preserving mechanism that makes the non-divisibility-by-three property derivationally transparent from within the system itself, without requiring an external observer to perform a meta-level induction.

In developing this position, please address the standard objection that identifying such an invariant is merely a redescription of the derivation mechanics rather than a genuine internalization of meta-reasoning. You should also consider whether this reading, if defensible, undermines Hofstadter's broader claim that formal systems are necessarily blind to properties of their own derivability and therefore must always be transcended from outside. I am not asking for a survey of the literature or for citations to specific commentators; I want a direct philosophical argument that treats the MIU rules as carrying implicit meta-structural information about their own derivational closure, and I want you to be explicit about where that argument is strongest and where it is most vulnerable.
