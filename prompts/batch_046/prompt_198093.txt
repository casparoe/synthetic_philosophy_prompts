I've got a 75-minute seminar with about 18 second-years next Tuesday — they've read some Rawls and a little Nozick, but never really stopped to ask what "freedom" is supposed to be. I want to teach negative, positive, and republican liberty, but I really don't want it to turn into the usual "Berlin said this, Pettit corrected that" routine they can sleep through. I'm thinking of using a running example of an algorithmically managed delivery driver: the app never actually stops them from working, but it can change pay, routes, or termination rules with zero notice and zero appeal. Compare that to a worker in a democratic cooperative with heavy collective obligations and formal veto rights over policy — lots of interference, but a different kind of vulnerability. The idea is that the first case is free in the negative sense and maybe even in the positive sense if they "choose" the shifts, yet there's still a kind of subjection; the second is constrained all over but not subject to arbitrary power.

Can you plan the explanatory arc, not the lecture script? Specifically: what dependency has to be in place before republican liberty even makes sense, and why I shouldn't open with Berlin's distinction? What's the carrying example — why this algorithm/coop pairing rather than the tired slave-with-kind-master story — and what does it need to have and lack? Then the misconception map: I'm guessing they'll conflate positive liberty with welfare-state handouts, and think republican liberty is just negative liberty plus democracy or "non-interfernce" (yes, I misspelled that in my notes). Where in the sequence do I head each off? Also, what's the check at the end that shows they've grasped non-domination as a modal status — being vulnerable to arbitrary will — rather than just a feeling of security or having a vote? And what should I explicitly leave out — maybe the full civic-humanist genealogy, or Pettit's formal conditions? — and just say so, no bibliography please, they never read them anyway.
