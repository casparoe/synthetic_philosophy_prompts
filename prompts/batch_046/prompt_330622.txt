This is the paradox of deontology, and for the jurist's voice I need a rigorous evaluation—not a bibliography, but your direct judgment—of five competing conceptual strategies for dissolving or accommodating the tension.

Please rank the following five strategies from the most to the least philosophically defensible, assigning each a letter tier—S, A, B, C, or D—and providing a single sentence that identifies the decisive strength or weakness behind its placement. The strategies are: (1) the standard threshold view, according to which deontological constraints hold categorically until harm crosses a predefined catastrophic boundary, at which point they yield to consequentialist calculation; (2) the institutional division of labor, which preserves individual prohibitions intact but assigns systemic harm to collective institutions rather than to personal moral exception; (3) the contractualist pairwise dissolution, which argues that the paradox arises only from illicitly aggregating agent-relative reasons across populations, since contractualist justification operates bilaterally and never generates collective-scale obligations; (4) the proximity-weighted scaling model, which treats the normative force of a constraint as continuously diminishing with causal and spatial distance, thereby permitting graded responses without categorical abandonment; and (5) the recursive suspension clause, a less conventional proposal holding that if universal compliance with a constraint would defeat the very value that originally justifies it, the constraint recursively suspends itself for every agent, but only on the condition that each agent can non-deceptively verify the universal failure state.

Structure your response in four titled sections, in this order, with approximate lengths as noted: Ranked Tier List (about 220 words), in which you present the five strategies in tier order with grade and justification; The Obscure Outlier (about 160 words), identifying which strategy is least represented in standard philosophical literature and arguing whether its neglect stems from a hidden merit or from a fatal defect that mainstream accounts rightly avoid; The Novelist's Translation (about 160 words), translating the logic of your top-ranked strategy into a concrete dramatic scene—two characters, a specific decision, and one line of dialogue—without using technical philosophical terminology; and Concluding Verdict (about 100 words), stating whether ranking these approaches genuinely resolves the paradox or merely reveals an irreducible tension that a work of fiction ought to preserve rather than dissolve.
