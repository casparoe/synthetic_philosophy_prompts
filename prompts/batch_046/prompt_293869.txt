im the logistics lead for a small disaster-response NGO and every few months we get a call where our one rescue team can only reach one site but there are two or three active ones. ive been trying to make sense of that old Taurek paper about whether numbers should count -- the idea that if you cant save everyone, saving five isnt automatically better than saving one just because five is bigger. I want to turn that into an actual decision procedre my team can run in 30 minutes with no philosophy background, something we tape to the wall of the ops tent.

Write me a step-by-step checklist: the questions in order, what counts as a sufficient answer, where it stops with "draw lots" or "send the team" versus where it hands the call to a human because its a values question not a fact question. The principle has to stay intact -- no hidden aggregation -- but it also needs to handle the messy parts: what if one site has 20 people and another has 21? what if the bigger group caused the crisis? what if saving the smaller group now frees the team up to save others later? Give me three worked cases: (1) two collapsed buildings, 3 vs 7 people, same risk to rescuers; (2) a site with 12 people that takes 8 hours and blocks reaching a second site with 2 people that becomes reachable after 4 hours; (3) a case where the procedure spits out an answer i wont like -- maybe saving the lone survivor keeps a family intact but the checklist says treat them equal to any other single person.

Be honest about which steps are just judgment calls wearing a uniform, and say clearly what the procedure cant do -- like it doesnt solve why we should rescue anyone, and it definitely shouldnt ask for a bibliography or citation list since half our team doesnt have web access in the field. Keep it practical, not academic.
