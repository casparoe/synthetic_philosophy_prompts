You chair a university curriculum board that reviews proposals for mandatory first-year "critical foundations" programmes. A recent wave asks students to treat basic certainties — that perception is generally reliable, that other minds exist, that arithmetic is consistent — as open hypotheses rather than working assumptions. You need a reusable review procedure, grounded in hinge epistemology (Wittgenstein's On Certainty), that board members without philosophical training can apply in a thirty-minute session. The procedure must distinguish legitimate critical inquiry from "hinge disruption" — undermining the conditions that make rational assessment possible — and must mark clearly where judgment is unavoidable and where values rather than facts decide the outcome.

In framing the procedure, introduce and apply a distinction between two kinds of hinge propositions: "pivot hinges" (propositions that, if systematically doubted, collapse the framework of inquiry itself) and "scaffold hinges" (propositions that support specific domains but can be locally suspended without framework collapse). Specify the ordered questions the board should ask, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, the point at which the procedure stops with a rejection, and the point at which it hands the decision to a person. Include a small set of test conditions, flag the cases the procedure cannot settle, and be explicit about its misfire risks.

Then apply it to three proposals: (a) a course assessing students on how well they defend solipsism against the claim that other minds exist; (b) a course teaching statistical reasoning by treating arithmetic consistency as a contingent empirical claim within specific contexts only; (c) a course using historical case studies to show how scientific communities revise what they once treated as unquestionable. Include at least one case where the procedure delivers a verdict the proposer will strongly dislike, and state clearly what the procedure is not able to do.
