I cover science and philosophy for a general-interest publication, and I recently moderated a symposium on non-human causation in historical explanation. Two panelists—Dr. Elena Vasquez, an environmental historian, and Dr. Marcus Chen, a philosopher of history—became locked in a disagreement I could not resolve, and I am asking you to adjudicate it as I write up the exchange.

Vasquez argued that certain non-human forces should be recognized as possessing what she terms "interventionist agency." Her central case was the 1815 Tambora eruption: she claimed it was not merely a background condition for the subsequent "Year Without a Summer," but an autonomous causal vector that restructured agricultural prices, migration flows, and state fiscal policies across the Northern Hemisphere in configurations that no human institution intended or could have generated internally. Crucially, she insisted this avoids environmental determinism because the eruption did not dictate specific outcomes; it expanded and altered the decision-space within which human societies then exercised retained autonomy.

Chen responded with what he called the "full mediation thesis." He granted Tambora's causal relevance but denied it any form of agency, arguing that every outcome Vasquez attributed to the volcano was in fact produced by human institutional responses—grain-storage protocols, trade policies, relief distributions—and that attributing agency to the eruption commits "causal displacement," obscuring the human choices that actually determined who suffered and who survived. He added that the real danger of treating climate or pathogens as actors is moral evasion: distributing blame onto a volcano makes it easier to excuse failures like the refusal to suspend corn laws.

Vasquez then proposed a "functional autonomy criterion" to defend her position without invoking intentionality. If an entity's causal profile is path-independent of the human structures it interacts with, and if substituting any human action for that entity fails to reproduce the outcome space, then treating it as an actor is analytically productive rather than metaphysically reckless. She pointed to variola major in the Americas: its demographic effects followed trajectories that no combination of indigenous or colonial institutional choices could replicate or prevent, which suggests the pathogen introduced a genuinely autonomous causal vector.

Chen countered that Vasquez's criterion collapses into ordinary counterfactual causation dressed in agential language, adding mystification without explanatory gain. He further argued that even if one grants path-independent effects, framing them as "agency" distracts from accountability—particularly for climate-related catastrophes, where the severity of outcomes depends on carbon-intensive economic structures designed by identifiable human actors.

Please adjudicate their exchange move by move: assess the strength of Vasquez's interventionist-agency claim about Tambora, Chen's full-mediation thesis, the functional autonomy criterion, and Chen's charge of moral evasion. State who is more right overall, specify exactly where each party is correct and where each overreaches, and identify the conceptual gap or synthesis—perhaps a distinction they both overlook—that neither Vasquez nor Chen has offered. Do not provide a bibliography or reading list; I need a verdict on the arguments as actually made.
