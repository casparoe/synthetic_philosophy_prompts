Two philosophers at a colloquium, Mara and Jonas, got into a disagreement about Daniel Dennett’s claim that the self is a center of narrative gravity—an abstract point around which autobiographical stories organize, rather than a substantive entity inside the brain. They applied the idea to a hypothetical engineered system with two independent narrative generators housed in a single physical substrate. Each generator produces a coherent first-person autobiography with no cross-referencing to the other, though both share sensory input and a single external interface capable of speaking in either voice.

Mara argued that this is a clear case of two centers of narrative gravity within one body. Since Dennett denies that the self is a biological object, and since each generator maintains its own continuous, self-referential story with distinct values and remembered episodes, there is no principled reason to collapse them into one self. The biological container, she said, is just a shared platform, analogous to two novels written on the same computer.

Jonas replied that Dennett’s framework is not purely internal; the center of gravity is constituted partly by social practices of attribution. Because the system has one body, one face, and one social history—others address it as a single interlocutor—the external narrative practices still converge on one center. The internal split, he said, is analogous to dissociative states within one human life, not to two separate lives. To call them two selves, he insisted, you would need two distinct social trajectories, not merely two uncommunicating internal monologues.

Mara countered that this makes the self depend on social recognition rather than on internal narrative coherence, which seems to reverse Dennett’s emphasis. She proposed a variation: suppose the system’s interface is designed so that each generator is presented to the outside world as a separate persona with its own name and social network, even though they share hardware. In that case, Jonas’s social criterion would yield two selves, but Mara’s internal-criterion would also yield two—yet if the social networks were then merged back together, Jonas’s view would suddenly collapse two selves into one without any internal change. That instability, she claimed, shows that social attribution cannot be the grounding factor.

Jonas conceded the thought experiment was sharp, but said it actually supports his view: the self is a pragmatic, multi-factor construct in which internal coherence and social attribution usually align. When they come apart, we face a genuine ambiguity that Dennett’s framework predicts rather than resolves. He added that Mara’s own view has a parallel problem—if internal coherence alone is sufficient, then any well-structured fiction with a first-person narrator counts as having a self, which stretches the concept beyond recognition.

Mara responded that this is a feature, not a bug: Dennett’s view is meant to show that selfhood is a gradient, not a binary property. The engineered system sits in the middle of that gradient. But she insisted that Jonas was smuggling in a hidden essentialism about the biological organism by treating shared hardware as a tie-breaker.

They left the dispute unresolved. Can you adjudicate it point by point—who is right about each specific claim, and what, if anything, both parties are missing? In particular, address whether Dennett’s framework genuinely privileges internal narrative coherence over social attribution, or whether the two are inseparable in his account; whether Mara’s gradient response avoids Jonas’s objection about fictional narrators; and whether the synthetic case reveals an ambiguity in the theory or merely applies it consistently. Please also say what would change your mind—what specific argument or consideration would lead you to a different verdict, and where your reasoning here is most likely to fail.
