I am preparing a seminar contribution for a graduate course on nineteenth-century liberalism and its afterlives, and I keep returning to a gap in the standard treatments of Mill's harm principle. Most classroom discussions stay within familiar territory—direct physical injury, fraud, defamation, or the canonical analogy of falsely shouting fire in a crowded theater—but I want to test whether Mill's framework can be stretched, without breaking, to cover harms that are structurally different from these paradigms.

Specifically, imagine that Mill were alive today and asked to evaluate two contemporary phenomena: first, content governance in federated, protocol-based social networks, where moderation is distributed across independent nodes rather than imposed by a single corporate authority; and second, the circulation of synthetic media—AI-generated audio or video that is not libelous in any conventional legal sense but is engineered to erode, over months or years, a community's shared epistemic baseline. I am calling this second harm slow-burn epistemic degradation: it is cumulative, largely invisible at the moment of utterance, and produces damage not through a single inciting event but through a persistent, low-intensity corrosion of the conditions for rational deliberation. Would Mill treat such degradation as a harm sufficient to justify interference, and if so, where would he locate the legitimate authority to act—the state, the architects of the underlying protocol, or the individual nodes? More importantly, would he classify it as analogous to direct incitement, or as a distinct category of social harm that demands a different kind of response? Please construct a reasoned, historically grounded prediction of his view, taking care to distinguish architectural or distributed harms from the immediate, individualized injuries he explicitly discusses, and without appealing to secondary literature I could not access offline.
