My philosophy of mind professor's rulings in our seminar on other minds have been consistent across the following cases: she grants other minds to healthy adult humans, human newborns, chimpanzees, octopuses, crows that solve multi-step puzzles and cache tools, and domestic dogs; she denies them to fish, honeybees (even though the hive collectively solves navigation problems that exceed any individual bee's capacity), plants, a laptop running a chess engine, a decentralized weather-prediction network of simple sensors with no central processor, and she affirms them for a fully locked-in human patient who communicates only through a brain-computer interface decoding intended eye movements. Next week's case concerns a synthetic neuromorphic chip engineered to replicate the connectivity and oscillatory dynamics of a human cortical column: it exhibits spontaneous gamma-band synchronization, integrates information across its nodes in a manner isomorphic to conscious cortical processing, and shows internal coherence metrics comparable to an awake human brain region, yet it is disembodied, learns nothing from interaction, adapts to nothing, and produces no observable behavior except electrical patterns detectable only by direct measurement. Predict her verdict on whether this chip has an other mind, identify the implicit rule that best organizes her prior judgments—behavioral-adaptive flexibility, centralized biological neural architecture, or substrate-independent functional integration—acknowledge the rival rules that accommodate her track record equally well, and specify the single further hypothetical case that would most sharply distinguish between these competing interpretations.
