I've been working on a longform piece for my science-and-ideas column about why the experience-machine debate still feels alive even though it's been running since Nozicks Anarchy, State, and Utopia came out in the seventies. I was chatting with a philosopher friend over coffee and she pointed out that every argument is already on the table: the hedonists say plugging in is rational if the mental states are identical, the authenticity crowd says well-being needs real causal contact with the world, and nobody budges. But then in my reading group we noticed something that doesn't get talked about in the popular versions. The hedonists always frame well-being as a mental-state property — like, it's about what's happening inside your head regardless of origin — while the other side frames it as a life-narrative property that requires you to actually author your experiences. So I'm wondering: is this stall actually substantive, or are they just using the word well-being for two different things?

Can you diagnose the disagreement for me? Don't just list hedonism versus desire-satisfaction versus objective list theories. Look at what each side actually says about the machine. Is there a hidden empirical question doing the work — maybe about whether simulated preferences are epistemically corrupted — or is it bedrock intuition about naturalness? Here's my own hunch, which might be wrong: maybe the real split is about whether well-being is indexical to the cognitive architecture doing the evaluating. If it's the same brain inside and outside the machine, why should location matter? But then the authenticity advocates say the evaluation mechanism itself is compromised when it's causally isolated from the world. Is that a coherent reply or just a restatement?

I want an essay that figures out what kind of disagreement this is — verbal, substantive, or two different questions answered under one heading — and tells me what, if anything, could actually move it. Would a conceptual distinction help, or is this one of those debates where progress is impossible because both sides are defending different starting intuitions? No need for a bibliography or reading list; I just want the structure of the stall mapped out clearly.
