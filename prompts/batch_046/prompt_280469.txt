You are drafting the joint statement of an adversarial collaboration between two camps that have debated the problem of dirty hands in politics for more than a year without convergence. Camp A, the Democratic Fiduciary Absolutists, holds that any intentional moral wrongdoing—deception, coercion, or promise-breaking—by an official in office structurally violates the fiduciary relation with citizens; good outcomes, later disclosure, or institutional role cannot redeem the act, because democratic authority derives from a mandate that excludes pre-authorized moral taint. Camp B, the Role-Contingent Pragmatists, holds that officials occupy roles with differentiated moral permissions; they may employ tainted means to prevent catastrophic or severely unjust outcomes, provided the decision is constrained by institutional checks and subjected to ex post transparency that permits democratic sanction, and they maintain that moral residue attaches to the decision rather than permanently to the office or the democratic system itself.

Your deliverable is the polished document both camps could sign, structured exactly as follows.

1. Agreed Claims. A numbered list of at least six substantive, non-vacuous claims both sides accept regarding political dirty hands, democratic accountability, institutional design, and the distribution of moral responsibility. These should commit both camps to specific positions—for example, about whether counterfactual harm is relevant to evaluation, whether partial disclosure differs from full disclosure in kind, and whether an institution can register moral costs without dissolving them—rather than generic platitudes.

2. Disagreement Matrix. A structured presentation, in neutral wording that each camp accepts as accurate to its own view, of the precise residual divergences. For each entry include: (a) Camp A’s formulation; (b) Camp B’s formulation; (c) a one-sentence clarification of whether the gap is conceptual, empirical, or normative. One disagreement must concern the injected concept of the Laundering Threshold: the claim that an institution can, through formalized ex post review and registered sanction, dissipate or contain the moral taint of an original dirty act, with Camp A denying that institutions can launder taint in principle and Camp B affirming that they can do so up to a specifiable limit tied to democratic knowledge.

3. The Cruxes. Identify three cruxes—sub-questions such that settling them in one direction would cause at least one camp to revise its overall stance. For each crux:
   a. State it in precise, neutral terms.
   b. Present a concrete case or institutional design that both camps agree is relevant and bears directly on it; include specific roles, stakes, mechanisms of disclosure, and available sanctions.
   c. Record in advance what each camp predicts about the case: the specific judgment or outcome each expects, and the reasoning from its broader view that generates that expectation.

The concrete cases must include:
   - A hidden-authorization case: a regional security director, with tacit but undocumented approval from a civilian oversight board, orders the fabrication of evidence in an interrogation to prevent an imminent attack; the deception succeeds, and the full record is revealed six months later during a routine audit, triggering a statutory review.
   - A structural-opacity case: a legislature passes an emergency budget containing an undisclosed provision that redirects funds from a visible social program to prevent a public-health catastrophe; the provision is designed to be discovered only after implementation has begun, and discovery triggers an automatic recall mechanism.

Additionally, include a crux concerning the Moral Residue Audit: a proposed institutional mechanism requiring that every dirty-handed decision be entered into a public registry tracking the moral cost, the official responsible, and the democratic sanction applied, with both camps predicting whether the introduction of such an audit would stabilize or destabilize democratic legitimacy over time, and on what empirical conditions that prediction depends.

The tone should be formal, suitable for a political-theory journal’s adversarial-collaboration section, and focused on producing a constructive text both parties could endorse, with residual disagreement rendered exact rather than obscured. Do not include requests for bibliographies, reading lists, citations, or references to specific page numbers. Do not adjudicate which camp is correct; the task is the joint drafting itself.
