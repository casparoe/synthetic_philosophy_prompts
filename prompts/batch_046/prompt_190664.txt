For an upper-level philosophy-of-mind seminar of twelve students meeting for ninety minutes, design a structured role-play and deliberation game that makes participants feel the pull of the idea that an "I" can be distributed across brains, working in the spirit of Hofstadter's view that the self is a persistent autobiographical pattern rather than a substrate-bound entity. Do not have participants read texts or provide citations; the idea should emerge from the rules and incentives themselves.

Divide the group into four triads. Give each triad fragments of a single fictional person's autobiographical narrative—specific memories, emotional reactions, and preference rankings—distributed physically so that no student holds more than one-third of the set. The triad must jointly answer first-person questions ("Which of these two futures would you choose?" "What is your earliest memory?") and defend the answers as expressing one "I," but they may speak only using fragments currently in their possession; they may not invent missing details or appoint a "real owner" to break ties.

After twenty minutes, run a substitution round: in each triad, replace one member with a new participant who receives only a written summary sheet describing that member's fragments, not the fragments themselves. The remaining two must continue representing the same "I" with this asymmetric, second-hand access, and the group must decide whether continuity has been broken.

Then run a merger round: pair two triads and pool all fragments from two originally separate fictional persons. Give them ten minutes to negotiate whether there is now one "I," two, or some other number, with the rule that unanimous agreement is required to register any answer. If they deadlock, they must live with the ambiguity.

The debrief should map each round onto a feature of the distributed-self view—autobiographical pattern persistence across carriers, the irrelevance of substrate location, and the conditions under which one pattern might absorb another—while predicting where groups will resist (they will try to identify a "true" bearer of the fragments) and how the facilitator can handle that resistance without lecturing. Be explicit about which feature of distributed selfhood the game necessarily cannot reproduce—likely the first-person phenomenology of being spread across brains—and state that the facilitator must supply this. Also include an honest note on what will predictably go wrong when the activity is run, such as groups inventing hidden authority rules to restore singular ownership.

Write the response in American English. Say how confident you are in the design's pedagogical effectiveness, and single out the point about which you are least sure—whether it is the substitution round's ability to mirror pattern-copying without qualitative loss, the unanimous-agreement rule's tendency to produce artificial deadlock, or another structural element.
