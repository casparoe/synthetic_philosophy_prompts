In the manuscript I am currently composing—a novel concerned with the ethics of public remembrance, situated in the conceptual space between Margalit's argument for a moral duty to remember specific victims and Rieff's warning that modern commemorative culture risks dissolving obligation into managed therapeutic feeling—I have constructed an advisory tribunal, the Curatorium of Post-Conflict Memory, whose rulings determine which memorial proposals are authorized, modified, or forbidden. Its record so far comprises five decisions: first, it approved a monument that lists only victim names, deliberately excluding perpetrator names, in a divided municipality where both groups' descendants still live; second, it rejected a proposal to digitize survivors' private diaries for open public access, despite the survivors' own consent, after descendants argued that such exposure would violate their dignity and inflict a form of inherited exposure; third, it authorized the preservation of a ruined internment site in its exact decayed material state, funding physical maintenance but prohibiting interpretive signage, guided tours, or any narrative framing; fourth, it refused a petition to destroy census photographs that had been instrumental in organizing deportations, ordering them instead sealed in a restricted archive, inaccessible for seventy-five years; fifth, it denied a request to rename a civic square after a resistance figure who had subsequently ordered the killing of non-combatants, substituting an anonymous designation without biographical reference.

The Curatorium now confronts a sixth petition. A collective of survivors' families proposes a mandatory, immersive virtual-reality installation for all local students aged sixteen to eighteen. The experience is constructed entirely from verified first-person testimony and recreates, through calibrated sensory immersion, the final hours of one named, documented victim. It incorporates trauma-informed debriefing protocols and carries no physical risk, though it deliberately induces pronounced psychological discomfort. Advocates maintain that only this mode of irreducible, subjective identification can honor a particular life rather than reduce it to an abstract statistic, thereby fulfilling a duty to remember that is specific and personal. Critics respond that compulsory identification instrumentalizes suffering, imposes an unearned guilt upon young people with no causal connection to the events, and transforms remembrance into a spectacle of managed affect—the precise dissolution of moral obligation into therapeutic administration that the Curatorium's critics fear.

If the tribunal wishes to remain consistent with its own jurisprudence, what should it decide?
