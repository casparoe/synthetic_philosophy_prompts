I just finished a long, somewhat heated argument with a friend about updateless decision theory, and I want to check whether my confusion is conceptual or merely verbal. She insists that UDT's prohibition on updating applies narrowly: you do not condition on your own action when evaluating a policy, but you otherwise Bayes-update on every observation in the standard way. I think there is a deeper tension. If UDT requires you to evaluate policies by weighting all logically possible worlds according to your prior over decision algorithms, then any observation you actually make—including seeing the contents of a box in Newcomb's problem—is logically determined relative to the policy you implement. So to remain genuinely "updateless" in a strong sense, wouldn't you have to refuse to revise beliefs about any variable that is logically entailed by your own algorithm? But that seems to make empirical learning impossible. Conversely, if you do update on outcomes, then after opening the box and seeing a million dollars you should update on the fact that you probably one-boxed, which looks like it undermines the ex ante reasoning that justified one-boxing in the first place. So my question is this: is there a principled boundary within UDT between "updating on your action" (forbidden) and "updating on evidence about the world" (allowed), given that both are logically correlated with your policy? Or does UDT actually commit us to logical omniscience and a refusal to revise beliefs about any state entangled with our decision algorithm, rendering it unusable for bounded reasoners? Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of the question.
