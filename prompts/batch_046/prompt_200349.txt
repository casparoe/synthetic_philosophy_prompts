Hey — I'm stuck on an argument my study group cooked up about whether dreams are experiences, tying Malcolm's skepticism to Dennett's reconstruction story but pushing it into weird territory. Here's the chain:

(1) Call it the decoupling criterion: if a mental state is genuinely experiential, the subject must retain at least some metacognitive access to its phenominal content — otherwise we're just attributing experience to blind processing.

(2) During REM there's prefrontal decoupling, so that access is offline.

(3) So REM states aren't experiences.

(4) Dreams are usually assigned to REM, so dreams aren't experiences either — Malcolm's conclusion, but now with a mechanism.

(5) But decoupling isn't unique to sleep; it also happens in deep flow states, alchohol blackouts, and some absense seizures, yet people report rich content afterwards.

(6) If we apply the decoupling criterion consistently, we have to deny experience in all those waking cases too.

(7) That's absurd — nobody thinks you're literally unconscious while driving home on autopilot.

(8) So either the criterion is false, in which case Dennett can say dreams are reconstructed experiences but then he can't block subliminal perception or blindsight from counting; or we keep the criterion and swallow that huge chunks of waking life aren't experiential after all.

My friend says this proves Malcolm was right all along: if rejecting the criterion saves waking experience only by letting in every subpersonal process, then we should just bite the bullet on flow and autopilot. I feel like there's an equivocation between access at the time and access in principle somewhere between (2) and (5), but I can't pin it. Which step is the weak one, and what's the cost of giving it up?
