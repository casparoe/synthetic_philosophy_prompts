Draft the joint statement that a moral particularist and a contextualist generalist could both sign after a good-faith adversarial collaboration on whether moral reasoning requires substantive, cross-domain principles. Structure the document as follows. First, a numbered agreed-claims section covering shared commitments: that moral judgment is feature-sensitive; that reasons can be holistic and non-monotonic; that both camps reject deductive-nomological models of moral inference; and that domain-indexed norms can accommodate contextual variation without collapsing into particularism. Second, a disagreements table stating, in wording each side accepts for its own position, the precise residual dispute—namely, whether any principle can be both genuinely general (not tautological or purely formal) and action-guiding across domains whose morally relevant structures differ substantially; include explicit formulations of what each camp takes principle-generality and feature-sensitivity respectively to entail. Third, identify the cruxes, including whether consistent moral expertise across domains requires tacit general norms, and whether particularist calibration of feature-weights can be systematically explained without such norms. Fourth, for each crux present a concrete hypothetical case—such as resource triage under partial information, or promise-breaking across institutional and intimate settings—that both sides agree bears on the issue, with each side's predicted resolution and reasoning written down in advance. Fifth, separate verbal disagreements, especially over whether principle denotes an exceptionless rule or a weighted pro tanto consideration, from substantive normative disagreements. Conclude by assessing whether any residual divergence is genuinely normative or rather a difference in explanatory emphasis regarding the architecture of moral reasoning, and present the entire document as a constructive text both parties could endorse.
