I'm a doctoral student in philosophy of language preparing material for a seminar on the scope of semantic externalism, and I've constructed an argument that seems to show the view collapses into a paralyzing parochialism if it applies to epistemic vocabulary. Here is the chain as I have it.

(1) If Putnam-style externalism is correct for natural kind terms, then the reference of a term is partly fixed by the actual nature of the entities in the speaker's environment, not solely by internal psychological states. (2) Epistemic terms such as "knowledge" and "justification" are not purely descriptive; they operate as normative kind terms that pick out real properties—specifically, properties instantiated by belief-forming processes that actually track truth in the local world. (3) Consider Twin Earth, where local physics permits a distinct but locally reliable belief-forming mechanism: beliefs are generated via acoustic resonance rather than photonic interaction. Twin-Earthlings possess a concept—call it "schmawledge"—that plays exactly the internal role our "knowledge" plays, but its success conditions depend on acoustic-resonance tracking. (4) By the reasoning in (1), when Twin-Oscar asserts that he has knowledge that the sun is hot, the content of his assertion concerns schmawledge, not knowledge, despite his being psychologically identical to Earth-Oscar. (5) Now suppose Twin-Oscar writes a rigorous treatise defending internalism, arguing that knowledge (as he terms it) requires only internal coherence, and his argument is structurally identical to one offered by an Earth philosopher. (6) Because his token of "knowledge" refers to schmawledge, his argument establishes only that schmawledge is internally constituted; it establishes nothing about knowledge on Earth. (7) Generalizing: any epistemological argument formulated in any world concerns only the local analog of knowledge, never knowledge as such. (8) But epistemology aims to state conditions that hold for knowledge across worlds, not parochial conditions for local analogs. (9) Therefore, either externalism does not apply to epistemic terms—undermining its natural extension beyond chemistry—or epistemology collapses into world-relative parochialism. Since parochialism is unacceptable, we must reject (1) or (2).

The conclusion feels absurd because it uses Twin Earth reasoning to undermine the very possibility of universal epistemology, yet my advisor insists the argument contains a subtle slide rather than a genuine reductio. I am inclined to preserve (1) and (8), but I cannot see which step fails. Which premise or inference should I reject, and what does giving it up cost? In particular, is there an equivocation between the reference-fixing conditions for a term and the subject matter of an argument formulated with that term, or is the mistake elsewhere—perhaps in treating normative epistemic properties as environment-sensitive natural kinds?
