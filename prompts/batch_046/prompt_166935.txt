In my course on Earth-system science and historical periodization, we have examined competing proposals for the Anthropocene's onset: the Orbis spike around 1610 tied to Columbian exchange and biotic homogenization, the fossil-fuel divergence associated with the Industrial Revolution in the late eighteenth century, and the Great Acceleration's plutonium and plastic signals after 1950. I understand that different research communities favor different markers, but I am struggling with what seems like a category error in how the debate is framed. If the Anthropocene is not merely a stratigraphic unit but a claim about a new Earth-system state—one in which human activity dominates biogeochemical cycles—then should not the boundary be determined by when the system actually entered that state, rather than by which isotopic or biological signal is most globally synchronous? Put differently, why do stratigraphers insist that formal recognition requires a Global Boundary Stratotype Section and Point, when historians and Earth-system scientists are describing a functional transition that was geographically and temporally uneven? Is the disagreement genuinely about empirical evidence, or does it rest on an implicit philosophical commitment—perhaps to a strict uniformitarian view of geological time—that forces a single, sharp date onto a process that was fundamentally diachronous? I feel as though I am conflating the ontological question of when humans became a dominant geological agent with the methodological question of how we correlate rock layers globally, but I cannot see precisely where my reasoning slips.
