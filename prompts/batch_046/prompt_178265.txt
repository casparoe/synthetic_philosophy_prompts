Here is an argument about killing and letting die that seems to prove too much once we allow what I will call an omission architecture: a system an agent designs whose default state withholds aid, after which the agent withdraws from active management.

(1) The standard defense of the killing/letting-die distinction holds that killing directs an existing causal process toward harm, whereas letting die fails to interrupt an independent one.

(2) In an omission architecture, the agent's initial design is a direct causal intervention—she authors the harm-pathway—while her subsequent absence is structurally identical to letting die, yet both phases belong to one continuous exercise of agency.

(3) If continuity of agency carries moral weight, then either both phases count as killing (since she authored the pathway) or both count as letting die (since she is now absent). They cannot be divided without breaking continuity.

(4) The only natural breaking point is the moment at which she could intervene but declines. But this makes the moral classification depend on monitoring frequency: checking every hour and declining makes her a killer each time, while checking once a year makes her mostly someone who lets die.

(5) That frequency-dependence is absurd, so the breaking point fails.

(6) Therefore (1) is false: once omission architectures exist, there is no stable causal-structural distinction between directing harm and failing to interrupt it; the distinction must rest on contingent social roles rather than on agency or causation.

(7) Hence running a for-profit logistics network that excludes unprofitable regions—where the exclusion is built into the algorithm and rarely overridden—is not merely letting people die but mass killing, unless we abandon continuity altogether.

Where does this slip? I would prefer to keep (2) and (4), but if I must reject (5) or (6) I want to know exactly what that costs—whether I am buying back the distinction at the price of making moral status depend on how often an agent checks her dashboard, or whether I am conceding that institutional omissions are always killings. Which step should I give up, and why did it look plausible?
