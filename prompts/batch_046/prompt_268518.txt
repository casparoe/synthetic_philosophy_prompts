I am a novelist, and I convene a fortnightly reading group for writers and philosophers — about twelve people, seated in a circle, for seventy-five minutes. I want to stage an in-room activity that makes participants feel the force of ambiguity aversion in the Ellsberg sense, but translated out of monetary betting and into the kind of interpretive commitment writers actually make: choosing between narrative resolutions whose evaluative status is either precisely risked or genuinely ambiguous.

To keep the exercise from being something participants have encountered in a hundred popularisations of decision theory, I want you to inject a less familiar framing. Rather than a single urn of unknown composition, construct a two-stage collective game. In the first stage, each participant receives one of two sealed 'editorial briefs' describing the same short, deliberately ambiguous prose fragment (which I shall supply): Brief A states that an external panel judges this resolution excellent with a known 50 per cent probability and poor with 50 per cent; Brief B states that the panel's criteria are undisclosed, so the distribution is genuinely unknown to everyone in the room, though the resolution is identical. In the second stage, the group must vote, without revealing their briefs to one another initially, on whether the fragment should be published under the evaluation conditions of Brief A or Brief B. The payoff scheme should be non-monetary — perhaps 'publication credits' that determine whose written interpretation is read aloud and receives structured feedback, with a collective satisfaction score revealed only after the vote is locked.

Please provide: clear rules and any materials needed; a minute-by-minute timeline for the seventy-five minutes; the debrief that maps specific moments of hesitation, strategic reversal, or collective pressure onto the formal distinction between risk and ambiguity; an honest account of what will predictably go wrong the first time — for example, whether participants treat ambiguity as mere risk with unknown probabilities, or whether collective behaviour masks individual aversion; and a frank admission of which feature of the Ellsberg phenomenon the activity cannot reproduce and must be supplied by me in explanation (for instance, the absence of second-order beliefs about the unknown distribution, or the specific failure of the sure-thing principle).

Crucially, please separate what is settled from what is genuinely contested among decision theorists and philosophers of economics: for example, whether ambiguity aversion is rational, whether it can be reduced to risk aversion with state-dependent utility, and whether the Ellsberg choices violate completeness in subjective expected utility theory. Do not present any contested interpretation as the consensus. Please write the response in British English.
