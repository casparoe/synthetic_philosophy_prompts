As a retired molecular biologist who has only recently begun to study philosophy in earnest, I find myself repeatedly returning to questions about how we justify knowledge claims that bridge different domains of inquiry. In particular, I have been reading about the Nyāya school’s classification of pramāṇas—the four legitimate sources of cognition, namely direct perception (pratyakṣa), inference (anumāna), comparison or analogical reasoning (upamāna), and verbal testimony (śabda)—and I am struck by how modern philosophy of science tends to treat analogical modeling either as a form of inductive inference or as a reliance on authoritative testimony, with little room for comparison as an autonomous epistemic source. At the same time, I have become persuaded by arguments for an accuracy-first epistemology, according to which the intrinsic value of any belief consists solely in its being true or approximately true, and practical or instrumental considerations enter only derivatively. Given these two commitments, I would ask you to set out the most important implications of taking the Nyāya fourfold taxonomy seriously within an accuracy-first framework, especially regarding the evaluation of analogical extrapolation in biomedical research—such as reasoning from murine immunological mechanisms to predictions about human clinical outcomes. Please present your answer in continuous prose without headings, bullet points, boldface, or tables, writing in the manner of a formal letter; begin with the single implication you regard as most fundamental, then proceed to the others. I do not require any bibliography or list of sources, and I ask that you not invoke obscure commentators or texts unknown outside specialist scholarship, since I wish to assess the argument on its conceptual merits alone.
