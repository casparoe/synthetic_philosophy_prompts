Avicenna's flying man thought experiment is typically read as establishing the immateriality of the soul: a subject suspended in a void, entirely deprived of sensory contact with his body, would nevertheless be aware that he exists. Rather than rehearsing that standard dualist interpretation, I want you to examine a conceptual tension that is internal to the scenario itself. The flying man is said to grasp his own existence without grasping any of his properties—not even that he is human, embodied, or persisting through time—yet he is still able to apply the first-person pronoun, to recognize the awareness as his own rather than as an anonymous occurrence of consciousness. Explain the difficulty of sustaining this purely indexical self-reference in the absence of any descriptive conditions that would ordinarily allow a subject to re-identify himself across moments. Does the argument genuinely show that self-awareness is independent of all bodily and descriptive properties, or does it only reveal the availability of a minimal, non-descriptive "I-thought"—one too thin to support the metaphysical conclusion that the soul is a substance distinct from the body? In your answer, treat the issue not as a debate about substance dualism but as a problem about the minimal conditions for first-person reference, and address whether Avicenna confuses the absence of bodily content within awareness with the absence of bodily conditions for the very capacity to refer to oneself at all.
