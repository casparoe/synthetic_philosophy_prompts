I serve as the senior evaluation officer for a regional school-funding authority, and every quarter I must sign off on whether districts may renew grants based on reported achievement-trend data; the reports always aggregate across demographic and mobility subgroups, I have ninety minutes per file, a statistics background but no causal-inference specialist on staff, and I must defend my call to a non-technical board that can override me only on values, not on arithmetic. Write a reusable decision procedure—an ordered checklist of tests I can run in sequence—that turns what the logic of Simpson's paradox actually requires into something I can apply repeatedly without reinventing the reasoning, specifying for each step the exact question to ask of the data, what counts as a sufficient answer to proceed, where the sequence stops with a clear "approve," "reject," or "hold for disaggregation," and where it must halt and hand the decision to human judgment because the issue is normative rather than statistical; the procedure must also flag the cases it cannot settle, especially when subgroup definitions shift between years, when mobility creates artificial membership changes, or when a variable such as prior funding level may be either a confounder or part of the intervention mechanism itself. Apply it to three proposals: (a) a district reporting aggregate math-score improvement despite every stable demographic subgroup showing decline; (b) a proposal where disaggregation reveals improvement only in a high-mobility subgroup whose membership changed radically between measurements, yielding a statistical reversal that the procedure should reject but that the board will politically want to approve; and (c) a case where the correct statistical path depends on whether prior funding is treated as a confounder or as part of the mechanism, forcing a judgment hand-off. For each case, show the step at which the procedure stops and what it outputs. Explicitly state what the procedure is incapable of doing—particularly regarding causal claims about the intervention itself—and include a brief misfire warning for analysts who confuse subgroup consistency with proof of efficacy. Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then give the reasoning and the full procedure, with no preamble and without restating this prompt.
