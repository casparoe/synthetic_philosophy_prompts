I'm working through whether any theory of phenomenal consciousness can survive the charge—pressed by Chase and Sanborn—that the very concept of qualia is incoherent, since it requires properties that are simultaneously intrinsic, non-physical, epistemically private, and causally inert. Before I evaluate particular accounts, I want a clear standard of adequacy: not just a list of phenomena, but the specific explanatory and structural obligations a theory must meet if it is to preserve the reality of subjective experience without falling into that contradiction, and without retreating into eliminativism.

Write the desiderata: what any adequate theory of phenomenal character must deliver to count as successful on these terms. For each requirement, specify the case or consideration that motivates it—such as which comparative phenomenal judgments it must preserve (e.g., knowing that this red-experience differs from that green-experience) versus which absolute phenomenal claims it can afford to weaken (e.g., knowing what an isolated experience is like in abstraction from all relational structure); which structural features of consciousness, like phenomenal unity or the transparency of perceptual states, it must accommodate; and which results from cognitive science about information integration and global availability it must remain consistent with. Inject the problem of phenomenal binding: can a theory explain why experiences feel unified without positing qualitative atoms, and does that strategy undermine inverted-spectrum thought experiments? Flag any pair of desiderata you take to be jointly unsatisfiable—especially any tension between explaining the apparent privacy of experience and retaining causal efficacy for mental content. Then, briefly and without requiring bibliographic detail, score how an eliminativist approach, a representationalist approach, and a relational or structuralist approach fare against your list, but keep the principal weight on the criteria themselves: which are non-negotiable, which are prejudices of one camp, and which single requirement does the most work in excluding candidates.
