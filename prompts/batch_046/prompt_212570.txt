Question 3. Consider Nozick's tracking conditions for knowledge that p: (i) p is true; (ii) S believes p; (iii) in nearby worlds where p is false, S does not believe p (sensitivity); and (iv) in nearby worlds where p is true, S believes p (adherence). The familiar closure failure arises when S tracks "this animal is a zebra" and competently deduces "this animal is not a painted mule," yet fails to track the conclusion: in nearby worlds where zoo staff have disguised a mule, S still believes the animal is a zebra and therefore still believes it is not a mule.

Now introduce a supplementary "derivational-robustness" clause: S tracks q (where q is competently derived from p) if and only if S tracks p, and in nearby worlds in which p holds and S actually performs the derivation, S comes to believe q. Some critics worry that this clause shifts the analysis from belief-centered counterfactuals to a process-centered evaluation of inference.

Answer parts (a) through (c):

(a) Explain Nozick's denial of closure using the zebra example. Specify exactly which tracking condition fails for the derived belief, and explain why the failure occurs despite the logical validity of the deduction.

(b) Does the derivational-robustness clause rescue closure? Address specifically whether logical entailment between p and q guarantees that the modal neighborhoods relevant to tracking p coincide with those relevant to tracking q. Also consider whether evaluating derivation across worlds introduces an evaluative mode structurally closer to reliabilism than to standard tracking.

(c) A critic observes that in a nearby world S tracks p but suffers a transient cognitive impairment that blocks derivation of q; S never forms any belief about q. The critic argues that derivational robustness is therefore too demanding: S avoids false belief about q only by failing to form it. Respond, being careful not to misrepresent the respective roles of sensitivity and adherence in Nozick's original framework.

Student Response:

"(a) Nozick denies closure because tracking applies belief-by-belief rather than to inferential structures. In the zebra case, S tracks the premise "this is a zebra" because in the nearest worlds where it is not a zebra—say, where it is an ordinary antelope—S does not believe it is a zebra. From this S deduces "it is not a painted mule." But the nearest worlds where this conclusion is false are worlds where zoo staff have painted a mule to look like a zebra. In those worlds, S still believes the animal is a zebra, so S still believes it is not a painted mule. Hence the sensitivity condition fails for the derived belief: were the conclusion false, S would still believe it. Adherence holds, but sensitivity does not, so S does not know the conclusion.

(b) The derivational-robustness clause does rescue closure. Because q is logically entailed by p, any world where q is false is a world where p is false. Therefore, if S tracks p—so that in the nearest worlds where p is false, S does not believe p—then in those same worlds S will not perform the derivation and will not believe q. The clause requires that in worlds where p holds and S derives q, she gets q right, which is satisfied since the deduction is valid. The worry that this collapses into reliabilism is overstated: we are not computing statistical frequencies across actual worlds, but checking specific counterfactual derivations in specific nearby worlds. The modal neighborhoods for p and q necessarily coincide because of the entailment, so the belief-centered structure is preserved.

(c) The critic is mistaken. Tracking is fundamentally about avoiding false belief, which is captured by sensitivity alone; adherence is a secondary condition that applies only when the belief is actually formed. In the impairment world, q is true (since p holds), but S never derives q, so there is no belief to evaluate for adherence. The derivational-robustness clause only applies when the derivation is actually undertaken. Because S avoids forming a false belief—indeed, avoids forming any belief about q—there is no tracking failure. The clause is therefore not too demanding; it correctly restricts knowledge-attribution to cases where the cognitive pathway is intact."
