Since my mother's death three months ago, I have served as co-executor of her estate alongside my sister, a responsibility that has required us to navigate a sequence of interdependent financial decisions—whether to sell particular assets immediately or hold them for potential appreciation, whether to liquidate investments in stages—each of which either party can unilaterally terminate to force an immediate partition. The structural resemblance to the centipede game has become impossible for me to ignore: at every monthly node, mutual cooperation promises a greater joint value, yet backward induction suggests that rational agents should defect at the very first opportunity. I have drafted notes for an essay that treats this estate administration as a lived case study through which to interrogate the backward induction paradox.

My central claim is that standard resolutions—appeals to imperfect common knowledge of rationality, or to bounded rationality—are insufficient because they preserve the hidden assumption of a unitary, strategically continuous agent across all nodes. I propose instead what I call diachronic preference fragmentation: over the prolonged course of administration, grief, fatigue, and shifting fiduciary obligations alter the preference orderings of each decision-maker so substantially that the agent at node n is not strategically identical to the agent at node n+1. If this is correct, it dissolves the induction proof not by denying rationality, but by undermining the identity condition required for the proof's recursive step. I also examine whether this fragmentation is genuinely explanatory or merely redescribes emotional variability, and whether it can be formalized without collapsing into a trivial denial of intertemporal consistency.

I would welcome critical comments on three points in particular. First, is my reconstruction of the backward induction logic accurate, especially with respect to the role of common knowledge? Second, does the diachronic fragmentation argument genuinely resolve the paradox, or does it merely relocate the problem into the philosophy of personal identity? Third, does my application to estate administration illegitimately conflate strategic reasoning with legal and ethical obligations that operate under different normative constraints? I am less concerned with stylistic revisions than with whether the conceptual architecture holds together.
