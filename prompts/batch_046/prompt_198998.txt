I've been working through some forecasting literature and recent discussions of epistemic modesty in a graduate seminar on rationality, and I've hit a conceptual snag that feels like it might be a confusion rather than a real paradox, but I can't locate the error. The standard framing—drawing on Tetlock's distinction—presents the inside view as reasoning from the particularities of a case and the outside view as reasoning from reference-class statistics and base rates. Epistemic modesty is then often characterized as giving appropriate weight to the outside view, especially in domains with poor feedback loops. But when I try to apply both consistently to my own belief-formation, I run into what looks like a recursive collapse. If I take the outside view seriously about my own cognitive profile—my calibration history, my domain knowledge, my reasoning biases—then my inside-view analysis of any specific forecast or judgment seems to be nothing more than one sample drawn from that reference class. In other words, my first-order reasoning appears to be double-counted if I both anchor on the base rate and then update on my own particularized analysis, since that analysis is already part of the distribution I'm using to generate the base rate.

So my question is this: if epistemic modesty genuinely requires privileging the outside view in this way, why doesn't rational consistency demand that I treat inside-view reasoning as epistemically vicious—something like using a broken instrument—and outsource all belief-formation to pure statistical reference classes? And if there is a legitimate role for inside-view updates, what exactly distinguishes them from the kind of illegitimate self-referential weighting I'm describing? I keep feeling that I'm smuggling in first-person authority under the guise of "updating," but I can't tell whether that's a necessary feature of rational agency or just a failure of modesty.
