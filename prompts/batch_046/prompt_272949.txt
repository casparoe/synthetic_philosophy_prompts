Write a dialogue between a disjunctivist philosopher of perception and a representationalist opponent debating whether veridical perception and illusion share a common mental kind. The disjunctivist should hold that genuine perception and illusion are categorically distinct—veridical seeing involves a direct relation to the world that illusion lacks—rather than being the same representational content with different accuracy conditions. The representationalist should defend a version of the argument from illusion, insisting that phenomenal indistinguishability entails shared representational structure.

Center their exchange on the following precisely described scenario: a subject executes a rapid saccade toward a digital clock that displays continuous seconds. Because of precisely timed saccadic suppression followed by post-saccadic visual integration, the subject experiences the clock as having stayed frozen for roughly two subjective seconds, even though the physical display never paused and the environment contains no corresponding static state. The representationalist should argue that because this illusory experience is phenomenally identical to genuinely observing a clock that has actually stopped for two seconds, both cases must instantiate the same representational content—thereby undermining the disjunctivist claim that veridical perception involves a direct worldly relation absent in illusion.

The disjunctivist should respond directly to this specific argument, not by denying phenomenal similarity but by explaining why shared phenomenology fails to entail shared mental kind—whether by appealing to the relational or causal etiology of veridical perception, by distinguishing the functional role of the two states, or by challenging the inference from phenomenal identity to representational identity. The representationalist should then reply to that particular response, pressing the point further or refining it, and so on.

The dialogue must exhibit real dialectical back-and-forth: each speaker should address the actual claim just made by the other, adjusting or sharpening their position in reply, rather than delivering parallel monologues. Both characters should be presented as equally competent, rigorous, and charitable. Write the dialogue in polished American English. Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, citations, or requests for further sources.
