I am defending a thesis I call distributed loop persistence at a philosophy of personal identity workshop next month. The view is Hofstadter-inspired but pushed into literal metaphysical territory: the self-referential symbolic loop that generates an "I" is not necessarily substrate-bound to one brain, and in conditions of sustained, high-bandwidth cognitive interdependence—such as long-term intimate partnerships with merged decision architectures, or conjoined twins with shared thalamic integration—the loop genuinely extends across substrates. I deny that this requires fusion into a single consciousness or collapses into mere metaphorical we-talk; instead, I treat the loop as having a center of causal dominance in one brain but with genuinely distributed fragments that are functionally necessary to its self-reference. The argument turns on defining the loop by causal-functional self-reference rather than spatiotemporal continuity of a single brain, and on claiming that the uniqueness constraint on personal identity applies only to complete loops, not to loop-fragments that are causally dominant in one substrate but genuinely extended into another.

The audience is precisely this mix: a reductionist metaphysician who holds that one brain with spatiotemporal continuity is necessary for identity; a phenomenologist for whom first-person singularity is non-negotiable and non-divisible; a cognitive scientist who studies collective cognition and accepts group-level processing but insists it is always derivative and never constitutes a literal self; and a logician specializing in the logic of identity who will press the uniqueness and transitivity constraints. They are not hostile, but they care about metaphysical rigor, not poetic metaphor.
