Please describe the most important implications of Samuel Scheffler's afterlife conjecture—the claim that the value and meaning of our present activities depend tacitly on the expectation that human life will continue long after our individual deaths, functioning as a kind of collective afterlife—for the comparative moral urgency of preventing human extinction versus mitigating other catastrophic risks. In particular, address whether Scheffler's framework requires specifically biological continuity, or whether a functionally equivalent but biologically distinct successor civilization (for instance, one composed of artificial minds or radically transformed descendants) could satisfy the psychological and axiological conditions that make present projects feel worthwhile. How should a longtermist who accepts the conjecture weigh extinction scenarios that preserve some form of intelligent, culturally continuous life against those that result in permanent uninhabitation, and does this distinction collapse once we examine what Scheffler actually takes the afterlife to provide?
