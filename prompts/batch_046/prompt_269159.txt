Compare the normative architecture of Max Weber's Protestant work ethic—specifically the conception of Beruf, in which labor is internalized as a moral calling rather than an instrumental burden—with the rights-based framework implicit in the 35-hour work-week movement and in critiques of karoshi. Treat both not as historical descriptions but as competing answers to the question of who holds the right to leisure and against whom that right is held. In your comparison, address three points of divergence. First, whether leisure is understood negatively (as the absence of contractual obligation) or positively (as sovereign jurisdiction over one's time regardless of employment status). Second, whether the obligation to work is bounded by an internal moral ceiling within the worker's conscience or only by an external statutory cap imposed by the state or employer. Third, where responsibility for overwork is located—whether in individual failure of ascetic discipline, in employer extraction, or in structural regulatory absence. To prevent the comparison from collapsing into generic labor-policy debate, apply the following conceptual distinction: separate leisure-as-absence (hours not spent in paid labor) from leisure-as-jurisdiction (autonomous authority over unstructured time that persists even when no contract is being violated). Use this distinction to diagnose whether karoshi represents primarily a deficit of absence or a deeper violation of jurisdiction, and to test whether Weber's ethic can accommodate either form of leisure right without undermining its own logic. Finally, assess which framework yields the more coherent account of leisure rights, identifying precisely where each position collapses under internal pressure rather than external critique.
