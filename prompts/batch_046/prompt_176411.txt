I've been noticing something in arguments lately and I can't tell if it's a real philosophical move or just people being sloppy. Last weekend I was at a dinner with friends — one works in urban planning, another in ecology — and we were talking about whether a new development was good or bad. What's weird is everyone agreed on the basic facts: it creates housing, it destroys a wetland corridor, it lowers commute times for some and raises them for others. But instead of weighing these, people kept switching ontologies. The planner talked about it as an optimization problem, then when pushed said it's really about social equity, then said it's fundamentally about ecological interdependence. Each frame didn't just highlight different values; it seemed to redescribe what the development actually IS. And nobody tried to combine them into one model. They just kept them in reserve and pulled out whichever one blocked the current objection.

I saw the same pattern in a podcast about AI risk: is alignment a technical control problem, a problem of value pluralism, or a problem of institutional legitimacy? The hosts cycled through all three without resolving whether they're describing one thing with three aspects or three different things sharing a name.

So is there an established concept for this? Not just perspectivism or value pluralism — those are too broad. I'm looking for the specific move where partial descriptions aren't synthesized or ranked, but kept in parallel and deployed tacticaly, with the multiplicty itself serving as a shield against committing to any single ontology. I think it might relate to that Jain doctrine about many-sided truth, anekantavada? — but in that tradition the multiplicity is supposed to be acknowledged humbly, not used as rhetorical cover. So is there a concept that captures the difference between genuine multi-perspectival humility and this strategic parking of incompatible frames? If not, can you coin one, define its boundaries, and say exactly how it differs from ordinary framework relativism and from simple equivocation? I don't want a bare label — I want the carving, especially where the edges get blurry. Also, is there a separate term for the rhetorical slide where you retreat from one frame to another not because you've changed your mind but because the first one got attacked?

Sorry for the ramble — I'm taking a course on comparative philosophy and this came up after we read about syādvāda, but I think what's happening in these debates is different because nobody is actually committing to the maybe-structure; they're just using the existance of many sides to avoid picking one.
