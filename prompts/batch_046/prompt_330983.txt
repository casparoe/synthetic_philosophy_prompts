Please defend the claim that emotions are best characterized neither as judgments, perceptions, nor raw bodily feelings, but as affective framing operations: sub-personal cognitive processes that restructure the salience landscape of working memory by binding interoceptive signals to conceptual schemas, without being reducible to either the bodily component or the conceptual appraisal. Your defense should proceed in three stages. First, distinguish framing operations from judgmental theories by showing that emotional framing can occur without explicit propositional endorsement—use the case of a sudden, pre-reflective fear response to an ambiguous shadow—while still explaining why the emotion carries evaluative direction. Second, distinguish them from perceptual theories of emotion by arguing that interoceptive binding is not representation of bodily change but a modulation of attentional priority; explain why this avoids treating emotion as passive observation of internal states. Third, address the transparency objection: if framing is sub-personal, why does emotional experience seem immediately present and authoritative rather than constructed? Resolve this by appealing to the integration of framing outputs into first-person narrative coherence without identifying the framing process itself with conscious judgment. Keep the argument conceptual; do not invoke specific named theorists, empirical studies, or textual citations. Construct the reasoning from first principles.
