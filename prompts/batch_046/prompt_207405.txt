I have come to defend a particular variant of expected-value fanaticism. On this view, rational choice is always determined by maximising expected value, but I extend this claim beyond standard risk cases to what I call "structurally open" decision problems: those in which (i) the outcome space includes states of radically different axiological scale, (ii) our credences are distributed over competing frameworks that disagree not merely about probabilities but about the very metric by which value is to be aggregated, and (iii) there is no principled upper bound on the magnitude of the goods or harms at stake. I further hold that in such cases there remains a single correct expectation—computed relative to a privileged aggregation rule—even though the agent lacks epistemic access to which rule applies.

Tell me what would count against this position. I am not asking for the familiar objections about Pascal's mugging or risk-averse intuition; I want the deeper defeaters.

Specifically:

- Which results in formal decision theory, measure theory, or the logic of imprecise probabilities would show that expectation is either undefined or non-unique in structurally open cases?
- Which distinctions—between practical and epistemic rationality, between decision under risk and decision under radical uncertainty, or between individual and collective aggregation—could be sharpened to show that tracking expectation is not rationally mandatory?
- Are there parallel debates in other fields—perhaps about intergenerational ethics, preference aggregation in social choice theory, or the limits of cardinal measurement—whose outcomes would analogically undermine my claim that there is always a privileged aggregation rule?
- Have defenders of expectation-maximisation introduced qualifications (for instance, about bounded utility, lexical priorities, or second-order uncertainty) that genuinely save the view, or have these qualifications rendered it unfalsifiable?

Please rank these potential defeaters from fatal to merely awkward. Indicate which objections a committed defender of this position would concede as genuinely damaging, and which they would dismiss as resting on a misapplication of the framework. I also want an honest assessment of whether the view risks being insulated from empirical or conceptual refutation, and whether such insulation is a theoretical vice or a virtue.

Please provide your answer in British English.
