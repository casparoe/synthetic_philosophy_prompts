I've been working through the Hart–Fuller debate with a study partner, and rather than continue talking past each other we want to produce a genuine adversarial collaboration document that both of us could sign. The two positions are these. One of us holds a hard separationist view: legal validity depends entirely on social sources—enactment by recognized authorities according to accepted criteria of recognition—and a rule can remain valid law even if it is secret, retroactive, grossly unjust, or impossible to obey; moral failure makes it bad law, or law that ought not to be obeyed, but it does not make it non-law. The other holds a procedural natural-law view: for a purported rule to count as law at all, it must satisfy minimal procedural moral conditions—generality, prospectivity, publicity, consistency, and congruence between official action and declared rule—such that systematic violation of these conditions undermines the very status of the rule as law, not merely its moral legitimacy.

Please draft the joint statement both camps could endorse. First, provide a numbered section of agreed claims, including that legal systems are normative social practices; that there is a conceptual distinction between describing what the law is and evaluating whether it ought to be obeyed; that retroactive and secret penal measures are gravely unjust; that well-functioning legal systems typically exhibit generality and prospectivity; and that citizens have stronger practical reasons to obey law that respects these procedural conditions. Second, present a disagreements table with columns phrased so that each side accepts the wording of its own view, covering at least (i) whether procedural moral failures ever affect legal validity rather than only moral authority, and (ii) whether the distinction between "not law" and "bad law" is stable or collapses upon analysis. Third, identify the precise cruxes—specifically, whether publicity and prospectivity are constitutive conditions of legality or merely regulative ideals, and whether the concept of law is purely descriptive or necessarily carries minimal normative content. Fourth, for each crux, present a concrete case both sides agree bears on it, record each side's prediction in advance, and explain the bearing. Use this case: a state enacts technically valid statutes through proper legislative procedures, but embeds the actual rules in an encrypted, inaccessible registry unknown to citizens; the rules are general in form but never published, and they are applied retroactively to a single named individual. The separationist should predict that these rules remain valid law, though they generate no moral obligation and perhaps no all-things-considered legal obligation either; the proceduralist should predict that the secrecy and retroactivity defeat legal status altogether, leaving not bad law but a failed or defective legal artifact. Format the deliverable as a polished, formal document with clear headings and neutral language throughout.
