I am preparing a fifteen-minute presentation for a regional high-school ethics symposium. My thesis is that, evaluated by a wild-animal-inclusive suffering calculus that weights mortality both by estimated sentience-duration and by caloric yield, beef consumption causes less aggregate wild-animal suffering per nutritional unit than either chicken consumption or plant-based diets, because ruminant grazing avoids the mechanized crop-cultivation mortality—rodents, amphibians, insects—that arable agriculture produces. My audience is a panel of advanced environmental-science students and their faculty advisor: they are committed plant-based advocates, fluent in factory-farming critiques, familiar with the standard crops-also-kill-animals rebuttal, and inclined to see livestock as ecologically destructive while treating industrial monoculture as a separate but parallel problem. They are not agricultural economists, not philosophers of mind, and not activists; they think like ecologists who distrust both feedlots and plowed fields. Draft my Q&A prep sheet: list the six or seven objections they will actually raise, ordered by how severely each damages my calculus, phrased in their vocabulary—trophic inefficiency, system-boundary ambiguity, sentience-weighting arbitrariness, aquatic-exclusion problems—each with a reply I can deliver in roughly two minutes and an explicit note on where that reply is soft. Include the objection I am hoping nobody asks. Throughout, distinguish what is settled in the relevant ecological and agricultural literature from what remains genuinely contested among specialists, and do not present any contested empirical claim—especially regarding crop-mortality rates or cross-species sentience comparisons—as established consensus.
