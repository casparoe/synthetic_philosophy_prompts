You serve as an external adviser to the Director of Contingency Planning for a regional water authority. The director must decide, repeatedly and under time pressure, whether to activate expensive, socially disruptive drought-emergency protocols—mandatory restrictions, agricultural shutdowns, and inter-basin transfers—when hydrological forecasts disagree, historical analogs are absent, and waiting for full convergence means acting too late. The director wants a reusable decision procedure, grounded in the ethics of belief, specifically in the tension between Cliffordian evidentialism—the prohibition against holding a belief without sufficient evidence—and James's framework permitting belief in options that are forced, live, and momentous. The procedure must be something a non-philosopher can run in under thirty minutes, with clear stopping rules.

Write the procedure as an ordered checklist or flowchart in words. It should include: (1) a forced-option filter that determines whether suspension of judgment is genuinely available or illusory given the decision timeline; (2) a live-hypothesis test that distinguishes a scenario the authority can actually treat as real from one that is merely abstract or dead for this institutional context; (3) a calibrated momentousness assessment, with explicit criteria for what makes the stakes high enough to trigger James's permission rather than Clifford's restraint; (4) a graded evidential-threshold scale—not a binary pass/fail—specifying what counts as weak, moderate, and strong convergence across model ensembles, and what role non-model evidence (soil moisture, upstream usage) may play; (5) a concept of epistemic collateral: if the authority activates on less than conclusive evidence and turns out wrong, what verification and accountability obligations are incurred, and how do those obligations feed back into the current decision; (6) a concept of the moral cost of suspension: a structured way to articulate the harm done by withholding belief and delaying action, without treating that harm as automatic license; and (7) explicit flags marking where the procedure stops with a clear "do not activate," where it mandates activation, and where it must hand the decision to human judgment because the question is one of values rather than facts.

Then apply the procedure to three proposals: first, activation based on a single outlier model predicting a forty-percent reservoir decline within ninety days, with all other models predicting stability; second, activation when seventy percent of models agree on severe shortage but none have direct historical analog for this basin, and the evidential gap is filled partly by upstream contractual disputes rather than hydrology; third, a case where model convergence is strong and the option is live, but the momentousness calibration reveals that the economic and social cost of a false alarm exceeds the projected harm of a delayed response, so the procedure yields an answer—do not activate—that conflicts with the director's intuitive belief that precaution demands action. In this third case, explain exactly what the procedure requires the director to do with that conflict.

Finally, state honestly what the procedure cannot settle, which steps are judgment calls dressed up as procedure, and under what conditions it will systematically misfire—for example, if institutional risk aversion shifts over time, or if the definition of "live" is contested among staff. Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citations; the director has no access to external sources and needs only the operational logic.
