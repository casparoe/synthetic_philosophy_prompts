I was recently in a seminar discussing Rousseau's Letter to d'Alembert on the theatre, specifically in relation to his seemingly contradictory praise for public festivals in the Social Contract. Two fellow participants, Elena and Marcus, disagreed sharply about what drives Rousseau's condemnation. Elena argued that the core issue is representational separation: theatre divides citizens into passive spectators and performing actors, substituting the consumption of fictional virtue for active civic participation. By contrast, the public festival is valuable precisely because it operates through co-presence without representation—everyone is simultaneously actor and spectator, so there is no structural alienation. Marcus countered that this structural account is incomplete and that Elena is downplaying the gendered and psychological mechanism that actually does the work. For Rousseau, he claimed, theatre is uniquely dangerous because it activates amour-propre through the mediated gaze, particularly corrupting women and youth by teaching them to value being seen over being virtuous. The public festival escapes condemnation not merely because roles collapse, but because its collective transparency prevents the private, feminised space of appearance from forming; without that affective and gendered layer, Marcus said, the structural distinction cannot explain why Rousseau singles out theatrical spectacle rather than other forms of public viewing, such as civic trials or assemblies.

Elena replied that Marcus was anachronistically overloading an 18th-century rhetorical convention—Rousseau's remarks about women and actors are conventional ornament, not ontological machinery, and the civic argument stands independently. Marcus responded that even if conventional, these remarks are structurally load-bearing: if the problem were purely structural separation, then any assembly with a seated audience should be equally corrupting, yet Rousseau endorses precisely those configurations in other contexts. Elena then offered a counter-distinction based on content rather than gender: theatre depicts fictional moral scenarios that deceive the audience about virtue, whereas civic rituals reinforce actual law; the corruption is representational falsehood, not gendered appearance. Marcus conceded that representational falsehood matters, but insisted it is insufficient—Rousseau's specific anxiety about actors playing women, and about women in theatre boxes seeking admiration, shows that the deception is dangerous precisely because it operates through the gendered dynamics of visibility, not through fiction per se.

They turned to me and asked whether one of them was closer to the truth, whether the co-presence without representation criterion genuinely distinguishes the festival from the theatre, and whether there is something both are missing—perhaps an economic or religious dimension—that would reconcile or undermine their positions. I found myself unable to referee. Please adjudicate their exchange point by point: identify where each is correct, where each overreaches, whether the gendered reading and the civic-structural reading can be integrated rather than treated as rivals, and what, if anything, both parties overlook in Rousseau's broader political ontology. Please reply in British English.
