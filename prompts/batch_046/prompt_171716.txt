To ground my analysis, I want to examine how four distinct formulations of the objection fare when applied to hypothetical collective agents—systems made of cognitively distinct modules that retain separate memory traces but operate within a unified decision architecture that permits internal welfare trade-offs. Please rank these four formulations from 1 (most resilient to internal aggregation) to 4 (least resilient): (A) the distributive-boundary view, which holds that principles of justice apply within a single life trajectory but do not govern comparisons or sacrifices across separate lives; (B) the first-person ownership view, which locates moral separateness in the irreducible fact that all experiences are phenomenologically anchored to a particular subject; (C) the counterfactual-independence view, which requires that the welfare of any one module not be causally or logically contingent upon welfare-reducing sacrifices made by another within the collective; and (D) the substrate-overlap view, which proposes that separateness is specifically undermined when the causal generation of individual welfare states involves shared computational substrates, even if phenomenological boundaries and memory traces remain intact. Provide concise reasoning for each placement, treating the arguments as conceptual structures rather than citing specific scholars, texts, or bibliographies.
