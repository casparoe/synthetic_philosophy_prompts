I was talking with a friend last week about those old split-brain studies—the ones where surgeons cut the corpus callosum to stop severe seizures—and we ended up arguing about whether the patient really ends up with "two minds" in one head. I've come to think the whole framing is wrong. My view is that there is never more than one conscious subject, but in these cases the left hemisphere's verbal system is essentially running with degraded inputs: it cannot access the right hemisphere's perceptual outputs, so it keeps generating its usual autobiographical narrative by confabulating continuity over gaps it does not know exist. The right hemisphere is not a hidden co-pilot with its own beliefs and hidden agenda; it is more like a sophisticated non-linguistic module whose activity gets either ignored or misinterpreted by the storytelling system. So the "unity of consciousness" is not some deep metaphysical glue holding everything together; it is just the left brain's tendency to weave a seamless first-person account out of whatever data happens to reach it. This is why patients do not report feeling split—there is no second reporter competing for authority, only one narrator filling in blanks it never notices are blank.

But I want to be honest about what could actually make me abandon this. What specific observations, experimental results, or conceptual arguments should count against it—ranked from genuinely fatal to merely awkward? I am not just asking for the standard fission objection replayed; I want to know what this view predicts that its rivals do not, and whether it risks being qualified into unfalsifiability—for instance, by treating every failure of behavioral unity as merely a failure of narrative access rather than a failure of consciousness itself. Would a committed defender of this narrative-binding picture concede anything as genuinely damaging, or has the view been insulated so thoroughly that nothing can touch it? And are there results in other domains—say, about integrated information in other kinds of brain injury, or about personal identity in severe amnesia—that either support or undermine this idea that unity is an achievement of interpretation rather than a baseline metaphysical fact? I would like an honest map of where this position is actually exposed, including whether its insulation, if present, is a problem for a view of this kind.
