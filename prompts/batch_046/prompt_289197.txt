I have been considering the Buridan's ass paradox in relation to contemporary debates about rational agency, and I find myself caught on a point that may be confused. The scenario is usually taken to show that if an agent is perfectly indifferent between two identical options, then rationality offers no guidance, leaving the agent suspended indefinitely. From this, it is often inferred that any resolution—whether by arbitrary selection, randomisation, or direct volition—must introduce a non-rational element into decision-making. But I wonder whether this inference conflates two distinct claims: that there is no comparative reason favouring one option over the other, and that there is therefore no reason whatsoever to act. If an agent can be moved by a non-comparative motive—say, a direct exercise of practical reason that does not depend on ranking—does the paradox still bite, or does it dissolve once we separate the absence of preference from the absence of agency? More specifically, is the real lesson of Buridan's ass that rational choice theory must smuggle in an arbitrary tie-breaker, or rather that the paradox rests on a false presupposition about what rational agency requires?
