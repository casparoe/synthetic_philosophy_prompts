I've been thinking on my own about the Anthropocene not as a scientific label but as a kind of historical verdict we're delivering about ourselves in real time. Most discussions I encounter focus on whether the boundary should be marked by the Orbis spike, the Great Acceleration, or some earlier agricultural transformation, and I understand those debates involve stratigraphic evidence and institutional votes. But the part that actually unsettles me is conceptual: we are trying to name an epoch while we are still living inside it, which means the decision about when it started also functions like a retroactive assignment of responsibility. If the boundary is placed in the mid-twentieth century, then the present generation inherits a named era of damage; if it is placed in the early modern period, then the problem becomes diffuse across centuries. I want to understand which conceptual questions in this space actually matter for how we govern climate and assign intergenerational obligations, rather than which ones merely refine geological taxonomy.

To make this concrete, I've been working with an idea that may be misguided but feels important: the question "who decides?" is usually treated as a matter of institutional authority, but it might really be a question about epistemic standing. Do those who suffer the effects have authority to name the era? Do those who can read the stratigraphic record? Or do future generations, who will live entirely within whatever name we choose, have a kind of veto? Alongside this, I'm interested in whether selecting a start date implicitly creates a moral "before" and "after" that shapes reparations, liability, and long-term planning in ways that geologists never intended. So if a small interdisciplinary group—philosophers, historians, and people who design policy—had about three years of focused effort, which open conceptual lines should they pursue? I am not asking for a survey of the literature, a bibliography, or a reading list. Instead, I want a defended ordering: which problems are upstream of others, which are tractable without waiting for new geological data, and what kind of argument or institutional development over the next few years would cause you to reorder them?
