Everyone in my philosophy-of-medicine circles keeps comparing Plato's symposium regulations in the Laws to modern supervised-consumption or harm-reduction sites: you do not prohibit the intoxicant, but engineer the setting—age gates, a sober symposiarch, regulated music and conversation—so that alcohol functions as a diagnostic stress-test for virtue rather than a source of harm. The analogy is used to argue that Plato was essentially a proto-harm-reductionist, closer to a public-health liberal than to a prohibitionist. There is, however, a less-noticed feature: the symposiarch is not merely reducing risk but running what amounts to a civic character-assay, treating wine as a chemical reagent that reveals whether a citizen's reason holds under perturbation, with failure carrying political penalties rather than medical ones. How far does the harm-reduction analogy hold? Give me the correspondence table—what plays the role of the site, the staff, the mortality metric, the reagent, the assay readout, the age gate—then separate the disanalogies that matter for the liberal conclusion from those that do not, say whether Plato's position survives translation into harm-reduction terms or whether the analogy was carrying the normative conclusion all along, and, if the frame collapses, propose a more accurate analogy for the symposiarch's institutional role.
