I am writing from the perspective of a student in an upper-level seminar on moral revolutions and norm collapse. Our professor has offered explicit verdicts on the primary driver behind four historical cases: she says aristocratic dueling in nineteenth-century Europe ended mainly through a transformation in honor codes, so that what once signaled gentlemanly courage came to signal vanity and poor taste; she says Atlantic chattel slavery collapsed primarily through structural economic and geopolitical interests, with moral rhetoric following rather than leading the shift; she says the abandonment of foot-binding in early-twentieth-century China was chiefly honor-driven, because the practice became a source of national shame that undermined elite prestige rather than reinforcing it; and she says the British nineteenth-century movement to abolish public execution spectacles succeeded mainly through explicit moral argument and the diffusion of humanitarian sentiment, with neither status competition nor economic restructuring playing a decisive role.

These four judgments seem jointly consistent with more than one overarching framework. One possibility is a domain-sensitive taxonomy in which she assigns honor dynamics to elite-status rituals, material interests to large-scale extraction and labor systems, and rational argument to practices involving visible cruelty and public spectacle. Another is the unified Appiah thesis that honor is always the root mechanism, with economic and argumentative changes serving as downstream expressions of prior status reconfigurations. A third is the Williams thesis that structural interests are always primary, treating appeals to honor or morality as ideological rationalizations that mask underlying material shifts.

To test which framework she is actually running, consider a case we have not discussed: between roughly 1970 and 1990, the near-universal expectation that junior faculty at American research universities wear formal dark suits and ties while teaching undergraduate lectures collapsed within about a decade. The change occurred without any prominent public moral campaign against formal dress, without an obvious economic restructuring of academia, and without a clear national or professional honor crisis comparable to the examples above. Some traditionally conservative departments retained the expectation slightly longer, but by 1990 it was effectively dead even there.

If she applies her framework consistently to this collapse, what should she identify as the primary driver, and which of her four earlier verdicts serves as the closest analog? I am less interested in the bare prediction than in the reasoning: please lay out the competing rules I have sketched, explain what principle she would be adopting if she extends the analog in one direction versus another, and specify what further case—ideally one that isolates honor dynamics from both economic restructuring and explicit moral argument—would best distinguish whether she is running the domain-taxonomy, the unified honor thesis, or the Williams interest-based view. Please answer in American English, and do not provide bibliographies, reading lists, or citations; I need an analysis that relies solely on conceptual reasoning about the precedents I have given.
