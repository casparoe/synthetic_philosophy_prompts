I was discussin Hofstadter's Le Ton beau de Marot with two mates from my translation course and we ended up in a loop about what 'le ton beau' actually commits you to. Sam said if you're translating Marot's little poem Ma mignonne you have to preserve the exact rhyme scheme and syllable count in English, even if the result sounds clunky, because the formal constraint is part of the artistic achievement — Marot suffered for his rhymes, so the translator should suffer too. Riley said that's just fetishising the container: in English, forced rhyme often sounds like a nursery rhyme, so preserving the 'beautiful tone' really means finding an English equivalent — maybe alliteration or stress pattern — that gives an anglophone reader the same emotional hit, not copying the same phonetic architechture.

Then Sam threw in a weird edge case: what if you're translating the poem into British Sign Language, where there's no sound but you can repeat handshapes for a kind of visual rhyme? He said if 'le ton beau' is about formal suffering, then you should reproduce the handshape repetition exactly, even if it makes the signing awkward. Riley said that's daft — it proves form is modality-specific, so forcing English rhyme is just as arbitrary as forcing BSL rhyme; the point is to preserve the poem's consoling function, not its structure. Sam came back that Marot's constraint was linguistic, not modal, so abandoning rhyme in English is just laziness dressed up as cultural sensitivity.
