Following the death of my father after a long decline, I have spent the past months in a state that feels less like grief and more like a sudden clarity: the ambitions, attachments, and intellectual commitments that once structured my days now appear as elaborate machinery for producing and then briefly relieving craving. From this personal vantage, I want you to construct a rigorous argument for tranquilism—the view that only the absence of craving and suffering carries intrinsic value, and that every other apparent good (knowledge, love, creative achievement, moral virtue) is valuable solely as an instrument for reducing or preventing those negative states. Defend the stronger thesis that a hypothetical being in permanent stasis—experiencing no desire, no pain, no action, and no relational attachment—occupies the highest possible axiological state, and that active, flourishing lives are therefore always second-best, compromised by the very cravings that make them dynamic. To do this, introduce and defend an unconventional distinction between negative tranquility (the sheer absence of perturbation) and positive flourishing (the exercise of capacities), arguing that only the former can serve as a non-derivative good. Then address the objection that such stasis is empty or meaningless by reconceiving meaning itself as a disguised form of craving—specifically, a craving for narrative coherence or significance. Keep the reasoning self-contained; do not invoke named philosophers, texts, or citation lists, since I am working without access to a library and need the argument built from first principles.
