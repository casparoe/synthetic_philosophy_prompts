As a retired physicist who has only recently begun studying philosophy and the history of science in earnest, I have been trying to grasp Fernand Braudel’s conception of the longue durée. At an interdisciplinary seminar last month, a historian colleague summarized Braudel’s position for me and then applied it to our present climate crisis. According to my colleague, Braudel divides historical time into three nested rhythms—geographical time, social time, and the time of events and individuals—with climate and physical geography forming an almost immobile foundation that strictly determines the range of possible social structures. Those structures, in turn, fully determine individual actions, leaving revolutions, political decisions, and the lives of prominent figures with no independent causal weight; they are merely froth on deeper currents. My colleague then argued that anthropogenic climate change has collapsed Braudel’s temporal hierarchy, because ocean circulation and ice-sheet dynamics are now shifting on decadal scales comparable to electoral cycles. In the Anthropocene, he claimed, the formerly immobile geographical layer has become volatile and is now the sole engine of history, rendering institutional reform and political agency essentially epiphenomenal. He concluded that historians should therefore abandon the study of events and individuals altogether and reorient the discipline entirely around Earth-system dynamics. I suspect this captures Braudel’s structural emphasis but distorts it in several places, particularly by turning constraint into determination and by drawing a normative conclusion that does not obviously follow. Could you audit this summary carefully? Specifically, I would like to know: which elements accurately reflect Braudel’s tripartite schema and his account of how deep structures relate to surface events; where does the summary conflate structural constraint with causal determination or overstate the impotence of individual agency; is the claim that climate change collapses the distinction between geographical and social time a legitimate extension of Braudel’s framework or a category mistake; and does the recommendation to abandon event-history actually follow from the premises? Please reach a definite verdict on whether the summary is fair to Braudel overall, and identify the precise points at which it misrepresents or overreaches.
