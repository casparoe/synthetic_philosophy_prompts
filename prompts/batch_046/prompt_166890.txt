I'm a journalist who writes on science and ideas, and I've been invited to deliver the opening remarks—about ten minutes—at an interdisciplinary workshop co-hosted by a philosophy of science group and a clinical data-ethics center. The room is split almost evenly: one half will be decision theorists and philosophers of science who know Good's result on the value of information intimately, and the other half will be clinicians, donors, and fellow reporters who generally assume that more data is always beneficial. I want the address to use that result as a genuine structural constraint rather than decorative citation—specifically, because the theorem holds that information carries non-negative expected value only when the recipient retains free choice over the subsequent action, the speech must avoid prescribing any uniform institutional response. The remarks need to land for both audiences, letting experts see the formal logic without leaving non-specialists behind in technicality. Please draft the speech, and append to it a brief speaker's note that clearly separates the settled core of Good's result from what is genuinely contested in applying it to collective clinical rules and in invoking the institutional-compulsion extension; do not present the contested parts as the consensus reading among decision theorists.
