I was hanging out with my friends Malik and Juno after our poli-theory seminar and they got stuck in this argument about the boundary problem — like, who actually belongs to the demos when decisions spill over borders. We were talking about this hypothetical floating city-state I invented for a paper, Meridian, which runs its own democracy but pulls all its drinking water from a river that runs through three other countries. So Meridian's citizens vote on rationing and pollution rules, but people living downstream in those other countries get hit hard by droughts or chemical runoff, even though they don't live under Meridian's laws. Malik said that's exactly why the 'all affected interests' principle should apply: if you're significantly impacted, you should have a say, maybe with votes weighted by how badly you're affected. Juno said no, democratic legitimacy comes from being subject to coercive authority, not from suffering side-effects, so expanding the demos to outsiders violates the self-determination of the actual citizens.

Then Malik tried to defend his view by analogy to stakeholder democracy in corporate governence — he said if companies now give board seats to affected communities and not just shareholders, then states should do the same, especially functional ones like Meridian that operate like firms. Juno shot back that states aren't corporations, and mixing the models just creates a governance hybrid with no clear line of accountability; she also said weighting votes by impact is impossible to measure without some paternalistic bureaucracy deciding whose suffering counts. I threw in that maybe they're both ignoring the temporal boundary — future generations who recieve the depleted river — but Malik said they could be represented by proxies, and Juno said that's just fictional representation that doesn't solve the legitimacy problem. They asked me to referee and I honestly couldn't. Can you go point by point and say who's right about what? And tell me if there's a third option they're missing about how authority works when it's functional instead of territorial?
