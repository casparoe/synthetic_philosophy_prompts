After accompanying my grandmother to an exhibition on twentieth-century state violence last month, I have found myself grading this final exam with unusual care. The question was: "Some philosophers maintain that ranking historical atrocities—whether by death toll, duration of suffering, or intensity—is either epistemically impossible because such events are morally incommensurable, or morally obscene because it reduces unique horrors to quantitative data points. Others defend limited comparative judgment for particular purposes. Evaluate this debate by addressing: (1) whether aggregate harm metrics can adequately register morally relevant structural differences between evils, specifically bureaucratic diffusion of responsibility versus ideologically targeted intentionality; (2) whether an absolute refusal to compare protects the dignity of victims or instead risks flattening important moral distinctions; and (3) whether a stable boundary can be drawn between comparing atrocities to understand historical causation and comparing them to assign moral blame."

Below is one student's answer. I am grading as a TA and should not signal my own view on the underlying debate. Please assign a specific grade or point range, then provide point-by-point comments that justify it: indicate where the reasoning holds, where it slips, and distinguish the visible strengths from the more subtle errors. Treat this as an assessment of the argument's structure rather than a general impression.

The student wrote:

"Comparing atrocities is not automatically obscene, though it requires caution. First, aggregate metrics such as death tolls do capture real differences in scale; if one event kills millions and another kills thousands, the magnitude of harm is genuinely different, and denying this seems false. However, metrics fail to register structural moral differences. Bureaucratic evil, in which harm emerges from distributed administrative decisions, differs in kind from ideologically targeted evil, in which perpetrators intend the destruction of a specific group. Because these involve different moral qualities—diffuse responsibility versus direct intentionality—their overall moral weights are incommensurable, analogous to comparing length with temperature. Thus, while scalar comparison within a single dimension is permissible, ranking events that differ in structure is conceptually impossible.

Second, the obscenity objection mistakes comparison for competitive minimization. To claim that one atrocity was larger in scale than another is not to diminish the smaller one; it is merely to make an ordinal judgment about magnitude. A complete refusal to compare would therefore protect ignorance rather than dignity, since it prevents us from saying that some regimes caused greater harm than others.

Third, historical explanation and moral blame operate in entirely separate domains. Causal comparison asks about economic conditions, institutional incentives, and bureaucratic mechanisms, whereas moral comparison asks about culpability. Because these domains share no inferential pathways, comparative judgments made for explanatory purposes must be quarantined from moral evaluation; knowing that a famine was structurally caused by policy tells us nothing about individual blame, and assessing blame offers no purchase on historical causation. Consequently, comparison should be permitted for causal history but avoided altogether for moral accounting, since blame requires agent-relative evaluation that quantitative metrics cannot supply.

In sum, limited scalar comparison is valid within uniform structures, but overall moral ranking is impossible due to dimensional incommensurability, and the two evaluative practices must remain isolated."
