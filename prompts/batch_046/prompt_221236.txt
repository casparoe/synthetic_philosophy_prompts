I am preparing notes for a seminar essay on the metaphysics of personal identity, and I would like your critical comments before I develop them further. The working title is "No Corporate Soul: Anatta and the Distribution of Karmic Accountability," and the argument proceeds as follows.

The Western debate about personal identity—whether psychological continuity, bodily continuity, or some hybrid relation is necessary and sufficient for persistence—presupposes that there must be some substrate or unifying relation that grounds both prudential concern and moral responsibility over time. The Buddhist doctrine of anatta, particularly as articulated in the Abhidhamma and Madhyamaka traditions, rejects this presupposition entirely: the five aggregates (form, feeling, perception, mental formations, consciousness) are impermanent, dependently originated, and devoid of any enduring self. There is no soul, no ego-substance, no hidden owner behind experience.

Yet Buddhist ethics retains a robust framework of karma and moral consequence. This generates a puzzle that Western identity theories rarely face: if there is literally no enduring subject, in what precise sense is a present being responsible for the actions of a prior causal continuum? The standard answer is that karma operates mechanistically—like a seed producing fruit—without requiring personal ownership. But I want to push a more specific and, I think, underexplored comparison. In modern legal philosophy, corporate personhood allows us to hold a corporation liable despite its lacking consciousness, bodily continuity, or a unified mental life. Corporate responsibility is distributed across roles, contracts, and institutional structures rather than anchored in an individual soul.

My essay proposes that anatta demands something more radical than corporate personhood, not less. Corporate law at least relies on a legal fiction of continuity and a designated agent-structure. Buddhism denies even that fiction for the individual: there is no "corporation" of the self, only a stream of causally linked moments. Therefore, karmic accountability should be understood not as personal responsibility analogized to corporate liability, but as distributed causal tracking—a normative system that assigns consequences to processes without ever attributing them to an agent-substance.

The difficulty is that this threatens to collapse Buddhist ethics into a kind of causal physics, eliminating the very notion of moral agency. But Buddhist practice—vinaya discipline, the cultivation of cetana (intention), and the path of purification—clearly presupposes an agent capable of reform and deliberate choice. So there is a deep tension: the metaphysics denies the self, while the soteriology and ethics seem to require a functional agent who can be held accountable and who can undertake transformation.

I am particularly interested in three points of feedback. First, does the comparison to corporate personhood illuminate anatta, or does it illegitimately import Western legal categories into a framework that rejects the very notion of institutional continuity? Second, is my characterization of karmic accountability as distributed causal tracking accurate to the tradition, or does it miss the way Buddhist texts actually preserve agency through the concept of cetana? Third, is the tension I identify between metaphysical denial and ethical presupposition genuine, or is it dissolved once we properly understand the conventional (sammuti) versus ultimate (paramattha) distinction?

Please lead with your bottom line in the first sentence and then provide the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of the question.
