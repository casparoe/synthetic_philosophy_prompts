Write a deadpan parody of an internal corporate governance memo—approximately 600 words, formatted with standard memo headers (TO, FROM, RE, DATE) and numbered procedural notes—documenting the quarterly review of a boutique architecture firm’s “Premise-Conclusion Alignment Protocol.” The firm must approve or reject a proposed pedestrian bridge, and under its charter the board votes separately on three premises: whether the load-bearing calculation exceeds the statutory safety threshold, whether the materials budget permits reinforced steel, and whether the design complies with wind-shear regulations; the final verdict on the bridge itself is then mechanically derived from the aggregated premises rather than put to a direct vote. In this session, each premise commands a clear majority in favor, yet the derived conclusion—approval—is rejected by a different majority, producing what the memo calmly classifies as a “Category-II Alignment Event.” Your satire should treat this discursive dilemma not as a logical paradox but as a routine operational friction, describing the firm’s managerial “resolution procedures”: strategic reassignment of dissenting board members to “perspective calibration,” the arbitrary selection of which premises are eligible for aggregation in future quarters, and a euphemistic appendix titled “Frequently Misunderstood Outcomes” that explains why holistic objections to the bridge are inadmissible under the protocol’s rules. The target is judgment aggregation in democratic or corporate bodies—specifically the tension between premise-based majoritarianism and conclusion-based majoritarianism—without ever naming it as such. Land the standard objections—strategic voting on premises, the suppression of integrated judgment, the hidden politics of framing which issues become premises—through bureaucratic language alone, so that a specialist recognizes the mirror of actual discursive-democracy proposals but a general reader simply encounters an absurdly self-assured administrative document. Keep the irony sustained and deadpan: no winking, no explicit admission that the system is broken, and no reference to philosophers, texts, or bibliographies. Do not include citations or ask for reading lists; the humor should come entirely from the internal consistency of the bureaucratic voice confronting a well-known aggregation paradox.
