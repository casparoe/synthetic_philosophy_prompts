I am assessing responses to the final examination in my graduate seminar on Classical Confucian Moral Psychology. The question required students to analyze the disagreement between Mencius and Xunzi regarding human nature (xing) through a specific conceptual framework: the distinction between "genetic moral potential"—innate capacities that render moral development possible, irrespective of directional aim—and "teleological moral orientation"—an innate directedness of the psyche specifically toward moral rather than non-moral ends. Students were asked (a) whether Mencius's doctrine of the four sprouts (si duan) commits him to both, to one, or to neither; (b) whether Xunzi's claim that human nature is evil (xing e) denies both, denies one, or reconceives them; and (c) whether the application of this distinction shows their disagreement to be substantive or collapsible into a divergence about the efficacy of ritual (li) and learning (xue).

Below is one student's complete answer. Please assign a letter grade and provide specific, point-by-point comments that justify it. Do not offer a general impression; address particular interpretive claims, inferential steps, and conceptual distinctions. You should not signal your own philosophical agreement with the student, only the accuracy, coherence, and precision of the response.

Student answer:

Mencius's doctrine of the four sprouts—compassion, shame, deference, and the sense of right and wrong—demonstrates that he accepts both genetic moral potential and teleological moral orientation. These sprouts are not merely neutral capacities that could develop in any direction; they are innate seeds that already contain the specific form of the virtues they will become. In particular, the sprout of right and wrong operates from birth as an innate moral compass, directing judgment toward righteousness rather than functioning as an undeveloped tendency that requires extensive cultivation to become active. Because all four sprouts are oriented specifically toward benevolence, righteousness, propriety, and wisdom, Mencius clearly holds that human nature is directed toward moral ends.

Xunzi, by contrast, denies teleological moral orientation. He argues that human nature is evil because people naturally seek profit, feel envy, and desire sensory satisfaction. This does not mean, however, that he denies all genetic potential. He concedes that human beings have innate appetites and emotional reactions, but he denies that these constitute capacities for morality. More importantly, because he denies any innate moral direction, he must treat the psyche as entirely passive—receiving ritual form as wax receives a seal—without any innate cognitive architecture for pattern-recognition, habituation, or learning. Ritual and righteousness therefore come wholly from the sages outside, and human nature contributes only the raw material of desire that makes transformation necessary.

Applying the distinction reveals that their disagreement is substantive but not total. Both acknowledge that there is innate material to be shaped; they disagree only about whether that material includes a predisposition to recognize moral patterns. Mencius says yes, through the four sprouts; Xunzi says no, leaving ritual to supply both form and receptive capacity. Consequently, the debate is not about whether education is necessary—both agree it is—but about whether education extends an internal moral direction or imposes form on an entirely inert substrate.
