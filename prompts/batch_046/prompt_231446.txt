I am a senior associate at a litigation firm governed by an unwritten rule: regardless of actual workload, no associate departs before the partner to whom they report, and leaving before ten in the evening is quietly interpreted as a deficit of vocation rather than a scheduling choice. These late hours shape promotion and partnership prospects not through formal evaluation but through a shared understanding of who possesses the requisite character. Last quarter the firm adopted an automated document-review system that eliminated the substantive need for overnight work, yet the partners have not relaxed the expectation; instead they now defend the remaining unnecessary hours as irreplaceable "character formation" that algorithms cannot provide. I recently refused to stay past midnight to proofread non-urgent materials, explaining to my partner that the practice produces documented harm and serves no client interest. He replied that the norm is not instrumental but constitutive: enduring shared hardship is how the group distinguishes genuine commitment from mere careerism, and its value lies precisely in being costly and apparently pointless from an outside perspective. Several junior associates have told me privately that they share my view but fear that any visible break with the custom will permanently damage their standing. I am weighing whether to organize a collective refusal, present empirical data on productivity and mental health to the partnership, or attempt to reframe early departure as itself a mark of elite efficiency and self-command. I sense that the partners are not simply ignorant of the costs—everyone recognizes the performative nature of the hours—but that the norm fulfills a social function that direct argument about welfare or fairness does not reach. Is this best understood as a system sustained by economic interests, by a shared conception of honor and belonging, or by a coordination problem in which no individual can deviate without confirming their own unsuitability? And if the latter two explanations hold, what kind of intervention—rational persuasion, structural incentive change, or symbolic redefinition—would actually dissolve the norm rather than merely drive its observance underground?
