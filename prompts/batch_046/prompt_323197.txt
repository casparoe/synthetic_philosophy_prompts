Modern democracies run this strange bundle: citizens vote, but only for representatives, who then pass laws that judges can strike down using a constitution nobody voted for line-by-line. We call that "rule by the people," yet if you propose direct voting on every law people call it mob rule and reject it. Meanwhile we exlude minors, non-citizens, and sometimes people with felony convictions, but we justify each exlusion differently — capacity, membership, dependency, whatever. So what philosophical theory of democratic authority actually makes that package coherent?

I'm especially curious about it through Spinoza's lens. In his political writings he treats democracy not mainly as a voting procedure but as the form of government where the state's right is literally identical to the collective power of the citizens, not alienated away to a sovereign. He also apparently thinks you should leave out people who live under another's power — dependents, not because they lack reason, but because adding them doesn't increase the actual collective power of free citizens.

So reconstruct the implicit theory behind our institutions. Do they presuppose that sovereignty can be transferred and represented, like in contract theories? Or are they compatible with Spinoza's view that democratic power is just the aggregate power of the citizens? Where is the practice philosophically underdetermined — could the same bundle fit both? And what would a democratic system actually built on Spinoza's commitments have to change? Would it drop representitive delegation, judicial review, or the idea that rights sit outside the democratic will?

Also check consistency: do the different exclusion rules (kids vs immigrants vs felons) all rely on the same idea of who counts as part of the democratic power, or do they quietly depend on incompatible theories?
