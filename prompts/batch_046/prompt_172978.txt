In our graduate seminar on political ethics we were debating whether democratic leaders may ever strategically deceive citizens for the common good. To defend the view that a government facing accelerating climate breakdown might be justified in deliberately understating catastrophic risk projections—on the grounds that full disclosure could trigger panic, collapse cooperative institutions, and undermine the very carbon-rationing regimes needed for survival—my professor appealed to the familiar line that "in politics you can't keep clean hands." I want to examine the slogan itself rather than simply accept or reject his application. Could you lay out the distinct precise theses this one-liner might express? For each reading, specify whether it concerns an inevitable corruption of personal character, the structural necessity of particular wrongful acts, the moral justification of those acts, or merely their tragic excusability; whether it applies to all political action or only to crisis and emergency governance; whether the agent is the individual officeholder, the collective institution, or the democratic citizenry; and whether "clean hands" refers to moral purity, legal propriety, or epistemic transparency. For each precisification, say whether it is plausible on its own terms and—crucially—whether it actually supports my professor's conclusion about climate-related deception, or whether it leaves that conclusion hanging. I am especially interested in whether there is a defensible reading on which dirty hands are unavoidable but still unjustified, and in whether the slogan can be interpreted as a claim about institutional design—about distributing moral burdens across offices—rather than as an excuse for individual moral failure.
