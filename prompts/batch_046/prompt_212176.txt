You are a professional moral philosopher specializing in the ethics of blame, hypocrisy, and moral standing. Please think carefully and step by step. Before offering your final analysis, briefly brainstorm at least two competing principles that could organize the judgments below, and note where they conflict.

Here are my reflective verdicts about who has standing to blame in cases involving parallel or potential moral failure.

Case 1: Elena recovered from opioid addiction years ago, now runs a harm-reduction clinic, and blames Marcus—a current user—for neglecting his children because of his addiction. Verdict: Elena clearly retains standing.

Case 2: Fiona has never used opioids and has no history of substance abuse, but clinical assessment shows she carries the exact psychological and genetic risk profile Elena had before her addiction developed. Fiona blames Marcus for the same neglect. Verdict: Fiona also has standing; an unactualized disposition does not undermine her.

Case 3: George systematically hides income through offshore schemes. He blames his neighbor for failing to declare a $200 cash gift. Verdict: George lacks standing; his own hidden failure is directly parallel and far graver.

Case 4: Hannah lives rurally with no transit, drives an old high-emission vehicle out of economic necessity, and blames a wealthy executive for frequent unnecessary private-jet travel that emits orders of magnitude more carbon. Verdict: Hannah has standing, despite both contributing to climate harm.

Case 5: Hannah, in the same circumstances, blames her equally poor rural neighbor—who also drives an old car out of necessity—for not biking twenty miles to work despite a chronic knee condition that makes cycling impossible. Verdict: Hannah lacks standing here.

The tension I keep returning to is between Case 2, Case 4, and Case 5: unactualized risk does not defeat standing, and large differences in scale or avoidability preserve it, yet identical unavoidable contribution seems to destroy it.

My questions: Is there a single precise principle—specifying conditions on actual failure, proportionality, and moral similarity—that captures all five verdicts? If not, which verdict should be overturned and why? What does the best principle predict for the following unlisted case: a researcher convicted of minor plagiarism in college thirty years ago, with an impeccable record since, now blames a junior colleague for a single sloppy citation well below plagiarism? Please state the principle precisely, map each clause to the case that motivates it, and be explicit about which of my verdicts it must reject.
