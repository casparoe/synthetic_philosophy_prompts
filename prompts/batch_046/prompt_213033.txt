Write the final exam essay question, model answer, and marking scheme for an upper-level distributive-justice seminar (advanced undergraduate or graduate). The topic is prioritarianism and the leveling-down objection, but the question must not be answerable by reciting the standard distinction between equality and priority. Instead, build in a specific conceptual twist: a discrete three-band weighting scheme (severe deprivation / moderate well-being / high flourishing) with constant intra-band weights and sharp discontinuities at the boundaries, applied to a redistribution case in which the option that maximizes weighted welfare also pulls members of the high-flourishing band downward into the moderate band while only partially lifting the severely deprived.

Deliver the response in three named sections, in this order:

1. Exam Question (about 250 words). Present a self-contained scenario with exact numbers: a fixed population of 90 divided initially into 30 at welfare level 2 (severe), 30 at level 9 (moderate), and 30 at level 17 (high). Define Policy A, which raises the severe group to level 6, leaves the moderate group at 9, and lowers the high group to 13. Define Policy B, which raises the severe group to level 9, leaves the moderate group at 9, and lowers the high group to 9. Then specify the discrete weighting rules: within severe (levels 1–5) each welfare unit gained or lost carries moral weight 4; within moderate (levels 6–12) weight 2; within high (levels 13–20) weight 1; and state that crossing a boundary changes which weight applies but does not generate any special transition value. Ask students to: (a) evaluate both policies under standard continuous prioritarian reasoning; (b) evaluate both under the discrete-band scheme, showing the arithmetic; (c) identify whether either evaluation is vulnerable to a leveling-down-style objection, and if so whether it is the same objection that targets telic egalitarianism or a structurally different variant tied to the boundary discontinuity; (d) defend which evaluation is correct, with explicit attention to whether avoiding downward boundary-crossing gives the high-flourishing group a protected status that prioritarianism should reject.

2. Model Answer (about 800 words). Begin with a concise clarification of why continuous prioritarianism avoids the classic leveling-down objection—because it cares about absolute weighted welfare, not equality as such—then apply both schemes to Policies A and B with explicit calculation. Show that under continuous concave weighting Policy A likely wins, while under the discrete scheme the arithmetic may shift depending on how boundary crossings are counted. Address the discontinuity problem: does the fact that Policy B pulls 30 people across the high-to-moderate boundary (thereby changing the weight applied to further changes) create a moral reason to prefer A, or is that reason spurious? Distinguish the classic objection (equality is intrinsically good, so leveling down is good even if nobody benefits) from any boundary-specific variant (e.g., that downward crossing destroys a category of moral status). Conclude with a defended position that addresses both the arithmetic and the conceptual distinction.

3. Marking Scheme (about 350 words). List the specific moves required for an A-grade answer: correct arithmetic for both policies under both schemes; explicit statement that continuous prioritarianism weights by absolute position, not by equality; identification of the boundary-crossing feature in Policy B; clear distinction between the classic leveling-down objection and any variant generated by the discrete bands; and a defended conclusion that does not collapse prioritarianism into egalitarianism. List specific mistakes that should cost marks: treating prioritarianism as concerned with reducing inequality per se; ignoring the discontinuity; conflating the two objections; failing to apply the scheme to both policies; or asserting without argument that boundary-crossing is intrinsically bad. Indicate what separates an A from a B (e.g., B answers apply one scheme correctly but miss the boundary-specific variant; A answers recognize that the discrete scheme introduces a novel structural worry without conceding that prioritarianism is therefore committed to protecting the well-off).

Constraints: Do not include any bibliography, reading list, or citation request. Do not quote verbatim from any published text; paraphrase without quotation marks if needed. The scenario and weighting rules must be fully specified in the question so that a student with standard familiarity with prioritarianism but no access to external sources can complete the task.
