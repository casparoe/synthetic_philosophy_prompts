I've been re-reading The Name of the Rose for a philosophy-of-science seminar im running, and I want to push past the usual "William is the rational hero" take. I'm trying to map the monastery onto Lakatos's idea of competing research programmes: the Benedictine hard core is the apocalyptic reading of the seven trumpets, with a protective belt that explains away every death as heresy, poison, or divine punishment. William's empirical programme has a hard core of natural causation, but he keeps patching his predictions—secret passages, mirror tricks, poison on the pages—to save his model of the killer's path. What's weird is that his programme looks degenerating: he predicts wrong, adds auxiliaries, and still fails to stop the deaths. Meanwhile the apocalyptic "programme" seems to generate correct predictions about the sequence of killings and the final fire. So can we read Eco as dramatizing that rationalism can enter a degenerating problem shift while a mystical framework makes progressive predictions? I dont want a simple allegory; I want to know where the analogy breaks. For instance, is William's negative heuristic actually directing him away from false positives, or is it just a belt of excuses?

I'd point my students to the poison debate in the scriptorium, William's failed prediction about the killer's next move, and the final conflagration where the "irrational" prophecy comes true. What would a careful Lakatosian say about whether William's programme is genuinely degenerating, and does the book resist being read as philosophy of science at all? I'm especially curious about the second book of the Poetics—when William thinks finding it will resolve the anomaly, but it actually accelerates the disaster. Is that a failed novel prediction, or a comment on what counts as a successful research shift? And does the novel end up saying something about the cost of being right that Lakatos's papers dont really address?
