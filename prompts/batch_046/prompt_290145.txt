I'm designing a graduate seminar handout on the ethics of military intervention, using Kosovo (1999), Iraq (2003), and Libya (2011) as reference cases. Please construct a decision tree that classifies normative positions on whether these interventions were justified, built from the logical space of commitments rather than from named theories. Each branch should be a concrete diagnostic question that a student can answer without already knowing which view it selects.

First split: Is state sovereignty violable only to halt an ongoing genocide or mass atrocity, or also to prevent anticipated ones?

Second split: Is multilateral authorization—specifically a UN Security Council mandate—a necessary condition for legitimacy, or merely a strong prudential desideratum?

Third split: Is regime change permissible solely as an unavoidable side-effect of civilian protection, or also as an independent war aim?

Fourth split: Does a successful intervention generate a continuing trusteeship or rebuilding obligation that survives the regime's fall, such that failure to reconstruct retroactively undermines the justification for entry?

The tree should include leaves for: strict Westphalian non-interventionism; UN-authorized protection without regime change; unauthorized multilateral protection (the Kosovo pattern); unauthorized regime change coupled with sustained rebuilding obligations (the Iraq pattern); and authorized regime change with minimal post-conflict obligations (the Libya pattern). Identify any logically possible combinations that remain unoccupied—particularly a view that permits unauthorized regime change yet rejects all post-intervention rebuilding duties—and briefly diagnose whether that gap reflects incoherence, unattractiveness, or mere neglect. Finally, flag any pair of leaves that your tree genuinely cannot separate.
