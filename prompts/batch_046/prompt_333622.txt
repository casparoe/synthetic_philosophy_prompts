As a journalist writing on constitutional design, I keep running into a culinary metaphor for federal architecture: American federalism is described as a layer cake, with sovereign tiers neatly stacked, while the European Union and post-war Germany are described as marble cakes, in which competences are so intermingled that separation is impossible. The analogy is routinely used to argue that the EU's principle of subsidiarity—decision-making at the lowest effective level—is essentially cooperative federalism in disguise, and that once economic interdependence reaches a certain density, centralization becomes structurally inevitable. Before I treat that as settled, I want to audit the comparison rigorously. Could you provide an explicit mapping—what corresponds to the baker, the ingredients, the blade, and the diner—then isolate the institutional disanalogies that actually matter? Specifically, does the metaphor quietly import the idea that mixing is chemically irreversible, or that separation requires homogeneous raw materials? I want to stress-test the analogy against two features that rarely appear in popular accounts: the fiscal autonomy embedded in Swiss cantonal direct democracy, and the EU's unresolved Kompetenz-Kompetenz ambiguity regarding who ultimately holds the authority to allocate authority. Once those are on the table, does the marble-cake framing still support the conclusion that subsidiarity collapses into centralization, or was the analogy smuggling that inevitability in through its imagery? If the dessert comparison fails, is there a more precise institutional analogy—perhaps drawn from distributed computing or corporate holding structures—that captures delegation without importing false culinary physics?
