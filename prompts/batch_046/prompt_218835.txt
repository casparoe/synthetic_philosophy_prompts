I am composing a narrative drawn from my family’s archives about a single evening in 1978 for which three credible witnesses offer incompatible accounts: one describes a reconciliation, another an irreparable rupture, and a third implies both occurred without specifying sequence. I must decide whether to impose one authoritative chronology, present the versions as permanently irreconcilable, or construct a synthetic account that resolves the tension for the reader’s sake. My concern is not merely aesthetic; forcing a single timeline feels like an act of epistemic violence, yet leaving the contradictions unresolved risks rendering the work incoherent. Is there a defensible philosophical position—perhaps one that treats certain realities as genuinely multi-faceted rather than partially known—according to which preserving contradiction is not a failure of narration but an ethical and cognitive obligation? And if so, can an individual practitioner, or the culture that shapes her, deliberately choose to inhabit such a framework rather than the default assumption that contradiction always signals error?
