Compose a philosophical dialogue between two interlocutors, designated A and B, both thoroughly familiar with G. E. Moore's Principia Ethica but divided over how to extend his analysis of intrinsic value to friendship and aesthetic experience. A should defend Moore's thesis that both friendship and beauty are instances of organic unity—cases in which the value of the whole exceeds and is not derivable from the summed value of the constituent parts—arguing that this renders them irreducible to psychological, evolutionary, or functional explanations. B should accept that organic unity captures something genuine yet challenge the parallel between the two cases: B ought to argue that friendship is essentially constituted by reciprocal recognition between conscious agents, so that its value is intersubjective and relational in a way that aesthetic appreciation of an object, artifact, or landscape is not; from this, B should conclude that treating both as the same type of organic whole risks obscuring the distinctive moral structure of friendship. The exchange must be genuinely dialectical—B should answer A's anti-reductionist point directly rather than ignoring it, and A should respond to B's agent-centered distinction by explaining whether reciprocal recognition is a necessary condition for all organic unities or only for friendship specifically. To ground the abstraction, have them apply their disagreement to a concrete case: whether an artificial system capable of generating complex aesthetic outputs and sustaining responsive, long-term interaction with a human being could be said to participate in either form of value, or whether Moore's framework implicitly requires a form of consciousness or agency that such a system lacks. Avoid invoking secondary scholarship by name, do not request citations, and keep the focus on conceptual analysis rather than empirical predictions about technology.
