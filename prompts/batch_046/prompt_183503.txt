I'm running a philosophy-of-mind workshop for about 14 grad students and advanced undergrads, 90 minutes, on whether we should ever say an AI literally believes things. I want them to feel why Dennett's intentional stance isn't just a lazy shortcut — it's a commitment that reshapes what counts as an explanation. But I don't want them to just talk about it; I want them to run into the problem through rules and roles.

Design an in-class game or structured deliberation with these specs, and write the answer in American English (no citations or reading lists, since the model might not have web access):

- Give them a fictional conversational agent (call it Mira) with a short ambiguous behavior log — outputs that could be read as deception, confusion, or just statistical noise.
- Split them into two sides: "Design" (must explain/predict using architecture, training data, weights) and "Intentional" (must use beliefs, goals, fears). Both sides have to jointly decide whether to approve, restrict, or retrain Mira, and they need a shared one-sentence justification.
- Inject a new mechanism: "explanatory debt." The rules should enforce it, not just suggest it. Whenever the Intentional side uses mentalistic language to justify the decision, they automatically owe the Design side a concrete "how was that state implemented?" answer before the recommendation can go through. And whenever Design uses pure mechanistic language, they owe Intentional a specific predictive emotional forecast about how users will react. The debts should be tracked with tokens or cards.
- Add real small stakes: the group's final recommendation gets "scored" against hidden criteria (predictive accuracy + coherence of justification), and the winning side picks the music or a coffee-run treat for the next session.
- Make sure the rules force them to smuggle concepts across sides — they should discover that pure stances break down when they try to negotiate the intervention.
- Then give me the debrief: map each phase onto parts of the intentional-stance idea, tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time (e.g., people hiding mentalistic talk inside mechanistic jargon), and be honest about which feature of Dennett's view the activity can't reproduce — probably that adopting the stance carries no ontological weight and is purely pragmatic — plus how I should supply that missing piece verbally.
