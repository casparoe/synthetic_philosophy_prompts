I'm in a macrohistory seminar applying structural-demographic cliodynamics to long-run societal crises. Our professor has ruled on several cases as follows: she counts the Roman Empire's Crisis of the Third Century through the Tetrarchic recovery as a secular cycle; the Qing dynasty's nineteenth-century crisis through the Tongzhi restoration as one; and the Classic-to-Postclassic Maya transition as one, even without a unified imperial bureaucracy. She rejects the French Revolution and Napoleonic wars, the American Civil War and Reconstruction, and Russia's post-Soviet collapse of the 1990s, treating them as exogenous, ideologically driven, or structurally modern rather than endogenous agrarian-state cycles.

These rulings fit at least three competing frames: (A) any crisis driven by elite overproduction, state fiscal strain, and popular immiseration qualifies, regardless of scale or how recovery occurs; (B) the dynamics apply only within agrarian-state polities with centralized surplus extraction, whether imperial or large city-state systems, but never to modern or non-state societies; (C) a true cycle requires a complete integrative-disintegrative-reintegrative arc, with internal systemic recovery rather than external annexation or rapid foreign resolution.

The new case is Iceland's Age of Sturlungs (c. 1220–1262): intense clan warfare among competitive chieftain elites, demographic and resource pressure on pastoral and fishing economies, no centralized fiscal state or land-tax administration, and a "resolution" only through submission to the Norwegian crown rather than internal state reformation. Predict her verdict—would she classify this as a secular cycle? For each frame (A)–(C), state what it predicts here. Then recommend the verdict that best coheres with her track record, identify the closest analog among her prior cases, and state honestly what principle she would be adopting either way. Finally, specify one further real or hypothetical case that would definitively distinguish (A) from (B) and (C).
