Student answer:
The nonidentity problem comes from Derek Parfit's Reasons and Persons (1984). In the classic case, a 14-year-old girl chooses to have a child now instead of waiting. If she waits, she'll conceive at a different time with different genetic material, so the child she actually has—call him Jim—would never exist. Jim's life is hard but still worth living. Normally harming someone is wrong, but Jim can't claim he was harmed by being conceived now, since if she had waited he wouldn't exist at all. So there's literally no victim.

Some philosophers propose a threshold harm view: harm requires falling below a neutral baseline where life isn't worth living. Since Jim is above that baseline, he hasn't been harmed. I think this actually solves the nonidentity problem, because once there's no victim below the line, the identity comparison issue just drops away. The mistake people make is treating the comparison between Jim and the hypothetical happier child as if it identifies a wronged party, but since they have different identities, that comparison is conceptually meaningless. Parfit later adopted this threshold approach in On What Matters to show that person-affecting morality can avoid the problem. So really, the problem just teaches us that we need a baseline, not that we should abandon person-affecting ethics.
