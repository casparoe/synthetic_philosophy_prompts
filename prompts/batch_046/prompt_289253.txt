Compose a villanelle—nineteen lines, ABA rhyme scheme with two refrains—on the problem of constitutional precommitment when modeled as a collective Ulysses contract. Let one refrain be spoken in the voice of the constituent assembly that locks the amendment rules (for instance, invoking supermajority thresholds or entrenched clauses), and let the other be spoken by a future citizen who encounters those rules as an inherited constraint she never authorized. Use the villanelle’s compulsive returns to enact the very problem: the refrains should accumulate new ironies as they recur, so that the formal repetition mirrors the binding mechanism of a constitution that outlives its authors. The turn should land on a precise conceptual crux rather than a vague lament—specifically, the question of whether precommitment can be coherent across generations when the agent who binds herself and the agent who is bound are not numerically identical, a problem usually discussed for individuals but here applied to democratic polities. Do not resolve the tension; let the closing tercet leave the two refrains in unresolved counterpoint. Ground the imagery in the actual machinery of constitutional design (entrenchment, sunset provisions, amendment procedures) rather than in generic metaphors of chains or freedom. The poem should be accurate enough that a reader familiar with the precommitment literature recognizes the conceptual architecture, and compelling enough that a reader unfamiliar with it feels the philosophical pressure without needing prior study.
