Write the full specification of desiderata that any adequate theory of individual moral obligation in large-scale collective-harm contexts—industrial climate emissions, factory-farmed meat consumption, and democratic voting—must satisfy if it is to overcome the inefficacy objection without collapsing individual responsibility into an exclusively collective property. The theory should be adequate specifically for answering whether, and on what grounds, an agent has reason to refrain from a contribution that is causally redundant at the margin.

For each desideratum, state the phenomenon or case that motivates it, the distinction it must preserve (for example, between causal redundancy and normative redundancy, or between having moral weight and the magnitude of that weight), and whether it is non-negotiable or a negotiable preference of one camp. Include at least the following considerations, integrating them into your ordered list rather than treating them as an appendix: (i) the theory must handle probabilistic efficacy in continuous-harm cases (climate) versus threshold-dependent efficacy in discrete cases (voting), and say whether these require different normative structures; (ii) it must account for epistemic opacity of contribution pathways—cases in which the causal chain linking an individual purchase or ballot to the harm is not merely small but structurally hidden behind supply-chain or institutional intermediation; (iii) it must distinguish symbolic or expressive contribution—acts that are causally redundant yet may function as expressions of solidarity, complicity, or normative endorsement—from purely instrumental contribution, and say whether symbolic weight can survive causal redundancy; (iv) it must be consistent with general results from social psychology and behavioral economics about norm-following and collective-action dynamics, without requiring agents to possess implausible computational knowledge of aggregate outcomes; (v) it must preserve the intuition that there is a meaningful difference between participating in a harmful collective practice and being coerced into it, without letting that distinction swallow all individual obligations.

Then identify any subset of these desiderata that appears jointly unsatisfiable—particularly whether a theory can simultaneously maintain that every redundant individual act is morally neutral, that collective harm is genuinely bad, and that no purely collective agent bears the full burden of repair—and explain what trade-off that forces.

Finally, briefly score three main contender families—consequentialist aggregation (with or without expected-utility framing), Kantian universalization, and virtue-ethical or relational accounts of complicity—against your list, keeping the scoring to a short paragraph per family and placing the conceptual weight on the criteria themselves. Do not provide bibliographies, reading lists, or citations; do not quote specific texts verbatim; and flag any desideratum you think does the most work in excluding popular theories.
