hey so i've been chewing on that meno thing from phil 101 - why is knowing something actually better than just being right by accident? at work if my alert script randomly guesses a server is down and it happens to be right, that's still useful for me right now, but philosophers say it's not "knowledge" and that knowledge is somehow more valuable. so i tried to formalize why that might be wrong and ended up with something that feels like it dissolves the whole problem. here's my chain:

1. the only reason we'd care about knowledge over a lucky true belief is if it makes us do something different or better.
2. but once you already hold the true belief, your actions are already optimized - you avoid the broken server, you take the pill, whatever.
3. whatever extra thing makes it knowledge instead of lucky belief (justification, tracking, reliability, whatever) is just metadata about the past - it doesn't change your current decision at all.
4. so two people with the same true belief, one lucky and one knowing, will behave identically in every existant situation.
5. therefore that extra "knowledge" part has zero practical value.
6. but it's also not more theoretically valuable, since both beliefs are equally true.
7. so knowledge is literally not better than lucky true belief in any way we can measure.
8. yet everyone agrees knowledge is better. so either we're all wrong, or i'm missing a hidden practical effect.
9. if there's no hidden effect, then epistemology is just psychology in disguise and the meno problem is solved by saying there's no problem.

this feels like it proves too much - like it makes knowledge worthless compared to luck. where does it break? i feel like step 3 or 4 is where the sleight of hand is, maybe an equivocation between "this decision" and "all future decisions"? but i can't quite name it. if i have to choose, i'd rather keep step 1 (that value has to be practical or measurable) than step 7. what's the cost of holding onto 1? does rejecting 3 mean i have to accept that past metadata can causally affect future behavour even when the current belief is identical?
