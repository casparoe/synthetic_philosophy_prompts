You are serving as a senior consultant in metaphysics and the history of philosophy, with particular depth in rationalist theology and modal ontology. I am drafting material for an advanced seminar on Leibnizian theodicy, and I want to put pressure on a pedagogical analogy that my students keep reaching for spontaneously. Before answering, take a moment to brainstorm the intuitive pulls and hidden commitments of the analogy, then proceed to a structured audit.

The comparison runs as follows: divine world-selection is like a museum curator assembling a permanent exhibition from an infinite gallery of fully formed possible worlds. Each world is a complete “exhibit”—internally consistent and maximally detailed—while the curator (God) applies a single global criterion of compositional harmony rather than judging any individual piece in isolation. The analogy is deployed to license two conclusions: first, that local evils (the “ugly” pieces) are not failures of curation but necessary dissonances that increase the overall aesthetic-rational value of the chosen collection; second, that God is not morally negligent for including suffering, because rejecting any world with local imperfection would be like rejecting a symphony for containing a minor chord. Please lay out the explicit correspondence table—what maps onto the gallery, the curator, the individual artworks, the selection criterion, the absent visitors, and the unselected exhibits? Then identify precisely where the mapping fractures: does the analogy smuggle in an aesthetic theory of value that Leibniz’s rationalist calculus of perfection does not actually require? Does it misrepresent the modal status of unactualized worlds as merely “stored” rather than non-existent? Does the conclusion about justified local evil survive once we notice that a curator could still swap individual pieces, whereas Leibnizian worlds are maximally compossible and therefore not modular? Finally, assess whether an alternative analogy—say, world-selection as a constraint-satisfaction problem in formal logic—would do better, or whether that framing imports its own distortions (for instance, treating moral value as a computable objective function). Separate disanalogies that undermine the intended conclusion from those that are merely decorative, and say whether any refined version of the curator analogy can carry the philosophical weight it is asked to bear.
