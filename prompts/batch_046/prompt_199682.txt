In my cognitive science and philosophy of mind course, we have been studying Hofstadter and Sander's Surfaces and Essences and their argument that analogy-making is not merely an occasional cognitive strategy but the fundamental mechanism of concept formation itself. For my response paper, I need to compare this position with Lakoff and Johnson's conceptual metaphor theory, but I want to avoid a generic survey. Specifically, I am examining whether Lakoff and Johnson's framework—which typically treats conceptual metaphors as unidirectional projections from embodied concrete domains onto abstract targets—can accommodate the kind of reciprocal, category-blending analogies that Hofstadter and Sander claim are central to cognition. Does the unidirectionality thesis in conceptual metaphor theory implicitly prevent it from explaining genuinely creative analogies that generate novel hybrid categories, or can the two views be reconciled by treating them as operating at different explanatory levels—one describing real-time analogical cognition and the other describing culturally entrenched linguistic structures? I am particularly interested in whether analogical blending, as described by Hofstadter and Sander, poses a direct challenge to the claim that abstract concepts are always parasitic on concrete embodied experience. Could you help me structure this comparison by identifying the precise points where the two theories converge, where they genuinely conflict, and whether the conflict is metaphysical, methodological, or merely a matter of scope?
