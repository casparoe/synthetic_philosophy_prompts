After a long argument last night with a close friend about the legitimacy of direct democracy, I am trying to formalize her position. She has made three specific judgments that she insists hang together, though I am not convinced they point to a single principle. First, she defends Switzerland’s frequent referendum practice—particularly the mandatory constitutional referendum with its double-majority requirement of both the people and the cantons—as a model of disciplined popular sovereignty, and she holds that the 2014 initiative against mass immigration, despite its outcome, was procedurally legitimate precisely because it met those high thresholds. Second, she regards the 2016 Brexit referendum as a cautionary failure: she says it was illegitimate not merely because of the result, but because it was advisory rather than binding, employed a simple nationwide majority without territorial weighting or a supermajority rule, and addressed what was in effect a constitutional transformation without constitutional safeguards. Third, she is skeptical of California’s ballot-initiative process, citing Proposition 22 in 2020 as an example in which a well-funded corporate campaign drafted statutory labor law through direct vote, bypassing legislative deliberation and amendment, even though the measure technically won a clear majority.

These verdicts seem jointly consistent with more than one underlying rule. One possibility is that she cares primarily about procedural safeguards—binding effect, supermajority or double-majority requirements, and protection against capture by narrow interests—so that Switzerland passes, Brexit fails, and California propositions fail. Another is that she distinguishes constitutional from statutory questions, holding that direct democracy is appropriate only for the former when equipped with appropriate thresholds, which would explain her approval of Swiss constitutional referendums, her rejection of Brexit’s pseudo-constitutional status with weak safeguards, and her discomfort with California’s statutory initiatives. A third possibility is that she is tracking the quality of preceding deliberation: Switzerland’s long cantonal debates, Brexit’s rushed campaign, and California’s advertising-driven propositions.

Now I want to test these interpretations against a new case. Suppose a jurisdiction proposes what we might call a “deliberative-filtered referendum” for a statutory carbon-pricing scheme: a randomly selected citizens’ assembly drafts the precise legislative text over several months, the proposal then goes to a single binding popular vote, but the vote requires only a simple national majority with no regional, provincial, or cantonal weighting, and there is no second confirmatory vote. Given her track record, do you think she would endorse this mechanism, reject it, or find it ambiguous? Which of her earlier verdicts—Switzerland, Brexit, or California—is the closest analog, and why? If her precedents genuinely underdetermine the case, what principle would she be adopting by endorsing it, and what different principle would she be adopting by rejecting it? Finally, specify one further hypothetical case—perhaps involving a constitutional rather than statutory question, or involving a regional weighting mechanism—that would best distinguish whether she is running the safeguard-based rule, the constitutional-domain rule, or the deliberative-quality rule. I am less interested in your own view of the mechanism than in a careful reconstruction of what consistency with her judgments demands.
