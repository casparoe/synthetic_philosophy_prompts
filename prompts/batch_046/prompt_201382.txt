I'm preparing a comparative essay on church-state financial arrangements in Western Europe, and in discussions with colleagues a recurring analogy has started to feel more like an unexamined premise than a heuristic. Defenders of systems like Germany's Kirchensteuer or the Church of England's establishment often describe them as operating on a "public utility" logic: the state treats religious provision as basic social infrastructure—comparable to water, electricity, or public broadcasting—collecting fees or granting privileges not to endorse doctrine but to maintain stability, with opt-outs functioning like disconnecting from a grid one still indirectly benefits from. The analogy is used to license the conclusion that such cooperation is essentially administrative and neutral, rather than coercive or confessional.

Audit this analogy rigorously. First, provide an explicit correspondence table: what plays the role of the utility provider, the grid, the consumer, the subscription fee, and the public benefit? Then identify where the mapping fractures—particularly around the non-rivalrous and non-excludable nature of spiritual goods, the irreversibility of religious socialization compared to utility consumption, and the fact that church taxes in Germany flow specifically to registered religious communities (including some non-Christian ones) through a corporatist mechanism, Körperschaft des öffentlichen Rechts, that has no analog in municipal water provision. Consider also what the analogy silently imports: does treating religion as infrastructure smuggle in a functionalist view that reduces belief to social utility, and does it obscure the asymmetry between a citizen who never requested baptism and one who never requested plumbing? Finally, assess whether the conclusion—that state-church cooperation is benign because it is "merely infrastructural"—survives once the disanalogies are on the table, or whether the analogy was carrying the normative weight all along. If you find the utility frame inadequate, suggest a better analogy for describing the German and English arrangements without collapsing them into either pure establishment or pure secularism.
