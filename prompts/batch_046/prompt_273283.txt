As part of a feature on counterfactual reasoning in modern historiography, I have been interviewing a historian of interwar Europe about her judgments on whether specific historical outcomes were structurally overdetermined or depended on a particular agent. Her track record in our conversations is as follows. She holds that German unification in 1871 was structurally inevitable: Bismarck accelerated consolidation, but economic integration and nationalist sentiment made a unified state probable by the 1890s regardless of the chancellor's identity. She judges the Bolshevik seizure of power in October 1917 to be agent-dependent: without Lenin's specific decision to override the Central Committee and seize power immediately, the party likely fragments or is absorbed into a parliamentary coalition. She regards the American New Deal as structurally replaceable, arguing that the Depression's severity made a major federal expansion inevitable, even if Roosevelt's particular programs would not have existed. She considers Mao's Cultural Revolution indispensable to its specific form, maintaining that no other leader in the Chinese Communist hierarchy possessed both the personal authority and the particular ideological synthesis required to mobilize mass violence against the party apparatus itself. Finally, she treats the collapse of the Weimar Republic as structurally overdetermined, holding that conservative elite hostility, military disdain for democracy, and the Versailles settlement made an authoritarian resolution likely by the mid-1930s, irrespective of any individual chancellor.

The new case is this. Suppose Adolf Hitler had died during the Beer Hall Putsch in November 1923. By 1941, would there still have existed a Nazi Germany — defined precisely as a one-party racial state led by the NSDAP, implementing genocide as systematic state policy, and initiating a war of territorial expansion for Lebensraum?

Please predict her verdict. Identify which of her earlier rulings serves as the closest analog, and explain why. Then articulate at least two plausible interpretive rules that jointly accommodate all five of her earlier judgments but diverge on this counterfactual: for each rule, specify exactly what it predicts about the absence of Hitler. Offer your best-guess prediction of her actual view, with the reasoning for preferring that rule over its rival. Be explicit about whether her precedents genuinely underdetermine the answer; if they do, identify the principle she would be committing to by selecting one option, and name one further historical counterfactual — not already discussed — that would best discriminate between the two rules.
