As a novelist who often constructs narratives with nested framing devices, I recently observed a sharp disagreement between two colleagues—Mara, a structuralist critic, and Elias, a cognitive narratologist—about where semantic authority actually resides in a multi-layered text. They were analyzing an experimental autofictional manuscript (not my own, though structurally kin to something I have drafted) organized around three embedded messages: a paratextual author’s note warning that the work is a deliberate misreading of history; the main narrative, presented as conventional historical fiction; and a recurring dream sequence inside that narrative, which systematically inverts the historical events. Their dispute turned on how to apply the tripartite distinction between frame messages, outer messages, and inner messages.

Mara argued that the frame message—the paratextual warning—occupies the dominant position in the hierarchy. By her reading, the frame instructs the audience to treat the outer historical narrative as unreliable, so the true thematic payload is skepticism about historiography itself. The dream sequence, she maintained, is merely an ornamental inner message: it provides emotional texture but possesses no independent semantic authority, because the frame has already determined the interpretive conditions for everything enclosed within it.

Elias countered that Mara was treating physical placement in the book as a proxy for logical authority—what he called bibliographic fetishism. He proposed that the frame is actually a parasitic frame: it borrows the rhetorical authority of an external, non-fictional position, yet because the “author” of the note is itself a fictional construct, the frame is ontologically on the same level as the narrative it claims to govern. Therefore, the frame cannot nullify the outer message from above. Instead, Elias claimed, the inner message—the dream—functions as a false exit, resolving contradictions in the outer narrative that the frame itself introduces. The dream’s inversions, he said, supply the coherence that the paratext only pretends to offer.

Mara replied that if the frame is ontologically internal, then Elias’s appeal to the inner message collapses into arbitrariness: nothing inside the fiction can claim special status over anything else, so elevating the dream is unmotivated. Elias responded that hierarchy need not depend on ontological transcendence; functional role within the interpretive architecture can differentiate messages even when all are fictionally embedded. Mara then accused him of smuggling in an unprincipled distinction between functional and ontological levels without explaining why the former should govern interpretation, and she added that his false-exit reading merely projects the reader’s desire for narrative resolution onto a sequence that is, by design, incoherent.

Please adjudicate their exchange point by point: assess Mara’s initial framing argument, Elias’s parasitic-frame objection, Mara’s collapse objection, Elias’s functional-role reply, and Mara’s final smuggling-and-projection accusation—rating each from clearly mistaken to clearly right with brief justification. Then say who is more right overall, and identify what both parties are missing, particularly regarding whether a frame message can be simultaneously fictionally embedded and interpretively authoritative, and whether the inner message’s coherence is genuinely independent or largely a projection of the interpreter’s expectations.
