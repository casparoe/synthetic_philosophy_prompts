hey, so ive been chewing on safe Pareto improvements lately -- the idea that you should only push institutional changes if literally nobody's made worse off, not just Kaldor-Hicks 'the winners could compensate.' in rationalist spaces this comes up with AI governance and longtermist policy design, but i think there's a deeper knot people skip over.

specifically: once you take seriously that future synthetic minds or even advanced invertebrate welfare might create moral patients whose baseline utility is undefinded, the whole 'no one is worse off' criterion starts looking shaky. add to that the problem of hidden counterfactual losers -- people who'd exist in counterfactual worlds but don't in ours -- and you've got three or four open conceptual lines that actually matter for whether Pareto reasoning can ground real-world decision procedures.

if you were advising someone with about two years, decent philosophy background but no formal econ, which of these open questions should they actually work on? i'm thinking of: (a) whether undefined baslines for synthetic patients collapse Pareto into something useless; (b) how to model hidden counterfactual losers without falling into population ethics quicksand; (c) whether dynamic consistency over generations can be saved if we allow present-biased compensation; (d) maybe something about risk-averse Pareto frontiers under deep uncertainty.

rank them, defend the ordering with actual criteria -- tractability, how upstream each is, and whether solving it changes anything for practitioners. tell me which are overrated dead ends and which two should get the bulk of attention. i'm writing this up for a small reasearch circle, not for publication, so i care more about the dependancy structure than about citations. also, don't bother with a reading list -- i can't check half those papers anyway without web access.
