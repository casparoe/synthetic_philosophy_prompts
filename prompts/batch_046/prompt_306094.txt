Hey — I've been trying to pin down a pattern for a lecture and I'm not sure if it has an actual name or if I'm just reinventing the wheel. I keep running into the same kind of story in three places people talk about: the Late Bronze Age, where palace economies and writing fall apart but villagers keep farming with the same pottery styles; the Classic Maya lowlands, where the big cities empty out but rural populations stay put speaking the same languages; and Easter Island, where they stop carving and moving the big statues, the trees are mostly gone, yet people don't disappear — they just settle into a smaller-scale life that lasts for centuries.

In all three occurances there's this high-coordination layer — big buildings, long-distance exchange, specialized record-keeping — and then it stops. The resources or knowledge that supported it seem permanently lost, not just temporarily interrupted, and what's left is a simpler form that actually sticks around. People call it collapse when they want to warn that complex societies are fragile, and they call it decentralization or adaptation when they want to argue that people always find a way to keep going. But I'm asking about the structural pattern underneath the labeling: a one-way shift to a simpler, stable form because the old supporting conditions are gone for good, not because of a temporary shock that could be repaired. Does that have a specific term in archaeology or historical sociology — maybe something like irreversible simplification or a named subtype of collapse? Or is it just folded into the general collapse-versus-transformation debate? And if there's no standard label, how do we seperate this kind of case from a society that genuinely recovers its old complexity after a crisis?
