Please take on the role of an expert moral philosopher specializing in distributive ethics and welfare economics. Before you finalize anything, spend a moment brainstorming the main competing frameworks—such as total and average utilitarianism, prioritarian weighting, sufficientarian thresholds, and equality of resources—then work through the logic step by step.

I have been trying to map out my own gut-level judgments about how to divide limited resources among people who convert those resources into wellbeing at radically different rates. Here is where I land, case by case.

If two friends both want the same weekend trip and would enjoy it equally, splitting the cost feels obviously correct. If one friend has untreated chronic pain that a specific medical intervention could eliminate, while the others could take the trip, directing the funds toward her relief feels clearly right, even though the trip might spread more total pleasure around the group. Now consider someone—I will call this the moderate high-conversion case—who, due to an unusual but stable neurological profile, derives perhaps ten times more lasting satisfaction from access to art, nature, or music than anyone else derives from comparable goods, yet is not suffering or deprived in any basic way. If we have a shared pool of $1,000, part of me wants to allocate it all to her because the aggregate wellbeing seems enormous; another part rebels and thinks it unfair to make everyone else subsidize her exceptional private capacity for delight, so I lean against funneling everything her way.

Then there is a sharper tension I cannot resolve. In one situation, a single person faces a life-threatening condition that $1,000 would remedy, producing a modest but vital extension of life, while several others could use smaller shares for genuinely enriching but non-essential experiences. Here I firmly choose to save the one life. In a different situation, one person has an extreme, stable high-conversion profile—perhaps fifty or a hundred times the typical rate—without any underlying suffering or need, and the alternative is addressing moderate but widespread discomfort among several others. Here I genuinely go back and forth: sometimes the magnitude of the potential joy feels decisive, and sometimes it feels wrong for need and equality to be overridden by sheer capacity alone.

There is also a profile I have started calling intermittent capacity spikes: a person whose ability to benefit from targeted support collapses for long unpredictable stretches, then surges during brief windows, producing profound fulfillment that is impossible to replicate in anyone else during those same windows. Committing resources continuously wastes them in the low periods; waiting for the spike means missing it because the timing is not knowable in advance. I keep revisiting this case because I am unsure whether consistency of support or responsiveness to peak opportunity should govern.

Please propose one precise principle, or a small set of tightly related candidates, stated clause by clause, with each clause tied to the specific case above that motivates it. In particular, I want to know whether your best account adds a temporal or predictability condition to handle the intermittent-spike case, and whether the conflict between the moderate and extreme high-conversion cases shows that capacity can never justify unequal shares, or only that it needs a need-based gate.
