Colleague A (Marcus) opened by arguing that a Ulysses contract only works if it is genuinely binding. He said that if a constitution — or a personal commitment device — includes an escape clause allowing revocation whenever one’s beliefs about the founding reason shift, then it is not precommitment at all, but merely a delayed decision procedure. He illustrated this with constitutional amendment rules requiring supermajorities, which function precisely because they do not depend on the current legislature’s changing moral outlook.

Colleague B (Nadia) replied that absolute binding is irrational in the face of genuine epistemic growth. She proposed what she called a “threshold-conditional Ulysses contract”: the agent binds herself now, but the contract explicitly dissolves if her credence in the founding justification falls below a specified level. She claimed this preserves precommitment’s core benefit — forcing explicit reasoning rather than silent drift — while avoiding tyranny by past values.

Marcus countered that this collapses the distinction between constraint and choice. He said that if the contract itself encodes the conditions of its own dissolution, then breaking it is not a breach but an execution of its terms; hence it was never a constraint, just a decision tree with extra steps. He added that Nadia was conflating rational belief revision with value drift, and that her threshold would inevitably be placed near the point where she already felt like abandoning the commitment.

Nadia responded that Marcus ignored the structural role of the contract: even an explicitly conditional one forces the agent to articulate and justify dissolution, which differs from unreflective change. She invoked constitutional sunset clauses as analogues, suggesting that a well-designed precommitment can include scheduled or conditional review without losing its identity.

Marcus then distinguished sunset clauses — which are time-bound and predictable — from Nadia’s epistemic conditions, which depend on subjective credence updates. He argued that hybridising the two creates a mechanism inheriting the weaknesses of both: it lacks the stability of a true Ulysses contract and the flexibility of open deliberation. He concluded that Nadia’s proposal was essentially slow value drift disguised as rational update.

Please go through their exchange round by round and say who is right about which specific point, and identify anything both are missing — for instance, whether a coherent middle ground exists between absolute binding and fully conditional dissolution, or whether the distinction between epistemic update and value drift can be maintained in practice.
