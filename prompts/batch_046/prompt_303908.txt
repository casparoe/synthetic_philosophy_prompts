I've been working through the conceptual foundations of meritocracy for an advanced course on distributive justice, and I find myself stuck on what looks like a hidden circularity in appeals to desert. The familiar defense holds that just institutions should allocate goods according to merit—whether effort, talent, or productive contribution—using criteria the institution itself defines, such as competitive examinations or market valuations. But this seems to generate a troubling loop: the system constructs the metric of merit, applies it to participants, and then cites the resulting rankings as independent moral justification for the rewards it distributes. To break the circle, one might appeal to what I will call pre-institutional desert—the claim that certain natural capacities or exertions possess moral weight prior to and independent of any social mechanism that measures them. Yet if such pre-institutional desert exists, its defense appears to rest on a prior theory of human value—perhaps that innate gifts or unaided effort are intrinsically worthy of recognition—that is not itself meritocratic, since it makes no reference to competition, social roles, or institutional design.

Given this tension, I want to ask whether meritocracy can be philosophically sustained at all: is institutional desert necessarily circular unless anchored in pre-institutional facts, and if so, does that not reduce meritocracy to a derivative application of some deeper, non-meritocratic moral theory about human worth? Or is there a way to defend the coherence of institution-relative desert without either falling into circularity or smuggling in an external account of human dignity? I would particularly like to know whether the distinction between pre-institutional and institutional desert is even well-drawn, or whether it rests on a false presupposition about how social practices relate to moral justification.
