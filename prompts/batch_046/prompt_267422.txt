You are an expert in moral philosophy and philosophical psychology, with particular familiarity with ancient ethics, the structure of practical reason, and debates about whether moral norms depend on social visibility. Before you answer, please spend a moment brainstorming the key distinctions at play—especially the difference between conditions of enforcement, conditions of existence, and conditions of rational integration for moral reasons.

Consider the following exchange, reported by a bystander at a graduate seminar on Plato’s Republic, between two participants referred to here only as A and B. They were debating what Glaucon’s ring of Gyges actually shows about whether justice is valuable for its own sake.

(1) A argued that the ring’s invisibility eliminates not only punishment and reputation, but also the intersubjective conditions under which moral reasons operate. If an action can never enter the shared space of justification—if no one can ever hold you to account, ask you why you did it, or weave it into the narrative they construct about you—then the reasons that depend on that shared space dissolve. Justice is therefore not merely socially enforced; it is socially constituted, and the ring reveals that without an audience there is no rational structure left to support it.

(2) B replied that this confuses the conditions of enforcement with the conditions of existence. Many moral reasons are first-personal: I have reason not to torture an innocent person even in total isolation, because the reason concerns the relation between my will and the suffering of the other, not between my will and an audience. The ring removes observers, but if a victim is present the reason remains; if no victim is present, the reason was never there to begin with. Either way, visibility is irrelevant to the reason’s force.

(3) A conceded direct victim-related reasons but introduced a more subtle structural claim. The ring allows not just hidden action, but practical compartmentalization: the agent can partition her practical identity so that invisible actions belong to a shadow self that need not be integrated with her visible, accountable self. Because maintaining a unified narrative across all actions requires cognitive and emotional labor—what we might call the burden of coherence—the invisible agent can rationally offload unjust actions to this shadow self, preserving the integrity of the visible self without paying the price of justice. This makes injustice not merely tempting, but structurally rational for a unified agent who wants to minimize psychological cost.

(4) B responded that this shadow-self model is unstable in two ways. First, any partition robust enough to isolate unjust actions is also robust enough to isolate the benefits they produce; the visible self cannot reliably enjoy the fruits of the shadow self’s labor without recognizing them as its own, which collapses the partition. Second, B argued that A has smuggled in an undefended psychological thesis—that coherence is a burden rather than a constitutive condition of being a single agent. If coherence is necessary for agency itself, then compartmentalization is not rational optimization but a form of practical self-fragmentation, and the ring reveals not that injustice is rational, but that it requires destroying the conditions under which one can rationally claim to be acting at all.

The seminar leader could not decide who had the stronger case. Please adjudicate this exchange move by move: rate each of the four moves from clearly mistaken to clearly right, with brief justification for each rating. Then say who is more right overall, and identify the single most important issue that both A and B are neglecting—specifically, something about how moral reasons retain or lose their force when the agent knows she is permanently unobservable by others but still must live with her own knowledge of what she has done. Do not provide bibliographies, reading lists, or citations; reason only from the arguments as presented.
