After a long argument with a friend about whether a legal system can contain rules that genuinely govern their own alteration without collapsing into circularity or smuggling in an external authority, I want to formalize what is actually at stake. Write the desiderata that any adequate theory of legal self-reference and self-amending rules must satisfy: the phenomena it must explain, the paradoxes it must accommodate or dissolve, the distinctions it must preserve, with the reasoning behind each. In particular, address how the theory handles a constitutional clause that makes its own validity depend on reaffirmation by a body whose membership rules are defined by the clause itself; a statute that authorizes an agency to revise the statutory conditions under which the agency's own authority may be revoked; whether a strict hierarchy of norms is required or flat recursive authorization is permissible; and whether amendment, interpretation, and meta-interpretation can be separated when a court redefines a rule that governs its own change. For each desideratum, flag whether it is non-negotiable or camp-specific, and identify any subset that appears jointly unsatisfiable—especially where hierarchy and recursion conflict. Then briefly indicate how a hierarchical positivist view and a reflexive institutionalist view fare against the list. Keep the focus on conceptual architecture; avoid bibliographies, specific citations, or lists of texts.
