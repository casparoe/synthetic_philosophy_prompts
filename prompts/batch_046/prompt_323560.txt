I'm working through Dewey's Democracy and Education (1916) in a seminar on political epistemology, and I'm stuck on a passage in the middle chapters where he shifts from education as social transmission to democracy as a mode of life. To paraphrase the text: Dewey claims that democracy is not fundamentally a form of government or an apparatus for selecting representatives, but rather a mode of associated living—a shared way of experiencing common interests through cooperative interaction. Immediately adjoining this, he argues that the scientific method—forming hypotheses, testing them through observation, and revising beliefs in light of consequences—is the proper model for how such shared experience should develop. He seems to be asserting that democratic association is continuous with experimental inquiry, since both depend on the free exchange of ideas, the practical testing of social habits, and the willingness to reconstruct customs when they fail to meet new conditions.

What I cannot quite grasp is the conceptual architecture that binds these two claims together. Is Dewey arguing that democratic association necessarily has the logical structure of an experiment, or is he prescribing that democratic citizens ought to adopt scientific habits of mind? There is a tension that keeps blocking me. If democracy is primarily a mode of associated living, why must its growth be modeled specifically on the scientific method rather than on narrative tradition, aesthetic collaboration, or moral sympathy? Conversely, if the scientific method is genuinely the necessary framework, does that not risk reducing democratic deliberation to a technical procedure and privileging expert knowledge over the distributed judgment of citizens? To organize my own confusion, I have been trying to apply a distinction I sketched for myself—between "epistemic democracy," in which participation is valuable because it pools distributed cognition, and "instrumental democracy," in which participation is mainly a mechanism for coordinating action toward agreed ends. Yet Dewey's passage seems to resist both: he denies that democracy is merely about achieving collective knowledge, but he also refuses to treat it as pure coordination. Could you help me understand exactly how Dewey's coupling of "associated living" with "experimental inquiry" avoids collapsing democratic life into either an epistemic market or an instrumental procedure? I would prefer that you work through the conceptual problem without offering bibliographies, citations, or reading lists; I want to see how the architecture holds together from the passage's own logic and from your direct analysis.
