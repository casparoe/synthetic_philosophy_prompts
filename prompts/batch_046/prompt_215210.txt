Write a short research proposal—approximately 1,500 words, for a three-month independent study grant—on the democratic boundary problem, framed through a conceptual distinction that the standard literature tends to collapse. State the precise question: when a democratic polity enacts an irreversible, long-binding collective commitment (for example, a constitutional ecological obligation or permanent infrastructural regime that will coercively govern future residents and materially alter conditions for present non-citizens), does belonging to the demos depend on being currently subject to law, being foreseeably affected, or being capable of reciprocal justification with present deciders? Explain why the three dominant answers—the all-affected-interests principle, the stakeholder principle, and the coercion-based account—leave this specifically open: each treats membership as a single status, so they cannot distinguish between being legally bound by a decision and being normatively owed an account of it, and therefore they either over-include every affected party as a full democratic member or under-include future generations who will live under the regime but do not yet exist. Propose a non-empirical, conceptual approach: construct a taxonomy of bindingness (jurisdictional, causal, and normative) and test whether membership can be split into two tiers—constituent membership assigned by subjection-to-law, and deliberative inclusion assigned by affected-interest—without the tiers collapsing into one another. Say why this approach is preferable to the obvious alternatives of either adding empirical case studies or refining a monistic criterion: the problem is not a lack of data but a category mistake in treating “who is bound” and “who must be heard” as the same relation. Describe what a successful result looks like: a defensible two-tier framework with a principled reason the tiers should not merge, applied consistently to two constructed scenarios of irreversible commitment. Describe what counts as failure: if every form of bindingness either entails full membership or entails none, then the taxonomy collapses and the project should concede that a monistic boundary criterion is unavoidable. Give an honest scope assessment: three months is sufficient for the taxonomy, the conceptual tests, and a preliminary defense, but insufficient for a full theory of democratic legitimacy or for resolving every downstream objection; identify that risk and explain how you will contain it by restricting the deliverable to the conceptual framework plus the failure conditions. Refer to the standard positions by their usual names only, and indicate that specific citations and a literature review will be supplied separately; do not request a bibliography or reading list.
