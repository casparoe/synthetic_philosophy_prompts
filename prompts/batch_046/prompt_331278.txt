Read Kazuo Ishiguro's The Remains of the Day through Daniel Dennett's idea of the self as a center of narrative gravity. Stevens, the butler, clearly weaves a gravitational center out of professional dignity and loyalty to Lord Darlington, pulling his actions into a coherent story. But I want you to press on whether that center actually holds, or whether it suffers from what might be called narrative debt: the accumulation of actions the self claims ownership of yet refuses to integrate—his service to a Nazi-sympathizing aristocrat, his suppression of Miss Kenton's affection—until the unacknowledged mass exceeds the center's pull. Does Stevens's quiet breakdown on the pier represent a collapse of narrative gravity, or only a reconfiguration around a smaller orbit? And does Ishiguro's novel suggest that narrative selves carry a kind of moral mass limit, beyond which coherence fails not because the story is false but because it is incomplete? Take both Dennett's functionalist account and Ishiguro's portrait of self-deception seriously: do not reduce the novel to an illustration, and do not distort Dennett to fit the plot. Say where the work resists the reading—perhaps in Stevens's continued refusal to fully own his past, or in the novel's implication that a center of gravity can be functional for others (Lord Darlington, Miss Kenton) even when it fails for the self. End by saying what the novel adds to the philosophical debate about narrative identity that argument alone does not capture, particularly about whether gravitational centers can survive internal contradiction or whether they inevitably fragment under the weight of the lives they organize.
