I direct the ethics training module for a large urban psychiatric residency program, and every quarter I must sign accreditation reports on whether each resident is developing practical moral character—specifically clinical compassion, appropriate courage in challenging authority, and practical wisdom in navigating ambiguous care—rather than merely accumulating technical competence. The evaluation instruments we inherited treat virtue as a stable, cross-situational trait, asking supervisors to rate residents on scales like "demonstrates empathy consistently" without regard to context. Having engaged seriously with the situationist critique in moral psychology—the body of evidence and argument suggesting that much apparently moral behavior is powerfully shaped by situational features such as time pressure, institutional hierarchy, and ambient norms rather than by robust internal dispositions—I want our committee to stop pretending that character is context-independent. Instead, I need a reusable decision procedure that the evaluation committee can run during case-review meetings: the precise sequence of questions to ask, what counts as sufficient evidence at each stage, where the procedure yields "insufficient evidence—do not certify," where it yields "proceed to deliberation because this is irreducibly a value judgment," and where it demands that we redesign the clinical environment rather than assess the resident. The procedure should remain grounded in virtue ethics—taking seriously the idea that the good clinician should possess certain excellences of character—but must explicitly incorporate the situationist insight that traits manifest differently across what I will call "moral micro-environments" (for instance, high-acuity emergency handoffs versus unhurried family conferences, or interactions with familiar versus unfamiliar attending staff). Include a concise set of observable test conditions across these micro-environments, specify how to weight contradictory behaviors across contexts, and apply the procedure to three residents: one who is reliably kind in calm settings but becomes dismissive during emergency codes; one who advocates for a vulnerable patient's autonomy against senior pressure, but only after being explicitly reminded of institutional safeguarding policy; and one whose behavior is uniformly excellent, yet tracks almost exactly with the presence of one particular supervising attending. In one of these applications, the procedure should deliver a verdict that the committee is likely to find unwelcome, and you must flag exactly where human judgment cannot be eliminated and must be owned by the signatory. Finally, state clearly what the procedure cannot settle—particularly whether environmental redesign should always take priority over individual character assessment, and how to handle cases where the resident's situational sensitivity itself might be a form of practical wisdom rather than a deficiency.
