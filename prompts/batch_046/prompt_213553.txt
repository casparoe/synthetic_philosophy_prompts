I've been thinking about this after a late-night argument with my coworker about AI safety. I'm a software engineer, not a philosphy person, so bear with me. I think we can formallize "avoid side effects" for an AI without having to fully specify what human values are. My rough idea is a structural side-effect budget: compare world trajectories with the agent acting versus doing nothing, but only penalize changes that are (1) physically irreversable, (2) outside the causal chain needed for the assigned task, and (3) not compensated by reversability elsewhere. So if the robot is cleaning a room, moving a chair is fine, but breaking a window counts as harm even if we never said windows are valuable — it's just off-path and permanent. That makes "low impact" definable structurally, not about values.

But I'm probably missing something obvious. I hold this view — what would count against it? Sort the defeaters by how decisive they'd be, not just standard objections. Which observations, arguments, or hidden assumptions should actually make me weaken or drop it? In particular, is there a value-laden choice hiding inside my three conditions — like deciding what's "task-linked" or what's "reversible" — that means I'm just smuggling in a full value spec after all? Also, is my view already qualified into unfalsifiability: if I keep adding exceptions whenever a counterexample hits, does that make it useless? I want an honest assessment of how exposed this structural-budget idea really is, including what its defenders (like me, I guess) would concede as genuinely counting against it versus what they'd just dismiss.
