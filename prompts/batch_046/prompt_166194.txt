I am grading submissions for a graduate seminar on philosophy of language and mind, and I need an external assessment of one response. The exam question I set asked students to apply Wittgenstein’s private-language considerations—found in the sections of Philosophical Investigations running roughly from §243 through §315—to a deliberately artificial case. Specifically, students were asked to imagine a large language model that develops an internal vector cluster correlated with its own outputs expressing uncertainty, assigns a proprietary token to this cluster, and applies it solely in hidden-state processing without any external interpretation. The prompt required them to distinguish two interpretive readings of Wittgenstein’s argument: (a) that private mental reference is inherently unstable and therefore cannot ground linguistic rules, versus (b) that the very distinction between seeming correct and being correct collapses without public criteria. Students then had to assess whether the model’s internal labeling falls under Wittgenstein’s critique, and whether the analogy succeeds.

Below is the answer submitted by a student, Jordan Lee. I am asking you to act as a grader: assign a specific letter grade (with plus or minus if warranted) and justify it with point-by-point comments on accuracy, subtle conflations, and steps that do or do not follow. Please treat this as an evaluation of Jordan Lee’s work rather than an occasion to present your own independent view of Wittgenstein, Kripke, or artificial systems. Do not offer a bibliography or reading list.

Jordan Lee writes:
Wittgenstein’s private-language argument targets the idea that a speaker could invent a sign for an entirely private sensation and apply it consistently without any external correction. In the AI case, the model assigns token T to vector cluster V whenever it occurs in hidden states. This is structurally identical to Wittgenstein’s private diarist: the system has an internal state and labels it. Wittgenstein demonstrates that such a practice fails because there is no distinction between seeming correct and being correct. Without other users or observers to correct the model, no criterion of correctness can be established. Hence the internal token is meaningless, confirming that rule-following requires a linguistic community. This aligns with the interpretation—famously developed by Kripke—that Wittgenstein’s core point is the social nature of meaning. The alternative reading, that private mental states are inherently unstable, is less central: Wittgenstein does not claim sensations fluctuate, but that their stability cannot be verified in isolation. Consequently, the AI analogy succeeds, and it shows that private reference is impossible for any system, biological or artificial, precisely because correctness demands communal checks.
