I've been trying to work out whether there's a real line between fixing something that's broken and making something better than it ever was, especially when it comes to changing people's brains or bodies before they're born. I think I've built an argument that shows the line doesn't exist, but the conclusion feels too extreme, and I want to know which part is wrong.

Here is the chain as I see it:

(1) Therapy is supposed to restore normal human functioning, while enhancement takes someone past that point.
(2) "Normal functioning" is usually measured against the statistical average for our species right now.
(3) But those averages aren't fixed. Because of better nutrition, education, and technology, the statistical center for traits like working memory, emotional regulation, and social attention has moved noticeably over the past century.
(4) So a child whose biology puts them at the 1920 average for working memory is, by 2020 statistical standards, below normal.
(5) That means giving that child a biological intervention to reach the 1920 average would count as "enhancement" in 1920 but "therapy" in 2020, even though the biological change is exactly the same.
(6) The only difference is the calendar year and the population around them.
(7) So whether an intervention counts as therapy or enhancement depends on demographic trends, not on the child's own biology or needs.
(8) But if therapy is ethically required and publicly funded, while enhancement is optional and private, then whether a child gets help depends on accidental population-level shifts.
(9) That seems wrong. A specific biological deficit should matter more than whether other people happen to have improved because of better schools.
(10) Therefore the therapy/enhancement distinction collapses, or at least can't do the work people want it to do.

Where does this go wrong? If I have to save either (1) or (9), I'd rather save (9), but I want to know exactly which step slips and what I have to pay to reject it. Please answer in American English.
