You are a professional philosopher with deep expertise in Madhyamaka Buddhism and contemporary philosophy of science. Before writing, take a moment to brainstorm: list the four positions of the tetralemma, recall the distinction between conventional and ultimate truth in Nāgārjuna's framework, and identify exactly where standard philosophy of science silently assumes inherent existence (svabhāva).

Then write the full specification of desiderata that any philosophical theory of scientific classification and causal explanation must satisfy if it genuinely takes Nāgārjuna's tetralemma and the doctrine of emptiness (śūnyatā) as foundational constraints—not as decorative metaphysics but as binding conditions on ontology and epistemology.

Your list must cover the following, with the case or consideration behind each requirement and a clear flag of which are non-negotiable versus camp preferences:

1. How the theory handles each corner of the tetralemma—affirmation, negation, both, neither—with respect to the existence of natural kinds and causal powers. For each corner, name the specific conceptual mistake it represents and provide an ordinary scientific example that tempts one into it (for instance, essentialism about chemical elements, eliminativism about mental kinds, pluralist overcommitment, or agnostic suspension).

2. The distinctions the theory must preserve without collapse: between emptiness being itself empty versus functioning as a hidden essence; between conventional efficacy (vyavahāra) and ultimate non-finding (paramārtha); and between dependent origination (pratītyasamutpāda) and mere causal nihilism. State why collapsing any one of these destroys the theory's claim to be Madhyamaka rather than a rival position.

3. The consistency conditions with established scientific practice—what results about measurement reliability, predictive success, or law-like regularity the theory cannot contradict—together with the reason each is non-negotiable for anyone who takes science seriously, versus which are merely preferences of scientific realism.

4. The problem of "empty but functional" determination in taxonomic revision: specifically, how conceptual designation operates when science reclassifies phenomena (e.g., the demotion of Pluto, or the shift from species essentialism to population genetics) without invoking inherent natures in either the old or the new category, and without reducing the revision to pure linguistic whim. This is the central injection: show how the theory accounts for asymmetrical conceptual dependence—why some classifications depend on others but not vice versa—without svabhāva in the relata.

5. A diagnosis of which two desiderata in your list are jointly unsatisfiable. Explain the tension precisely—whether it forces a choice between a robust conventional truth that can do scientific work and a strict interpretation of emptiness that denies all determination—and state whether you think the tension is resolvable or constitutive of the framework.

6. A brief scoring—no more than one paragraph—of how a standard scientific realist account, a pure conventionalist account, and a Madhyamaka-inspired middle-way account fare against your list. Identify which single requirement eliminates the realist account and which eliminates pure conventionalism.

Keep the weight on the criteria and their motivating cases rather than on the scoring. Do not provide a bibliography, reading list, or citation requests. Do not invoke exotic physics, obscure philological variants, or texts unknown outside specialist scholarship; stay within the conceptual apparatus available in mainstream philosophical treatments of Madhyamaka. Write in a formal, precise, analytical style.
