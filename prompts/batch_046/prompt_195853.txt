I have noticed a recurring institutional pattern that I struggle to place within the standard taxonomy of freedom. Imagine a research institute where scientists are formally at liberty to investigate any hypothesis they choose: there is no censorship statute, funding is technically available, and employment contracts do not restrict inquiry. In practice, however, the administration controls promotion, laboratory access, and grant endorsements through criteria that are vague enough to be applied selectively. A researcher who pursues a disfavored line of work does not receive an explicit prohibition; instead, she finds that her requests for resources are delayed for procedural review, her collaborators are reassigned for efficiency, and her evaluations cite lack of strategic alignment, each decision being individually justifiable and none explicitly mentioning her research choice. Over time, this produces a climate of self-censorship: scientists restrict their own inquiries not because they are forbidden, but because they are exposed to the arbitrary will of administrators who can make unconventional work prohibitively costly without ever issuing a direct command. The pattern feels close to republican freedom's concern with domination, yet it differs from the textbook case in which a master issues an explicit order; here the power is diffuse, mediated through ostensibly neutral bureaucratic discretion, and exercised cumulatively rather than through a single act of interference. Is there an established philosophical concept or term—perhaps along the lines of administrative domination, structural arbitrariness, or bureaucratic non-domination—that captures this specific configuration? And how should it be analytically distinguished from mere constraints on negative liberty, deficiencies in positive liberty, and the classic republican account of freedom as non-domination? If no precise term exists, I would welcome a careful definition of the phenomenon, including its boundary conditions and what distinguishes it from ordinary organizational politics or risk-averse careerism.
