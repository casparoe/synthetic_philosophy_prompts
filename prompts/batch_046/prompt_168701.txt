I am a novelist working on a manuscript about a filmmaker who reconstructs the private death of a real, long-dead philosopher for cinematic release, and I have become preoccupied with a question that standard bioethics does not quite touch: what is the precise nature of the debt we incur when we turn a dead person's suffering into structured spectacle? Rather than answer this in the abstract, I want to read Peter Shaffer's Amadeus—particularly as it exists in Miloš Forman's cinematic realization—through a lens I am calling posthumous representational violence, by which I mean not merely factual distortion but the non-consensual performance of a dead subject's agony for an audience that holds no ethical relation to the original person. The film invents Salieri's poisoning of Mozart, stages Mozart's deathbed dictation of the Requiem as a kind of aesthetic expropriation, and filters everything through Salieri's confessional narration from an asylum. I want a reading that asks whether Amadeus is aware of its own complicity: does the masked commission scene acknowledge that Mozart's genius is being consumed as narrative fuel? Does the deathbed sequence treat Mozart's body as dramatic property, or does it preserve some irreducible dignity that resists dramatization? And where does the film distinguish—if it does—between corrupting the historical record, which harms the living, and violating the dead subject's narrative autonomy, which may or may not be a coherent harm? I am not asking for a general defense of historical fiction, but for an interpretation of specific scenes—the Requiem commission, the final dictation, Salieri's closing admission of mediocrity—that takes both the philosophical problem and the film's emotional architecture seriously. If the work ultimately refuses the ethical frame I have imposed, say so clearly; I need to know exactly where the reading breaks down and what the film adds to the debate that argument alone cannot reach.
