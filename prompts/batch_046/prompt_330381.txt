Two members of a study group argued about Kierkegaard's diagnosis of the press and the crowd. The first claimed that his essential point is existential: modern media invent an abstract collective—the public—that is simultaneously everyone and no one, allowing individuals to adopt opinions anonymously and evade personal commitment. The second replied that this captures only the surface; the deeper problem is epistemic leveling, in which the press flattens qualitative distinctions and grants every opinion equal authority regardless of expertise, corrupting judgment itself. She then observed that if the core issue is epistemic, modern algorithmic media should defeat Kierkegaard's critique, since personalization fragments the monolithic press into specialized niches and restores contextual authority. He countered that fragmentation does not revive individual responsibility but merely generates smaller abstract collectives—tribal publics—within which people still hold opinions without existential engagement, so leveling persists in distributed form. Please adjudicate their exchange point by point: rate each move as clearly right, partly right but incomplete, or clearly mistaken, and identify what both interlocutors overlook about the relation between the crowd and the single individual in Kierkegaard's thought.
