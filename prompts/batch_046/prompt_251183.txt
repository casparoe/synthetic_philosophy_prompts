I run a seminar with about 8 grad students in philosophy of mind, 90 minutes, and I want them to actually feel the tension in attributing beliefs to AI when you already know it's just weights — not hear a lecture about Dennett. Specifically I want them to run into the 'predictive delegation' squeeze: you keep using belief-talk because the mechanistic model is too expensive to run in real-time, even though you fully know the mechanisim. They should build the problem themselves before anyone names it.

Start with the bottom line — the one visceral insight the activity should produce — in the first sentence. Then lay out the rules, roles, stakes, and debrief with no preamble and no restating the question. Write casually; a typo or two is fine.

Design a runnable activity. Split them into 'mechanists' (get full architecture docs for a fake AI system — actully a deterministic decision tree with weighted nodes) and 'coordinators' (only interact via a chat interface with that system, trying to predict its outputs). Give them small real stakes: a shared pool of points they can win or lose based on prediction accuracy and explanation speed. Coordinators must submit predictions; mechanists must either explain the move mechanistically or delegate to an intentional model. The catch: the clock is tight — 90 seconds per round — so mechanistic explanations usually miss the window, but intentional ones hit it. After round 3, reveal that the system was never learning, just a scripted tree, yet the intentional model was still the only practical predictor. In round 4, they must decide whether to keep using belief-language knowing the mechanism is fully transparent, with points now tied to group consensus.

The debrief should map each round onto the idea, flag what's predictable to go wrong (mechanists refusing to delegate, coordinators treating belief-talk as discovery rather than tool), and be frank about what the game can't reproduce — probably the long-term ontological slide, since 90 minutes isn't long enough to make that stick. Don't ask for citations or reading lists. Keep it niche but not obscure — inject the predictive-delegation framing — and don't have them hear 'intentional stance' until the very end.
