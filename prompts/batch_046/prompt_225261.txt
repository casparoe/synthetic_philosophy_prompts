I'm on the clinical ethics committee at Riverside General and we have this bizzare case. Patient M had a full corpus callosotomy about five years ago for intractable epilepsy, and now he's up for an elective cochlear implant — not life-or-death, just quality of life improvement. During consent evaluation they ran split-field presentation: present the option to the left visual field (right hemisphere, left hand control) and right visual field (left hemisphere, verbal).

The left hemisphere, which can talk, says yes, proceed, and gives a coherent narrative about wanting to hear music again. The right hemisphere, responding via left-hand pointing and writing with the left hand, consistently refuses, and at one point wrote "dont change my body" on a card. The surgical team argues that the language-dominant hemisphere is the "real" patient, and that the right hemisphere's refusal is just a kind of reflexive aversion without full understanding. They want our memo saying it's okay to go ahead with left-hemisphere consent alone.

M's sister is pushing back hard. She says they've always experienced M as one person since the surgery — he cooks, drives, holds conversations — and that treating one hemisphere like a subroutine is basically denying his personhood. She also mentioned that outside the lab split-brain patients usually act as one integrated agent, so the "two minds" result might be an artifact of how they isolate the hemispheres during testing.

We need to circulate a written opinion by Thursday recommending either: proceed with left-hemisphere consent, require agreement from both sides, or delay until there's some unified preference. The memo should actually take thier argument seriously — not just cite legal capacity law — and it should say something about whether "unity of consciousness" is the right lens here, or if we're conflating personal identity with decision-making authority. Also need to address the strongest point on the losing side honestly.
