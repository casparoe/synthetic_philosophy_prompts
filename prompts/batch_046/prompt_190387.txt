I'm delivering the retirement toast for my former supervisor, who spent thirty-five years in international mediation and is now stepping down. The audience is divided: on one side sit philosophers of language and moral psychologists who will notice any imprecision around Gricean maxims, face-threatening acts, or cross-cultural pragmatics; on the other sit her family and former diplomatic colleagues, for whom the occasion must remain warm and human. She was raised in the Netherlands and worked extensively in Japan; she is known for being brutally direct in internal briefings — she once told a junior negotiator that his proposal was intellectually lazy — yet equally adept at the kind of Japanese-style indirect refusal that allows both parties to preserve honour without either having stated a falsehood outright. I want the toast, which should run about five minutes, to turn on whether the white lies embedded in politeness are morally wrong, but not as a settled lecture. Rather, I want it to introduce and hold open a distinction I have not seen named precisely: between falsifying one's belief (a propositional lie) and falsifying the strength of one's commitment to what one says (an illocutionary mask). On this framing, Dutch directness risks the first kind of failure; Japanese indirectness risks the second. Her career, I suggest, navigated both. The speech must therefore avoid concluding that white lies are either categorically impermissible or harmlessly conventional. Instead, it should leave both halves of the room with the sense that her directness and her diplomacy were two responses to the same coordination problem — how to manage belief and social cost simultaneously — and that the problem admits no tidy resolution. Please write the toast accordingly.
