I facilitate a philosophy seminar of about 16 graduate students in a single 90-minute session on moral comparison and historical evil. Design a structured deliberation activity — not a lecture or debate — that makes participants feel the force of the problem before they can name it: that ranking atrocities seems both unavoidable and obscene. Use four anonymized case profiles of mass violence, stripped of proper names and dates but retaining real-world analogues, that vary along three axes: whether the harm was structurally distributed or centrally directed; whether victims were targeted by an immutable group characteristic or by contingent political affiliation; and whether the harm is in principle reversible by descendants or irreversibly destructive of a form of life. Divide participants into groups of four. Within each group, assign hidden standing roles: a descendant of victims, a member of the perpetrator society, a future citizen with no direct tie, and an archivist who must preserve every narrative without hierarchy. Give them 25 minutes to reach unanimous agreement on how to allocate exactly 100 points of symbolic commemorative resources across the four cases, with a written joint justification required. The incentive is reputational and procedural, not monetary: groups that agree post their allocations anonymously; any group that fails to agree must submit a joint statement declaring comparison impossible, which is then read aloud. Only after allocations are revealed do you disclose the real-world analogues. Provide a debrief that maps specific moments of the deliberation onto parts of the philosophical problem — when quantification felt appropriate, when it felt like desecration, which axis drove conflict, and how the hidden roles distorted or clarified judgment. State precisely what will go wrong the first time you run this — for instance, that participants will revert to implicit death-count metrics despite the framing, or that one standing role will dominate conversation — and how to handle it without undermining the activity. Be explicit about which feature of the problem the activity necessarily cannot reproduce — such as the irreducible particularity of survivor testimony — and how you will supply that verbally. Do not include any bibliography, reading list, or citation requirement in the instructions given to participants, and do not ask them to invoke named authors during the exercise.
