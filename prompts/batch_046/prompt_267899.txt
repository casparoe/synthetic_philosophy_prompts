After three decades of calibrating analytical instruments—where the critical task is always to distinguish a malfunctioning detector from a properly functioning one that happens to be measuring an anomalous sample—I have lately been studying teleosemantic theories that naturalize mental content through evolutionary function. These views maintain that a representational state acquires its content from the biological purpose for which its underlying mechanism was selected. The familiar reply to the problem of misrepresentation is that the mechanism can execute its proper function even when activated outside the "normal conditions" that defined its selection history, thereby producing false content rather than breaking down. Yet I find myself unable to stabilize that distinction. If the proper function of a mechanism is constituted entirely by the selective regime that fixed its design—say, the conditions under which ancestral prey-detection contributed to survival—then the category of "normal conditions" seems to reduce to a statistical description of that regime. On this reading, activation outside that range is not a case of correct operation in abnormal circumstances; it is simply a failure to perform the function that defines the mechanism. In other words, teleosemantics appears to collapse misrepresentation into malfunction, treating every false belief as analogous to a burnt-out sensor rather than to a well-calibrated instrument reporting an outlier. Is this collapse unavoidable, or does it rest on a false presupposition—perhaps that evolutionary history determines token-level performance conditions rather than type-level norms, or that the consumer mechanism's present state plays no constitutive role in fixing content? More generally, can a naturalistic account of mental representation preserve a robust distinction between error and defect without importing normative notions that outstrip biological history?
