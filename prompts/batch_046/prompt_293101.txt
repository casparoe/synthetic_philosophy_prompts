You advise a state environmental board that reviews chemical discharge permits, and the board has adopted a policy that approvals must be defensible under imprecise-probability reasoning—using credal sets with explicit lower and upper probabilities rather than point estimates—because toxicological data are typically sparse, conflicting, and reported with incompatible confidence intervals. Design a reusable one-day review procedure that translates this policy into steps a reviewer with no statistics PhD can follow using only paper, a calculator, and the submitted dossier. The procedure must specify, in order: how to extract lower and upper harm probabilities from each study and expert opinion; how to combine them into a joint credal set using natural extension rather than averaging; which decision rule—E-admissibility versus maximin—applies to the set; the exact conditions that yield “approve,” “approve with monitoring conditions,” “defer for more data,” or “reject”; and the precise points where computation stops and the decision is handed back to the board because the question is one of statutory risk tolerance, not arithmetic. Be explicit about which steps are judgment calls disguised as mechanics—especially how to treat studies using different endpoints or species, and how to interpret the statutory phrase “unreasonable risk” when it must be mapped onto an interval. Include worked applications to three hypothetical permits: (A) a standard industrial solvent with two rat studies giving non-overlapping but adjacent 90% intervals for liver toxicity; (B) a new polymer additive where pooling three expert ranges causes dilation—the upper harm probability rises when evidence is combined—so the procedure produces an answer you, as reviewer, are inclined to resist; and (C) a legacy pesticide with extensive data but contradictory endpoints, where the lower probability of ecological harm falls below the statutory trigger while the upper probability exceeds it by an order of magnitude. In each case, run the steps, state the procedural output, flag where human judgment must override, and say exactly what the procedure cannot settle—such as whether dilation signals hidden correlation or mere ignorance. Lead with the bottom line: the single principle that determines whether the procedure can render any decision at all. Then present the reasoning, the step-by-step procedure, and the three applications, with no preamble and no restatement of the question.
