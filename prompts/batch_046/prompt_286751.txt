As a retired physicist who has only recently begun reading moral philosophy with any rigor, I find myself stuck on a boundary problem. In physics we integrate over fields without asking which particle “owns” the energy; in ethics, the idea that well-being is just a scalar sum over people strikes me as wrong, yet I cannot articulate the precise condition that blocks aggregation in some cases and permits it in others. Below are six concrete judgments that feel mostly stable, though at least one pair pulls in opposite directions.

Case 1 — Direct harvest: A surgeon kills one healthy, non-consenting patient and distributes his organs to save five dying ones. Verdict: impermissible. The one is not a reservoir to be tapped for the net surplus of others.

Case 2 — Distributed construction risk: A public infrastructure project will certainly kill fifty informed, well-compensated workers over its lifetime, but will prevent ten thousand deaths through improved safety. Verdict: permissible, perhaps obligatory. No individual is singled out as fuel; the risk is borne ex ante under a universal rule.

Case 3 — Adaptive trial with continued placebo: Researchers run a vaccine trial with an interim analysis showing efficacy, yet they maintain the placebo arm for statistical power, causing thirty preventable deaths among volunteers. Verdict: impermissible, closer in structure to Case 1 than to Case 2, despite the aggregate scientific benefit.

Case 4 — Progressive redistribution: A tax scheme slightly reduces the welfare of ninety-nine percent to prevent catastrophic deprivation for one percent. Verdict: clearly permissible.

Case 5 — Covert universal optimization: The state secretly adds a compound to the water supply of ten percent of randomly selected citizens, generating large aggregate happiness but violating autonomy without consent. Verdict: impermissible. I feel it should remain wrong even if the selection were universal—everyone receives it—and the net welfare gain enormous.

Case 6 — Rescue triage with risk: A captain can save one trapped child now with certainty, or wait ten minutes to attempt saving five children with an eighty percent success rate (twenty percent chance all six perish, including the one she could have saved now). Verdict: she should attempt the five. Yet this seems to treat the one child as substitutable in a way Case 1 forbids.

My tentative framing is a “non-substitution constraint combined with an ex ante fairness filter”: aggregation across persons is permitted only when every affected party faces the burden or risk under a prior universal rule that no one could reject without arbitrary privilege, and never when an identifiable individual is used as a mere input to generate surplus well-being for others. Case 5 troubles this, because the burden is universal yet I still reject it; Case 6 troubles it, because the one child is not being used as an input, yet I am willing to risk her.

For each clause or component principle, identify the specific case that motivates it. The principle should address aggregation across persons, the role of consent, direct agency, and the distinction between risk imposition and actual harm.
