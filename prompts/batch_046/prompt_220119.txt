In my philosophy of collective action seminar, a recurring analogy compares joint intentional action to playing a string quartet: each musician reads an individual part, forms private intentions about tempo and dynamics, and adjusts to the others through auditory feedback, yet no single consciousness directs the ensemble, and the quartet-performance is treated as nothing over and above the meshed individual performances. The analogy is used to defend a reductive claim — that collective intentionality is fully analyzable as interlocking individual intentions, that there is no need for a group mind or a plural subject, and that so-called we-intentions are merely convenient descriptions of this mesh. I want to audit how far this holds. Could you lay out the correspondence table explicitly — what plays the role of the score, the absent conductor, the acoustic space, each musician’s private mental states, and the finished sound — and then identify where the mapping breaks? Specifically, does the quartet model quietly import a pre-given coordination structure that genuine joint action often lacks? Does it obscure the normative dimension emphasized by accounts that invoke joint commitment or we-mode attitudes, where participants become answerable to one another in ways that are not reducible to each player’s individual contract with the ensemble? And if we take seriously the idea that in authentic joint action there is a collective obligation that survives even when individual sub-plans diverge, does the reductive conclusion the analogy was meant to license still stand, or was the analogy carrying that conclusion all along? If the analogy collapses on these points, what would be a more accurate model — perhaps one drawn from shared practical reasoning rather than from pre-scored performance?
