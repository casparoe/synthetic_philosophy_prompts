I've been working through some puzzles about deduction on my own, and I keep returning to Lewis Carroll's dialogue between Achilles and the Tortoise. From what I can gather, the tortoise demonstrates that if you want to justify using a basic inference rule—say, modus ponens—you have to invoke a further rule that tells you how to apply it, and then yet another rule for that one, which creates an endless regress. It strikes me that Carroll must be pointing toward the need for a special "ground-level" principle that licenses itself without requiring any higher-order authorization, otherwise deduction could never actually get started. I wonder whether modern logic has identified a specific rule that plays this self-licensing role, or whether the regress is meant to show that deduction ultimately rests on something outside logic altogether.

As I tried to think this through, I drew a distinction that I haven't seen clearly articulated: between what I'd call "horizontal" inference steps, where you apply a rule to premises you already accept, and "vertical" authorization steps, where you move up to justify the rule itself. It seems to me that Carroll's tortoise might only be blocking the horizontal path—each time Achilles attempts to apply a rule to reach Z, the tortoise demands a vertical justification for that very rule—while leaving open the possibility that a properly vertical climb eventually reaches a self-justifying principle at the top. So I'm asking whether this horizontal-vertical distinction resolves the paradox: does Carroll's challenge collapse once you separate applying a rule within a proof from authorizing the rule across levels, or does the tortoise's objection cut through both directions?

If I'm right that there's a self-applying rule that stops the regress at the top, I'd like to understand exactly what that rule is and whether it avoids circularity. But if I've misunderstood Carroll—perhaps by treating logical rules like mechanical actions that need pushing rather than recognizing that the tortoise's real difficulty concerns accepting a hypothetical premise rather than justifying an inference—then I'd appreciate being corrected on that point, and being shown where the horizontal-vertical framing goes wrong.
