Hello — I'm a retired wetland ecologist who got into philosphy last year after too much time with muddy boots, and I've been trying to write about whether leaning on AI for reasoning rots our understanding from the inside. I've sketched some rough notes that borrow from trophic cascade theory, and I'd be grateful for your comments. Specifically: does the analogy actually illuminate anything, or am I just dressing up a familiar worry in biological jargon? Am I mangling the knowing-how versus knowing-that distinction? And is the "phase transition" bit pure melodrama? Please reply in British English — I'm trying to sound less American. I'm not after a bibliography or reading list; I want to see if the idea stands up on it's own.

My notes:

Using AI for reasoning isn't like outsourcing a job; it's like introducing a non-native filter-feader into a pond. At first the water looks clearer because the AI chews through the hard reasoning (the algae). But the native grazers — our own confusion, error-correction, slow digestion of contradiction — stop reproducing because there's no food left for them. I call this a trophic decoupling of cognition. The scary part isn't linear decline; it's a sudden collapse, like a pond flipping state but in reverse. Right up to the collapse the user feels sharper, because the AI keeps producing clear answers. Once decoupled, understanding doesn't gradually fade — it collapses, and the user may not notice because the outputs still look healthy.

Does that hold water, or have I just invented "trophic decoupling" to sound clever? And please be honest about where the argument breaks.
