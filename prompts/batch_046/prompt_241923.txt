As a doctoral student working on temporal phenomenology and post-perceptual construction, I am trying to clarify a conceptual knot in the Orwellian versus Stalinesque framing of retrospective revision, particularly as applied to the color-phi phenomenon. The standard dichotomy holds that either the system literally overwrites an earlier discrete representation of color at a specific location (the Orwellian route), or it fabricates a coherent narrative of motion-and-change without ever having hosted the intermediate sensory state (the Stalinesque route). I want you to explain the conceptual problem that arises if we allow a third structural option—retrospective binding with temporal smearing—in which the cognitive system assigns a transitional phenomenal property to a spatiotemporal interval that was never occupied by a unified perceptual object, yet does so without altering any prior discrete token-representation and without constructing a fully formed prior experience ex nihilo. Specifically: does this intermediate option preserve the intuition that undergoing the transition has subjective character, or does it force the abandonment of the principle that phenomenal continuity requires a correspondingly continuous neural vehicle? Furthermore, explain how this framing interacts with the two standard constraints—avoiding the memory-corruption objection typically pressed against Orwellian accounts and respecting the no-privileged-present condition that motivates Stalinesque ones. I am not asking for an empirical survey or a bibliography; I need the conceptual mechanics unpacked with precision, including which of the three options best accommodates the idea that the binding occurs after the constituent stimuli have been registered but before they are consolidated into working memory.
