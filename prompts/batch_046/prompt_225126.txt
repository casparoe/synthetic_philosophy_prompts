The question under examination is whether present indicators of a contemporary superpower’s relative decline can be meaningfully forecast through historical analogy.

Structure the deliverable in two parts. First, a numbered section of agreed claims (approximately eight to ten items) covering: the logical structure of a reference class; the distinction between epistemic uncertainty about class membership and ontological uniqueness of the case; the conditions under which selecting a comparison class reduces versus amplifies bias; and the minimal conditions for treating institutional adaptability as either a parameter within the class or as part of a singular causal narrative. Second, a disagreements table with columns labeled Crux, Outside-View Formulation, Inside-View Formulation, and Agreed Bearing Case.

The table should address two cruxes. Crux one: whether institutional adaptability can be parameterized within a reference class or must remain embedded in a non-comparable causal story. Crux two: whether the available sample of post-hegemonic transitions is large enough to support probabilistic prediction rather than narrative explanation. For the agreed bearing case, both sides should treat the paired comparison of Britain’s devolution of direct imperial control (c. 1945–1965) and the Ottoman Empire’s administrative centralization under the Tanzimat reforms (1839–1876) as a shared test.
