I lead engineering for a clinical-decision-support chatbot. The system computes an internal confidence score for every answer. Our current protocol is straightforward: above 95 percent confidence, the bot states the answer directly; below that, it opens with "I am not certain, but..." and advises consulting a physician. In recent user studies we found an unexpected pattern: patients and nurses place greater trust in the bot when it uses the cautious opening, apparently interpreting the hedge as evidence of careful judgment rather than ignorance. Our product team now proposes lowering the confidence threshold for the hedge to 98 percent, so the bot will routinely downplay its certainty even on high-confidence recommendations. The goal is behavioral—more users will seek second opinions, which improves safety metrics—but one senior engineer argues that this amounts to deliberately making the system misrepresent its own state of assurance, which is wrong regardless of the outcome. Another team member counters that misrepresentation requires a mind that can be deceived, and since the bot has no beliefs or intentions, we are merely adjusting an interface tone. I must decide whether to approve the threshold change. Is the engineer correct that this practice is a form of deception, and does the absence of human-like belief or intent in the system change whether that matters?
