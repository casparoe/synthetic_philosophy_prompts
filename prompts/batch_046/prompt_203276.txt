I've been in a political philosophy reading group for about three years and we've been going in circles on open borders versus the right to exclude. Everyone's read the standard stuff, we all agree suffering is bad and that immigration has complicated economic effects, but the open-borders folks and the restrictionists haven't moved an inch. Lately I've been playing with this idea I call "membership velocity" — basically the rate at which a society can absorb newcomers before its norms of reciprocity and shared expectation start to fray. The open-borders people in our group seem to treat velocity as basically infinite if institutions are well-designed, while the restrictionists talk as if it's effectively zero for any community with a real history. So I'm wondering: is our deadlock actually a hidden empirical disagreement about velocity and institutional capacity? Or is it that the restrictionists are smuggling in a value — collective self-determination, or the idea that "we" have a right to preserve our cooperative framework — that the other side doesn't recognize as a legitimate moral reason? Maybe it's bedrock intuition about whether borders are just administrative lines or whether they track something real about shared fate. I want a diagnosis, not a verdict: what kind of disagreement is this actually, and given that diagnosis, is there anything — a distinction, a study, a change of framing — that could actually move it? And please don't just run through the usual brain-drain and cultural-preservation arguments; I'm tired of hearing those for the millionth time and they never get us anywhere.
