I just finished a long, somewhat heated argument with a close friend about whether Tolstoy’s late aesthetic theory—especially the distinction in What Is Art? between true art, which infects its audience with a sincerely felt, morally unifying emotion that is accessible to everyone regardless of education, and counterfeit art, which appeals to obscure technique, elite sensibility, or base instinct—can be applied without distortion to modern novels that deliberately cultivate horror, pessimism, and symbolic ambiguity. We kept circling back to Lord of the Flies: my friend insisted that Golding’s novel is precisely the kind of counterfeit work Tolstoy condemns, because it replaces brotherly feeling with visceral dread, uses allegorical symbols—the conch, the beast, Piggy’s spectacles—that risk obscurity for ordinary readers, and ultimately denies any possibility of moral redemption. I pushed back that the novel’s refusal to hide the boys’ descent behind stylistic ornament might actually satisfy Tolstoy’s demand for sincerity, and that the rescue scene, with Ralph weeping before the naval officer, could be read as a moment of shared human vulnerability that achieves the union Tolstoy demands, even if the irony of the officer’s own war-torn world complicates it.

Read Lord of the Flies through Tolstoy’s lens, but do not reduce either to an illustration. Where does the theory illuminate specific scenes—the breakdown of the conch’s democratic authority, the murder of Piggy as a catastrophic failure of shared feeling, Simon’s solitary encounter with the beast as a private rather than communal religious experience? Where does the novel resist Tolstoy, particularly in its refusal to offer a redemptive religious framework and in its apparent reliance on savagery and horror to move the reader rather than on love? And where does Tolstoy’s framework itself seem inadequate to the work—for instance in judging whether symbolic allegory necessarily obstructs emotional transmission, or whether irony can serve as a vehicle for the very sincerity he requires?

I am preparing an essay that takes both the novel and Tolstoy’s criteria seriously, and I want to know whether the work adds something to the philosophical debate that argument alone does not—whether Golding’s dramatization of failed moral community reveals a limit in Tolstoy’s assumption that true art can always bridge the gap between individual feeling and universal brotherhood, or whether it instead confirms that art which abandons that bridge collapses into the counterfeit. Give me a disciplined reading that says where the mapping holds, where it breaks, and what an honest critic must concede about both.
