I'm planning a teaching session for a small rationalist reading group—people fluent in Schelling points, evolutionary game theory, and Bayesian updating, but with no background in philosophy of language. The target idea is David Lewis's account of convention: a regularity that solves a coordination problem, sustained not merely by mutual expectation but by common knowledge of each agent's preference for conformity, and capable of evolving without central design.

Please lay out the explanatory arc rather than delivering it: the exact dependency order (what must be secured before coordination problems are introduced, before common knowledge is added, and before evolutionary dynamics are discussed); the single carrying example that should anchor the idea and the features it must possess and lack; the three predictable misconceptions this audience will bring (for instance, collapsing convention into social contract, treating common knowledge as mere shared belief, or substituting evolutionary stability for the rational-preference layer), mapped to the specific point in the sequence where each should be neutralized; a brief check for understanding that proves they have grasped the normative-binding aspect rather than only recognizing that conventions are arbitrary; and what should be explicitly omitted at this level.

Finally, weave in one modern twist without letting it dominate: the group will inevitably ask about explicitly bootstrapped online norms—e.g., a forum rule against low-effort replies that begins as moderator policy but becomes self-sustaining. Indicate where in the dependency sequence this example belongs and how to use it to illuminate rather than confuse the core idea.
