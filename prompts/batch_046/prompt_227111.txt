I'm preparing a unit for a graduate seminar on time and physics, and my students keep treating the Rietdijk–Putnam argument as though special relativity alone dissolves the A-theory of time. I want them to see the conceptual machinery rather than just the slogan, so please explain—at an intuitive but technically serious level—why the inference from the frame-relativity of simultaneity to a four-dimensional block universe requires a non-obvious philosophical step, not just a physical observation. In particular, focus on the tension that arises if a presentist responds not by denying Minkowski spacetime, but by localizing "the present" to each observer's worldline: does this salvage genuine temporal becoming, or does it merely fragment "now" into an incoherent patchwork of incompatible local presents with no shared metaphysical structure?

Address whether the argument subtly conflates the absence of a globally privileged hyperplane of simultaneity with the absence of any metaphysically robust relation of "being present to," and clarify exactly where the physics of Lorentz transformations ends and the ontology of temporal passage begins. I am not asking for a bibliography or a history of the debate; I want the conceptual knot untangled so that someone who grasps the geometry can still see why the metaphysics does not automatically follow, and where a defender of passage might consistently resist the block conclusion without misunderstanding the relativity of simultaneity.
