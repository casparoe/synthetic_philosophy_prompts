One side is the behavoral economists who model weakness of will as hyperbolic discounting — you know, the beta-delta thing where present bias makes your future prefferences reverse. The other side is the practical-reason philosophers who think akrasia is a real failure: you genuinely judge that X is best and still do Y, and that's not reducible to a parameter in a utility function.

I want them to draft the joint statement they'd both sign if they were being honest. First part: numbered agreed claims about what hyperbolic discounting predicts, what the phenomomology of acting-against-better-judgement is like, and whether self-binding contracts of the Ulysses kind actually restructure preferences or just block them. Second part: a disagreements table with the exact splits, worded so each camp recognises its own view — not some strawman. Then the cruxes: is present bias itself irrational, or just non-standard rationality? Does binding your future self restore diachronic authority, or does it just move the irrationality back to the binding moment? For each crux they need a concrete case both accept is relevant — maybe the deposit-contract smoking study, or that scenario where you lock your phone in a timed box — and each side writes its prediction about what that case shows.
