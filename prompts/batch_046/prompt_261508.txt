My half-formed idea concerns Fernand Braudel's distinction between the longue durée—geography, climate, and slow-moving social structures—and the level of events and individual agency. Most interpretations treat this as a hierarchy of temporal scales: climate changes over centuries, economies over decades, politics over years. I suspect this temporal framing misses the underlying logical distinction, which is about counterfactual stability rather than duration. Specifically, I want to ask whether "slow structure" explanations in history are best understood as claims about modal robustness—the claim that altering a geographical or climatic parameter requires changing background conditions that are themselves invariant across the relevant historical counterfactuals—whereas event-level explanations depend on interventions that leave those background conditions intact. If this is right, then debates about whether geography or institutions explain, say, the divergence of Mediterranean and Atlantic commercial networks in the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries are not competing on empirical ground alone, but on different implicit models of what counts as a permissible counterfactual.
