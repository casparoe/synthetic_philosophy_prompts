Write a parody comparison review in the deadpan technical voice of a Consumer Reports buyer’s guide, approximately 650 words, evaluating three competing smart-home “emotion-recognition” appliances—each engineered according to one major philosophical theory of emotion. Name them the Stoic Module (judgment or cognitivist theory: emotions are evaluative judgments about the world), the Perceptual Array (perceptual theory: emotions are direct perceptions of value or affective salience, analogous to seeing color), and the Somatic Sensor (feeling or James-Lange-style theory: emotions are conscious registrations of bodily changes).

The satire must arise from treating these philosophical positions as literal engineering specifications and stress-testing them against ordinary domestic failures—burnt toast, a misplaced set of keys, a neighbor’s loud party—so that each device’s practical malfunction mirrors a recognized philosophical objection. The Stoic Module should fail when a user holds contradictory judgments yet reports a single unified feeling; the Perceptual Array should generate false positives in “value-illusion” conditions (e.g., labeling routine indigestion as existential dread); and the Somatic Sensor should be unable to distinguish anger at an injustice from anger caused by low blood sugar, requiring the user to manually input heart-rate variability and skin conductance before the device can declare what emotion is present.

To keep the prompt from being generic textbook rehearsal, frame the review within a fictional but precisely described regulatory context: a 2024 EU Affective-Device Certification Directive that requires every emotion-classifier sold in member states to declare which ontology of affect it presupposes. Include a deadpan sidebar on the “inter-rater reliability crisis” in multi-user households—referencing, without citing actual literature, a plausible-sounding internal study in which couples calibrating the same device produced divergent emotion-labels for identical physiological events, apparently because partners held different implicit theories about whether anger is a judgment, a perception, or a bodily feeling. The humor should stay entirely deadpan; the reader should recognize that the philosophical problems are genuinely embedded in the product failures, and the piece should be readable even to someone unfamiliar with the philosophy of emotion.

Conclude with a refusal to crown a winner: note that all three devices received statistically identical user-satisfaction scores from households that had never encountered philosophy, then add a brief deadpan “quietist” postscript stating that the testing laboratory has now adopted a policy of measuring only galvanic skin response and filing all outputs under “undifferentiated arousal, cause unknown.” The target of the satire is the implicit assumption that cognitivism, perceptualism, and feeling-theory are competing empirical hypotheses that could be settled by better sensors or larger datasets, rather than conceptual disagreements about what emotions essentially are. Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or requests for citations; the review should read as a self-contained document.
