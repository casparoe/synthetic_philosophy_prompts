I am part of a small interdisciplinary seminar on comparative development, and a recurring tension has emerged between two of us regarding the epistemic limits of macrohistory. My interlocutor maintains that, within the coming decades, advances in synthetic counterfactual inference—applied to naturally occurring partitions, colonial administrative boundaries, and sudden institutional ruptures—will converge on a settled, institution-first account of national income divergence, complete with quantified predictive error bounds that rival those in observational epidemiology.

I am skeptical, not because I deny that institutions matter, but because I suspect the deeper obstacle is structural overdetermination: geography, institutional path dependence, and cultural norms are so recursively coupled in actual historical trajectories that isolating any one factor via counterfactual design necessarily severs the very mechanism that produces the outcome. Consequently, I doubt that any single-factor explanation could ever bear the full explanatory burden we typically assign it in policy debates.

Given this framing, I want your actual credence—expressed as a precise number between zero and one—that it is in principle possible for a single explanatory factor, whether institutional, geographic, or cultural, to be shown to carry more than seventy percent of the identifiable causal weight in explaining cross-national variance in long-run economic prosperity. Assume unlimited archival data and computational resources, but no possibility of laboratory repetition or true societal randomization. Please decompose your reasoning into explicit sub-questions: (a) the likelihood that macrohistorical processes are sufficiently modular to permit clean counterfactual isolation without destroying the phenomenon under study; (b) the likelihood that institutional arrangements are genuinely exogenous to geographic and cultural conditions over multi-century horizons; (c) whether the notion of percentage-based causal weight is conceptually coherent in domains characterized by interactive, path-dependent causation; and (d) one further term of your own choosing that you think materially affects the aggregate. For each term, provide a rough probability and a single line of justification, then show how they combine to yield your final figure. Finally, identify which of these terms is the weakest link in your reasoning, and describe—without naming specific texts—what kind of conceptual argument or empirical pattern would most substantially revise it.
