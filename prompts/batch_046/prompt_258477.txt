I am presenting a seminar paper to a joint audience of political philosophers and historians of nationalism at my university’s centre for collective memory studies. My thesis is that enduring lost-cause national myths—exemplified by the postbellum American Confederacy, the Masada narrative in Zionist historiography, the Kosovo Polje myth in Serbian national consciousness, and the Dunkirk evacuation in British collective memory—share a structural feature I term “sacrificial deferral”: the historical defeat is not denied but reinterpreted as a necessary, generative sacrifice that suspends rather than terminates the collective’s claim to sovereignty. I contend that this distinguishes genuinely mythologised lost causes from mere historical revisionism, and that assessing these narratives solely by factual fidelity therefore misses their functional role in maintaining what I call “post-defeat civic continuity”.

The audience comprises: (i) two historians of the American South who regard analogising the Confederacy with emancipatory narratives as a category error that risks moral equivalence; (ii) a philosopher of collective intentionality committed to the view that collective memory requires representational accuracy for moral agency to persist; (iii) a critical theorist suspicious that functionalist accounts sanitise authoritarian nostalgia; and (iv) several comparative-nationalism scholars who are agnostic but protective of case-specific historiography. I need a Q&A prep sheet: the objections I will actually receive, ordered by how damaging they are to my core argument, phrased in the vocabulary each subgroup would use. For each, indicate what I should concede up front, where I should hold firm, the two-minute reply I should deliver, and the weakest point in that reply. Please also include the one objection I am hoping nobody raises—the one that exposes the tension between my functionalism and the normative evaluation of causes that were unjust from the outset.

Please state, for each reply you propose, how confident you are in its adequacy, and single out the point about which you are least sure—particularly regarding whether my distinction between sacrificial deferral and ordinary revisionism can survive scrutiny across all four cases without collapsing into an ad hoc taxonomy.
