I am a doctoral student in philosophy of mind and cognitive science preparing assessment materials for a graduate seminar on personal identity, substrate-independent cognition, and the ontology of mental patterns. I want a take-home essay prompt that forces students to work through Douglas Hofstadter’s claim that the self is an epiphenomenal strange loop—a symbol-level pattern capable, in principle, of being copied or distributed—without letting them fall back on a summary of his well-known monograph on strange loops and self-reference, or on standard psychological-continuity arguments they could recite from memory.

The assessment must center on a specific, somewhat niche conceptual tension. If a strange loop is genuinely substrate-independent, then distributing its functional correlates across two physically separated but causally coupled brains (for instance, via sustained high-bandwidth inter-brain interfaces) seems to permit exactly two outcomes, neither of which is intuitive: either (i) a single token-identity of the loop is spread across both substrates, so that one and the same “I” is literally distributed, or (ii) token-identity is destroyed altogether, leaving only two type-similar copies that jointly instantiate no unified self—what one might call a “we-without-an-I.” The exam question should ask students to determine whether Hofstadter’s own resources—particularly his distinction between symbol-level patterns and their physical instantiation, and his rejection of localized “soul-stuff”—can adjudicate between these outcomes. They must also address whether the intuition that an “I” must be localized within one brain-boundary is a metaphysical prejudice or a constraint internal to the strange-loop account itself.

Please provide the following three components, written in a formal, polished register appropriate for a PhD-level exam design brief:

1. The exam prompt for students. It should specify a word limit of approximately 1,200 words and a 72-hour window, and it should be phrased so that a satisfactory answer requires constructing an original argument rather than reciting literature. The prompt must explicitly require students to distinguish token-identity of a strange loop from type-identity of a self-pattern distributed over coupled substrates, and to evaluate whether causal coupling between brains is sufficient to preserve token-identity or only reproduces a similar copy.

2. A model answer of approximately 700 words. It should take a clear, defended position on whether distributed instantiation across coupled brains preserves token-identity of the self, use Hofstadter’s concepts (strange loops, symbol-level patterns, epiphenomenalism about the self) correctly without relying on verbatim quotation, and directly address both the “we-without-an-I” objection and the possibility that brain-boundary conditions are metaphysically necessary.

3. A marking scheme that separates an A-range answer from a B-range answer by listing the specific conceptual moves each must contain—for example: correctly distinguishing token- from type-identity in loop instantiation; explaining why causal coupling is not by itself sufficient for token-preservation; evaluating whether Hofstadter’s rejection of soul-stuff commits him to accepting distributed selves; and addressing the “we-without-an-I” collapse explicitly. The scheme should also flag common misconceptions that should cost marks, such as conflating distributed cognition (e.g., extended mind scenarios) with distributed selfhood, treating physical brain boundaries as metaphysically necessary without independent argument, or assuming that substrate-independence automatically entails token-copying.

The assessment should avoid asking students for bibliographies, reading lists, citation lists, or references to specific page numbers, since it is designed as an open-book conceptual examination rather than a literature-review task. Do not include verbatim quotations from real texts in the model answer; if you reference specific claims, paraphrase them without quotation marks.
