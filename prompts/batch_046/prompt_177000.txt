I'm preparing material for an interdisciplinary workshop on epistemic responsibility in collaborative science, and I've become stuck on a tension that the standard philosophical framing of assertion norms doesn't seem to address directly. The familiar debate asks whether one should assert only what one knows, only what one believes, or only what is true; but this framing appears to presuppose an individual asserter who holds the relevant epistemic position in some direct, first-personal way. In much of contemporary scientific and clinical practice, however, experts routinely assert conclusions—such as that a particular ensemble climate model projects 2.5°C warming by 2100, or that an AI diagnostic system indicates a specific pathology—where their own direct understanding of the underlying computation, parameter tuning, or data pipeline is partial at best, and the epistemic weight is genuinely distributed across instruments, algorithms, and sub-teams.

Write an essay that takes a definite stand on whether the knowledge norm of assertion can survive this challenge from distributed epistemics without collapsing into incoherence. Weigh the knowledge norm carefully against both a revised belief norm (assert if you rationally believe p, regardless of whether you personally know it) and a process-reliability norm (assert if you are justified in trusting the distributed process that produced p, even in the absence of personal knowledge). Your essay must reach and defend a clear verdict—either that distributed expert assertion is compatible with the knowledge norm provided we reconceptualize knowledge as collective or systemic, or that maintaining a strict individual knowledge norm forces us to condemn much legitimate expert communication as epistemically improper. In justifying your conclusion, analyze in detail two concrete scenarios: a physician who asserts a diagnosis generated by a deep-learning model she cannot fully interpret, and a climate scientist who asserts an ensemble projection based on sub-models she did not personally code or verify. Use these cases not as mere illustrations but as genuine tests of whether your chosen norm can distinguish permissible epistemic delegation from reckless outsourcing, and defend your verdict without ending on a non-committal note.
