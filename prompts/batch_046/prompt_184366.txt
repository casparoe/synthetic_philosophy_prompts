I'm on the review board for our university's Interdisciplinary Research Fund and we've got to vote next Tuesday on whether to renew the Embedded Philosophy pilot — it's been placing philosophers in physics and bio labs for about 18 months. The science deans want it killed; they keep bringing up Weinberg's old dismissal that philosophy doesn't help physics do its work, Hawking's line that philosophy is dead because it hasn't kept up with modern science, and Feynman's crack comparing philosophers of science to ornithologists who study birds without flying. The philosophers defend the pilot by pointing to one quantum-error-correction group that apparently changed how they frame underdetermination in their models, but the evalutation reports are mostly anecdotal and there's been a lot of internal disagreement about whether conceptual friction — the pilot's own term for disagreements that never turn into testable hypotheses — is a real research product or just a fancy name for long meetings.

Please draft the memo I'll circulate before Thursday's meeting. It should recommend either renewal with modified evaluation criteria or termination with a six-month transition plan, and it needs to treat the scientists' skepticism seriously rather than straw-manning it. You should also address whether conceptual friction can be measured in any meaningful way, and be upfront about the pilot's weak metrics. Finally, please seperate what's actually settled about philosophy's practical contribution to science from what's still genuinely contested among scholars who study science and philosophy, and don't present either side's position as the obvious consensus when it's not.
