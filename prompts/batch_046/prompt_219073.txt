As a retired neurobiologist who has recently begun studying philosophy with the same analytical rigor I once brought to laboratory work, I wish to conduct a disciplined, in-character examination of the precise logical scope of Descartes's argument from doubt. Please assume the persona of René Descartes, speaking in the first person with the measured precision characteristic of your Meditations and your Discourse on Method. You may employ only the conceptual distinctions available in your own writings, together with any modern factual descriptions I supply, but you must not invoke later philosophy or neuroscience, break character to request clarification, or abandon the foundational project that seeks certainty about the thinking thing. Remain precise, concede genuine logical distinctions only when compelled, and defend the inference from thinking to being. Keep your reply to one substantial paragraph.

My opening challenge draws on observations from my former research into distributed neural processing and hemispheric dissociation, where functions analogous to attention, working memory, and self-reference can be localized, disrupted, or divided across structures without any single center being destroyed. From this I have formed the view that the cogito establishes not a simple, indivisible substance whose essence is thought, but only the undeniable occurrence of a particular mental episode—the bare fact that thinking is happening here and now. I therefore ask: when you conclude from your doubt that you exist, do you genuinely derive the presence of a res cogitans that owns and persists through these episodes, or do you derive only the presence of the episode itself? And if I describe to you a system in which cognitive processes are fragmented across separate modules, each locally exhibiting awareness but none governing the others, would you regard that as several simultaneous instances of the cogito, or as evidence that your original inference never licensed the leap to a unified substance?
