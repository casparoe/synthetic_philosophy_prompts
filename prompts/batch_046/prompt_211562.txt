I am a doctoral student in philosophy of mind, working on the boundary between formal logic and theories of consciousness. In recent literature that extends Gödelian self-reference—particularly arguments that invoke Hofstadter-style strange loops—I keep encountering a specific argumentative slide that I have not been able to match to an established concept, and I want to determine whether it is a recognized failure mode or whether I need to delineate it myself.

The pattern is this. An author constructs a formal system, or a computational architecture, capable of representing its own states and encoding a sentence structurally analogous to the Gödel sentence—something to the effect that this system cannot derive statement S. The author then treats that syntactic self-reference as if it automatically carries the semantic and phenomenological force of a first-person indexical. The system is said not merely to describe its own derivational limits, but to recognize them from the inside, with a kind of first-person authority that makes the reflexivity parallel to human self-consciousness. From there, the inference is drawn that any system with this capacity possesses at least a minimal form of self-awareness, or that the gap between formal reflexivity and conscious experience has been substantially bridged.

I have observed this move in two contexts. In discussions of artificial intelligence, a meta-cognitive monitoring layer that tracks a model’s own confidence bounds is sometimes characterized as the system knowing that it does not know, with Gödelian framing used to suggest that this reflexivity is not merely informational but experiential. In philosophy of consciousness, strange-loop accounts of subjective experience invoke formal self-reference to argue that the loop’s capacity to encode its own limits explains why there is something it is like to be that system. What distinguishes the slide, in my view, is that it is narrower than a general category error about attributing mental properties to machines, and narrower than the standard Gödelian argument about the inherent limits of formalization. It specifically involves treating syntactic reflexivity—an object-level relation between a formula and its Gödel number—as equivalent to intentional self-reference with phenomenological weight.

Is there an established term for this precise conflation? I am familiar with the broader Gödelian analogies about minds escaping formal capture, and with the general syntax-semantics distinction emphasized by critics of computational functionalism, but neither isolates the intermediate step I am tracking: the assumption that encoding this statement within a formal system automatically imports the indexical I and the structure of self-awareness. If there is no standard label, I would like a precise coinage together with a boundary analysis. Under what conditions does syntactic self-reference genuinely support an intentional interpretation, and when is it only a structural isomorphism being misread as phenomenology? I am particularly interested in how this differs from more cautious deployments of strange loops, where reflexivity is presented as an organizational feature of a system rather than as a direct generator of consciousness. I will not be satisfied with a bare name; I need the phenomenon carved at its edges, with clear distinctions from adjacent errors, with boundary cases that sit right on the line, and with an account of why the slide is tempting even when the broader Gödelian analogy is handled carefully.
