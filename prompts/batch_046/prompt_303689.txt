I'm preparing an argument for a seminar in philosophy of physics on whether Newton's bucket genuinely forces us toward substantivalism, and in trying to formalize the relationist reply I've ended up with a chain that seems to prove far too much. The argument runs as follows.

(1) Substantivalists read the bucket experiment as demonstrating absolute rotation: the concavity of the water reveals motion with respect to space itself, not merely with respect to other material bodies. (2) If absolute space exists only to ground this specific distinction—between genuinely rotating and merely relatively rotating systems—then it is an unobservable theoretical posit with a single explanatory function. (3) There is a principle of explanatory exclusivity: when a theoretical entity's sole empirical role can be performed by other entities already in our ontology (here, distant cosmic matter, as Mach proposed), we should eliminate the redundant entity. (4) Applying (3) to absolute space yields the standard relationist result: eliminate space and explain inertial effects, including the bucket's concavity, through relations to distant matter. (5) But (3) is domain-general: it applies whenever a structure serves only one correlational or explanatory role that its relata can discharge without it. (6) In contemporary physics, gauge potentials, the spacetime metric, and quantum fields frequently play exactly this kind of singular relational role—fixing correlations among observable events—without being independently detectable. (7) Therefore, by the very reasoning that eliminates absolute space, we ought to eliminate the metric, gauge structures, and indeed any theoretical framework that is not directly observed. (8) Since this would gut modern physics, the reasoning must fail somewhere.

My instinct is to reject (5) and restrict (3) to cases where a genuine relational substitute exists, which would preserve the Machian critique of space but block the slide toward general eliminativism about unobservable structure. But I'm unsure whether that restriction is ad hoc, or whether the real error lies in (2)—perhaps absolute space has other indispensable roles, such as grounding the geodesic structure that distant matter alone cannot determine. I want a committed answer, not a survey: identify the exact step that breaks the chain, explain why it looked plausible, and tell me what I must pay if I reject it—specifically, whether saving modern physics forces me to abandon relationism about space altogether, or whether I can keep (4) intact while paying a different price.
