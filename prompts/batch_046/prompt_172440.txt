I am a doctoral student in moral philosophy working on the boundary between legitimate persuasion and manipulation, with particular attention to cases in which the persuader designs the epistemic path—ordering, framing, or selectively highlighting premises—rather than merely supplying arguments. I have the following intuitive verdicts about concrete cases, and I want to know whether a single principle can capture them, or whether I must abandon one or two.

Case 1: A climate advocate structures a public workshop so that participants encounter evidence in a fixed sequence—first localized personal narratives, then regional impact data, then global models—knowing that this ordering maximizes acceptance of a carbon-tax conclusion without misrepresenting any individual datum. I judge this legitimate persuasion.

Case 2: A therapist, recognizing that her patient suffers from a known cognitive bias that obscures recognition of emotional abuse, repeatedly directs attention to specific incidents through Socratic questioning but never explicitly states the conclusion. The patient eventually forms the belief that she is being abused through what feels like independent reasoning. I judge this legitimate.

Case 3: A political campaign uses psychological profiling to deliver different emotional frames of an identical healthcare policy to different demographic groups, selecting frames calculated to bypass critical scrutiny in each group. I judge this manipulation.

Case 4: A physics instructor deploys an intentionally false mechanical analogy for quantum entanglement, knowing it is technically inaccurate but that it will correctly orient students’ practical reasoning and that technical correction will follow. I judge this legitimate persuasion.

Case 5: A friend, attempting to convince me to apply to graduate school, deliberately omits her own history of rejections, not because she regards the information as irrelevant, but because she predicts I will overweight it due to an availability bias. I judge this manipulation.

Case 6: A philosopher in a public debate arranges her arguments in an order calculated to exploit her opponent’s known commitment to logical consistency, thereby forcing him into a conclusion he would reject if the premises were presented differently. The moves are entirely rational; only the architecture is engineered. I judge this manipulation, despite its rational form.

My attempts to systematize these cluster around the idea that manipulation occurs when the persuader exploits a feature of the target’s reasoning architecture that is locally reason-bypassing rather than globally reason-responsive, combined with a misalignment between the persuader’s design aim and the target’s own reflective values. Yet this seems to make Case 1 problematic—if the narrative-to-data sequence exploits well-known primacy and affective-anchoring effects—while Case 2 seems to exploit a bias (the obscuring bias) for rational correction. Meanwhile, Case 6 involves no local irrationality at all, only strategic ordering of rational premises, yet I still want it to count as manipulation.

Is there a more precise principle—perhaps one that distinguishes between restructuring the path of reasoning in ways that preserve or enhance the target’s second-order monitoring capacities versus those that disable or circumvent them—that can accommodate all six verdicts? Or must I drop Case 1 or Case 6? Please state the principle precisely, indicate which clause is motivated by which case, say what it predicts for cases not listed (for example, a salesperson who uses a default-opt-in design for a subscription that is genuinely valuable but hard to evaluate, or a physician who offers a false mechanistic explanation of a placebo in order to trigger a real placebo analgesic effect that she knows will benefit the patient), and be explicit about which of my six verdicts the principle cannot save.
