You are helping a speaker prepare for a Q&A session at an interdisciplinary seminar on moral emotions and institutional design. The speaker’s thesis is that shame—reconceived as “vector shame,” a status-oriented disapproval tied to a repairable social trajectory rather than to a fixed identity—outperforms guilt as a cooperative mechanism in large societies, and that a culture can partially select for it by replacing anonymous punitive sanctions with public, ritualized acknowledgment procedures that make disapproval visible without permanently diminishing standing. The speaker grants that standard “indexical shame” (audience-dependent, identity-anchored) is destructive, concedes that guilt has advantages for private conscience and precise attribution of wrongdoing, but holds that vector shame scales better, avoids the withdrawal effects documented in cross-cultural emotion research, and is culturally selectable through institutional architecture rather than through psychological indoctrination.

The audience is precise: half are experimental moral psychologists familiar with the guilt-culture/shame-culture taxonomy and with meta-analytic patterns linking shame proneness to aggression and social withdrawal; the other half are normative ethicists skeptical of emotional “engineering,” committed to autonomy, and inclined to see moral emotions as organically emergent; there is also one legal philosopher specializing in dignity and humiliation who has argued against shaming penalties in criminal justice. They care about empirical generalizability, the autonomy of moral development, and the risk of state-sanctioned or community-sanctioned humiliation.

Draft a Q&A prep sheet, not a defense of the thesis. Order the objections by how likely they are to arise and how damaging they would be to the proposal. Phrase each objection in the vocabulary and framing of the audience subgroup most likely to raise it: psychologists should sound like methodologists worried about construct validity and replication; ethicists should sound like autonomy defenders or critics of social construction; the legal philosopher should sound like a dignity theorist worried about irreversible status harm. For each objection provide: (1) the core worry in one or two sentences; (2) a concise reply the speaker can deliver in roughly two minutes; (3) an explicit flag of the weakest point in that reply; and (4) a brief note on what the speaker should concede up front rather than defend. Include the one objection the speaker is hoping nobody asks—the one that targets the hidden premise separating vector shame from indexical shame—and sketch how to survive it if it comes, noting where the reply is softest. Keep everything within reach of a well-read model without web access; do not ask for bibliographies, citations, or reading lists, and do not quote any real text verbatim.
