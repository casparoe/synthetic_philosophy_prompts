I am a software engineer without formal philosophical training, and I have been working through Nietzsche’s essay on the uses and disadvantages of history for life, particularly the division of historical consciousness into monumental, antiquarian, and critical modes. In the discussions I have encountered—both in general-audience forums and in more focused reading—the same disagreement about critical history appears to have stalled for years without movement. One side holds that critical history, which dissolves and judges the past in service of present life, is genuinely autonomous: it operates through pure negation and requires neither the heroic exemplar of monumental history nor the preservative reverence of antiquarian history. The other side insists that any critical dissolution necessarily presupposes either a monumental vision of what life ought to become or an antiquarian attachment to the fragments being preserved, so that critical history is always conceptually parasitic and can never stand alone.

What makes the stall puzzling is that both sides accept the same broad descriptions of the three modes, agree that “life” functions as the ultimate criterion, and can cite parallel examples—yet neither has conceded ground. Drawing on my own background in system design, my working hypothesis is that the disagreement is not really about textual interpretation but about whether a “clean deletion” can exist within a conceptual repository without leaving dependency traces: in other words, whether negative critique can be self-grounding or must always import positive commitments from the other two modes. Is that diagnosis correct, or is the impasse instead hiding a divergence about what “service to life” demands—perhaps one side treating it as a formal constraint and the other as a substantive vision? Alternatively, could the sides be answering different questions—one about therapeutic prescription, the other about structural possibility—under the single heading of “autonomy”?

I want a diagnosis of the disagreement’s structure rather than an adjudication of which side is right. Please determine whether this is a verbal dispute about the word “autonomous,” a substantive disagreement obscured by shared vocabulary, or a clash of bedrock intuitions about whether destruction requires construction. Support your diagnosis by engaging with what each position actually says, and tell me what, if anything, would count as progress given that diagnosis: a sharper conceptual distinction, a concrete historical case that tests separability, an argument that the question is ill-posed because the three modes are not modular components, or a reason to think no further resolution is possible. I am not asking for a survey of scholarship, a bibliography, or citations; I want an analysis of why this particular debate keeps looping and what kind of move—if any—could break the loop.
