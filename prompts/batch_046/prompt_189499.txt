My mother was recently diagnosed with early-onset dementia; she executed an advance directive years ago refusing invasive treatment once she no longer recognises her family, and I have found myself questioning whether that earlier self ought to govern the person she is now becoming. Rewatching Eternal Sunshine of the Spotless Mind, I was struck by how Joel and Clementine’s decision to erase each other functions like a self-authored directive that the post-erasure selves subsequently discover and must choose whether to honour. Read the film through the philosophy of advance directives and diachronic authority, specifically applying the reversibility objection—the claim that if a patient can still form new attachments or experience well-being, the prior refusal loses binding force—to Joel’s partial recovery of memory fragments and his renewed attraction to Clementine. Does the film treat the pre-erasure consent as authoritative over the emerging post-erasure person, or does it dramatise a failure of that authority? Focus on the train encounter, the Montauk reunion, and the tape playback: where does the emotional logic of these scenes illuminate the philosophical problem, and where does the film resist being reduced to an illustration of it? I want a reading that takes both the film and the debate seriously, and that concludes by saying what Eternal Sunshine adds to the argument about the authority of the earlier self that conceptual analysis alone cannot provide.
