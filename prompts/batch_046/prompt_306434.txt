I'm trying to work out what I actually think about epistemic isolation, judging case by case, and I want to know whether there's a coherent principle behind the pattern or whether I have to drop some verdicts.

Here are my gut judgments:

1. A closed online forum for flat-earth advocates: members share only confirming videos, actively ban anyone posting satellite imagery, and mock outsiders as "sheep." Verdict: a harmful echo chamber.

2. A remote agricultural village where residents hear only local news via a single radio station; they have never encountered reports of a distant conflict, not because anyone suppresses them, but because the signal never reaches them. Verdict: an epistemic bubble, not an echo chamber, and not morally blameworthy.

3. A patient with a chronic condition who joins a support forum that shares personal coping stories and explicitly tells members "follow your doctor's orders"; the forum never discusses clinical trials, and members don't seek them out. Verdict: a benign bubble, perhaps even beneficial; not an echo chamber.

4. A patient with the same condition who joins a forum that shares only anecdotal cures, deletes posts mentioning peer-reviewed studies, and tells members that doctors are poisoning them. Verdict: a harmful echo chamber.

5. A philosophy graduate student who spends five years reading only modal logic papers; she knows ethics and philosophy of science exist, finds them uninteresting, and never engages with them, but respects colleagues who work in those areas. Verdict: a benign bubble, not blameworthy.

6. A graduate student in the same field and with the same reading habits, but she tells junior students that ethics "isn't real philosophy," refuses to serve on committees with ethicists, and organizes a reading group that explicitly excludes non-modal work. Verdict: this strikes me as an echo chamber, and blameworthy, even though no false information is being circulated.

7. A news reader with limited time who subscribes to one reputable newspaper and rarely reads others; she knows alternative outlets exist and occasionally checks headlines elsewhere. Verdict: a benign bubble.

8. The same reader, but she insists that every other outlet is deliberately lying, refuses to check any source that contradicts her paper, and ridicules friends who cite them. Verdict: a harmful echo chamber.

The tension is between cases 5 and 6: both involve selective exposure within an academic discipline, yet I treat one as harmless isolation and the other as a harmful echo chamber. Cases 2 and 7 are parallel in a different way—limited exposure due to circumstance versus choice—and I treat them the same, so the real work seems to be done by whatever distinguishes 5 from 6.

Is there a precise principle—perhaps involving active suppression, awareness of alternatives combined with hostility, or the presence of social sanctions—that captures all eight verdicts? Please state it exactly, indicate which case motivates each clause, predict its verdict on a case I haven't listed (a scientific research team working within a dominant theoretical framework that sets aside anomalous data for years, treating them as puzzles to be solved later, with full awareness that the anomalies exist), and be honest about which of my verdicts it must reject if it is to stay simple and coherent.

Also, apply your best principle to the boundary case I'm unsure about: a community of traditional restoration carpenters who use only hand tools, know that power tools exist, do not forbid them, but structure their apprenticeship so that trainees never encounter them, and treat questions about power tools as distractions from craft mastery rather than as heresy. Is this a benign bubble, an echo chamber, or something else?
