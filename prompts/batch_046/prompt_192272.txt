I'm a doctoral student in philosophy of science working at the boundary of formal learning theory and epistemology, and I've noticed a recurring argumentative structure in interdisciplinary discussions of induction that I can't place in the existing literature. The pattern unfolds in two moves. First, a speaker invokes a no-free-lunch result—for instance, that no learning algorithm can dominate all others across every possible distribution of target functions—to argue that artificial prediction lacks a principled inductive foundation unless supplemented by strong, domain-specific priors. Second, in the very same discussion, the speaker treats human inductive practices—reliance on simplicity biases, projectibility judgments, or analogical reasoning—as if they escape the NFL constraint not by bearing a hidden performance cost elsewhere in the space of possible worlds, but by virtue of being "natural," "evolved," or "rational" in a way that exempts them from the bookkeeping. The asymmetry is not straightforward special pleading, because the speaker often denies that human cognition possesses a universal prior; rather, they seem to treat the cost that NFL theorems require as a liability that attaches specifically to formally specified algorithms, not to cognitive architectures that lack explicit loss functions. I have seen variants of this in debates over whether deep learning addresses Hume's problem, in arguments about whether Occam's razor needs an independent epistemic justification, and in critiques of statistical learning theory that suddenly become permissive when the learner is biological. Is there an established concept for this selective deployment—using NFL reasoning as a hard boundary for artificial systems while treating it as inapplicable to human induction unless the latter is fully formalized? If there is no name, how should the phenomenon be distinguished from ordinary domain relativism, from the empirically grounded claim that biological priors are justified by evolutionary success, and from the simpler error of overlooking that human cognition also fails on adversarially constructed problem sets? I am less interested in a bare label than in a precise carving: what the phenomenon is, where its edges lie, and which nearby mistakes or legitimate positions it must be separated from.
