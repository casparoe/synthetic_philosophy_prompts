I'm delivering the opening remarks at the retirement colloquium for Dr. Elena Vasquez, a statistical mechanician who, for the last two decades of her career, insisted that her graduate students attend a philosophy-of-science seminar she co-taught, despite frequent objections from colleagues who treat philosophy as ornamental to working science. The audience is sharply divided: half are physicists and engineers who will immediately recognize—without needing citation—the familiar trio of objections in the spirit of Weinberg's charge that philosophy fails to predict, Hawking's declaration that the field is intellectually dead, and Feynman's image of philosophers analyzing rules without playing the game; the other half are Vasquez's family, non-scientific friends, and humanities students who will understand the emotional stakes but not the disciplinary references.

I need a ten-to-twelve-minute address that treats these three objections not as strawmen to defeat but as structural constraints that determine what I can legitimately claim. Specifically, the speech must avoid arguing that philosophy generates hypotheses, substitutes for experiment, or teaches scientists their craft; instead, it should defend a narrower, less familiar function—philosophy as conceptual maintenance that identifies when a scientific dispute is about framing rather than data, and that succeeds precisely when its work becomes invisible.

To make this concrete without turning into a lecture, anchor the remarks in Vasquez's own field: the long-running confusion, in statistical mechanics, between treating the second law as an empirical generalization and treating it as a consequence of boundary conditions, a confusion that persisted not because experiments were lacking but because the distinction between epistemic and ontic descriptions of entropy was not made explicit—a clarification that came from philosophical analysis of physical law rather than from the laboratory. The speech should speak differently to each half of the room: the scientists should hear an acknowledgment that philosophy does not do their measurements for them, and the family should grasp why Vasquez might value a discipline that operates like proofreading a language no one notices is being spoken. End with a toast that honors her specific, somewhat eccentric choice to keep philosophers in the room, without claiming that every laboratory needs one.
