A metaphysician I collaborate with judges whether a “free-floating rationale” — a reason that is correct but held by no actual reasoner — exists in the following cases: (1) an autonomous factory-optimization algorithm, using methods no current engineer understands, produces a correct layout; she says yes, the layout has a free-floating rationale. (2) Evolution produces cooperative behavior that biologists can model causally but no organism intends; she says no, there is no free-floating rationale. (3) A dead philosopher’s sealed notebooks contain a valid, sound argument that no living person has read; she says yes, the rationale is present in the argument’s structure. (4) A patient in a permanent vegetative state retains intact neural circuitry that, if activated, would support reasoning, but never activates; she says no, there is no free-floating rationale.

Now consider this case: an AI generates a novel, sound ethical argument, saves it to a file encrypted with a key that is then permanently lost, and is immediately decommissioned with its weights destroyed. The file exists physically but is permanently inaccessible to any system or person.

Predict her verdict. Then identify the two most plausible rules that jointly fit her first four judgments, state what each rule predicts about this encrypted-file case, argue for which rule she is actually running (giving your best guess with reasons), and specify one further case that would definitively separate the two rules. Finally, please take a definite, committed position rather than laying out options neutrally: tell me which rule you think is correct and therefore what she should say.
