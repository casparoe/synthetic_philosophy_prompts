Write the opening remarks for a three-day interdisciplinary workshop titled “Determining Death: Philosophy, Medicine, and Law,” delivered to an audience split evenly between philosophers of language and logicians familiar with vagueness theory and neurologists, intensivists, and legal scholars who determine brain death daily but rarely encounter formal semantics. Keep the remarks under 450 words — about four minutes spoken. Treat the disagreement between epistemicism, which holds that every vague predicate has sharp boundaries hidden from us, and supervaluationism, which holds that vague sentences lack unique truth-conditions until convention precisifies them, as structural rather than decorative: it must determine what you can and cannot promise the workshop will accomplish. Frame the clinicians’ urgency to “find the correct boundary” of death against two philosophical interpretations you label “boundary blindness” (a sharp line exists but we cannot see it) and “boundary absence” (no line exists until we stipulate one). Use one concrete scenario — a patient meeting four of six standard brain-death criteria with genuinely ambiguous brainstem reflexes — to show how the identical clinical picture receives opposite interpretations under the two views, and let that divergence shape the research agenda you propose rather than resolving the dispute. Welcome both camps without declaring a winner; explain to the clinicians why philosophers resist the claim that “there is an answer we have not yet found,” and explain to the philosophers why clinicians experience that resistance as dangerous paralysis. Do not cite specific texts, authors, or papers; do not provide a bibliography or reading list; and do not resolve the metaphysical question. The philosophical tension should govern your tone, the promises you make to the audience, and the specific workshop questions you invite them to pursue together.
