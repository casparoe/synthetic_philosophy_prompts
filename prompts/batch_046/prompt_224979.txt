I work as a software engineer with no formal philosophy training, and I have noticed a puzzling institutional practice in the leadership-development track at my company. New engineering managers are required to read curated biographies of singular technology founders, presented not as historical case studies but as unified moral exemplars whose personal character, intuition, and vision explain their success. The curriculum never pairs figures for comparison, never examines structural or market conditions in depth, and never asks us to extract contradictory lessons from different lives; instead, it asks us to internalize one coherent narrative of decisive judgment under uncertainty. This format feels closer to hagiography than to history, yet the same organization claims that engineering culture should be data-driven, systemic, and skeptical of hero narratives.

What philosophical commitments about moral learning from lives does this practice presuppose? Does it assume that practical wisdom or virtue is acquired primarily through narrative identification with a unitary moral agent, and that character is stable enough across contexts to be imitated? How does its implicit model relate to the tradition of parallel lives—where moral understanding arises from juxtaposing figures to highlight shared virtues and divergent failures—versus the hagiographic mode of singular sanctification? Must the practice deny that moral luck plays a significant role in outcomes, or can it accommodate luck while still treating one biography as exemplary?

Please reconstruct the implicit theory of moral education embedded in this onboarding design, then distinguish what the practice strictly requires from what it merely accommodates: could the same structure be justified under a view that treats biographies as heuristic repositories of decision patterns rather than as models of character? Is the practice philosophically underdetermined—compatible with several theories of moral learning—or does it quietly rule out comparative, contextual, or systemic approaches? Finally, if we replaced the underlying theory with one that treats moral learning as fundamentally comparative and embedded in institutional structures rather than extracted from singular lives, what concrete changes would be required in the curriculum? Would we need paired case studies, explicit counterfactual analysis, or structured discussion of structural factors that the current format excludes?
