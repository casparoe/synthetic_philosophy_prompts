I'm writing this without a formal philosophy or economics background, though I've been reading about decision-making under uncertainty on my own. Two friends of mine, Jordan and Maya, got into a long argument about the Ellsberg paradox over coffee, and they asked me to referee because I was the one who brought up the urn example. Here's how the exchange went, as best as I can reconstruct it.

Jordan started with the classic setup: an urn with 30 red balls and 60 balls that are black or yellow in some unknown proportion. Most people prefer betting on red over black, even though there's no information favoring one color. Jordan said this is plainly irrational. If you genuinely have no reason to think black is more likely than yellow, symmetry requires treating every possible split as equally likely, which collapses to a subjective probability of 1/3 for black—exactly the same as red. Choosing red over black means you're violating your own beliefs; you're just scared of the unknown and dressing it up as caution.

Maya pushed back hard. She said Jordan is smuggling in a controversial assumption: that rationality always requires a single precise prior. When information is genuinely missing, she argued, your mental state shouldn't be a sharp number at all; it should be a range of acceptable probabilities. Preferring the known 1/3 over the ambiguous option is rational epistemic humility—you're refusing to pretend you know something you don't. It's not fear; it's intellectual honesty about the limits of your evidence.

Jordan replied that Maya's "humility" can't explain the actual pattern. In experiments, people will take the ambiguous bet if the payout is large enough, which shows they're not consistently avoiding ambiguity on principle. If they were truly humble about lacking a prior, they'd avoid the bet regardless of stakes. Instead, they behave exactly like ordinary risk-averse people—except they apply an extra penalty specifically to ambiguity. So Maya hasn't shown ambiguity aversion is rational; she's just relabeled risk aversion.

Maya then introduced a twist she said Jordan was ignoring. There's a version of the problem where you can pay a small fee to learn the true black-to-yellow ratio before placing your bet. Even people who refuse to pay that fee still avoid betting on black. If they were rational but information-starved, they'd either pay for the information or treat the ambiguity like ordinary risk once they decided not to pay. Instead, they seem to treat ambiguity itself as carrying a distinct kind of cost—separate from both risk and information value. Jordan said that just proves people have a quirky psychological bias, not that ambiguity aversion is rational. Maya answered that a bias can still be rational if it tracks a real feature of the world—namely, that some unknowns are structurally different from calculable risks.
