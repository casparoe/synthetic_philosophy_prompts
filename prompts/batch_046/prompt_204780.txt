Here are notes for an essay I am drafting on Keynes's vision of the good life. The core claim is that Keynes's 1930 essay "Economic Possibilities for our Grandchildren" harbors an unrecognized structural tension: he frames the economic problem as a matter of efficient planning and public investment to reach abundance and full employment, yet he describes the resulting good life in terms of activities—poetry, gardening, conversation, artistic patronage—that are valuable precisely because they escape instrumental rationality. I argue that Keynes distinguishes absolute needs, which production and planning can satisfy, from relative needs, which are insatiable because they depend on social comparison; he then implicitly assumes that once absolute needs are met, individuals will voluntarily abandon status competition for intrinsically valued leisure. The essay asks whether this assumption is plausible, and whether Keynes's own commitments to state-led investment and cultural patronage actually conflict with his claim that the good life requires stepping outside instrumental reasoning altogether. I also briefly contrast this with the Stoic concept of scholē—structured leisure pursued as an end in itself—without asserting that Keynes was directly influenced by Stoic sources.

Please comment on the accuracy of this reading: is the tension genuinely present in Keynes's text, or is it artificially imposed? Does the comparison to Stoic scholē strengthen or weaken the argument? Are there important counter-arguments or passages in Keynes's other writings on leisure, aesthetics, or the role of the state that I have overlooked? I am particularly interested in whether the essay's framing of "planning" versus "purposelessness" obscures a more coherent middle position in Keynes's thought.
