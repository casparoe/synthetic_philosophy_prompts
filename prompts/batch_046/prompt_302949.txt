Following a recent family health crisis that has left a close relative in a prolonged disorder of consciousness, I have been asked to advise a small hospital ethics working group—comprising one philosopher, one neurologist, and one clinical mathematician—on whether measures derived from Integrated Information Theory should inform assessments of conscious awareness and, by extension, recommendations regarding life-sustaining treatment. I have roughly six months of part-time capacity to direct this group’s conceptual labor, and I need a defended agenda for where that limited effort should go rather than a survey of the field.

Among the open conceptual problems in IIT—the clinical interpretation of the exclusion postulate in brains that contain localized but competing high-integration regions; the conceptual gap between a quantitative integrated-information value and the qualitative presence of phenomenal experience; the tractability of computing integration from noisy clinical neuroimaging data; and the normative question of whether the presence of integrated information carries any direct moral weight in end-of-life decisions—which two or three are genuinely upstream for ethically defensible practice, and which are philosophical distractions that would not change our clinical recommendations even if fully resolved? Rank them with explicit criteria—importance to judgment about withdrawal of care, tractability for philosophers rather than empirical researchers, and how many other questions depend on this one—explain the trade-offs between them, and specify what kind of conceptual result or argument over the next year would cause me to reorder them.

My aim is not bibliographic coverage but strategic focus: given that our ultimate goal is to determine whether IIT offers anything more than technical sophistication to clinical ethics, where should we concentrate our thinking so that a resolution actually matters for the protocol we are designing?
