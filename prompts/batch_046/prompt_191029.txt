A recent essay in an interdisciplinary journal summarizes the paradox of fiction like this: "The paradox is that we genuinely fear for a character's life or grieve their death, yet we simultaneously know they do not exist, so our emotional response is directed at nothing. Coleridge solved this by introducing the idea of willing suspension of disbelief, which shows that fiction operates through voluntary ontological bracketing: we temporarily suspend our reality-monitoring mechanisms without actually deceiving ourselves. Once we understand this bracketing, the paradox dissolves—our emotions are not judgments about real value but what philosophers call affective simulations, triggered by representational content that lacks an ontological anchor. To treat them as genuine moral or aesthetic evaluations is therefore a category error, like attributing color to a number. The real philosophical task is not to justify these responses but to map the cognitive architecture that produces them." Something about this feels too neat—particularly the claim that Coleridge solved the paradox and that our responses are merely affective simulations lacking rational status. But I am unsure which parts reflect actual positions in the literature and which are distortions. Is this a fair summary? Please separate what is accurate, what is a common simplification, and what misrepresents the actual debates—especially regarding Coleridge's historical role, the distinction between emotional response and belief, and whether the paradox is dissolved or merely relocated by appeal to simulation or bracketing. Also, does the conclusion that treating fictional emotions as value judgments is a category error follow from the accurate parts, or does it depend on a conflation that defenders of the rationality of fictional emotion would reject?
