For my advanced seminar in political philosophy—specifically the module on revolutionary change and historical explanation—I am constructing a diagnostic taxonomy that sorts interpretations of the French Revolution according to where they stand on three questions that survey treatments usually conflate: whether the Revolution's origin lies in structural class conflict, in autonomous ideological dynamics, or in exogenous contingency; whether the Terror and Thermidor demonstrate that revolutions structurally devour their own architects; and whether "the people" functions as a pre-existing sociological agent or as a performative category constituted only through revolutionary discourse.

Please present this as a branching tree in which each internal node is a concrete claim or case-distinction that a student could answer without already knowing the historiographical labels, and each leaf carries the name of the position (or "unnamed" if the combination is unoccupied) together with one sentence on its most awkward commitment. The tree must include at least the following named positions: the classic Marxist-social interpretation; the revisionist contingency school; the cultural-linguistic or ideological-turn account; a Tocquevillian continuity thesis (the Revolution accelerates rather than ruptures administrative centralization); and a view that treats revolutionary violence as following a logic of institutional mimicry—where the new state reproduces the arbitrary coercive structures of the ancien régime in intensified form—rather than as either class consolidation or pure contingency.

In addition, please flag any pair of leaves your tree cannot genuinely separate (for instance, if two positions answer every diagnostic question identically but differ only in rhetorical emphasis or normative framing). Where a branch produces a combination that no named school occupies, briefly diagnose whether the gap is likely incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored. Finally, include one cross-cutting axis that forces respondents to decide whether Thermidor represents an inevitable self-cannibalization of revolutionary purity or an exogenous interruption by military-bureaucratic necessity, and map that decision onto the other branches so that the "revolutions devour their children" thesis operates as a structural coordinate of the taxonomy rather than as an afterthought.
