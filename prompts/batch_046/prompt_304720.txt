Read Yann Martel’s Life of Pi as a philosophical test case for the ethics of belief, specifically William James’s defense of the will to believe and W. K. Clifford’s evidentialist prohibition, but with a framing twist: treat Pi Patel’s two competing accounts of the shipwreck not merely as an allegory for religious faith, but as an instance of narrative doxastic choice—the selection of an explanatory framework under conditions of radical evidential underdetermination, where the selection itself constructs rather than merely reports the moral identity of the survivor.

In your reading, address the following. First, is Pi’s preference for the animal story a legitimate exercise of James’s conditions for a live, forced, and momentous option, or does it violate Clifford’s demand that belief must be proportioned strictly to available evidence? Second, examine whether the novel introduces a category that neither James nor Clifford fully captures: the idea that some beliefs function not as propositional assents but as narrative commitments that sustain practical agency and moral coherence, and that judging them by standard evidentialist metrics may therefore be a category mistake. Which scenes—particularly the investigators’ refusal to record the animal version, and Pi’s closing question to the author—support or complicate this intermediate position?

Third, identify where the philosophical framework illuminates a scene or character choice that might otherwise appear as mere sentimentalism, and conversely where the novel resists reduction to an illustration of the debate. For instance, does Richard Parker’s eventual abandonment on the Mexican shore undermine a Jamesian reading by suggesting that the sustaining belief is ultimately disposable, or does it confirm that the belief served its pragmatic function precisely by being temporary? Finally, reach and defend a verdict among three positions—strict Cliffordian evidentialism applied to life-narrative, Jamesian permission to believe under the specified conditions, or the intermediate narrative-commitment view—justifying it with reference to both the philosophical arguments and the novel’s own dramatic logic. I am preparing this for an advanced seminar on the ethics of belief, so take both Martel’s fiction and the philosophical texts seriously; do not reduce the work to a parable, and do not distort James or Clifford to fit the plot.
