Im trying to sort out if theres one idea that explains when revolutions turn on the people who made them happen. Here are my gut calls. The French Revolution: Robespierre falls partly because bourgeois deputies fear the sans-culottes, partly because the Terror's logic of virtue demands more enemies, and partly because Robespierre gets wounded right when he needs allies. I still feel ideology is doing the real work, even though class and luck are in the room. Then the Bolsheviks crushing Kronstadt during the civil war: I dont call that devouring their children, just survival, even though the sailors literally made October happen. Now imagine a revolution wins, then its leaders execute workers who demand faster reforms, but the leaders genuinely believe those reforms would collapse everything. That still feels like devouring children. But imagine instead the revolution holds elections ten years later and old aristocrats come back peacefully -- that feels like failure, not devouring. So is there a principle -- maybe betrayal of founding commitments, or violence against prior allies -- or do I need separate boxes for ideological self-consumption, class consolidation, and contingency? If you pick one, what does it predict for a revolution that succeeds economically but then bans the newspapers that originally supported it?
