I cover philosophy of science and comparative logic. For an upcoming feature, I'm examining whether Jain anekāntavāda—specifically the syādvāda and saptabhaṅgī frameworks—should be read as an ancient precursor to modern dialetheism. The texts seem to allow contradictory predicates, such as "the self is eternal" and "the self is non-eternal," to hold of the same subject without the usual logical explosion, which suggests that anekāntavāda accepts true contradictions in the same respect rather than merely distributing them across different perspectives or naya. If that reading is correct, then anekāntavāda is not just epistemic humility but a full paraconsistent logic, and its only real difference from contemporary dialetheism is historical context. So does the Jain tradition contain an internal distinction—perhaps tied to the conditional syāt or to aspect-specific predication—that blocks contradiction in the same respect and saves the view from literal incoherence, or is the comparison to dialetheism essentially right?
