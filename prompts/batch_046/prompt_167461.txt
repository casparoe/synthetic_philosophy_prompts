I'm designing a classification instrument for a small philosophy/political-economy reading group—mostly longtime rationalist-blog readers—who want to diagnose what they actually believe about strategy-proofness rather than which economist they cite. Build a decision tree (or branching taxonomy) that sorts commitments on the Gibbard-Satterthwaite result, not people by label. Start every respondent from the shared formal premise: with three or more alternatives, universal domain, non-dictatorship, and Pareto efficiency together imply that strategic manipulability is unavoidable. From there, use concrete branching questions answerable without knowing social-choice jargon: whether strategic vulnerability is a fatal flaw or an acceptable cost of full preference expression; whether the right response restricts the domain (e.g., single-peakedness or separability), weakens the mechanism requirement (approximate or probabilistic strategy-proofness), or shifts to cardinal or deliberative aggregation; and—this is the angle I want included—whether you think the impossibility transfers analogously to epistemic aggregation, such as combining probabilistic forecasts or logical beliefs across agents, where "manipulation" means misreporting credences rather than misordering ballots. Each leaf should carry either a named position or "unnamed," plus one sentence on its most awkward commitment. Flag any pair of leaves your tree genuinely cannot separate (for instance, certain cardinal-scoring defenses and certain deliberative-democracy views may converge on the same practical recommendation despite different foundations), and briefly diagnose unoccupied combinations—whether they seem incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored. In the framing notes that accompany the tree, clearly separate what is settled (the theorem's conditions, Arrow's parallel conditions, and standard domain-restriction escape routes) from what is genuinely contested among people who know the topic: whether an epistemic analog of Gibbard-Satterthwaite holds, whether approximate strategy-proofness is normatively sufficient, and whether domain restrictions are realistic for complex multi-issue policy spaces. Do not present contested interpretations—such as that cardinal mechanisms fully dissolve the problem, or that the result proves democratic aggregation is impossible—as if they were the consensus.
