I'm preparing notes for a seminar essay on the ethics of commemoration—specifically whether societies should retain, modify, or remove statues, street names, and institutional buildings when the honored figure's legacy includes serious moral harm. In discussion with a friend, I proposed a tentative framework: a commemorative object should be retained only if it can be separated into either an indexical function (fixing geographic or institutional reference without attributing virtue) or an honorific function that genuinely tracks a separable public contribution, and should be removed only when its primary purpose is the celebration of harm itself. Yet my intuitions about concrete cases seem to strain this framework.

Here are my verdicts. The bronze equestrian statue in the central square honoring a 19th-century senator who championed abolition but later endorsed reconstruction policies that forcibly displaced indigenous communities: keep the statue, but add an explicit counter-plaque that names the displacement; do not remove it. The Miller Avenue named after a factory owner who funded the town's first hospital in 1910 but personally directed the violent suppression of a 1912 strike that killed three workers: rename the street, because the honor is inseparable from the violence. The Henderson Laboratory named after a chemist whose 1940s research was later weaponized, though he died in 1950 unaware of those applications and had advocated for open international science: keep the name, but institute an annual ethics lecture in the building rather than renaming. The Victory Arch erected in 1923 solely to commemorate a military campaign celebrated at the time for destroying indigenous villages: dismantle it entirely, since contextualization cannot neutralize a monument whose only purpose is atrocity celebration. The Corcoran Bridge, named for seventy years after a mayor convicted of financial embezzlement in the 1960s; locals use it only for navigation, the corruption involved no violence or oppression of marginalized groups, and the bridge is mundane infrastructure: retain the geographic name but formally strip its honorific status. A public garden mosaic titled Harmony, installed in 1985 to celebrate civic peace; the artist was later convicted of domestic violence unrelated to the work's message: keep the mosaic intact, since the object commemorates the civic value rather than the artist's biography; perhaps note the separation. But I keep oscillating on a case I haven't resolved: a high school named after a beloved local children's author whose private, posthumously discovered diaries reveal virulent racist beliefs he never acted upon or expressed in his public work—should the school be renamed, or does the separation between private belief and public contribution hold?

Is there a coherent principle—perhaps refining my indexical/honorific distinction or replacing it—that captures all six verdicts without ad hoc adjustments? If not, which of my judgments should be overturned, and why? What does the best principle predict for the unresolved school case? Please lead with your bottom-line principle or set of principles in one concise statement, then briefly justify it case by case, and be explicit about which verdict it cannot save. Keep the entire response under 350 words, in a single continuous argument without bullet points.
