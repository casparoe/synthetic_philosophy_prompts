You are grading an undergraduate philosophy examination. The exam question was: "In Fear and Trembling, Kierkegaard presents Abraham’s binding of Isaac as a case of the teleological suspension of the ethical. Explain what this suspension consists in, distinguishing it carefully from a mere exception to universal moral rules, and then apply the same structural relation to a modern institutional conflict—specifically, a physician’s duty of confidentiality versus a personal vocation that demands disclosure of a patient’s information."

The student’s answer is as follows:

The teleological suspension of the ethical occurs when Abraham sets aside the universal commandment against killing in order to serve a higher telos—his absolute relation to God. This is structurally analogous to a utilitarian exception: the universal rule is violated, but only because a greater good (in this case, Abraham’s faith and obedience) outweighs it. From the ethical standpoint Abraham therefore appears irrational, but from the religious standpoint he is fully rational, since he acts in accordance with a higher law. The knight of infinite resignation gives up the finite for the infinite, like a monk who abandons worldly attachments, whereas the knight of faith resigns the finite and then receives it back by virtue of the absurd. Applying this to the physician: if the doctor breaks confidentiality not for a legal or medical reason, but in order to fulfill a deeply personal calling—say, a religious duty to warn the community—then she is performing a teleological suspension. She is not merely making a moral mistake or a calculated exception; she is acting as a knight of faith, placing her individual relation to the absolute above the universal ethical demand of her profession.
