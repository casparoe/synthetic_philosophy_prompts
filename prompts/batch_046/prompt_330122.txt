In a seminar on historical ethics, the same phrase keeps surfacing whenever we discuss figures like Jefferson, Churchill, or Columbus: someone insists that "you have to judge them by the standards of their own time," or that "he was just a man of his time." The slogan is deployed interchangeably across cases that feel structurally different—Jefferson owning people he lived among and wrote about as property, Churchill defending empire and expressing views about racial hierarchy, Columbus initiating violent extraction and enslavement in contexts where he was not merely inheriting an existing system but actively constructing one. I want to bring a precise disambiguation to the next meeting rather than just repeating my intuition that the defense collapses every case into a single move.

Could you lay out, as a menu of distinct theses rather than a single interpretation, what that slogan might actually be claiming? I am looking for at least: a purely causal or explanatory claim about how historical context shaped belief; an epistemic claim about whether we can access the counterfactual moral knowledge available to the agent at the time; a normative relativist claim that moral standards themselves were genuinely different then; an excuse-based claim that feasible alternatives were absent; a moral-luck claim that being embedded in a corrupt moral ecology diminishes blame without eliminating wrongness; and any other serious reading that belongs in the taxonomy. For each, say whether it is trivial, defensible, false, or incoherent, and—crucially—indicate whether it actually supports dismissing criticism in Jefferson's case, Churchill's case, and Columbus's case separately, since I suspect the slogan does different work for each. Also identify which reading people typically invoke when they want to end an argument, and whether that reading survives once you rigorously separate explanation from excuse and apply it consistently across all three figures.
