I am drafting the take-home final for a graduate seminar on the metaphysics of personal identity, aimed at students who have worked through the rationalist literature on decision-theoretic self-interest and want to test whether debates about survival can survive contact with practical reasoning about future welfare. Rather than asking for a recitation of the standard teletransporter summary, I want one essay question built around a modified scenario—call it the Branching Coherence Protocol. In this protocol, a subject’s complete neural-state is scanned without destroying the original body, which is placed in suspended animation. The scan generates up to three substrate-independent copies, each instantiated with a different coherence score measuring the fidelity of belief-desire-network integration relative to the original (scores of 0.92, 0.85, and 0.71). Before scanning, the subject must pre-commit to a coherence threshold; only copies meeting or exceeding that threshold are activated, the others are dissolved, and the original remains in stasis regardless. The exam should ask students to analyze whether this pre-commitment framework solves, dissolves, or merely relocates the question of what matters in survival. Specifically, they must address: (1) whether psychological continuity, physical continuity, or the coherence metric itself should ground the choice of threshold; (2) what happens to the claim that identity is not what matters if multiple copies meet the threshold while the original still exists in stasis; and (3) whether framing survival as a decision problem under uncertainty about future welfare begs the metaphysical question rather than answering it. Please write the exam prompt (specify a 1200-word limit, 48-hour window, worth 50 percent of the final grade), a model answer of about 600 words, and a marking scheme that separates an A answer from a B answer by listing the specific analytical moves an A must contain—such as distinguishing operational coherence from metaphysical continuity, recognizing that suspended animation blocks the standard destruction premise, and evaluating whether decision-theoretic framing presupposes rather than proves psychological-continuity reductionism—and by naming the precise errors that should cost marks, such as treating the coherence score as a physical fact, assuming only one copy can be activated, or ignoring the stasis condition altogether. The question must be answerable without searching for obscure texts, but it should be unanswerable by anyone who has only memorized the textbook summary of the teletransporter argument.
