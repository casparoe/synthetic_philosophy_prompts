I direct a research group that studies disorders of consciousness, and we have adopted an illusionist framework for interpreting first-person data: we treat subjective reports of phenomenal experience not as transparent windows onto an inner reality, but as outputs of an introspective mechanism that can systematically misrepresent both the presence and the character of experience. I need a reusable decision protocol my graduate researchers can apply when they design or evaluate any study that depends on subjective reports—whether a psychophysical task requiring ratings of qualitative vividness, a clinical interview asking a minimally conscious patient to describe an inner visual life, or a psychedelic trial in which participants claim to have encountered pure awareness without content.

Write the protocol as an ordered procedure: the specific questions a researcher must ask in sequence before treating a subjective report as usable data; the conceptual distinctions that must survive operationalization, especially the difference between a report being false about an experience and the experience itself being an illusion constructed by the introspective model; the points at which the illusionist commitment forces a reinterpretation of standard methodology rather than a simple dismissal of the report; and the explicit flags where judgment cannot be eliminated and must be escalated to senior review. The protocol should be runnable in about fifteen minutes by someone without specialist training in philosophy of mind, but it must not dilute the illusionist premise—if the framework implies that a standard practice is conceptually confused, the procedure should say so.

Then apply the procedure to three cases. First, a patient with locked-in syndrome who reports a rich, continuous inner visual life despite absent behavioral markers. Second, a psychedelic study in which participants describe a state of awareness lacking any intentional content or sensory quality. Third, a case in which the procedure produces an answer the researcher will not want: specifically, that a widely used clinical scale for conscious awareness should be suspended because its items presuppose the transparency of introspection that illusionism denies. For each case, identify the exact step at which the decision turns, and note whether the protocol ends with acceptance, reinterpretation, rejection, or escalation.

Finally, be explicit about what the procedure cannot do: which categories of report it leaves methodologically untouched, which disputes within illusionism it does not resolve, and where it risks overreaching by treating every phenomenological claim as an error rather than as data about the structure of the introspective model itself. Mark any point at which the protocol might misfire if applied by a researcher who mistakes the framework for a general skepticism about patient testimony rather than for a specific thesis about the ontology of phenomenal properties.
