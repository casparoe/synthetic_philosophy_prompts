The question is this: Some people argue that treating earlier ages as mere preparations for our own is mainly an intellectual or moral failure on the part of historians — they are too pleased with the present to see the past on its own terms. Others propose a structural explanation: because records survive mainly from institutions and ideas that endured, the evidence itself forms a filtered ladder that makes progress look inevitable. How do these two explanations differ in kind, and does the structural explanation make Whig history unavoidable in domains such as mathematics or art, where we commonly believe that genuine advancement occurs?

Here is the student’s answer:

Whig history is when historians view the past only as a stepping-stone to the present, for example describing medieval medicine purely as an inferior version of modern medicine. The structural explanation claims that we possess records mainly from people and ideas that survived, so the historical record naturally appears to show improvement, since all the failures and dead ends have disappeared. However, I believe this structural point is really just another form of the moral failure: historians choose to focus on survivors because they want to construct a narrative of progress. In mathematics, by contrast, this problem cannot arise, because mathematical truths are objective and do not depend on which texts happen to survive; two plus two equals four regardless of archival loss. Consequently, Whig history is impossible in mathematics, whereas in politics it is unavoidable, since politicians always seek to justify current arrangements. The proper remedy, therefore, is to apply greater self-criticism to political history, while we need not worry about Whig distortions when studying mathematics.
