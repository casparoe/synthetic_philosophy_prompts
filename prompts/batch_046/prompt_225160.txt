I have no formal training in biblical studies or philosophy of history, but over the past year I have read widely in popular and semi-scholarly accounts of the quest for the historical Jesus, and I have become stuck on a problem that seems to sit right at the boundary between history and conceptual logic. Authors regularly invoke standard criteria of authenticity—multiple attestation, embarrassment, dissimilarity, and coherence—to separate likely authentic sayings and deeds of Jesus from later church development. What strikes me is that these criteria are almost always presented as separate, independent checks, with very little discussion of how they ought to be combined, ordered, or weighted. Different reconstructions seem to apply them in different sequences, and the resulting portrait of Jesus often appears to reflect those methodological choices as much as the ancient sources themselves. I want to pitch a six-month independent research project—structured more as a conceptual study than an empirical excavation—to ask a precise question: can these criteria ever genuinely settle who the historical Jesus was, or does the absence of any agreed-upon aggregation rule make the reconstructed figure unavoidably an artifact of the method?

The specific question I want to investigate is whether applying the standard criteria through different but defensible aggregation schemes—sequential filtering, weighted scoring, and coherence-driven reconciliation—converges on a single stable profile, or instead generates divergent portraits that are rationally incommensurable. Existing scholarship tends to defend particular reconstructions of Jesus without defending the logical architecture behind them, leaving it open whether the criteria function as independent confirmations or as interdependent lenses that reshape the evidence pool at each step. For instance, applying dissimilarity first might filter out material that aligns with early Christian belief, yet the remaining counter-cultural fragments could just as easily be later interpolations designed to shock; applying coherence first might preserve sayings that fit an already-assumed profile, circularly reinforcing it. I propose to address this through philosophical analysis rather than archival or archaeological work: I will formalize three explicit aggregation models, apply them conceptually to a shared set of well-known Gospel passages, and determine what each model implies about Jesus’s teachings, social context, and relationship to Judaism. A useful deliverable would be a framework paper specifying, for each model, the reconstructed profile it yields, the internal contradictions or breakdown points it encounters, and what would count as failure—either perfect convergence across all models, which would show the framing concern is illusory, or irreducible divergence with no rational basis for selecting among them, which would confirm that authenticity criteria alone cannot settle the historical question. Given the six-month scope, the project would remain limited to logical reconstruction and conceptual argument, avoiding dependence on unpublished manuscripts or new field reports, and the final write-up would be intended for an intellectually curious but non-specialist audience. Please write this as a polished one-page proposal in American English, stating the question precisely, identifying clearly why current answers leave the aggregation problem open, explaining the proposed approach and why it is preferable to obvious empirical alternatives, outlining the deliverable and failure conditions, and briefly justifying why a conceptual project makes genuine progress on a question usually treated as purely historical.
