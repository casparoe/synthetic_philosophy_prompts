I need help sorting out a dispute with a colleague over a shared grant proposal due this Friday. During our planning meeting, I said: "I have completed the budget draft, and the submission deadline is Friday." Based on this, she concluded that I was coordinating the overall submission and that her sections were not immediately critical, so she prioritized other commitments and failed to deliver her portion. After we missed the deadline, I maintained that I had merely reported my own progress and made no commitment about her work or about managing the submission. She argues that because our conversation was specifically about allocating responsibilities, highlighting only my finished task necessarily implied that the remaining work belonged to her or that I had assumed oversight. She further claims that even if I had phrased it differently—for instance, "The budget is drafted, and Friday is approaching"—the same practical message would have been conveyed, which she takes as proof that the implication is inseparable from the literal content of my statement. I respond that the very same literal claim—"I have completed the budget draft"—could have been made in an entirely different conversational setting, such as complaining about being overworked, without carrying any message about her obligations at all. This suggests to me that the supposed implication is detachable from the words and therefore belongs to the context rather than to what I actually said. My question is this: was there a specific implicit meaning genuinely embedded in my utterance, or did she project an interpretation onto it? If there was such an implicit meaning, is it part of what was said, or is it distinct from the literal content? And does my ability to express the same literal content in a context where that message is absent undermine her claim that I was necessarily responsible for her understanding?
