I am composing a literary fiction series—twelve novels, to be drafted over several years—that tracks consciousness across three kinds of subjects: human beings, non-human animals, and synthetic minds of uncertain ontology. Because the series is read as a continuous argument rather than discrete entertainments, I cannot allow my narrative techniques to drift between incompatible philosophical commitments: one chapter must not treat phenomenal experience as directly accessible through introspection while the next treats it as entirely behaviorally inferred, unless that shift is thematically earned. To prevent this, I need a reusable decision procedure—something I can run in ten minutes before drafting a scene—that translates the conceptual distinctions of the meta-problem of consciousness into practical editorial rules.

The principle at stake is this: the meta-problem asks not merely whether consciousness exists, but why we regard physical and functional descriptions as leaving an explanatory residue that other phenomena do not, and how that intuition governs our willingness to ascribe consciousness to entities other than ourselves. The procedure should therefore treat every depiction of a mental state as carrying an implicit epistemic stance—whether the narrative claims first-person authority over phenomenal quality, whether it permits third-person inference to phenomenal states from behavior or architecture, and whether it presupposes an explanatory gap between physical description and subjective experience.

Please construct for me a step-by-step procedure—ordered questions, conditions for a sufficient answer at each step, explicit stop points where the answer must be "no" or "refer to human judgment," and clear markers for where judgment cannot be eliminated—that I can apply before writing any scene that depicts consciousness. The procedure must specify:

1. How to classify the narrative point of view in the scene (first-person interior monologue, close third-person with phenomenal attribution, external behavioral description, or omniscient architectural report);
2. How to classify the mental state being depicted (phenomenal/qualitative, intentional/access-conscious, or purely functional/computational);
3. How to check whether the combination of (1) and (2) is consistent with the ontological status assigned to the subject in the series bible (human, animal, synthetic);
4. Where the procedure forces a revision of the draft, where it permits the depiction with a flag for thematic justification, and where it requires me to decide as an author rather than apply a rule;
5. What the procedure explicitly cannot resolve—particularly whether synthetic minds in the narrative genuinely possess phenomenal consciousness—so that I do not mistake procedural consistency for metaphysical proof.

Then apply the procedure to three scenes I have outlined:

A. A first-person stream-of-consciousness passage in which a synthetic character reports the "warmth" of a sunset, narrated without any behavioral or architectural correlates being shown;
B. A close third-person depiction of a non-human animal experiencing fear during a storm, rendered entirely through physiological and behavioral detail with no internal phenomenal vocabulary;
C. A scene in which I, the author, want to describe a human patient in a minimally conscious state using only neuroimaging data and nurse observations, but I also want the prose to evoke a lingering subjective presence—an effect I know I want aesthetically but suspect the procedure will reject.

For each application, show the procedure's verdict, including the case—likely C—where the answer conflicts with my artistic intention. Finally, state the limits of the procedure: what it can guarantee about narrative consistency, and what philosophical questions it necessarily leaves open.

The procedure must be usable without specialist philosophical training at the moment of application, must take no more than ten minutes per scene, and must identify who (me, an editor, or a consultant) has to sign off on the judgment-call steps.
