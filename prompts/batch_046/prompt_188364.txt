I just came away from a long argument with a friend about Galileo’s hermeneutics, and I have been trying to organize my thoughts into an essay outline. Rather than the familiar story that Galileo simply separated science from religion, I want to defend a more specific and, I think, more uncomfortable reading. The outline runs as follows.

Galileo’s argument in his letter to Christina of Lorraine is usually summarized as saying that scripture is accommodated to common understanding and should not be used to overturn demonstrated science. But I think this misses the structural asymmetry he actually builds. He treats scriptural references to nature as operating in two registers: a literal-historical register that serves moral and narrative purposes, and a figurative-cosmological register that is only triggered when a demonstrated conclusion in natural philosophy conflicts with the literal text. Crucially, the decision about which register applies is not made within hermeneutics alone; it is made by natural philosophy. This means science does not merely defend itself against scripture—it determines, ex ante, which parts of scripture are open to literal interpretation in the first place.

I also think Galileo relies on an unstated principle that we might call the uniformity of non-deception: just as God does not deceive us through direct revelation about salvation, He does not deceive us through the regularity of sensory experience about nature. Therefore, a literal cosmological reading that conflicts with physics is not just unlikely but theologically impermissible, because it would impute deception to God via nature. My friend pushed back hard on this. She said the argument is circular: Galileo uses scientific findings to fix the interpretive key, then uses the fixed key to insulate science from scriptural challenge. I tried to respond that the circle is broken by Galileo’s distinction between doctrines necessary for salvation—where the literal sense is binding and science is silent—and incidental cosmological descriptions—where the literal sense is defeasible and science is authoritative. But I am genuinely unsure whether Galileo applies this boundary consistently, especially in his discussion of the Joshua miracle, where he allows that the sun might literally have stood still if God suspended natural law, without giving a clear rule for when suspension is permitted versus when reinterpretation is required.

Could you comment on these notes? I am particularly interested in whether the two-register framing is a defensible reading of Galileo’s actual argument or whether it imports modern distinctions he would not recognize. Is the circularity charge serious? Does the salvation-versus-incidental distinction actually do the work I need it to do? And is my reconstructed principle of uniformity of non-deception genuinely implicit in Galileo’s reasoning, or is it an anachronistic projection?

Please be explicit about how confident you are in each part of your assessment, and identify the single point about which you are least sure.
