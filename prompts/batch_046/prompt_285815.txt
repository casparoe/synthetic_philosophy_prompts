For a graduate seminar of sixteen students in political philosophy, meeting for eighty minutes, design a runnable simulation that makes the collective version of constitutional precommitment feel like a lived problem rather than a theoretical device. Do not frame it as an individual Ulysses contract; instead, have the group split into rotating roles—“Constitutional Framers” and successive “Legislative Sessions”—who share a pool of points that can only be distributed according to rules the Framers write, but who also retain a formal amendment clause they can invoke by vote. Build in an asymmetry: some Legislative members were also Framers, so the costs of adherence fall unevenly across what is nominally the same collective agent. Deliver a staged “crisis” via sealed role instructions that makes adherence personally costly for a subset of the Legislative group, generating internal pressure to amend. The rules, roles, timeline (including transitions between framing and legislative rounds), and point-allocation mechanics should be specified precisely. The participants should receive only role cards and rule sheets—no lecture up front—so they discover the tension between sovereignty and self-binding through the voting dynamics themselves. Then provide: (i) the debrief mapping specific moments in the simulation onto features of democratic precommitment (the hidden amendment vote as holding the keys to one’s own cage, the uneven cost distribution as the non-identity of the collective self across time); (ii) an honest prediction of what will go wrong (coalition formation, framing being ignored) and how to handle it without rescuing the concept; and (iii) a frank note on which feature of real constitutional precommitment—intergenerational binding, in which the framers are absent and do not suffer the costs—the activity structurally cannot reproduce and must therefore be supplied by the facilitator. Keep the design free of required citations, bibliographies, or named living scholars.
