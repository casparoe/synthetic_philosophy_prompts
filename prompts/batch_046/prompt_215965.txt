As a philosophy professor preparing a graduate seminar on early modern epistemology, I want to establish the criteria of adequacy for any interpretation of what Descartes's cogito argument actually establishes, before we evaluate particular readings. Write the desiderata: what must an adequate interpretation deliver regarding the argument's conclusion—whether it proves the existence of a thinking substance, a bare particular, a temporal slice, or merely the occurrence of thought; whether the ergo signals an inference, an intuition, or a performative contradiction; and whether the result survives the hyperbolic doubt of the evil demon in full strength. For each requirement, provide the motivating case or textual consideration (e.g., the Lichtenberg-style grammatical objection, the modal ambiguity between contingent and necessary existence, the distinction between de dicto and de re self-reference, and the tension between immediate certainty and inferential form). Flag any pair of desiderata you take to be jointly unsatisfiable—particularly any conflict between requiring a substantial self and requiring immunity to the demon's deception about substance. Indicate which requirements are non-negotiable for a viable interpretation and which reflect camp-specific preferences (e.g., anti-substantialist readings versus Cartesian orthodoxy). Keep the emphasis on the criteria and their grounding considerations rather than on scoring particular commentators, though you may briefly note how the standard substantialist, deflationary, and performative interpretations fare against the list. The aim is a specification sheet: the phenomena an interpretation must account for, the distinctions it must not collapse, and the constraints from Descartes's own text that it cannot violate without losing textual plausibility.
