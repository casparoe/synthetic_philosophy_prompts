I've been trying to work out, on my own, whether Einstein's theory of relativity really forces us to say that the past and future are just as real as what is happening right now—the view often called the block universe. I understand the argument associated with Rietdijk and Putnam: because observers in different states of motion disagree about which distant events are simultaneous with their own present, there can be no single, objective hyperplane of now slicing through the cosmos. From this they infer that every moment—yesterday, today, and a billion years hence—must exist on equal footing, arranged in a four-dimensional spacetime manifold like frames in a film reel.

But I have also been wondering whether this inference can be blocked by a more local picture. Suppose we grant that there is no universal now, yet still insist that temporal becoming is tied not to a global slice but to each individual worldline. Along any particular path through spacetime, events might genuinely come into being as they are encountered, then recede into an accumulated past, without any privileged cosmic clock. There would be no static loaf containing all times at once—only overlapping, path-dependent histories of becoming, each carrying its own successive present.

Please compare these two lines of reasoning. In particular, consider whether the worldline-bound view genuinely escapes the Rietdijk-Putnam conclusion or merely relocates the problem to a local scale; whether either account can make sense of the intuition that time genuinely flows rather than just being laid out; and what each position implies about a specific case, such as a distant supernova, being both already real for one observer and not yet real for another without contradiction. I am not asking for references or a reading list—just a careful conceptual comparison worked out from the ideas themselves.
