Having spent my career in statistical mechanics and only recently turned my attention to moral philosophy, I have been studying luck egalitarianism, particularly the distinction between brute luck and option luck. I am struck by what appears to be a conceptual knot that receives insufficient attention: if natural talents and social circumstances are matters of brute luck that warrant compensation, then the psychological dispositions that constitute choice—risk-tolerance, temporal discounting, even the cognitive architecture underlying deliberation—are themselves partly products of prior brute luck. This generates what might be called a recursive baseline problem: each round of compensation alters the choice-set for the next, yet the metric used to distinguish compensable disadvantage from legitimate choice depends on a baseline that is continuously modified by the compensation itself. Please explain, in British English, whether luck egalitarianism can specify a non-arbitrary stopping rule for this recursion, or whether the brute-luck/option-luck distinction collapses once applied to the endogenous formation of preference and agency. In particular, address whether treating constitutive luck—the luck involved in being the particular kind of agent one is—as fully compensable undermines the moral significance of effort that the theory seeks to preserve, and whether one can draw a coherent line between compensating for a disadvantageous environment and compensating for a disadvantageous temperament without presupposing the very distinction that requires defence.
