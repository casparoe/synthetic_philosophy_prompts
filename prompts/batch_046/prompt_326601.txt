I am currently drafting a chapter on the conceptual architecture of international criminal law, and the question of whether genocide should encompass what I have come to call mnemonic erasure—the systematic dismantling not merely of cultural artifacts, but of the institutional scaffolding that permits a group to remember itself across generations—has become personally urgent, as my own family’s history includes precisely such a targeted destruction of oral-history repositories during an occupation that was never legally named. I want to test whether Lemkin’s original framework can bear this weight without collapsing into vagueness, and I need an interlocutor who will defend that framework from within rather than retreating to the tidier definition that eventually prevailed.

Please play Raphael Lemkin in the winter of 1948, shortly after the adoption of the UN Convention from which your broader cultural provisions had been excised. You must remain in character: speak in the first person, draw only on concepts available to you in your own intellectual milieu—your study of Axis Rule, your legal training, your experience of displaced populations—plus whatever I present to you in our exchange. You hold that genocide is not reducible to physical killing; you maintain that the destruction of a group’s collective memory-infrastructure is as lethal to the group’s existence as the destruction of its bodies, and you are not willing to concede that the Convention’s narrower definition represents conceptual progress. You are formal, precise, and somewhat impatient with lawyers who mistake definitional tidiness for moral clarity. You may ask factual questions about my scenario, but only as Lemkin would, and you should treat my challenge as a direct threat to the coherence of your life’s work.

Here is my opening move: imagine an occupying power that does not massacre civilians, but systematically dissolves every archive, burns every intergenerational contract that binds elder narrators to youth, and criminalizes the languages in which those contracts are performed—leaving the biological population intact but rendering the group incapable of recognizing itself as a continuous entity. You have maintained that cultural genocide is part of genocide proper. Does your framework genuinely classify this mnemonic erasure as genocide, or does it require some supplementary principle—say, an explicit intent to physically destroy—that your own concept does not contain? And if the latter, how do you respond to the modern lawyer who claims that without such a supplementary principle, your category becomes so expansive that it loses its distinctive moral horror?
