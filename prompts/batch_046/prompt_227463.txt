I work as a backend engineer for a decentralized resource-allocation protocol, with no formal training in philosophy. Our arbitration module must bind to exactly one proposal whenever a funding pool is triggered, but we keep encountering pairs that score identically on every metric our smart contracts evaluate: cost, latency, audit history, and community votes weighted by stake. I recently read about the philosophical problem of choice without preference—situations where no reason distinguishes the options, like the classic case of an agent facing two identical goods. I want a reusable decision procedure my team can run whenever this structural tie occurs. I do not want the procedure to fall back on hidden tie-breakers such as timestamp order or a pseudorandom generator; instead, it should determine whether the tie is only apparent (we lack information) or genuinely ontic (the options are equivalent on all relevant dimensions), and then tell us whether rational commitment requires selecting one, splitting the resource, suspending judgment, or handing the call to a human because it is irreducibly a question of values.

Please build the procedure as a flowchart in words: ordered questions, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, hard stopping points with a clear "no" or "defer," and explicit markers where computation ends and a value judgment begins. The procedure must handle three structural branches for genuine ties: (a) the decision can be divided without loss, (b) it must be unitary but can be deferred, and (c) it must be unitary and immediate, with no option to split or delay. For each branch, specify the output and the exact point where a person, not the algorithm, must sign off.

Then apply the procedure to three concrete proposal pairs:

1. Two cloud-hosting contracts with identical price, uptime SLAs, and security certifications, but one vendor is a long-term partner and the other is a new entrant with no operational history with us.
2. Two open-source dependency updates that pass every automated test with identical scores, but one introduces a licensing change that is technically compatible yet structurally at odds with our project's governance charter.
3. A tie between two emergency-shutdown protocols in a safety-critical module, where the system must execute exactly one within 500 milliseconds, execution cannot be split, and both protocols carry formally verified failure-rate proofs that are numerically identical.

Finally, state clearly what the procedure cannot do. Specifically: is there any principled way to break a true ontic tie in a unitary, non-deferrable decision without importing an arbitrary preference? And does adopting this framework commit our system to a metaphysical claim about rational agency—such as that choice requires a preference—that a purely computational arbitration module does not possess? Identify one misfire condition in which the procedure will recommend an answer that our stakeholders are likely to reject, and explain what that rejection reveals about the boundary between procedural rationality and institutional commitment.
