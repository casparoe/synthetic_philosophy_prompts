Read William Golding’s Lord of the Flies (1954) through the philosophy of collective intentionality and joint action, but with a specific framing: treat the boys’ island society not merely as a failure of cooperation, but as a sustained experiment in whether a plural subject can form without pre-existing institutional scaffolding or shared normative language. Focus especially on the conch assemblies, the maintenance of the signal fire, and the organized hunts led by Jack.

In particular, work with two well-known distinctions. First, Michael Bratman’s account of shared cooperative activity, which requires mutual responsiveness, commitment to the joint activity, and commitment to mutual support. Second, Margaret Gilbert’s notion of a plural subject formed by joint commitment, in which individuals pool their wills to act as a unit and are entitled to rebuke one another for defection. Apply both to the boys’ attempts at collective governance.

Then introduce and apply a more niche framing: the problem of epistemic fragmentation in joint action, where participants perform what looks like a single collective act but operate with incompatible, unacknowledged descriptions of what “we” are doing. Use this to analyze moments where Ralph and Jack appear to be cooperating—maintaining the fire, organizing hunts—while actually pursuing incommensurable collective goals.

Your response should have the following sections.

1. Conceptual setup (about 200 words). Briefly lay out Bratman’s conditions, Gilbert’s plural subject, and the idea of epistemic fragmentation in joint action. Do not survey the literature; just define the tools you will use.

2. The reading (about 450 words). Analyze the novel’s major collective scenes—the first assembly with the conch, the division of labor around the fire, Jack’s hunters as a parallel collective, and the final chase—through these three lenses. Say where the framework illuminates a scene or a character’s choice, where the boys’ behavior fits or violates the conditions for genuine joint action, and how epistemic fragmentation explains apparent cooperation that collapses under pressure.

3. Resistance points (about 250 words). Identify where the novel resists this philosophical reading. Consider whether the boys’ age and psychological development make them poor analogues for rational agents; whether the beast introduces a collective delusion that collective intentionality theory is not equipped to explain; and whether Golding’s emphasis on innate savagery undermines the idea that joint commitment is primarily a rational achievement rather than a fragile social artifact.

4. What the novel contributes (about 200 words). Conclude by saying what Golding’s dramatization adds to the philosophical debate that argument alone does not—particularly regarding the fragility of plural subjects in the absence of external authority, and whether collective irrationality can be understood as a failure of joint attention rather than merely a failure of shared intention.

Keep the analysis faithful in both directions: do not reduce the novel to an illustration of the philosophy, and do not distort Bratman’s or Gilbert’s conditions to fit the plot. End with an honest assessment of whether the work vindicates, complicates, or undermines the idea that collective intentionality is primarily a matter of shared mental states.
