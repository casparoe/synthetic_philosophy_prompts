I'm in this political philosophy study group and we've been stuck for like six months on whether republican freedom — Pettit's idea about non-dommination — is actually different from negative liberty or just negative liberty with extra steps. One camp says the only difference is that republicans care about whether interferrence is arbitrary, which is still just a reliability condition on the absence of obstacles, so Berlin's framework already covers it if you're careful. The other camp insists that domination is a status thing: being subject to someone else's will even when they don't actually interfere, which is structurally different from just having fewer options. We keep re-running the same examples — the benevolent master, the law that protects you but was passed by a dictator — and nobody's convinced anyone.

My working theory is that the stall isn't about the examples but about a hidden disagreement on what makes interference 'arbitrary' — is it about the procedure that made the rule, or about the substance of what the rule permits? But then I came across this weird niche idea in some recent literature on algorithmic governance: call it 'epistemic domination,' where you technically have all the procedural protections Pettit wants — democratic oversight, contestability — but the rules are enforced by opaque models you can't understand, so you don't actually know what counts as complying. Does this break republican freedom, or does it just show Pettit missed an epistemic dimension? And more importantly, is the reason the classic debate never resolves that both sides treat domination purely as a power relation, ignoring whether knowing the rules matters?

So I want a diagnosis: is this a verbal disagreement about 'arbitrary,' with both sides answering different questions — one about institutional design, one about phenomenology? Or is there a real substantive split about whether freedom requires epistemic access to the norms that govern you? And given your diagnosis, what would actually count as progress — a new conceptual distinction, a formal model of arbitrary power, or maybe accepting that the question is ill-posed because 'freedom' is doing two jobs? Don't just pick a side; tell me what kind of disagreement this really is and whether injecting this epistemic-domination thing resolves anything or just moves the stall downstairs.
