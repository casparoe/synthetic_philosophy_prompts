Next month I present at an interdisciplinary security-ethics seminar a thesis I am developing for a longer monograph: that armed neutrality is morally coherent only under universal conscription, because outsourcing lethal risk to a professional or selectively drafted minority breaks the distributive symmetry required to justify a state's refusal to ally. I draw on the Swiss militia tradition, Sweden's 2017 reactivation of gender-neutral universal service, and Finland's post-2022 shift to a selective reserve model with rapid mobilization, arguing that Finland's two-tier system—where a shrinking cohort bears sustained risk while the majority enjoys protected neutrality—undermines the democratic legitimacy that neutrality claims. The audience will be about fifteen people: liberal egalitarian political philosophers, two Kantian republicans, comparative security scholars who study Nordic defense reform, and one invited Finnish policy advisor who helped design the 2022 conscription overhaul. They are not pacifists and do not reject neutrality in principle, but they are deeply skeptical of "citizen-soldier" romanticism, will demand empirical evidence that universal conscription actually produces more egalitarian risk distribution, and will test whether my argument illegitimately collapses strategic efficacy into moral justification.

Prepare me for the Q&A. I want the objections in roughly the order they will come, phrased in the vocabulary each subgroup uses—particularly the liberal egalitarians' concerns about coercion and gender equity, the security scholars' empirical counterexamples, and the Finnish expert's institutional defense. For each objection, tell me what I should concede up front, what I should hold firm on, and provide a reply I can deliver in about two minutes, with an explicit note on where that reply is weakest. Include the single question I am hoping nobody asks—likely something that forces me to choose between bodily-autonomy objections to conscription and the democratic-legitimacy framework I rely on. At the very end of your response, provide a one-sentence strategic summary on its own line beginning "Bottom line:".
