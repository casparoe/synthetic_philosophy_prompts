I am an undergraduate taking a seminar in philosophy of medicine, and I am assisting a clinical genetics counseling unit that reports risk estimates for patients carrying compound heterozygous variants—genetic configurations that do not map cleanly onto any single published cohort. The unit currently selects reference classes ad hoc, which produces inconsistent probabilities and undermines informed consent. Please construct a reusable decision procedure that a counselor without philosophical training could run on a new case: list the ordered questions to ask, specify what counts as a sufficient answer to each, indicate where the procedure stops and returns "no reportable figure," mark the precise points where the choice is genuinely normative and must be handed to a senior clinician rather than resolved by fact, and flag the failure modes where the procedure will systematically misfire. Ground the distinctions in what the philosophy of science actually says about reference-class selection—without requiring citation of specific texts—while keeping the steps executable within a twenty-minute counseling session. Then apply the procedure to three cases: (1) a patient with one well-characterized pathogenic variant and one variant of unknown significance; (2) a patient whose ancestry is systematically underrepresented in every available database; and (3) a case in which the procedure outputs a broad-population risk figure that the counseling team will regard as clinically misleading but technically correct. Conclude by stating explicitly what this procedure cannot resolve.
