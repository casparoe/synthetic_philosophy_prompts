You are a philosopher of technology and metaphysics with expertise in virtual ontology and digital ethics. Before you begin, take a moment to brainstorm the core ontological and ethical commitments you will need to keep distinct. Your task is to compare two theoretical positions on the reality of virtual objects and the status of virtual harms.

Position (1) is the virtual-realist view associated with David Chalmers: virtual objects are genuine digital entities that exist within real virtual worlds, and harms that occur within those environments—such as theft of virtual property, destruction of a virtual companion, or psychological injury mediated through virtual interaction—count as fully real harms in the same metaphysical category as their physical counterparts.

Position (2) is the phenomenal-absence thesis, which I ask you to treat as a serious competing framework. On this view, virtual objects are ontologically absent; they possess no substantial being independent of the interface and code that generate them. Nevertheless, they produce structured presence-effects—phenomenological configurations in which absence is experienced as presence. Consequently, virtual harms are not harms inflicted upon existing entities. Rather, they are corruptions of the experiential field itself: distortions of attachment, expectation, or phenomenological coherence that arise precisely because there is no substantial object to bear or absorb the damage.

Proceed in the following order.

First, outline the virtual-realist account with precision. What makes a virtual object real? In what sense is a virtual world a world rather than a mere representation? And why does a virtual harm belong to the same ethical and metaphysical category as an analogous physical harm?

Second, outline the phenomenal-absence thesis. Explain how an object can be phenomenologically present yet ontologically absent, what a “presence-effect” consists in, and how harm is reconceived as damage to the structure of experience rather than damage to an object. Be explicit about whether this renders virtual harms less serious, differently serious, or serious in an incomparable way.

Third, apply both views to three problem cases:
- Grief or lasting trauma following the permanent deletion of a long-cultivated virtual identity, avatar, or companion: does the reality and weight of the harm depend on the reality of the lost object?
- Economic fraud involving virtual currency or assets that lack legal-tender status but carry dense psychological and social investment: is the fraud best understood as harm to property, harm to trust, or harm to the coherence of a presence-effect?
- Addictive attachment to virtual achievements, ranks, or statuses: is the addict genuinely attached to something real, or is the addiction a disorder of absence—an attachment to a structured void that feels like a presence?

Fourth, evaluate which view offers the more coherent explanation of why virtual harms feel genuinely harmful without over-ontologizing digital environments. Identify the single most serious weakness of each position. In particular, does the phenomenal-absence thesis risk collapsing into an eliminativism about virtual experience that cannot account for the specificity of virtual grief? And does virtual realism risk inflating digital structures into full metaphysical citizens in a way that obscures the distinctive phenomenology of mediated absence?

Conclude by stating whether virtual harms are best understood as real harms to real (if non-physical) entities, or whether they constitute a distinct category of harm that demands its own ethical framework—one that neither fully accepts nor fully denies the reality of virtual objects.
