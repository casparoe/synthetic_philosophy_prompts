I'm currently taking an advanced placement course in comparative ethics, and yesterday my study group became divided over a question our textbook raises only in a footnote: whether classical Buddhist ethics is best understood as a form of consequentialism. Two of my classmates, Maya and Leo, argued through several rounds, and I am writing to ask whether you can adjudicate their exchange move by move, since I genuinely do not know who is right about what.

Maya opened by observing that the Buddha repeatedly advises weighing the fruits of action—avoiding harm because it produces suffering for others and unfavorable conditions in future rebirth. From this she concluded that Buddhist morality is fundamentally outcome-driven: an act is wrong precisely when it generates more dukkha than an available alternative, which sounds like long-run welfare maximization.

Leo replied that this misses the central Abhidharmic teaching that the moral quality of an action resides in cetana—volitional intention—rather than in its external result, vipaka. He noted that the tradition sharply distinguishes an act done with pure intent that accidentally causes harm from an act done with malicious intent that accidentally produces benefit, implying that moral evaluation tracks the mental origin, not the consequence. If ethics were consequentialist, Leo argued, these verdicts ought to be reversed.

Maya countered that Leo was describing a causal mechanism, not a normative criterion. She granted that karma operates through mental causation, but insisted that pure intention matters instrumentally: it is valued because it reliably produces aggregate welfare across the cycle of rebirth. On her reading, Buddhist ethics is a long-horizon consequentialism about suffering over multiple lives, with cetana serving as the dependable cause of good outcomes rather than as an intrinsically right-making feature.

Leo then introduced the Mahayana concept of upaya—skillful means—where advanced bodhisattvas are said to violate conventional precepts, for instance by deceiving or even harming, when doing so prevents far greater suffering. Maya seized on this: if outcomes never override intentions, she asked, why would tradition celebrate such violations? Leo responded that upaya is not justified by a utilitarian calculus but by the bodhisattva's non-dual wisdom, prajna, and compassionate motivation; the act remains technically transgressive, and the bodhisattva accepts any negative karmic residue precisely because no cleaner path exists, not because the ends wash away the means.

At this point Maya offered a synthetic challenge: if Buddhist ethics cares about both intention and outcome yet gives them incompatible verdicts in cases of accidental harm versus intentional self-sacrifice, then the tradition lacks a unified criterion and collapses into an unstable hybrid. Leo answered that this is not instability but layered analysis—conventional morality, sila, operates through intention for ordinary practitioners, while ultimate wisdom, prajna, sees through the agent-object distinction altogether—so asking which single criterion wins presupposes a false choice between two levels of teaching.

Please go through their exchange move by move—rating each step from clearly mistaken to clearly right with brief justification—and say who is more right overall. In particular, I want to know whether Maya's long-run consequentialist reading actually captures the normative structure of Buddhist ethics, or whether Leo's two-level account avoids that reading without becoming ad hoc, and whether either of them is missing something important about how karma and intention interact.
