I am drafting a novel in which a philosopher-character debates her sister over whether human beings have any moral reason to bring a child into existence. The sister relies on what she calls the invitation analogy: deciding to procreate is like deciding whether to send an invitation to an unknown stranger to a party you are hosting—if the party is genuinely good, the stranger benefits from attending, but if you never extend the invitation, no one is harmed by the absence, since there is no particular person yet standing outside who can be said to miss it. She deploys this to license the familiar asymmetry: no obligation to create happy people, but a clear obligation not to create suffering ones. I want an audit of how far this correspondence actually carries.

Please lay out the mapping explicitly—what plays the role of the host, the invitation, the guest-list, the quality of the party, the act of withholding, and the state of never having been invited—then show where each correspondence fractures. Does an invitation presuppose an addressee who already exists in some minimal sense, and if so, does that collapse the parallel to creating ex nihilo? Does the analogy quietly import a copycat conception of potential persons, treating them as interchangeable guests who might fill a seat rather than as singular origins whose non-existence is not a deprivation? Examine whether the party framework smuggles in the idea that absence is a neutral default rather than a foreclosed possibility, and whether it hides an asymmetry in agency—since not inviting requires only inaction, whereas not procreating is also an active reproductive choice in a world of available means. Finally, tell me whether the asymmetry conclusion survives once these disanalogies are on the table, or whether the analogy was doing all the argumentative work by making non-creation look like a harmless omission. If the invitation picture breaks down, suggest whether a different analogy—perhaps drawn from artistic creation or from the ethics of founding an institution—might better capture the procreative relation without importing these hidden commitments about hospitality, interchangeability, and the moral neutrality of absence.
