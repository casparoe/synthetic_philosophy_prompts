I am a doctoral student in philosophy of religion, and I have been asked to deliver the opening remarks—roughly ten minutes—at an interdisciplinary workshop my department is co-hosting with the university's divinity school, titled 'The Ethical and Its Limits.' The audience will include analytic philosophers of religion, theologians who have worked extensively with Kierkegaard's pseudonymous authorship, and graduate students from ethics and political philosophy who have little background in nineteenth-century Danish thought. I want to build the remarks around Kierkegaard's Abraham and the teleological suspension of the ethical, but not as decorative reference: the idea must structure what I can and cannot say about our collective task, which is to examine whether ethical reasoning has boundaries it should not cross.

Specifically, I want to reject the standard introductory framing—what I have been calling in my dissertation the 'rupture reading,' according to which Abraham's willingness to sacrifice Isaac represents a temporary exemption from universal moral law—in favor of an alternative I am developing: that the suspension is better understood as an 'intensification of the ethical through teleological orientation,' in which the universal is not negated but re-contextualized by a commitment that outstrips the vocabulary available to ethical judgment itself. Because part of the room knows this literature well, I cannot misrepresent the pseudonymous framing or treat Abraham as a straightforward moral exemplar; because the other part does not, I must explain any technical distinction in accessible language and avoid implying that scholars are literally called to suspend ethical norms in their research. The speech should therefore navigate a tight structural constraint: it must honor the seriousness of Abraham's faith as something that challenges ethical complacency, without licensing anyone to treat institutional or interpersonal obligations as disposable.

Please write the remarks in a formal, polished register appropriate to a ceremonial but intellectually serious occasion—something that could be spoken to a mixed audience without either condescending to the non-specialists or alienating the philosophers. Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citations; do not quote any specific passage verbatim unless you can verify it, and in any case paraphrase freely rather than relying on direct quotation. The speech should land as an invitation to the workshop's theme rather than as a lecture on Kierkegaard.
