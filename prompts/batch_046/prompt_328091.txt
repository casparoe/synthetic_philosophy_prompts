Recently, in preparing for a seminar on nineteenth-century naturalism, I have been working closely with Darwin’s Descent of Man and with his private notebooks on ethics, and I keep encountering a particular argumentative pattern that I cannot comfortably place within the standard taxonomy of his mistakes. In several passages Darwin traces a moral sentiment—sympathy for the weak, or the disapproval of incest—through a continuum that begins with instinctive behaviors in social insects, proceeds through tribal groups who display the sentiment only within narrow kin circles, and culminates in Europeans who extend the same feeling to strangers and even to other animals. So far the account is purely genealogical. But then, without announcing any shift in register, he describes the broader, more inclusive form not merely as more recent but as higher, treats its absence in other cultures as a kind of moral stunting rather than a local adaptation, and suggests that further evolutionary development will necessarily produce still more expansive altruism. The evaluation seems to ride inside the evolutionary narrative rather than being separately defended.

A second instance occurs in his treatment of religious belief. Darwin explains religion as a byproduct of evolved cognitive tendencies—excessive agency detection and fear of unknown causes—operating in minds that have not yet developed scientific reasoning. Having offered this causal history, he then remarks that as reason advances, religious belief naturally withers, and he characterizes that withering not just as a historical change but as an intellectual and moral improvement. Once again, the normative weight appears to be smuggled in through the directional structure of the story itself.

I have tried to fit this into familiar categories, but none seems precise. It is not the naturalistic fallacy in its classic form, because Darwin is not deriving an isolated ought from an isolated is; he is embedding evaluation inside a progressive historical trajectory. It is not exactly social Darwinism, since he does not invoke selection to justify competitive cruelty but rather to justify a humanitarian, expansive ethics. And it differs from plain ethnocentrism because the ranking is tied to a developmental scale rather than to simple national or racial preference. Is there an established concept that captures this specific move—using an evolutionary genealogy to generate an implicit normative hierarchy without separating descriptive and prescriptive stages? If there is no precise term, I would like a definition that distinguishes it from the naturalistic fallacy, from Whig history, and from straightforward cultural prejudice, together with an account of its boundaries: for instance, whether it requires belief in evolutionary progress, or whether an analogous move could occur within a cyclical or non-progressive historical narrative.
