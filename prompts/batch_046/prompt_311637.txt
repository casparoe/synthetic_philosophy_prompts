I have been trying to work out the conceptual architecture behind a specific institutional practice that has become central to contemporary political science and international law: the classification of regimes—most prominently Hungary, Poland, and Turkey, with growing debate about whether the United States belongs in the same category—as cases of "democratic backsliding," followed by the deployment of institutional remedies such as EU rule-of-law conditionality, suspension of voting rights, or targeted sanctions. The label is not merely descriptive; it operates as a normative diagnosis that authorizes external interference with domestic constitutional processes that would otherwise be protected by norms of sovereignty and democratic self-determination.

What philosophical commitments does this practice presuppose? I want you to reconstruct the implicit theory of democracy, legitimacy, and historical change that makes the backsliding framework coherent. Does measuring decline require treating democracy as a unidimensional, scalar property anchored to a canonical liberal model—complete with independent courts, competitive multiparty elections, and protected civil liberties—rather than as a cluster concept that permits substantial institutional variation? Does the practice depend on a teleological assumption that liberal democracy is not just one form among others but the privileged endpoint of political development, so that deviation is naturally read as regression rather than as divergence or innovation? And when international bodies declare that a member state has backslid, do they presuppose a universalist moral claim that democratic legitimacy can be assessed and overridden from outside, or can the same interventions be justified on thinner, procedural grounds?

Please also distinguish what the practice strictly requires from what it merely fits well with. Is the backsliding concept philosophically underdetermined—compatible with republican theories of non-domination, liberal perfectionist accounts, and even realist stability concerns—such that different monitors might apply the same label for fundamentally different reasons? Or does the very structure of the remedies, which often tie funding to specific judicial and media-pluralism metrics, smuggle in substantive commitments about the proper content of democracy that are not conceptually necessary for democratic governance? Finally, tell me what would have to change in measurement, adjudication, and remedy if we replaced the backsliding metaphor with an alternative framework—say, one that treats constitutional change as democratic experimentation or as a shift between incommensurable democratic forms—without thereby abandoning all normative evaluation of authoritarian drift.
