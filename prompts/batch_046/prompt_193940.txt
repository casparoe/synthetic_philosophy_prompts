I am preparing an essay for a graduate seminar on philosophy of history, and my study partner—who has been reading recent work on structural causation in historical explanation—has constructed an argument from Fernand Braudel’s idea of the longue durée that now strikes me as proving far too much. We are discussing Braudel’s well-known distinction between the deep, almost immobile structures of geography, climate, and demographic rhythm, and the surface history of events, individuals, and political narrative. My partner accepts Braudel’s claim that these slow structures form the primary explanatory framework, but he thinks this commitment, combined with a standard interventionist difference-making account of causation, entails that all event-based historiography is systematically false. I have laid out his reasoning below with numbered premises, and I want to know precisely where it slips, because I would like to preserve both Braudel’s structural priority and our ordinary belief that we can genuinely explain particular outcomes—say, why a specific treaty included one clause rather than another—by citing particular agents and contingent choices. The argument runs: (1) For Braudel, long-term geographical and climatic structures do not merely influence human societies; they determine the range of historically possible social, economic, and institutional configurations at any given epoch, while individual actions and contingent events are confined to selecting among options already permitted by that structural framework. (2) A factor is genuinely causally explanatory of an outcome only if, holding background conditions fixed, altering that factor would alter the outcome—the standard interventionist difference-making criterion. (3) Individual decisions and specific events—such as a diplomat’s acceptance of a clause or the accidental death of a monarch—do not alter the underlying geographical or climatic structures, and therefore do not change the range of possible outcomes; they determine only which option within the pre-fixed range is actualized. (4) Applying the criterion in (2) consistently, then, long-term structures are the true causes of the range of outcomes, but the selecting events are not causes of the specific outcome within that range; they are merely the occasion on which the structural cause manifests one of its permitted results, a form of structural overdetermination that renders selection causally inert. (5) Consequently, to cite a specific agent or event as explaining a particular historical result—claiming, for instance, that the treaty was signed in 1648 with that specific clause because the negotiators made a particular choice—is a category mistake: the genuine explanation is always the long-term framework, and the specific selection adds nothing causal. (6) If all particular selections are causally inert, then political history, biography, military narrative, and indeed any historiography centered on individuals or discrete events is not merely superficial but systematically false, attributing efficacy where there is none. (7) Yet we clearly possess justified, knowledge-constituting explanations of particular outcomes—why one clause was included rather than another, why a revolution broke out in one decade rather than the next—that depend essentially on citing specific agents and contingent occurrences. (8) To avoid outright contradiction with (7), we must reject (1): long structures are not determinative causes but only background conditions, and Braudel’s claim that they constitute the true level of history collapses into a mere preference for large-scale description.

My partner insists that if we reject any step other than (1), we either abandon the difference-making criterion—which he thinks is indispensable—or we smuggle in a mysterious form of downward causation from agents onto structures. I am not convinced, but I need to identify the exact premise or inference to reject, explain why it looked plausible, and say what rejecting it costs for both Braudelian historiography and for our ordinary explanatory practices. I would especially like to keep both (1) and (7) intact if that is defensible.
