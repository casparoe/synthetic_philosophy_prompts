I am a novelist delivering the opening remarks at an interdisciplinary workshop attended by moral philosophers and fiction writers in equal measure. I want you to write a ten-minute address built around the philosophical question of whether maximal moral goodness is actually desirable—not whether it is achievable, but whether a life of complete moral determinacy would remain a life worth narrating. The idea must do structural work: within the logic of the speech, I should be unable to celebrate unalloyed virtue or to offer a redemptive arc of moral perfection, because I am advancing the claim that such perfection eliminates the "narrative remainder"—the unexplained, unmotivated residue of choice that makes a life legible both to others and to oneself. The philosophers should recognize the underlying debate without being lectured; the writers should feel the constraint on storytelling rather than receiving an abstract thesis. If the premise that moral sainthood renders a life narratively empty is confused, or if this task cannot be done well without distorting either philosophy or fiction, tell me plainly rather than smoothing over the difficulty.
