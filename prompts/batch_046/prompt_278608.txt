I'm giving the retirement toast for my uncle, who ran a cooperative hardware store for thirty five years. About half the room are old co-op members who used to argue with him about Aristotles distinction between oikonomia and chrematistike back in the seventies; the other half are family and neighbors who just remember him letting people pay with labor when they were broke. I want the toast to honor his refusal to treat money as anything but a measure of real need, but I also dont want it to sound like a lecture on ancient economics. There's this idea I've been turning over lately — that for Aristotle money isnt just convention, its a kind of pledge, a symbolon, that works only when its tied back to actual use, and that interest is wrong not just because its greedy but because money is barren and cant reproduce like wheat or animals, so charging for its offspring is ontologically weird. I want the speech to use that barrenness idea structurally: it should explain why he never charged interest on store credit, why he kept prices tied to replacement cost rather than market rate, and why that wasnt charity but a kind of honesty about what exchange is for. But it needs to land for the family side too — they shouldnt feel like they're being taught Greek. Can you write a five-minute toast that does this? Keep it warm, keep the philosophy doing real work, and dont pretend he was perfect — he was stubborn and it cost him.
