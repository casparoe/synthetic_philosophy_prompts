(1) When scientific peers disagree, conciliation should only happen at the same reasoning level — I reject level-splitting because in actual lab work you can't neatly separate object-level measurements from meta-level doubts about your instruments.

(2) Epistemic akrasia is never rational: if your total evidence makes P more likely false than true, holding onto P is irrational regardless of practical costs like grant money or reputation.

(3) I often maintained theoretical interpretations in my career despite knowing that most specialists disagreed and despite feeling privately unsure, and I think that was rational because I had direct access to raw calibration logs that others never examined.

(4) The calibration literature shows people are systematically overconfident, so higher-order disagreement should always push your credence downward — never leave it flat or push it up.

(5) There's no deep distinction between first-order and higher-order evidence; it's just evidence with different causal paths, so talking about levels is only a bookkeeping device.

(6) The diachronic Dutch book argument for conciliation fails, but not because the premises are false — rather, you avoid the book by revising your prior about your own reasoning reliability instead of revising your belief in P directly.

So do these hang together? I feel like (3) pulls against (2) and (5) undermines (1), but I'm not sure if those are real conflicts or just me being sloppy. Which pairs are genuinely in tension, which ones only look like it, and what's the least costly revision?
