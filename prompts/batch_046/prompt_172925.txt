An argument I have been developing for a workshop now strikes me as suspicious: its premises seem defensible, but the conclusion is far stronger than I can accept.

(1) The separateness-of-persons objection targets utilitarianism because there is no collective moral subject over and above individuals; imposing severe burdens on one for smaller benefits distributed across many treats separate lives as fungible containers of value.

(2) If that objection is sound, then any normative framework permitting non-consensual interpersonal trade-offs—where one person's welfare is sacrificed for gains to others—commits the same error.

(3) This extends well beyond act-utilitarianism. Social-choice aggregation rules (including expected-utility frameworks such as Harsanyi's) justify policies that impose certain losses on identifiable individuals for probabilistic benefits spread across populations. Even democratic taxation and triage protocols involve imposing costs on some for goods to others without explicit individual consent.

(4) If separateness genuinely blocks all such trade-offs, then routine practices—public-goods funding, risk-pooling, or emergency rationing—are morally impermissible.

(5) That result is implausible; at least some of these practices are justified.

(6) So (1) must be false, and separateness provides no real constraint on aggregation.

I am reluctant to abandon (1) entirely, since it captures something important about why distribution-sensitive objections feel compelling. Where does the reasoning slip? Is (2) an equivocation between sum-maximizing aggregation and any interpersonal comparison of welfare? Or is there a narrower formulation of (1) that blocks utilitarianism without collapsing into (4)? Give a committed diagnosis: identify the weakest step, explain why it looked plausible, and state precisely what salvaging (1) requires and what it costs.
