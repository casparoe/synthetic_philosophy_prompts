I'm stuck on an argument that uses Al-Ghazali's occassionalism to do more damage than I think he intended. (1) Al-Ghazali denies that fire has any power to burn cotton; God creates the burning directly whenever fire is present, with no necessary connection. (2) If there's genuinely no necessary connection between cause and effect, then observing fire burn cotton a thousand times gives us literally zero rational basis to expect it next time—Hume's problem becomes absolute, not just skeptical. (3) But Al-Ghazali clearly relied on regular experience in his own reasoning and didn't think he was destroying science. (4) So either he failed to notice that his view annihilates induction, or he secretly assumes God will keep the regularities going—which is just occasionalism plus a theological guarantee. (5) That guarantee doesn't come from the philosophy of causation itself; it comes from theology or divine custom, so occasionalism as a standalone causal thesis still kills induction unless you import something external. (6) Therefore occasionalism either makes scientific knowledge impossible or it isn't really a standalone causal theory. Where does this go wrong, if it does? If I have to choose, I'd rather keep (1) and (2) than (6), but I want to know what I'm paying.
