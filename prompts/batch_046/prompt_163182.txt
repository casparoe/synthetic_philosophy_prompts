I'm preparing a taxonomy handout for a graduate seminar on philosophy of technology and labor, and I want it to avoid the standard encyclopedia layout that simply lists Aristotelian craft-virtue, Marxist alienation, and post-work utopia as three static columns. The students already know Arendt's separation of labor, work, and action, and they know the basic Marxist claim that capitalism alienates workers from their products; what they need is a diagnostic instrument that sorts them by the logical commitments they actually hold about meaningful activity in a fully automated economy. Please construct a decision tree—phrased entirely as concrete branch questions a student could answer without knowing which philosopher the answer maps onto, with leaves carrying named positions, unnamed but coherent combinations, or explicit "cannot be separated here" flags—that addresses the root commitment: "If autonomous systems eventually perform all necessary material production, can human activity still be genuinely meaningful, and if so, what feature must it preserve?"

Build the tree from the logical space rather than from a roster of authors. The first split should ask whether meaningfulness requires that the activity contribute to material survival at all. From there, branch through at least four further distinctions: whether social recognition alone can substitute for material contribution; whether hidden human labor sustaining apparently seamless automated interfaces—sometimes discussed under the concept of ghost labor—restores meaning even when the visible output seems machine-made; whether the activity must express an irreducibly human capacity such as embodied judgment or risk regardless of economic function; and whether full automation reveals that work was always primarily about status allocation rather than production. I want at least eight leaves, including an Aristotelian virtue-ethics essentialist view; a Marxist post-work liberationist view; an Arendtian view that abandons labor but preserves action in a public realm; a secularized Protestant-work-ethic view that treats disciplined activity as an end in itself even without economic necessity; and one unnamed leaf for the coherent but rarely defended combination that accepts both the ghost-labor restoration thesis and the status-allocation revelation—namely, that the only meaningful post-automation roles are certified human positions that maintain the illusion of full automation purely for social distinction. Wherever two positions genuinely overlap on every branch you have defined—such as certain craft-expressivist views and narrative-identity theories of occupation—flag the ambiguity explicitly rather than inventing a false separation. Note any unoccupied logical combinations briefly, diagnosing whether the gap seems incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored. Fix the audience as advanced undergraduates or early graduate students, do not include bibliographies, citations, or reading lists, and keep the whole instrument to a size that could fit on a single seminar handout page.
