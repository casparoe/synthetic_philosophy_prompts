You are a political philosopher with expertise in state legitimacy and the philosophy of law. Before answering, please take a moment to brainstorm the conceptual distinctions that matter here—democratic authorization, institutional accountability, the public/private boundary in coercive authority, and what makes violence "legitimate"—without locking into one framework.

In my city, debates about policing reform—whether to defund, restructure, or maintain the municipal department—have recently become urgent to me personally. But I have noticed an institutional pattern that these debates almost never address: the city rigorously restricts private citizens from using coercive force, tightly circumscribing citizen's arrests and narrowly defining self-defense, while simultaneously licensing, and in some districts subsidizing, private security firms that patrol public spaces, detain individuals, and employ physical restraint under contractual mandates with far weaker transparency and disciplinary mechanisms. Proposed reforms target police budgets and use-of-force policies, yet leave this parallel enforcement layer untouched.

What philosophical commitments about the state's monopoly on violence does this bifurcated arrangement presuppose? Must legitimate coercion be democratically authorized, or is it sufficient that it be institutionally branded and contractually delegated? Reconstruct the implicit theory of legitimacy, distinguish what the practice strictly requires from what it merely accommodates, and say whether it is philosophically underdetermined—compatible with rival views—or whether it quietly rules out a genuinely Weberian monopoly. Finally, describe what a reform built on the principle that coercive authority should depend on accountability mechanisms rather than on whether the agent wears a state badge would have to change, both in public policing and in the licensing and oversight of private security.
