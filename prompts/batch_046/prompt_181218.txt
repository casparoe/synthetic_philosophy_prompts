I'm a journalist writing on philosophy of language and speculative fiction, and I'm preparing a feature on Denis Villeneuve's Arrival—adapted from Ted Chiang's Story of Your Life—read through Willard Van Orman Quine's thesis of the indeterminacy of translation. In Quine's classic scenario, a field linguist observing a native utter gavagai as a rabbit runs past can never be sure whether the term refers to a rabbit, a temporal stage of rabbit, or an undetached rabbit part; behavioral evidence alone underdetermines the translation manual. In the film, linguist Louise Banks decodes heptapod B, a logographic script that expresses meaning simultaneously rather than sequentially, and gradually comes to perceive time as the aliens do—non-linearly, with future and present co-present. I want a reading that treats the heptapod writing system not as a linear string to be parsed but as a behavioral output that lacks the temporal sequencing Quine implicitly assumes in radical translation. Does the film suggest that when an interlocutor's ontology—specifically a simultaneous, non-causal view of time—is radically different, the underdetermination of translation collapses into a deeper problem: is Banks translating heptapod B, or is she being translated into a heptapod ontology, and does Quine's behaviorism have the resources to distinguish the two? Please take both the film's visual grammar—the circular logograms, the flash-forwards that blur cause and effect—and Quine's actual argument seriously, without reducing either to illustration. Which scenes best dramatize the moment when semantic translation becomes ontological conversion? Where does the film resist a Quinean reading—perhaps by framing Banks's transformation as emotional or epistemic rather than a shift in analytical hypotheses? And what does this pairing reveal about the limits of behaviorist theories of meaning when the minds in question do not share our linear temporal architecture? I'm writing for readers who know Quine's Word and Object in outline but have not seen this analysis applied to the film, and I'd like the essay to conclude by saying what Arrival adds to the philosophical debate about indeterminacy that a standard argument alone cannot capture.
