I'm putting together a little zine for our philosophy reading group — we just finished Bradley's Appearance and Reality and everyones was arguing about whether the regress is vicious or just a feature of analysis. I want to close the zine with a poem, maybe 20-24 lines, using a broken sestina form. Here's the twist: the six end-words should be 'term', 'bind', 'whole', 'fall', 'again', 'real' — and the usual spiraling repetition should literally enact the regress, so that every time 'bind' shows up it's trying to hold two terms together but needs another binder. The envoi (the last three lines) should refuse to resolve: instead of tying the knots together, it should leave the relation dangling, maybe by repeating 'fall' and 'again' side by side. I want it to be accurate enough that someone who's actually read Bradley wouldn't wince — especially the part where the relation can't be both internal and external without spawning a new relation — but I don't want a bibliography or footnotes, just the poem. Could you write that? Also maybe include one line that sounds like it's addressing Russell's objection (that relations are real and don't need further relations) but then lets it collapse back into the regress. Keep it casual, like something you'd actually read in a zine, not an academic essay in verse.
