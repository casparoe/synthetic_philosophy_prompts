A recent essay in an unnamed popular philosophy magazine summarizes Hegel’s position on war as follows. The essay claims that Hegel regards war not as a contingent misfortune but as a structural institution that completes ethical life, functioning analogously to the corporation: just as the corporation mediates between private economic interest and the universal will of the state, war mediates between the atomistic particularity of civil society and the state’s universal ethical substance. According to the essay, this mediating role explains Hegel’s preference for citizen-based military service over purely mercenary forces—only the citizen-soldier, like the corporate member, relates to the state as an end rather than as a means. The essay then connects this political logic to the master-slave dialectic, proposing that war supplies the collective life-and-death struggle through which states achieve mutual recognition, paralleling the individual recognition achieved through the dialectic. Finally, the essay interprets Hegel’s remark that war is the health of the ethical state as part of this mediating framework: rather than describing a destructive exception, it identifies war as a necessary corrective that prevents the state from stagnating into private interest, and the essay concludes that this makes Hegel’s philosophy of right compatible with modern doctrines of collective security provided we understand ethical life as a form of group self-actualization.

How fair is this characterization? Examine it claim by claim. For each element—(1) the corporation-war analogy as mediating institutions, (2) the explanation of military organization through this analogy, (3) the extension of the master-slave dialectic to interstate relations, and (4) the interpretation of war’s health-preserving function and its compatibility with collective security—indicate whether it is textually supported by Hegel’s writings, a plausible but partial simplification, or a misrepresentation that conflates distinct doctrines or imports an alien framework. Be precise about where Hegel’s actual argument stops and where the essay’s reconstruction begins.
