I'm trying to determine the principle behind my judgments about which national narratives count as lost-cause myths, as opposed to other forms of collective memory about defeat.

My verdicts are these. The postbellum Southern narrative that recasts the Confederacy's defeat as noble martyrdom for constitutional liberty: clearly a lost-cause myth. The Israeli commemoration of Masada, framing the defenders' suicide in 73 CE as heroic resistance to Roman tyranny despite the crushing of Jewish autonomy: also clearly in the family. The Serbian Kosovo narrative, transforming the 1389 military defeat into a sacred covenant guaranteeing spiritual sovereignty and eventual redemption: again, clearly a lost-cause myth. The British framing of Dunkirk in 1940, which celebrates communal resilience during strategic withdrawal without any defeated ideological program: I think this is not one, since survival and successful withdrawal were the actual outcomes. The Alamo in 1836: the defenders died, yet Texan independence was eventually achieved; I judge this is not a lost-cause myth, because the cause was not ultimately lost. The Czech commemoration of the 1968 Prague Spring, where reform was crushed by Warsaw Pact forces but commemorated as moral victory for reform socialism, partly vindicated in 1989: I am pulled to call this a lost-cause myth, despite the eventual partial success, because the immediate defeat was total and the myth emphasizes moral purity over strategic outcome.

These seem to turn on whether the narrative requires treating the defeat as permanent, the cause as morally pure regardless of outcome, and the myth as compensatory rather than celebratory — but that still does not cleanly separate the Alamo from the Prague Spring, since both involve eventual vindication yet I classify them differently.

What principle, if any, captures all five settled verdicts? Please state it precisely, identify which clause each case motivates, say what it predicts for the unsettled Finnish Winter War narrative (where Finland lost territory in 1940 but preserved independence, framing the conflict as a moral victory despite territorial defeat), and be explicit about which of my verdicts — especially the Alamo versus Prague Spring tension — it must overturn if it is to remain coherent.
