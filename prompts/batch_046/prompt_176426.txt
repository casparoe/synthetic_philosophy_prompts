A philosophy-of-risk blog summarized the Pasadena paradox for its readers in roughly the following terms. The game is essentially the St. Petersburg setup, except that both positive and negative exponential payoffs are assigned to the same tail-probability sequence, so the expectation splits into two halves that diverge separately—one toward positive infinity, one toward negative infinity—rather than converging to a single infinite value. From this structural feature, the blog infers that any decision framework relying on expected-value maximization is conceptually broken whenever outcomes scale exponentially with small probabilities, which is allegedly why mean-cost optimization in long-tail climate-risk assessments is incoherent. The post further claims that because the expectation does not exist at all, rational agents should fall back on a maximin rule, and it presents this fallback not as a later repair but as the original insight of the Pasadena analysis itself.

Something about this feels too tidy, but the author is usually rigorous. Is this summary fair? I would like an accuracy audit: for each claim, indicate whether it is a correct description of the paradox, a standard simplification, a subtle misrepresentation, or an unsupported conclusion. Specifically, address whether describing the divergence as two halves that cancel or remain in tension is accurate; whether the paradox genuinely undermines expected-value frameworks in applied domains such as climate modeling; whether maximin is the standard or defensible response to an undefined expectation in decision theory; and whether the historical claim about the original purpose of the analysis is plausible. Separate what a careful reader of the decision-theoretic literature would accept from what they would correct, and flag any conclusions that do not survive once the misrepresentations are fixed.
