I sit on the scientific advisory board for our city’s Office of Climate Resilience. By next Friday we must vote on whether to formally adopt the “MetroGrid” regional flooding model for the 2026–2030 capital plan. The model assumes uniform elevation variance across neighborhoods and ignores all informal drainage channels—premises its developers concede are false—yet it has outperformed every mechanistic alternative in back-testing across three prior storm seasons. Several board members invoke Friedman-style reasoning about unrealistic assumptions—sometimes called the “F-twist”—to defend adoption: false premises are methodologically harmless, even virtuous, if predictions are accurate. Others counter that in public planning, relying on causally false assumptions invites catastrophic failure when conditions shift outside the tested range, and that our duty is to transparent causal understanding, not predictive accuracy alone.

Please draft the memo I will circulate before Thursday’s meeting. It should open with a concrete recommendation—full adoption, a conditional pilot with a hard sunset clause, or rejection coupled with an alternative mandate—followed by a clear implementation timeline with specific deadlines. The reasoning must treat the F-twist defense seriously, explaining its actual logical structure rather than a strawman, and it must honestly address the strongest counter-argument: that past predictive success does not license causal trust under unprecedented conditions. Include the single consideration that, if left unanswered, would most likely persuade the board to reject your recommendation. Keep the language accessible to non-philosophers. Maximum 600 words; bottom-line recommendation in the first paragraph.
