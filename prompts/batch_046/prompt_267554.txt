Two colleagues in my philosophy of mathematics seminar disagreed about the implications of a hypothetical discovery that ZFC is inconsistent. One argued that the damage would be localized: only advanced set theory and its direct applications would need revision, while ordinary arithmetic and applied mathematics remain intact, since working mathematicians rarely invoke the full strength of replacement or choice in everyday proofs. The other countered that consistency is not modular—if the foundational framework collapses, the logical authority shared by all its subsystems is undermined, including the rules used to establish arithmetic results. She added that treating mathematics as fragmentable ignores that the very boundary between safe and unsafe domains is drawn using the same set-theoretic tools now shown unreliable, so the infection is total. He replied that foundational work has demonstrated that most ordinary theorems require only weak fragments of arithmetic, and that mathematicians already work within de facto restricted domains; the collapse of the full system therefore need not reach them. She granted the descriptive accuracy but insisted that the normative justification of those fragments depends on embedding them in a consistent larger framework; if that framework is inconsistent, the fragments lose their foundational warrant, not merely their theoretical luxury. He then proposed a paraconsistent resolution: accept contradictions in the set-theoretic realm but quarantine them, leaving arithmetic untouched. She responded that applying non-classical logic to mathematical objects does not quarantine contradiction but dissolves the universal distinction between valid proof and disproof, which undermines arithmetic as thoroughly as classical inconsistency does.

Please adjudicate this exchange move by move. Assess whether each position advanced is clearly right, clearly mistaken, or partly both, with brief justification. Then indicate which party is more right overall, and identify what both miss—specifically, whether a fragmentation strategy can genuinely preserve classical logic in ordinary mathematics without begging the question against the holist about justification, and whether the hypothetical reveals anything about whether mathematical knowledge is essentially hierarchical or essentially plural.
