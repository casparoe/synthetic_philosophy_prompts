Here is where I have landed on institutional design, and I want to know whether the package holds together or whether I am smuggling in contradictions.

(1) I think the core legislature should be selected by lot—citizens serving single, non-renewable terms of roughly three years—because this eliminates campaign incentives, removes the need for fundraising, and produces a descriptively representative body.

(2) For any offices that remain elected—executive positions and, if retained, an upper chamber—I support hard term limits of no more than two terms, on the grounds that career politicians become structurally captured by donors and lobbying networks and gradually lose epistemic contact with ordinary life.

(3) I take democratic equality to require broad rotation in governing roles: no persistent class of rulers should emerge, and citizens should not be permanently divided into governors and governed.

(4) At the same time, I think legislative competence and institutional memory are genuine public goods. So I want former members of the sortition chamber to be eligible for appointment—not election—to a small upper council with staggered, long (say, twelve-year) but non-renewable terms. This council would have drafting authority and advisory power, but no vote on final passage, and its members would be ineligible for any other public office afterward.

(5) I reject the very idea of a "career politician": anyone who spends decades seeking or holding elected power, cycling through offices to remain in government, represents a democratic failure that institutional design should prevent.

My questions: Is this a consistent institutional vision, or do (2) and (4) together recreate the careerist class I condemn in (5), just relocated to appointed roles? Does (3) genuinely survive (4), or does the long-serving council become a permanent ruling stratum despite the rotation in the lower house? And is there a hidden tension between (1) and (2)—if sortition already solves the careerist problem by design, why preserve elected offices with term limits rather than replacing them? Or is that tension only apparent?

Please audit these commitments in order of severity: which pairs genuinely conflict, which conflicts dissolve once the institutional details are specified, and what is the cheapest revision that restores consistency—whether that means abandoning the hybrid model, redefining "career politician" to exclude appointed drafters, or giving up rotation in favor of expertise. Finally, please be explicit about where your reasoning is most likely to fail: what feature of real-world politics or what normative argument about democratic equality would cause you to change your verdict on the coherence of this package?
