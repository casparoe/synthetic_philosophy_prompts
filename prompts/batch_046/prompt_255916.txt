I'm trying to sort out my actual intuitions about whether honoring sunk costs is ever rational, and I keep running into cases where my gut pulls in opposite directions despite the situations looking structurally alike. Here are several concrete scenarios with my verdicts.

First, the concert ticket: I bought a nonrefundable $180 ticket to see a band I loved in college; now I find their music embarrassing, and the show is on a night when I'm exhausted and a close friend is hosting a small dinner I would genuinely enjoy. I think I should skip the concert and treat the $180 as gone regardless—going just because I paid feels clearly wrong.

Second, the novel: I've spent nearly seven years on a literary novel that I now see has an irreparable structural flaw. Rewriting from the midpoint onward would take at least two more years, the market for this kind of fiction has collapsed, and no agent is likely to take it. Still, my gut says I should finish the draft, not because I expect publication or praise, but because abandoning it after this much labor feels like a kind of self-betrayal, even though I know that sounds like the fallacy speaking.

Third, the marathon: I've trained for six months, but two days before the race I have a minor knee injury that running the full distance will worsen, possibly with lasting damage. I think I should withdraw; the months of training do not create any obligation to injure myself further.

Fourth, the family garden: After my father died, he left a note asking me to keep his vegetable garden alive—not for utility, since he knew I dislike gardening, but as a continuing connection to him. I have tended it for three years, and though it takes real time and money, I think I should keep it up. Here the past care I've invested and his explicit request feel like legitimate reasons, not irrational ones.

Fifth, the municipal subway: A city has spent $4 billion on a subway extension that is now severely over budget; completing it requires another $3 billion, but independent ridership projections show the line will never cover operating costs, and the same funds could build a bus network serving far more people. I think they should cancel the project; the $4 billion already spent should play no role in the decision.

Sixth, the dead language: I spent four years becoming fluent in Ancient Greek, and I now have no practical or professional use for it. I am considering stopping all study and letting the skill atrophy. Here I am genuinely torn—part of me says the fluency is woven into my intellectual identity and that letting it die wastes something I built, but another part says that is exactly the sunk-cost reasoning I reject in the concert-ticket case.

My rough, probably ad hoc principle is that honoring sunk costs is permissible only when the past investment has become integrated into one's narrative identity or into an explicit commitment to another person, but not when the investment is purely financial or instrumental. Yet this feels strained: it seems to save the novel and the garden while condemning the subway, but it struggles with the marathon and leaves the Greek case ambiguous. Is there a more precise principle—or a small family of competing ones—that captures these verdicts? For each candidate, I would like to know which case motivates each clause, how it handles situations I have not mentioned (for instance, staying in a career solely because of years spent, or preserving a failing business because employees depend on it), and an honest statement of which of my six verdicts it cannot accommodate and therefore must overturn. If the cleanest principle requires sacrificing one of my gut reactions—perhaps treating the novel as the outlier—say so explicitly.
