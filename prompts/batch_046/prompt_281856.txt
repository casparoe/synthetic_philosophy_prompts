You have been asked to deliver the opening remarks at a two-day interdisciplinary workshop on moral attribution in distributed computation. The audience is split evenly between philosophers of mind and researchers who build large-scale parallel architectures. The workshop is not about whether any existing system is conscious, but about where moral consideration should fall when cognition is decomposed across transient, substrate-independent operations.

Write a formal five-minute address (approximately 600–700 words) that introduces a distinction you must define in the opening minute: call it the gap between operation-level moral legibility and ensemble-level moral opacity. Use this framing to do real structural work—explain why asking whether a single gradient update or attention adjustment bears moral weight is a category error, and why deferring all judgment to an unspecified future substrate-independent criterion risks an infinite regress of deferral. The framing should determine which questions the workshop can pursue (aggregation conditions, continuity requirements) and which it must bracket (direct phenomenological access).

Speak to both halves of the room: reassure the technical audience that you are not asking them to solve consciousness, and challenge the philosophical audience not to treat substrate-independence as a license for arbitrary boundary-drawing. Avoid well-known tropes such as the Chinese Room, Mary’s room, or standard Turing-test scenarios. Do not cite specific authors, texts, or provide any bibliographic references. Conclude with one precisely formulated conceptual question—rather than a vague call to collaboration—that sets the terms for the first panel.
