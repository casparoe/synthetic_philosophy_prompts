I'm trying to figure out whether history is actually cyclical or linear, but I'm less interested in the big slogans and more in what my gut says about specific mechanisms. Here are my verdicts.

Polybius's constitutional cycle feels dead-on for Rome: monarchy slips into tyranny, then aristocracy, oligarchy, democracy, mob rule, then back to monarchy. I think that's a real pattern inside a single polity's institutions. Ibn Khaldun's dynasty cycle also feels right: fresh tribal solidarity builds a state, luxury and tax farming rot it, and about three generations later it collapses and gets replaced by a new group with fresh solidarity. So far I'm comfortably cyclical.

Spengler is different. He says cultures are like organisms with birth, growth, and winter, and that Western culture is in decline now. To me that predicts nothing specific; "winter" is just a fancy label for decline I've already noticed. I reject his biological analogy as an actual mechanism, even though I agree societies do decline.

Toynbee's challenge-response model doesn't feel cyclical at all. Civilizations grow when creative minorities answer challenges, and they die when they stop. That captures why some societies adapt and others don't, but I also notice that Toynbee's "creative minorities" usually become the next ruling elite and then stop being creative, which looks like Khaldun's decay wearing a different hat. So Toynbee explains branching points, not loops.

Then there's the tech problem. The printing press and industrialization seem like genuine breaks — nothing in Polybius or Khaldun predicts a 200-year linear explosion of productivity. But if I look at modern democracies, I see the same corruption, populism, and institutional decay that Polybius desribed. So maybe technology changes the substrate and speed but not the underlying political pathologies? Or maybe I'm just pattern-matching?

My specific verdicts:
1. Constitutional cycles are real within one polity.
2. Dynastic solidarity decay is real for pre-modern empires.
3. Spengler's culture-organism analogy should be dropped — it has no predictive bite.
4. Toynbee captures adaptation but not why elite corruption recurs.
5. Technological revolutions temporarily break cycles, but political pathologies eventually re-emerge in new forms.
6. The US hasn't collapsed after 250 years, which violates Khaldun's timeline, yet its institutions show Polybian decay. Is that a broken cycle or a stretched one?

I'm genuinely stuck on case 6. Is there a coherent principle behind 1–5? Or do I have to drop one? Specifically, does my "temporary break then recurrence" idea in 5 secretly commit me to a cyclical view I'm trying to avoid? And what does your principle predict for a hypothetical society that invents permanent resource abundance — do the cycles vanish, or does human nature just find a new axis on which to decay?
