I'm preparing a discussion for a philosophy and literature seminar that pairs Plato's Republic—specifically the passage in which Glaucon issues his challenge to justice and introduces the myth of Gyges' ring—with J.R.R. Tolkien's The Lord of the Rings. The standard classroom move is to treat the One Ring as a literal ring of invisibility and therefore a direct dramatization of Glaucon's thought experiment: if you could act without being seen, would you still be just? I want to push past that cliché. Instead, read Tolkien's narrative through a more precise distinction: between invisibility-to-others (escaping social accountability and punishment) and invisibility-to-oneself (the erosion or distortion of moral self-recognition). In Glaucon's framing, invisibility is purely epistemic for the observer and liberating for the agent; but Tolkien's Ring-bearers often suffer an intensified, almost hyper-visible internal corruption—Gollum's body transforms into an external map of his degradation, Frodo feels Sauron's eye upon him constantly even when hidden, and Boromir's hidden intent at Amon Hen is witnessed and resisted by the Fellowship even as he conceals it from himself. Does Tolkien therefore reverse the logic of Gyges? Does the Ring grant invisibility to ordinary social eyes while making the bearer more, not less, visible to some form of moral scrutiny—whether internal, supernatural, or communal—and if so, does the novel suggest that the real danger is not doing wrong unseen, but being isolated from the kinds of recognition that stabilize moral identity?

Treat both texts seriously: do not reduce the Ring to an illustration of Plato, and do not distort Glaucon's challenge—which is about whether justice is rational absent external reward and punishment—to fit Tolkien's mythology. I want a reading that asks what the novel adds to the philosophical debate that pure argument misses, particularly about the communal or narrative conditions under which moral character survives unobserved action. Focus on specific dynamics: Frodo's burden during the Council of Elrond and his decision at Mount Doom; Gollum's relationship to the Ring as both possession and being possessed; and Boromir's failure and death. You do not need to quote verbatim; paraphrase scenes as needed. I'm teaching this next week, and I want to avoid the familiar claim that power simply corrupts. I'm after the more precise thesis that different forms of invisibility corrupt differently, and that Tolkien may have dramatized a problem with Glaucon's framing itself—namely, that morality might depend less on being watched by others and more on remaining within a structure of recognition that the Ring systematically destroys.
