You serve as the senior ethics advisor for an international advocacy organization that evaluates proposals to dismantle entrenched harmful norms—practices such as bride-price exchanges that function as elder retirement security, caste-based occupational exclusion, or industrial livestock confinement—roughly twice each month. The board has adopted a framework, drawn from the debate over moral revolutions, holding that practices like dueling and Atlantic slavery collapsed not chiefly through rational persuasion but through shifts in material interest (the Williams thesis) and in codes of honor and shame (Appiah’s thesis that honor drives moral change). Your role is not to defend this framework but to make it operational.

Write a reusable decision procedure that your review team—staff without formal philosophical training and without access to ethnographers—can apply in thirty minutes per proposal. The procedure must move through ordered stages:

First, a diagnostic stage that classifies the sustaining mechanism of the practice as primarily interest-based, honor-based, or argument-based, with explicit criteria for each classification and a mandatory “stop and flag ambiguity” condition when the evidence is equally compatible with two mechanisms.

Second, mechanism-specific tests. For interest: determine whether the benefits are individually compensable or structurally distributed, whether compensation would destroy the social fabric that makes the practice necessary, and whether there is an “interest lock-in” that makes individual defection impossible even if collective change is rational. For honor: distinguish horizontal honor (peer-to-peer status competition) from vertical honor (elite or institutional sanction); determine whether a proposed campaign can trigger an honor cascade or will be absorbed as virtue signaling; and identify whether the shame is direct (attached to the practice itself) or reflected (attached to association with lower-status groups). For argument: determine whether practitioners already experience an internal contradiction they suppress, or whether the argument is purely external to their conceptual framework.

Third, decision nodes. Specify the exact conditions yielding “recommend funding with this mechanism mix,” “recommend rejection due to mechanism mismatch,” and “refer to board for values judgment” (for example, when ending the practice requires recognizing perpetrators’ property rights, or when an honor-targeted campaign would manipulate gendered or caste-based status in ways the organization has prohibited). Include explicit “stop” conditions where missing ethnographic knowledge makes further analysis unreliable rather than merely uncertain.

Then apply the procedure step-by-step to three proposals:

A. A legal ban coupled with elder pension compensation to end regional bride-price exchanges that currently serve as retirement security for older family members.

B. A media campaign framing factory-farm management practices as shameful for executives who identify as progressive and socially conscious.

C. A campus campaign distributing philosophical texts on animal sentience to university students in a culture where meat consumption is tightly bound to masculine horizontal honor.

The third case must produce a recommendation the board will find unwelcome—specifically, that the proposed argument-based intervention is mismatched to the mechanism, and that an effective intervention would require manipulating honor dynamics the board has forbidden. Show exactly where the procedure forces this conclusion despite lacking ethnographic data, and where it demands a judgment call.

Conclude by stating precisely what the procedure cannot do: where it risks mistaking correlation for causal mechanism, where the horizontal-vertical honor distinction collapses in practice, where interest analysis depends on economic data the team does not have, and where the decision to compensate or not compensate is irreducibly a values question that no procedure can settle. Do not ask for bibliographies, citations, reading lists, or references to further literature.
