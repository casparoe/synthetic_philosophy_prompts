My study group was arguing about disjunctivism and the argument from illusion last night, and we got stuck. Here's how it went.

Alex went first. He said take the classic bent-stick-in-water case. Whether the stick is actually bent or just looks bent because of refraction, your experience is the same from the inside. So there has to be a common mental factor — a sense datum or representational content — shared between the veridical case and the illusion. That's the heart of the argument from illusion, and he thinks its pretty decisive against direct realism.

Sam said thats assuming the highest common factor principle, which is exactly what disjunctivists deny. She said in the water case you're still genuinely seeing the straight stick, but your experience misrepresents its shape. If there actually were a bent stick in front of you, you'd be in a completely different mental state — seeing a bent stick — even if you couldn't tell the difference introspectively. So there's no shared inner object.

Alex pushed back on supervenience grounds. He said imagine two people with identical brain states: one is in the water illusion, the other is genuinely seeing a bent stick. Disjunctivism says they're in different mental states purely because of whats outside the head. That violates internalism about mental content, and it makes mental states depend on the world in a spooky way.

Sam replied that mental states aren't just brain states on the direct realist view; they're constituted by relations to the environment. She also brought up what she called an illusion of absence — like change blindness where you miss an object that's actually there. She said disjunctivism can treat genuinely not seeing something and hallucinating an absence as disjoint states too, which actually strengthens the view rather than weakening it.

Alex said she's just multiplying distinctions without explaining why subjective indistinguishability doesn't matter. If I can't tell from the inside whether I'm seeing, misseeing, or failing to see, then for all practical purposes I'm in the same epistemic boat, and disjunctivism hasn't solved the argument — it's just relabeled the states.

We couldn't figure out who was right. Can you adjudicate this move by move — rate each step from clearly wrong to clearly right and briefly justify? And say who's more right overall, and what both are missing that would actually move the debate forward. Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then give your reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of the question.
