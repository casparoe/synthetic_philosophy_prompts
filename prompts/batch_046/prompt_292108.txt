In a graduate seminar on practical reason we have been circling the same question for months: what exactly makes a promise binding? By that I do not mean why it is prudent to keep one's word, but what normative mechanism transforms an utterance like “I promise” into an obligation that survives changes in desire and that wrongs the promisee specifically, rather than merely disappointing them. The participants divide roughly into two camps. One holds that promising is an exercise of normative power: the promiser voluntarily authorizes the promisee to hold them to a particular course of action, thereby creating a directed obligation analogous to consent. The other holds that promising is a socially constituted practice that generates legitimate expectations; the wrong of breaking a promise is not a breach of self-imposed authority but a betrayal of the reliance-structure the practice makes rational. Both sides accept the same core cases—promises made under partial deception about content, promises that become ruinous to fulfill, promises made to entities that lack full moral status—and both concede that breaking them is pro tanto wrong. Yet the arguments never seem to intersect: the authority camp treats expectation-based accounts as missing the internal normative shift, while the practice camp treats authority-based accounts as mystifying social coordination with metaphysics of the will.

My working hypothesis is that the stall is not about the phenomenology of promising but about an unacknowledged disagreement over temporal ontology—whether the binding force is diachronic, tying together stages of a single agent across time, or synchronic, functioning as a present-tense coordination mechanism. But I also wonder whether the two camps are simply describing different phenomena—what we might call commitment versus accountability—and forcing them under one term, “promise,” creates a pseudo-debate. Before I commit to either diagnosis, I want your view: is this disagreement verbal, substantive, or a case of bedrock intuitions about personal identity and social cooperation that neither side can argue for? Is there a hidden formal or conceptual question—perhaps about whether obligations can be self-created without circularity, or about whether expectation-based wrongs can be genuinely directed—doing the silent work? And given your diagnosis, what would actually move things: a distinction between two kinds of promissory force, an empirical result about how people actually reason about broken promises, a clarification of what counts as voluntary authorization, or should we conclude that the question is ill-posed and split it into separate inquiries?
