Two colleagues of mine debated, over coffee, whether the labels "Middle Ages," "Renaissance," and "Scientific Revolution" track real historical structures or are purely retrospective constructions.

(1) The first argued that all three are heuristic fictions with no objective referent. "Middle Ages" was coined by 15th-century humanists to disparage the preceding centuries; "Renaissance" was popularized by 19th-century cultural historians to celebrate secular individualism and artistic revival; and "Scientific Revolution" was assembled by 20th-century scholars to impose false coherence on disparate 16th- and 17th-century practices. She said none correspond to genuine breakpoints in material or social history, and that using them systematically imports later values into earlier contexts.

(2) The second agreed that the labels carry ideological baggage, but insisted that explanatory utility, not ideological purity, should govern their retention. He said "Middle Ages" can be defended as a long-duration institutional framework—feudal law, manorial economy, Latin Christendom—that genuinely structured European life across centuries, regardless of humanist rhetoric. By contrast, he claimed "Scientific Revolution" is uniquely suspect because it bundles figures with incompatible metaphysical commitments—Aristotelian mechanists, Neoplatonic mathematicians, Baconian experimentalists—under a single methodological essence that none of them actually shared. He placed "Renaissance" in between: it captures a real shift in artistic representation and classical textual recovery, but not a unified causal process.

(3) The first replied that this concedes far too little. If "Middle Ages" survives only as an institutional container, then it too is a fiction, since those very institutions changed radically across the period and were never uniform across regions. She added that selecting which 16th- and 17th-century practices count as "scientific" requires precisely the 19th-century professionalized, secular, methodologically unified definition of science that the second speaker was tacitly using—thereby rendering his defense of "Scientific Revolution" circular.

(4) The second responded that circularity is avoidable if we substitute family resemblance for essentialism. There is sufficient overlap—rejection of scholastic textual authority, mathematization of natural phenomena, and new institutional spaces such as courts and academies—to justify grouping these figures together, even if no single feature is universal. He said the first speaker's demand for perfect institutional or methodological uniformity is a strawman that no serious periodization could satisfy, and that partial, domain-specific validity is enough to keep some categories while discarding others.

Please adjudicate this exchange move by move: rate each of the four turns from clearly mistaken to clearly right, with brief justification for each rating. Then indicate whether either party misses a deeper issue—particularly whether the reality of a historical period must be all-or-nothing, or whether we might consistently retain "Middle Ages" as an institutional container, reject "Scientific Revolution" as an essentialist projection, and treat "Renaissance" as a domain-specific artistic shift without contradiction.
