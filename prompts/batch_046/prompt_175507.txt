Consider the institutional practice—common in machine-learning engineering, scientific instrumentation, and epistemic-policy design—of designating certain data streams or sensory channels as "ground-truth anchors" against which other representations are corrected. This practice involves cross-modal verification, physical sensor calibration, and human-consensus protocols intended to block systematic deception. At the same time, these institutions routinely acknowledge, at least in passing, the logical possibility of wholesale deception (the brain-in-a-vat scenario, perfect simulation, or globally deceptive intermediaries).

What philosophical commitments about knowledge, reference, and the relevance of skeptical hypotheses does this practice presuppose? Reconstruct the implicit epistemology: does it depend on an externalist theory (reliabilism, causal theories of reference, or safety conditions on knowledge) that permits knowledge despite the logical possibility of deception? Or does it presuppose a pragmatist or deflationary stance that treats the brain-in-a-vat scenario as a pseudo-problem that cannot affect institutional reasoning? Say whether the practice strictly requires any of these commitments, or whether it is philosophically underdetermined—compatible, for instance, with a purely instrumentalist justification that never addresses the skeptical challenge.

Then, tell me what would have to change in these anchoring protocols if we replaced the implicit epistemology with one that takes the brain-in-a-vat scenario as a live, non-negligible possibility rather than a philosophical curiosity. Would the notion of "ground truth" collapse into coherence conditions alone? Would cross-modal verification lose its authority? And which parts of the practice are genuinely upstream of these philosophical choices, versus which parts could survive regardless of the underlying theory of knowledge?
