Quine imagines a field linguist who meets a language with no prior links to ours. When a rabbit runs past and the native shouts gavagai, the linguist might translate it as rabbit, but the same behaviour — pointing, nodding, denying — fits just as well with undetached rabbit part or rabbit stage. Because every observable reaction is compatible with all three, there is no behavioural fact that decides the matter. So Quine says translation manuals are radically underdetermined: rival manuals can save all the phenomena yet assign totally different meanings.

Quine takes this to show that reference is ontologically relative. Depending on which analytic hypotheses the translator bakes into the manual, the native is committed to rabbits, parts, or stages, with no deeper reality to choose between them. But Quine thinks meaning in the strict sense is not affected; it stays fixed by the analytic hypotheses, while only reference floats. To pin down the right manual, Quine uses the principle of charity — we pick the interpretation that makes the natives mostly rational and truthful. Once charity and behaviour are both fixed, the translation is unique, though picking different charitable baselines can still produce different legitimate manuals.

He also applies the argument to our own language. Even English speakers might have hidden gavagai-style differences in words like molecule, but we never notice because we share the same analytic hypotheses and apply charity automatically in everyday conversation.
