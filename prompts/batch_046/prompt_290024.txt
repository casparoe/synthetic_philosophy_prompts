I've been trying to figure out why different countries handle sex work so differently and I keep going in circles. Sweden and Norway use the nordic model - you can sell but you can't buy, so the buyer gets in trouble but the seller dont. Germany and the Netherlands went the other way and legalized it with rules like health checks, taxes, and registered brothels, treating it basically like any other job. Then New Zealand just decriminalized it completly, no special licensing or police oversight at all.

What I'm really stuck on is what each of these setups has to believe about people and choice for it to even make sense. The nordic model treats the buyer like hes doing something harmful but the seller like she cant be blamed, which makes me think it assumes she doesnt really have full agency in that moment - like shes a victim of the situation. But then why dont they apply that same logic to other risky work? In Sweden people still take dangerous construction jobs for pay and we dont arrest the contractor. So is the model secretly saying sex is different because it involves your body in a deeper way than your muscles? Or is it just about gender and power?

Then with Germany and Netherlands, they seem to think consent works fine here - that a worker can negotiate terms, report abuse, and pay taxes like anyone else. But they still have all these special rules just for sex work, not for other personal service jobs, so maybe they still think theres something unique about it that needs extra guarding?

And New Zealand removing everything makes me wonder if they think the state should have zero opinion about whether sex for money is different from any other labor, which sounds like they see bodies as tools you can rent out with full autonomy.

Can you break down what philosophical idea about consent, harm, and whether our bodies are like selves or like tools each policy actually needs? And is the nordic model consistent with itself, or is it kind of picking and choosing when to say someone lacks agency?
