I'm a phil prof who chairs the cross-disciplinary ethics review panel at my university — we evaluate research proposals involving vulnerable populations, and we're constantly hitting the discursive dilemma. You know: panelists vote seperately on premises like 'this study poses more-than-minimal risk' and 'participants can't fully recieve informed consent,' but then the majority vote on the final approve/reject doesn't line up with what you'd derive from the premises. I've been trying to formalize a hybrid rule for us: premise-based aggregation for empirical premises, but if 40% or more of the panel rejects a normative premise, we don't treat it as settled — instead we flag it for explicit values deliberation rather than just counting heads. That's the principle I need turned into an actual procedure.

We have 7 panelists, I'm the only philosopher, the dean has to sign whatever we recommend, and we have 90 minutes max per proposal. I need a step-by-step checklist: the questions in order, what counts as a good enough answer at each step, where we stop with a clear reject or approve, where we have to hand the decision off because it's values not facts, and where the whole thing breaks down and I just have to make a call. Then run it on three proposals:

1. A psych study recruiting recently-arrived refugees for trauma interviews, paying $30 each, with no mental-health follow-up.
2. A clinical trial testing a cheap generic drug on undocumented migrants who lack insurance, with a placebo arm.
3. The hard one: 5 of us agree the risk and autonomy premises both point to rejection, but 4 members still vote to approve because they think the scientific payoff is enormous. I actually agree with the 4 — I want this approved — but I need to see what your procedure says, even if it's not what I want.

Also be honest about what this can't handle — like strategic voting, or members changing their premise votes after seeing the conclusion. And flag the misfire points.
