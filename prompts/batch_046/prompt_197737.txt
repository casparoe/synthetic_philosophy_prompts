The canonical illustrations, however—autism spectrum revisions, the history of multiple personality disorder—feel to me like territory that has been mapped too often, and I am trying to understand whether the mechanism remains coherent when the loop is mediated not by human clinicians but by an automated, continuously updating diagnostic infrastructure. To explore this, I would like you to write a short story that treats the loop not as a background concept but as the central narrative engine.

Set the story in a near-future municipal health district where a new psychiatric category—call it “prospective grief disorder,” initially defined as a sustained, maladaptive emotional orientation toward future losses that have not yet occurred and may never occur—has been integrated into a real-time taxonomic system maintained by ambient biometric and linguistic monitors. The protagonist should be a retired epidemiologist, now working part-time as a diagnostic validator, who gradually realizes that her own behavioral adaptations to the category are altering its statistical boundaries: as subjects learn to avoid being flagged, the algorithm’s feature weights shift, splitting the disorder into contradictory subtypes that describe opposing behaviors. Please embed the philosophical tension directly into the plot—particularly the question of whether the disorder is being discovered or manufactured—through concrete details: clinical vocabulary that changes between report drafts, sleep and linguistic patterns that feed back into the biometric criteria, and a moment when the protagonist recognizes that the classification has become ontologically prior to the suffering it was meant to capture. The narrative should be disciplined about the loop’s mechanics, showing exactly how a change in human self-understanding becomes a change in the measurement apparatus, and then a further change in human self-understanding.
