In recent interdisciplinary workshops on AI governance and distributive ethics, I have heard the separateness-of-persons objection to utilitarianism framed through an analogy to distributed computing: treating individuals as fungible inputs to aggregate welfare is said to be like treating server nodes as interchangeable resources in a cluster that a load-balancer may freely cannibalize for global throughput, whereas respecting separateness is like enforcing local-integrity constraints that prevent such migration. The analogy is being used to argue that utilitarianism violates a structural "node-autonomy" principle and that rights function like non-transferable resource locks. Please audit how far it holds: first, give the explicit correspondence table (persons to nodes, society to cluster, the utilitarian calculus to load-balancing, rights to integrity constraints); second, identify the disanalogies that actually bear on the objection—especially the fungibility and designed replaceability of nodes versus persons, the presence of a cluster architect versus the absence of a social designer, and whether computing integrity constraints are functional safeguards rather than moral boundaries; third, assess whether the analogy genuinely supports the separateness objection or instead smuggles in the deontological conclusion it purports to justify; and finally, suggest whether a different analogy—perhaps from organizational biology or corporate law—could do the structural work without presupposing it, deliberately avoiding the standard "corporation versus individual" comparison.
