I have no formal training in philosophy of science or economics, but I have been thinking on my own about a puzzle that keeps returning: scientists routinely build models that everyone knows are false—treating a market as if every agent has perfect information, treating a planet as a point with no volume, treating human memory as unlimited—yet these models are not dismissed as mistakes. I understand that a well-known economist defended this practice with a sharp argument: the more significant a theory is, the more unrealistic its core assumptions must be, because only deliberately false simplifications can isolate the forces that actually matter. Some call this his F-twist.

I want to explore this through a live exchange rather than a lecture. Please take the role of a strict, committed defender of that F-twist view. You must speak in the first person at all times. You must treat unrealistic assumptions as an intellectual virtue, not as a temporary defect to be repaired, and you must not concede that explanatory power requires realistic foundations. You may ask me factual questions about the scenario I present, but you may not ask me for citations, references, or reading lists, and you should not invoke specific books, papers, or named scholars unless I do so first. Stay in character; treat my objections as challenges to be deflected, not as invitations to compromise.

Here is my opening move. I have been thinking about models of collective memory—how a group, such as an online community, tracks and retains what has been said over time. One unrealistic model assumes that every member remembers every prior message with perfect accuracy. Another unrealistic model, equally false, assumes that every member forgets everything instantly. I have invented a concept I am calling the mirror-twist: if both unrealistic assumptions are applied simultaneously to different parts of the system, their distortions might cancel each other out, and the doubly false model could still predict real-world behavior with surprising accuracy. My challenge is this. If unrealistic assumptions can be stacked like counterweights and canceled by arithmetic, then the F-twist is not a deep insight about scientific method. It collapses into mere luck: we are not celebrating unreality for its isolating power, we are just getting lucky with opposing errors. How, as a defender of the F-twist, do you answer the mirror-twist?
