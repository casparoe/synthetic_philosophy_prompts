I'm drafting a novel whose central figure, a reproductive ethicist, holds these five judgments: that bringing a child into being knowing it will suffer severely is wrong; that declining to conceive a child who would have lived gloriously is not wrong; that generating a temporary, discontinuous consciousness experiencing only isolated pleasure is permissible but not obligatory; that generating a continuous being whose life is mostly joyful but contains one unavoidable, unforgettable trauma is wrong; and that leaving an already-constructed virtual being—capable only of serene experience—in dormancy is not wrong. The next chapter introduces a being designed to live continuously, experiencing intense bliss for ninety-five percent of its existence and brief agony for five percent, but with the agony immediately and permanently erased from memory so that the subject never integrates it or anticipates its return. Would she permit its creation? I ask for the two or three implicit principles that could jointly explain her track record, what each predicts here, your best guess at which she actually holds and why, and the further case that would best distinguish them. I am working away from my library, so please offer no bibliographies or citations and let the reasoning stand alone; and if my framing rests on a confused premise—say, that the narrative integration of suffering carries ethical weight it cannot—say so plainly rather than accommodating it.
