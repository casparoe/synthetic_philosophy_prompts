I've been a biologist for thirty years and only started reading philosophy after I retired, so I might be mixing things up, but I've been fascinated by tranquilism — the view that only the absence of craving and suffering has value for well-being, and pleasure or acheivement are basically neutral. In my old field we think of craving as interoceptive prediction error, so the idea that only negative error matters for welfare felt almost natural at first. But I built this argument and it feels like it proves saving lives is harmful, which can't be right. Here's the chain:

(1) Tranquilism: well-being is constituted solely by the absence of suffering and the absence of craving; positive states carry no weight.
(2) If absence is what's good, then the value of a life is the intergral of suffering-plus-craving avoided over the span of that life — lower summed negative load is always better.
(3) Death permanently eliminates both suffering and craving, and it does so across an indefinite future duration, yielding an unbounded accumulation of absence.
(4) So for any living person with a nonzero rate of craving, death maximizes the integral in (2) relative to continued existence.
(5) By parity, preventing death — pulling someone from a river, treating an illness — forces them back into a condition where future craving and suffering can accumulate, reducing their well-being integral.
(6) But if I save a drowning stranger and they recover with no trauma, I haven't harmed them; I certainly haven't made their life worse.
(7) So either tranquilism is false, or life-saving is generally harmful, which is absurd.
(8) Therefore reject tranquilism.

Where does this crack? I'd rather keep (1) than (6), but I want to know what I'm paying. Is the mistake treating absence across non-existence as summable? Or is there a hidden slide in (2) between absence-in-a-subject and absence-of-a-subject?

Please lead with your bottom line in the first sentence and then give your reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of the question.
