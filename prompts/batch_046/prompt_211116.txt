Since my therapist noted last month that I have been framing a long-abandoned career path as an "intentional sabbatical," I have been unable to stop thinking about whether self-deception is even conceptually possible, or whether I am merely misdescribing ordinary mental compartmentalization.

Here is an argument that seems to prove self-deception cannot exist, yet each step feels unavoidable.

(1) If self-deception occurs, then a single subject S simultaneously holds belief B and belief not-B about the same proposition.
(2) If S holds B, then S possesses first-person epistemic authority that B is true; if S holds not-B, then S possesses first-person authority that B is false.
(3) No single subject can possess first-person authority for both B and not-B without undermining the very conditions required to be an authoritative self-ascriber.
(4) Therefore, if S holds both beliefs, S is not a unified epistemic agent but a collection of sub-agential compartments lacking a single point of authority.
(5) But if S is not a unified agent, then there is no single "self" being deceived; there is only one compartment deceiving another, which is intra-mental interpersonal deception, not self-deception.
(6) So either self-deception involves a direct contradiction that destroys the unified self (making the phenomenon impossible), or it dissolves into compartment-to-compartment deception (making it not genuinely self-deception).
(7) Therefore the very idea of self-deception is conceptually incoherent.

The conclusion feels wrong—people clearly deceive themselves about health, finances, and relationships—yet I cannot locate the weak link. Which premise should I reject, and what does rejecting it cost? I would most like to keep (1) and (3), but I will pay whatever price is necessary.

Please lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then give your reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of the question.
