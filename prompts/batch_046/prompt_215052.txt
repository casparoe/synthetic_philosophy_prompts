I'm drafting a scene for my novel where two old friends, Mira and Jon, get into it over dinner about whether going vegan actually saves wild animals or just makes things worse. I want the argument to be genuinely smart on both sides, not strawmen, but I keep losing track of who's right about what. Can you referee?

Mira says she mostly stop eating chicken and switched to beef because she read that crop farming kills enormous numbers of small wild mammals—mice, voles, rabbits—with harvesters and pesticides. She thinks grass-fed beef uses land that can't grow crops, so per calorie it might cause less wild suffering than plants. Plus chicken is worst of all because you kill dozens of birds for one meal, and the broiler conditions are brutal even before slaughter.

Jon pushes back that she's conflating accidental harm with intentional harm. The moral point of veganism, he says, isn't just a body count; it's that we breed chickens specifically to kill them, which is different from wild animals dying as collateral in fields. He also says pasture still displaces wild habitats, and that counting every field mouse as "caused by crops" is questionable—those animals live and die in the wild anyway, harvesters just change the mechanism. Then he adds that if Mira really cares about wild suffering she should be advocating for predator control or wild contraception, not just changing her plate, since reducing wild animal populations globally might do more good.

Mira replies that intentionality is mostly a feel-good distinction. A mouse crushed in a combine suffers regardless of our intent, and if suffering is the metric then the numbers matter more than the mental state of the farmer. She also notes that beef still usually involves feed crops, so she's not fully escaping crop deaths, and that Jon's habitat point cuts both ways—cattle grazing can destroy ecosystems too. She asks why he's so quick to defend eating animals if he's not also willing to defend factory farming conditions.

Jon says he's not defending factory farming—he agrees chicken is horrific—but that doesn't make crop agriculture innocent. He thinks both of them are missing whether "wild animal suffering" is even the right frame, since wild lives involve constant predation, starvation, and disease, so maybe the problem isn't diet but whether humans should intervene in nature at all.
