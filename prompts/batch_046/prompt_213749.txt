I'm currently navigating a choice between accepting a position abroad and remaining to coordinate my father's long-term care, and the experience has made Bernard Williams's integrity objection feel less like a classroom example and more like a precise diagnosis of my own paralysis. Rather than a summary, I would like a dialogue between two interlocutors—one who holds that remaining expresses a ground project that is constitutive of the agent rather than merely one preference among others, and one who holds that framing the choice in terms of integrity is an elaborate rationalization for partiality that a properly impartial perspective should reject. The dialogue should be anchored in the specific case of care obligations that are inherited and culturally embedded rather than actively chosen, because that distinction is exactly where my own reasoning stalls. Each side should respond directly to the other's arguments: the defender of integrity should address the claim that ground projects are just strongly weighted preferences with no special authority over aggregation, and the critic should address the reply that some commitments define the self that is doing the weighing. They should also engage, at least once, with whether the charge of having one thought too many applies differently to commitments we inherit than to those we construct for ourselves. Both voices should be sharp and equally competent, with no straw positions.
