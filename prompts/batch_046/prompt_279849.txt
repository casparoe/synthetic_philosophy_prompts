I recently observed a clinical-ethics case conference in which a senior resident maintained a firm diagnostic judgment even after acknowledging that her sleep deprivation and her documented miss-rate on similar presentations should have undermined her confidence; she could not honestly claim her belief was rational, yet she could not bring herself to suspend it either. That tension has stayed with me, and I now need to design a ninety-minute workshop for third-year medical residents that uses this kind of failure as an entry point into higher-order evidence and epistemic akrasia—not to explain the concepts to me, but to plan how I should explain them. I want the sequence of ideas laid out with the dependency structure made explicit: what must come first (perhaps the distinction between first-order clinical reasoning and second-order evaluation of one's own reliability), what can only be introduced once that is secured, and in what order the pieces should build so that nothing is assumed prematurely. For the carrying case, I need one concrete scenario—likely drawn from emergency-medicine decision-making—that includes both a first-order judgment call and a genuinely available higher-order indicator of unreliability, but that lacks confounding variables like moral stakes or team dynamics, so the philosophical structure stays visible. I also need a map of the misconceptions these residents will predictably bring from clinical training: for instance, that awareness of bias automatically corrects for it, or that epistemic akrasia is merely ordinary self-doubt rather than a specific structural conflict between levels of belief. Please specify exactly where in the sequence each misconception should be headed off, and include one closing question I can pose that would show whether they have genuinely grasped the idea rather than just memorized the vocabulary. Finally, tell me what should be deliberately left out at this level—perhaps the full taxonomy of defeat relations or debates between evidentialist and pragmatist accounts of rational belief—and say so explicitly. I also want to inject one niche framing: we should treat the resident's prior error-rate not merely as statistical background but as a form of testimonial evidence about her present cognitive state, which alters how the higher-order belief is formed and whether it can rationally defeat the first-order judgment.
