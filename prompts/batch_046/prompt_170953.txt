Having worked through the heterogeneity problem in our philosophy of value seminars, I have come to defend a refined hedonist view that distinguishes a thin phenomenal 'core'—a determinable affective resonance said to be invariant across all pleasures—from a variable 'modal envelope' comprising the sensory and intentional vehicles that carry it. On this account, bodily, intellectual, and aesthetic pleasures are genuinely uniform in the value-relevant respect, despite their manifest phenomenological diversity. I want to stress-test this position. What would actually count against it—not merely the familiar counter-cases involving sadistic or masochistic pleasures, but the deeper vulnerabilities? Which phenomenological intuitions or conceptual distinctions (for instance, between liking and wanting, or between sensory and propositional enjoyment) could undermine the core/envelope split? What does the view predict about the cross-cultural or intersubjective comparability of well-being that rival theories do not, and might those predictions be disconfirmed? If the distinction collapses, does the position risk reducing to a desire-satisfaction account or an objective-list theory? Please rank the potential defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, indicate which ones a committed defender would concede as genuinely damaging and which could be absorbed by further qualification, and say whether the view is genuinely exposed or has been insulated to the point of unfalsifiability.
