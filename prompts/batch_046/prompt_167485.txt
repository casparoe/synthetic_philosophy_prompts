In preparing my graduate seminar on moral contractarianism, I have been reviewing a summary circulated in a recent working paper that attempts to capture T.M. Scanlon's contractualism, particularly its response to the aggregation problem. The author presents the view as holding that wrongness is determined by principles that no affected party could reasonably reject as a basis for informed, unforced general agreement. When applied to familiar rescue dilemmas—saving five people at the cost of one innocent life—the summary claims that Scanlon's framework grants each individual a kind of lexical veto: the single victim's reasonable rejection of a principle authorizing her death is always decisive, irrespective of the number of beneficiaries. From this, the author derives a stronger thesis: that contractualism treats all cross-personal numerical comparison as a category error in moral reasoning, that it dissolves rather than solves the numbers problem by denying that aggregate welfare has any independent moral standing, and that distributive principles apply only to the allocation of goods within a single life, never between lives. The summary concludes that Scanlon's position is best understood as an extreme form of moral individualism in which collective considerations are entirely excluded from the justification of harm.

This characterization strikes me as capturing something of Scanlon's resistance to straightforward utilitarian aggregation, yet I worry that it overstates his commitments in ways that might mislead advanced students. I would like you to audit the summary sentence by sentence: indicate which elements a defender of Scanlon's view would accept without qualification, which are standard simplifications that obscure distinctions he actually draws—particularly between imposing severe harm for trivial gains versus distributing risks equitably across a group—and which are straightforward misrepresentations, such as the claim that he assigns lexical veto power regardless of numbers or that he denies all cross-personal distributive reasoning. Please also assess whether the conclusion that contractualism amounts to extreme moral individualism follows from the corrected premises, or whether it depends on conflating Scanlon's specific objections to aggregation with a general rejection of collective moral consideration. If there are subtle structural features of contractualist justification—say, the role of pairwise comparisons or the distinction between what is owed to each versus what is best overall—that the summary collapses, I want those reconstructed as well.
