Hey, I was arguing with my friend about whether Nāgārjuna's tetralemma is just a rhetorical sledgehammer or actually a fine-grained logical map, and I ended up writing this little essay for my philosophy of religion course. The usual reading is that the catuskoti gives four answers to whether something exists with svabhāva—yes, no, both, neither—and Nāgārjuna knocks them all down to prove emptiness. But I think that misses the architecture. In the MMK chapters on conditions, motion, and self, each corner maps onto a different metaphysics of parts-to-wholes: linear transmission, total absence of connection, identity-in-difference, and radical discontinuity. The rejection isn't blanket; it's tailored to each failure mode. So the tetralemma isn't just denying intrinsic existance; it's surveying the conceptual space of svabhāva-commitments and showing none of them capture actual dependance relations.

I'm especially unsure about the fourth corner. Usually people treat 'neither exists nor not exists' as just the denial of the first three, but in my reading it's the attempt to escape all conceptual determination—which is performatively impossible for any discursive practice—so Nāgārjuna rejects it on pragmatic, not metaphysical, grounds. Is that defensible, or am I importing analytic philosophy of language into Madhyamaka?

Can you comment on whether this framing holds water, where it's likely wrong, and if there's a simpler way to say it? Don't worry about citations, I'll fill those in myself if I use any of this. (Also, is my spelling of 'catuskoti' right? I never remember.)
