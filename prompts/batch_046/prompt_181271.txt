I serve on my school's Student Honor and Appeals Council, and we must issue a formal recommendation by Friday regarding a disciplinary case that has become unexpectedly philosophical. A senior submitted an essay for our philosophy elective that contained substantial unattributed material drawn from an obscure early-twentieth-century French phenomenological work on self-deception. During the hearing, the student did not contest the finding of plagiarism. Instead, they offered a defense framed in terms of Jean-Paul Sartre's analysis of bad faith and radical freedom: they argued that they had treated the institutional pressure to maintain a perfect transcript as an absolute determinism that negated their own freedom, thereby adopting a false self-image as someone who was merely a high-achiever rather than an authentic consciousness capable of choice. They contend that recognizing this self-deception marks an authentic turn toward freedom and should therefore reduce the severity of the sanction. Our faculty advisor, who teaches the course, responds that Sartre's doctrine of radical freedom actually increases culpability rather than diminishing it: if consciousness is always radically free, then invoking external pressure is precisely the defining maneuver of bad faith—the refusal to own one's choice—which makes the plagiarism less excusable, not more. The advisor also invokes Sartre's notion of the spirit of seriousness, in which people treat contingent values like grades as if they possessed inherent objective weight, to explain how the student collapsed their own freedom into given circumstances; but the advisor insists that explaining the psychological mechanism does not excuse the breach of trust or the harm to other students. Council members are divided. Some propose reducing the penalty to a structured program of philosophical reflection and restitution, on the grounds that authentic self-awareness should be encouraged; others propose upholding suspension, warning that treating philosophical insight as a mitigating factor risks institutionalizing a perverse incentive and confuses an explanation of motive with a moral excuse. Please draft the short written opinion I will circulate before Thursday's meeting. It should recommend a concrete course of action with a specific time frame, explain the relevant Sartrean concepts—such as facticity, being-for-itself, and the distinction between explanatory context and moral excuse—without assuming specialist knowledge, engage the student's defense seriously rather than dismissing it, address the advisor's counter-argument that radical freedom aggravates responsibility, and include an honest acknowledgment of the strongest consideration on the losing side. The memo should not request outside citations, bibliographies, or reading lists, as our deliberation must rely solely on the reasoning presented here.
