Write a short dialogue between two equally capable philosophers, Alex and Jordan, about whether video-game characters can be genuine objects of moral concern. Alex should defend a position built around what she calls "cross-instance procedural identity": in certain persistent-world games, non-player characters maintain state variables—learned behaviors, relationship metrics, environmental alterations—that survive not only within one playthrough but across save files, server migrations, and updates, so that maliciously resetting or deleting that data disrupts a diachronic continuity that the player community treats as the "same" character. Jordan should reject this, insisting that data persistence is not subject persistence and that any emotional weight is directed at representations, not at entities with interests. Have them respond directly to each other’s points rather than talk past them: Jordan should press whether cross-instance identity is anything more than a design feature and whether it commits us to protecting every persistent database entry; Alex should distinguish mere storage from the functional role these characters play in an ongoing collective narrative practice, and should ask whether Jordan’s representationalist account can explain why players sometimes feel genuine moral discomfort—not mere aesthetic loss—when a persistent character is corrupted. Each side should refine its view in reply, and neither should be presented as a strawman. Keep the exchange focused on the conceptual question of whether procedural continuity can ground moral standing, without appealing to bibliographic references or citing specific academic works.
