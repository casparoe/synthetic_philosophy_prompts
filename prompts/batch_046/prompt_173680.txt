As a software engineer without formal training in philosophy, I have been trying to understand the debate between self-sampling and self-indication assumptions—particularly how they interact with what I have read is called the "presumptuous philosopher" problem—because the reasoning looks structurally identical to a sampling-bias issue I encounter in distributed log analysis. I have constructed an argument that applies both assumptions to a reference class I can actually relate to, but it seems to generate predictions that depend on my personal career choices in a way that cannot be right. Before I conclude I have simply misunderstood the philosophy, I want to know exactly which step collapses.

Here is the argument. (1) Under the self-sampling assumption, I should treat myself as a randomly drawn member from my reference class—say, "engineers who have ever worked on AI alignment." (2) If that class is large, being near its beginning (I am among the first few thousand) is improbable, so SSA predicts the class will remain small and that the field will not expand much further—implying that AI safety work is near its end. (3) Under the self-indication assumption, my very existence is evidence for worlds that contain more members of that class, since more observers make my particular observation more likely; so SIA predicts a vast future population of alignment engineers. (4) Now apply the presumptuous philosopher move: my decision tomorrow to leave the field and become a gardener either removes me from the class or keeps me in it, and by adjusting my reference class accordingly I can shift the predicted size of the field. (5) Therefore, by choosing my career, I can determine—via SSA or SIA—whether AI alignment succeeds on a large scale. (6) But my personal employment decision should not causally or evidentially determine the global trajectory of a technical field. (7) So at least one of the premises (1)–(4) must fail.

The conclusion feels absurd in exactly the way the presumptuous philosopher is supposed to be absurd, yet I cannot tell whether I have misapplied the reference-class logic, whether the two assumptions are meant to be used together rather than separately, or whether the real error is treating career membership as the kind of observer-property that anthropic reasoning applies to. Which premise or inference should I reject? If I want to preserve the intuition that my career choice does not determine cosmic-scale outcomes, does that force me to abandon SSA, abandon SIA, restrict reference classes in a specific way, or deny that anthropic reasoning applies outside cosmological contexts? I would like to know not just the standard reply to the presumptuous philosopher, but precisely how it maps onto this constructed case and what it costs to block the argument at each point.
