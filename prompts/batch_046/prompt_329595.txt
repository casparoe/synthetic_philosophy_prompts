I am working through a problem set for my course on formal epistemology and have constructed an argument that seems to show geometric rationality collapses into an implausible demand for total decisiveness, but I suspect there is a hidden slide. Here is the chain: (1) Geometric rationality requires that an agent maximize the expected geometric growth rate of epistemic accuracy across a sequence of judgments. (2) In the domain of betting, the Kelly criterion demonstrates that the strategy maximizing expected log-wealth is to wager in proportion to one's edge, and that diversifying across mutually exclusive outcomes within a single trial dilutes that growth rate. (3) By direct analogy, epistemic credences should be assigned in proportion to each hypothesis's relative "edge," and holding intermediate credences across competing hypotheses should be penalized just as diversification is penalized under Kelly betting. (4) Consequently, geometric rationality demands near-maximal decisiveness—assigning credence near 1 or 0 to every proposition—because any split allocation reduces the expected log-score improvement. (5) Yet rational agents can remain genuinely uncertain when evidence is genuinely ambiguous, so (4) is unacceptable. (6) Therefore, geometric rationality fails as a general account of belief revision. Which step should I reject? I am inclined to preserve (1) and (2), but I am unsure whether the error lies in (3)'s analogy between portfolio allocation and belief calibration, or in (4)'s assumption that intermediate credences necessarily reduce geometric growth when judgments are evaluated by a proper scoring rule rather than by a single payoff. If I must choose, I would rather keep (1) than abandon the intuition behind (5), but I want to understand what preserving (1) costs me.
