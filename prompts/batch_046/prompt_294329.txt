Write a sestina—six stanzas of six lines followed by a three-line envoi—using the end-words time, mirror, self, death, book, now, rotated through the standard pattern (1-2-3-4-5-6, then 6-1-5-2-4-3, and so on, closing with all six compressed in the envoi). Take as your premise Borges’s metaphysical claim that linear time is a fiction sustained only by memory, and that if every moment is eternally coexistent then personal identity does not endure through change but dissolves into repetition. The speaker should be an immortal archivist who realizes that every sentence he writes has already been written and will be written again—someone for whom the Library’s circularity is not metaphor but lived structure. Let the formal repetition of the end-words enact the philosophical problem: have “time” and “now” gradually converge until they sit side by side in the envoi, suggesting the collapse of succession into simultaneity, while “death” is displaced or isolated in the rotation, structurally representing mortality not as an endpoint inside time but as the external condition that forces the illusion of sequence upon an otherwise static existence. Do not resolve the tension with consolation or sermon; let the mechanical return of the form carry the weight. Write it for a seminar audience that has just finished discussing whether personal identity can survive eternal recurrence, and make the poem accurate enough that someone familiar with Borges’s arguments about eternal recurrence, the illusory nature of succession, and the paradox of immortality erasing individuality would recognize the view rendered rather than named.
