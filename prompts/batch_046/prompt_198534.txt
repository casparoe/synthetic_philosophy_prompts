I am preparing a 90-minute postgraduate seminar in philosophy of mind and bioethics for around sixteen students, and I would like a classroom activity that makes them feel — rather than merely hear about — the conceptual tension in pain asymbolia: the possibility that the badness of pain might reside in its affective-motivational dimension rather than in its raw sensory character. I was discussing this with a colleague who teaches clinical neuroscience, and she mentioned that patients with certain insular lesions report intact nociceptive localisation and intensity ratings yet display no aversive behaviour; I want the students to encounter a version of that dissociation through action, not through lecture.

Please design a structured role-play with rules, roles, and a scoring mechanism that does not involve real money. Introduce a hypothetical biotechnology — call it a Selective Affective Dampener (SAD) — that can suppress the suffering component of nociception while leaving the discriminative sensory map fully intact, thereby producing an artificial asymbolia-like state. Divide the group into three rotating roles: resource-allocation clinicians, patient advocates presenting first-person reports, and ethics-board members who must rank claims under shifting criteria. Give them four concrete cases: a burn survivor who describes intense heat and precise location but reports no distress; a chronic-pain sufferer whose tissue damage is minimal yet whose aversive response is overwhelming; an athlete who requests full sensory preservation with zero affective reaction for a dangerous sport; and a control subject with normal integrated pain. The rules should require them first to allocate scarce SAD units according to sensory intensity, then — without warning — force them to re-rank according to reported suffering or functional impairment, so that they discover their priorities have inverted.

Include a debrief that maps each phase onto philosophical distinctions (for instance, sensory-discriminative versus affective-motivational accounts of pain, or representational versus imperativist theories), predicts what will go wrong on the first run (for example, students conflating the absence of suffering with the absence of harm, or treating the SAD state as ethically neutral without noticing the phenomenological gap), and is explicit about which feature of genuine clinical asymbolia — likely the first-person inability to grasp what is missing — the simulation cannot reproduce and the facilitator must supply verbally. Also include an honest note on what happens if the SAD framing is removed and the cases are presented in purely clinical terms. Write in polished British English; do not include bibliographies, citation lists, or requests for further reading, since I want the design to stand on its own.
