Consider the institutional practice—now widespread in cognitive science and philosophy of mind graduate programs—of requiring every dissertation proposal on consciousness or cognition to include a staged mechanistic reduction roadmap. Under this protocol, applicants must show how their target phenomenon will eventually be explained solely through lower-level neural or computational dynamics, and they face explicit procedural penalties if they invoke organizational properties, emergent dynamics, or irreducible high-level patterns that are not themselves decomposed into sub-mechanisms. What theory of scientific explanation does this practice presuppose? Does it commit the institution to Daniel Dennett's notion of greedy reductionism—the error of explaining too much too fast by stripping away the very cranes that build explanations—rather than to legitimate crane-building reduction? Or does it merely operationalize a healthy methodological preference? If a researcher proposes what Dennett would call a skyhook—say, that conscious integration depends on a global organizational property that is lost whenever one analyzes only the constituent parts—does the roadmap rule out that proposal on conceptual grounds, or does it treat it only as empirically premature? Reconstruct the implicit philosophy of science embedded in this administrative rule, distinguish what the practice strictly requires from what it merely incentivizes, and assess whether the same procedural framework could be adopted consistently by someone who rejects greedy reductionism but still believes all valid explanations are ultimately crane-built.
