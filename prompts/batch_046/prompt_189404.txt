I work as a software engineer in a firm that maintains Architecture Decision Records documenting why particular technologies, patterns, or structures were adopted at specific points in our product's history. Every quarter, we conduct a retrospective architecture review in which senior engineers assess these past decisions against our current design principles and classify each one as sound, dated, or mistaken. Separately, when system failures occur, we hold blameless post-mortems that explicitly avoid attributing fault to individuals who followed the standard engineering practices of the period, even when those practices now appear reckless.

These two practices seem to pull in opposite directions. The architecture reviews treat our present norms as the correct benchmark for judging institutional choices made years ago, so that a decision taken in 2019 can be labelled erroneous because it conflicts with 2025 guidelines. The post-mortems, by contrast, insist that people should not be blamed for adhering to the norms that prevailed at the time they acted. What philosophical view of practical or historical judgement does this pairing presuppose? Must we hold that present engineering standards are objectively superior, or can we consistently say that past institutional choices were wrong by today's lights without implying that the individuals who made them were at fault? Does grading past organisational decisions against current criteria commit us to evaluating history solely through present eyes, and if so, is that commitment undermined by our refusal to assign personal blame? Or might these practices rest on separate foundations—one concerned with institutional knowledge and correction, the other with moral accountability—such that they do not actually require a single, unified theory?
