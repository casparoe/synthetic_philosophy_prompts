I am a doctoral student in philosophy of labor and comparative social ethics, and I am developing an argument about what I call vocational colonization: the idea that the ascetic logic of the Protestant calling (Beruf) has outlived its theological origins and now structures secular labor norms so that non-work time is treated not as a protected sphere of negative liberty but as a residual category available for reclamation by productivity. To ground this, I am returning to Weber’s The Protestant Ethic and the Spirit of Capitalism (1904–1905).

Consider the following passage, which I have paraphrased from Weber’s discussion of ascetic labor and the moral status of leisure. In the ascetic Protestant view, labor in one’s calling becomes an absolute end in itself, not merely a means to economic maintenance. The duty to work is transformed into an inner compulsion that requires relentless application to the vocational task. Idleness is condemned not simply because it threatens economic efficiency, but because it signifies a withdrawal from the proof of one’s state of grace; the enjoyment of leisure or worldly goods is treated as a dangerous distraction that dissipates the energy owed to the calling. Wealth, if acquired without labor, is therefore morally suspect, yet labor itself—regardless of its external rewards—retains an intrinsic spiritual value that makes any reduction in work time appear as a moral failure rather than a legitimate claim.

I am trying to understand whether this framing supports my claim that modern movements for a 35-hour work week and critiques of karoshi (death by overwork in Japan) should be interpreted primarily as external, regulatory attempts to impose boundaries on a normative structure that still treats non-work time as unclaimed territory, or whether they indicate an internal normative collapse of the vocational ethic itself—such that the ethic can no longer generate its own limits and must be constrained from outside.

Could you help me understand the logic of this passage and whether it genuinely implies that leisure lacks independent moral standing, or whether Weber’s own historical narrative allows for a secularized version of the calling that could accommodate a right to leisure?
