I'm drafting a short philosophical essay on archaeological inference, and I'd value critical comments on its coherence, its novelty, and whether the central distinction actually holds up under pressure. The argument concerns how we move from standardized material objects—uniform weights, canonical building orientations, or repeated burial configurations—to claims about centralized institutions. I want to challenge the default inference that material uniformity necessarily implies a coordinating authority, and to do so I introduce what I'm calling "distributed convergence": situations where nearly identical forms emerge independently across separated communities because they face shared environmental constraints or slowly calibrate expectations through trade, producing patterns that look institutionally enforced but lack any governing body. To make this concrete, I ask the reader to imagine two non-adjacent river valleys where independent agricultural communities develop almost identical grain-storage containers not because a state mandated them, but because the same hydrological conditions made particular volumes optimally stable against seasonal flooding, and long-distance exchange gradually synchronized local practices without central direction.

The harder part of the essay is proposing a criterion to distinguish true institutional standardization from this kind of convergence. I suggest looking for what I term "error asymmetry": if deviations from the standard are systematically corrected in one direction—say, only undersized containers are adjusted upward, suggesting a regulatory floor—while oversize deviations remain tolerated, this directional correction might indicate an enforcing authority. By contrast, random variation in both directions might indicate local adaptation without oversight. But I then press the worry that recognizing "correction" already seems to presuppose the very institutional framework I'm trying to identify, risking circularity. The essay asks whether this circularity can be broken by examining the material traces of adjustment—wear patterns, discard practices, or secondary modifications—rather than inferring authority from the uniformity itself.

I'd especially welcome comments on three things. First, whether "distributed convergence" and "error asymmetry" are genuinely distinct from familiar ideas like bottom-up norm emergence or peer interaction, or whether I'm just relabeling existing concepts. Second, whether the argument risks unfalsifiability: if any uniform pattern can be explained either by hidden institutions or by hidden convergence mechanisms, does the distinction collapse into a methodological stalemate? Third, whether there is a more parsimonious way to frame the problem without inventing new terminology, or whether the terminology is justified if it sharpens an actual gap in how archaeologists and philosophers of science talk about institutional reconstruction. The intended audience is philosophers of science and theoretically inclined archaeologists, not regional specialists, so I'm less concerned with empirical coverage of any particular site and more with whether the logical structure of the inference is sound and whether the essay addresses a real problem rather than a manufactured one.
