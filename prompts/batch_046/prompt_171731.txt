I was arguing with a friend last week about whether history is cyclical or linear, and I think I've ended up with a view that doesn't fit either camp cleanly—maybe a bit too neat. Basically I think Polybius's constitutional cycles, Ibn Khaldun's decay of group solidarity, Spengler's culture-to-civilization shift, and Toynbee's challenge-and-response model all catch something real but misidentify what's actually repeating. My claim is that what's cyclical isn't political form or cultural vitality but a specific cluster of cognitive-institutional pathologies: quantitative growth in technology, population, and data periodically outruns the qualitative frameworks we use to govern it, producing crises that look like decline but are really integration failures. The linear part is the accumulation of tools and global connectivity; the cyclical part is the recurrence of these misalignments, though I think the period is stretching because interconnection makes total collapse harder, even if local disruptions stay about as severe. So history is helical with flattening amplitude—same structural errors, longer intervals, less catastrophic collapse.

What would count against this? I want more than just "history is messy." I need specific evidence, formal arguments, or historical counter-cases that should actually move me—like societies that scaled quantitatively without any corresponding integration crisis, or proof that modern disruptions are genuinely milder rather than just distributed across borders and hidden in supply chains. Has this view been qualified into unfalsifiability? If I can always relabel a crisis as "local, not a true cycle" or claim hidden integration failures in datasets I haven't looked at, is that insulation a problem for a view of this kind? Rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, and tell me which ones a defender of the helical/integration-failure model would honestly concede versus which they'd dismiss by redefining the cycle. I'd especially like to know if there's anything in complexity theory or institutional economics that either supports or undermines the idea that connectivity lengthens cycles without lowering peak damage. Definately don't need a bibliography—just the arguments sorted by how decisive they are, plus an honest call on whether the view risks anything real at all.
