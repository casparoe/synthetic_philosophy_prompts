I serve on the disciplinary appeals board of the chartered professional institute that regulates my field. Earlier this year I was personally involved in reviewing a case against a senior practitioner—someone with whom I completed my training—who had caused serious harm through sustained negligence rather than malicious intent. Our present framework addresses such breaches through what I shall call a guilt-paradigm: private hearings, individual censure, confidential remediation, and contractual restitution to affected parties. A reform proposal now before the board argues that we should instead adopt a shame-paradigm: public acknowledgment of the breach before the professional community, ritualised reintegration rather than confidential restoration, and honour-based restitution in place of contractual reparation.

The question before us is philosophical as well as procedural. We must determine whether guilt or shame is the more fitting moral emotion for institutional discipline, and—more fundamentally—whether a professional culture can genuinely choose between them, or whether the emotional architecture is so embedded in our existing rituals, evidentiary rules, and restoration protocols that selecting one amounts to selecting an entirely different kind of institution. I am particularly concerned with the problem of emotional path dependence: once an institution has constructed its procedures around one moral emotion, switching to the other may not merely alter how practitioners feel, but may change the very meaning of the harm done and the moral identity of the wrongdoer.

Please draft the board's decision memo for circulation before our meeting next Thursday. Recommend a concrete course of action—retain the guilt framework, adopt the shame framework, or implement a hybrid with explicit conditions—and provide reasoning that could genuinely guide policy revision. You should treat seriously the strongest argument for the framework you reject, and you should engage directly with whether a culture can choose its dominant moral emotion or whether such a choice is structurally illusory.
