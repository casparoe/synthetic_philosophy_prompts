I've been trying to work out my own version of the analogy that Hofstadter draws in Gödel, Escher, Bach between Gödel's self-referential formulas and human self-awareness. Specifically, I think the parallel is structurally exact, not just poetic: a self-aware mind must contain a representation of itself that genuinely refers back to the system producing it, and—this is the part I have added—must also be able to evaluate the semantic content of that representation entirely from within, without stepping outside into an external interpreter. I imagine this as a nested hierarchy of interpretive levels, each interpreting the one below, producing a kind of internal closure. Simple recursion or behavioral self-monitoring, on my view, is not enough; without this specific loop, a system however complex should not count as genuinely self-aware. I want an honest map of what would have to be true for this to be wrong. Which observations about consciousness, which formal results about self-reference, or which architectural findings in cognitive science would count as decisive defeaters rather than merely awkward adjustments? Would it kill the view if consciousness turned out to depend on global workspace dynamics with no logical self-reference at all, or if distributed architectures achieved self-representation without any central interpretive layer? I also want to know which of these challenges defenders of the Gödelian analogy themselves concede as damaging, and whether the position risks being insulated by qualifications—say, retreating to the claim that the loop is only sufficient, not necessary. If it has been qualified that far, is that a weakness for a view of this kind, or a reasonable defense? Please rank the exposure from fatal to merely inconvenient, and say what predictions this view makes that rival accounts of consciousness do not.
