Consider the institutional design now adopted by an increasing number of universities: students progress through modular, competency-based pathways in which advancement is triggered not by temporal duration but by the verified mastery of discrete, atomised learning outcomes, each certified through standardised rubrics and digital micro-credentials; yet these same institutions preserve ceremonial and curricular structures—matriculation rites, disciplinary honours, and capstone rituals—that treat education as an irreversible, unitary transformation of character rather than an accumulation of separable skills. What philosophy of education, and more broadly what ontology of the learner, does this hybrid practice presuppose? Does it implicitly assume that knowledge is fundamentally inert and decomposable into transferable units, or can it be reconciled with a process view—such as Whitehead’s—according to which education should follow an organic rhythm through romance, precision, and generalisation, and according to which reality itself consists not of static substances but of interrelated occasions of experience? Reconstruct the implicit theory, distinguish what the practice strictly requires from what it merely accommodates, assess whether its two halves are philosophically consistent, and specify what would have to change in assessment, credentialing, and institutional ritual if the university genuinely adopted Whitehead’s conviction that education is the disciplined cultivation of living thought rather than the verification of discrete competencies. Please write your answer in British English.
