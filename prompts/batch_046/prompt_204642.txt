Construct a realistic, non-science-fictional thought experiment—no longer than one paragraph—situated within the problem of individual contributions to collective harms (for example, climate-related consumption, meat purchasing, or voting in a large election). Engineer the scenario so that three specified moral frameworks yield three predetermined verdicts on whether a particular individual ought to perform the action. First, standard act consequentialism with linear expected-utility aggregation must judge the action morally permissible: the agent’s marginal causal contribution to the harm is imperceptible and is outweighed by personal cost, so the classic inefficacy objection applies. Second, a threshold-sensitive collective deontology must judge the action impermissible: the harm is discontinuous and only realized if a precise collective threshold is crossed, and each agent’s participation is a necessary constituent of the collective scheme that realizes it, regardless of counterfactual redundancy in any single case. Third, introduce and apply a niche conceptual framework—call it normative-echo theory—which holds that even when an agent’s causal contribution is fully redundant and the collective threshold will not be crossed by this act alone, the act still expresses and marginally reinforces a social norm that raises the probability of future threshold-crossing; on this view the agent must refrain, but solely for this expressive, non-causal reason, and the verdict must remain intact even under the stipulation that the causal threshold is never actually reached.

After presenting the scenario, demonstrate that it satisfies every specification: show the reasoning by which each framework reaches its designated verdict, identifying exactly which structural feature—causal redundancy, discontinuous aggregation, or normative echo—does the decisive work for each theory. Then provide an honest diagnostic: isolate the feature that makes normative-echo theory diverge from both act consequentialism and threshold deontology; state whether removing that feature collapses the case into a standard inefficacy problem; and reconstruct how a defender of act consequentialism would attack the normative-echo verdict, especially by arguing that moral reasons must be anchored in causally efficacious differences rather than in purely expressive or symbolic effects. The scenario should be accessible to an undergraduate philosophy audience, must not invoke demons, perfect predictors, exotic physics, or implausible coordination mechanisms, and must not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citations.
