People often explain conceptual creativity with an evolutionary landscape analogy: a concept space contains local peaks of coherence, valleys of contradiction, and the thinker is an explorer who can temporarily lower barriers to move between valleys. Audit this analogy when applied to Hofstadter's knob-twiddling model, in which concepts are structured spaces with adjustable parameters and insight arises from systematically varying these settings. Give the explicit correspondence: what plays the role of elevation, valleys, peaks, barrier-lowering, and the explorer's agency? Identify the disanalogies that matter for the intended claim — particularly regarding random versus intentional variation, the presence or absence of selection pressure, and whether dimensions are continuous or discrete — and separate these from disanalogies that do not undermine the comparison. What does the landscape metaphor quietly import about teleology and the value of reaching "higher" ground that the knob-twiddling account may reject? Finally, state whether the analogy can genuinely support the conclusion that creativity is essentially constrained variation, or whether it collapses into a different theory of conceptual change altogether.
