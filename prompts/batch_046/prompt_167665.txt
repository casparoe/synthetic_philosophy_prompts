In my graduate seminar on moral philosophy we have been working through Scanlon's contractualist framework, and my study partner constructed an argument that seems to prove the view collapses into total moral paralysis whenever interests conflict—not merely in classic aggregation cases, but everywhere. I find each premise individually plausible, yet the conclusion is plainly unacceptable, and I cannot locate the precise slip. The argument runs as follows. (1) On the contractualist view, an act is wrong just in case it is permitted by a principle that at least one affected person could reasonably reject, where reasonable rejection turns on comparing the burden the principle imposes on the rejecter with the burdens others would bear if that principle were rejected. (2) Consider any non-trivial choice between option A, which benefits party X, and option B, which benefits party Y, with X and Y disjoint and each party preferring the option that serves them. (3) The principle authorizing A can be rejected by members of Y, since under A they bear the burden of being denied B. (4) Symmetrically, the principle authorizing B can be rejected by members of X, since under B they bear the burden of being denied A. (5) Because contractualism grants each affected individual an independent standing to reject principles that burden them—what my partner calls a "veto-like" individualism—both principles are rejectable. (6) Therefore neither A nor B is morally permissible. (7) Since every actual moral decision, from distributing scarce medicine to resolving disputes to choosing whom to rescue, produces winners and losers, step (6) applies universally. (8) Hence contractualism implies that every moral choice is impermissible, an absurd result. (9) So the principle of reasonable rejection must be abandoned.

My partner insists the error lies in treating every burden as equally weighty regardless of magnitude, but I worry that introducing cross-personal weighting is just smuggling aggregation back in through a different door, undermining the very feature that makes contractualism distinctive. I want to preserve the idea that contractualism does not aggregate welfare across persons in the utilitarian manner, yet I also want to avoid (8). Which numbered step should I reject, why did it look defensible on its face, and what does giving it up cost? In particular, if fixing the argument requires comparing burdens across individuals in a way that allows the number of affected parties to matter, does that dissolve the individualist core of the view?
