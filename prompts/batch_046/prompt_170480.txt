Can you read Groundhog Day through Borges's view of time — specificaly the idea he pushes in that essay where he argues time only feels real because we imagine a stable self watching it unfold? Borges thinks if you tear down the self, succession collapses too, since there's no subject left to experience 'before' and 'after'. In the movie Phil is stuck in the same Tuesday, but he also develops: he learns piano, stops being a jerk, saves people. The film treats him like one continuous guy banking experience, which is exactly the linear, progressive self Borges is suspicious of. So I'm wondering: does the loop actually dramatize Borges's denial of real succession, or does Phil's moral developement secretly re-introduce the kind of enduring subject that makes time possible? And when he finally breaks free — is that an escape from eternal repetition, or is it just the movie caving to a humanist fantasy of progress that Borges would reject? I want a reading that takes the film's emotional logic seriously without turning it into a philosophy lecture, and that doesn't pretend Borges and Hollywood agree more than they do. No need for citations or a reading list, just a real engagement with both.
