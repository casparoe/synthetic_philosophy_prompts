I'm delivering the opening paper at next month's workshop on Agency and Temporal Structure, defending what I call the diachronic-fragmentation account of weakness of will. The core claim is that hyperbolic discounting should not be read as a rationality failure within a unified agent, but as evidence that the present practical self fails to identify the future self's preferences as normatively binding on it—not because it undervalues them, but because the continuity conditions required for diachronic authority are not satisfied. On this view, the standard paradox of akrasia dissolves: there is no single agent that both makes an all-out judgment and acts against it, because the judging self and the acting self stand in an intertemporal bargaining relation rather than in a relation of strict identity over time. The phenomenology of struggle is preserved, but reconceived as negotiation between partially autonomous temporal perspectives rather than as internal contradiction.

The audience is deliberately interdisciplinary and hostile in different directions. One faction consists of analytic philosophers of action who treat the Davidsonian framework of all-out judgment as the baseline and regard hyperbolic curves as merely empirical descriptions that leave the conceptual problem untouched. A second faction comprises cognitive scientists and behavioral economists who take intertemporal choice models as primary and are inclined to dismiss talk of normative binding or diachronic authority as untestable metaphysical ornament. A third, smaller group are moral psychologists specializing in self-control, who tend to conflate weakness of will with impulsivity or addiction and will press me on whether my view preserves that distinction.

Draft my Q&A prep sheet. I want approximately eight objections, ordered by the actual damage each would do to the central thesis if left unanswered, not merely by speaking order. Each objection should be phrased in the vocabulary and conceptual framework of the faction most likely to raise it—the philosophers should sound like philosophers of action, the scientists like empirical researchers, and the moral psychologists like clinicians of self-regulation. For each: (i) the objection as it would actually be voiced, (ii) a concise reply (no more than two minutes spoken) that holds the line on diachronic fragmentation without conceding the framework, (iii) an explicit note on where that reply is weakest or depends on a contested premise, and (iv) whether I should concede any part of the point up front to maintain credibility with that faction.

Crucially, include the one question I am hoping nobody asks—the boundary-condition challenge that forces me to specify exactly when a future preference counts as mine in the binding sense rather than merely predicted, and that threatens to collapse my position into standard temporal-parts theory if I cannot draw the line with precision.
