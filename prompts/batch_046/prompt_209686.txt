I have noticed a recurring asymmetry in how failure narratives are deployed in meritocratic cultures, particularly where American Dream mythology and Scandinavian-influenced norms overlap. When an established figure recounts an early career setback, the story is usually interpreted as an authentic, redemptive stepping stone: the failure proves that eventual success was earned rather than accidental. Yet when someone still attempting to rise suffers a comparable setback, the same narrative vocabulary is often turned against them in a manner closer to Jantelagen or the tall poppy syndrome — the failure is read as evidence that they should not have presumed to stand out, and the story is used to diminish rather than to excuse them. The rhetorical materials are identical — modesty, authenticity, the moral value of struggle — but their function reverses according to whether the subject has already been recognised as successful. Is there an established concept that captures this status-contingent switch in how failure-tolerance is applied? If not, can you define and delimit a term that distinguishes it from plain hypocrisy, from survivor bias, and from a consistent application of either meritocratic individualism or egalitarian modesty? I am not looking for a bare label: I want the carving — what exactly triggers the switch, where the boundary lies between legitimate scepticism of pretension and punitive cutting-down, and whether there are contexts in which the asymmetry collapses entirely.
