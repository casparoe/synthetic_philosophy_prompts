I'm in an upper-division seminar on ancient economic philosophy and I've volunteered to lead a fifty-minute discussion section for mixed philosophy and economics majors on Aristotle's account of money and exchange. Most of the group will arrive with a caricature drawn from second-hand summaries: that Aristotle condemns all market activity as greedy and unnatural because he thinks wealth should have a fixed limit, and that this is just an archaic version of anti-capitalist sentiment. I want to design the explanatory arc so that the idea lands as a specific conceptual dependency rather than as a historical curio. Don't explain Aristotle to me directly; plan how I should guide the students through it.

First, lay out the exact order in which the pieces must be introduced and why: which distinction—between household acquisition ordered to self-sufficiency and acquisition ordered to accumulation—has to be secured before money can be discussed at all, and how the conventional status of currency relates to that prior limit without collapsing into the claim that money itself is ethically corrupting. Choose one concrete carrying example—not the standard shoe from Politics I.9, since that is the first example every summary reaches for—that can hold both legitimate exchange between specialists and the precise point at which exchange tips into the unlimited form; specify what the example must include (a use-value with a natural terminus, a mediating instrument that is not consumed in the exchange) and what it must avoid (anything that makes specialization itself look suspicious). Then map the misconceptions in the order they will arise: I expect they will first conflate oikonomia with mere thrift or household budgeting; next, because they will learn that money is established by convention, they will infer that it carries no ethical constraints at all; finally they will restrict Aristotle's critique to usury alone, missing that retail trade also falls under the unlimited form. For each, indicate the exact point in the sequence where it should be headed off and with what counter-case. Include one check for understanding at the end—a comparative sorting task involving two exchange scenarios—that reveals whether a student grasps the dependency between natural acquisition, conventional mediation, and unlimited accumulation, rather than merely recalling that Aristotle was skeptical of banking. Finally, state explicitly what should be left out at this level, such as the full analysis of proportional reciprocity in Nicomachean Ethics V.5 and the Politics I.8 discussion of monopoly pricing, and indicate how I should acknowledge that omission without making it sound like a suppressed contradiction.
