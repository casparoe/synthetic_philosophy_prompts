I work as an interpretation officer at a mid-sized regional museum that mounts temporary exhibits on local identity practices — flag ceremonies, revived folk dances, founding narratives, commemorative rituals — and I must write the wall labels for each one. I have no graduate training in history; I have thought through the basic arguments of Eric Hobsbawm and Benedict Anderson well enough to grasp that many traditions claiming ancient origins are in fact modern reconstructions, and that nations are imagined communities shaped by print culture, state institutions, and bounded membership rather than timeless natural entities. But my staff needs a reusable decision procedure they can apply every quarter without a PhD, under a two-week deadline, with me alone signing off on the final label and no peer-review board available.

Turn those ideas into a practical, ordered checklist — with specific test questions, yes/no branches where the theory permits them, and explicit markers for the points where judgment cannot be eliminated — that classifies any proposed tradition into one of three categories for labeling: (a) practices with demonstrable, continuous institutional transmission over generations; (b) practices deliberately reconstructed or invented in modern times to serve present political, social, or emotional needs; and (c) hybrid cases in which an old performative form survives but has been given a radically new national or communal meaning. Ground every step in what Hobsbawm and Anderson actually claim about repetition, symbolic reference to the past, bounded membership, and the fixing of identity through institutions and media, but translate those claims into criteria a non-specialist can apply to an exhibit proposal.

Include a small set of concrete test sentences an editor can run against any case. Provide worked applications to three examples: one clear continuous tradition, one clear modern invention, and one hard hybrid case where the procedure yields a result I will find unwelcome — specifically, labeling a beloved local revival that carries deep emotional weight for the community as an invented tradition despite its importance. State precisely what the procedure cannot settle, flag where the editor must decide on values rather than evidence, and say what misclassifications are most likely.

Please separate what is settled among scholars who study nationalism and tradition from what remains genuinely contested — for instance, whether the invention framework applies with equal force outside Western European contexts, or whether continuity and invention can ever be separated cleanly in practice — and do not present any contested interpretation as if it were the consensus view.
