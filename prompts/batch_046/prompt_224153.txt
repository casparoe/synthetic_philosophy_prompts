I'm a final-year philosophy undergraduate in a seminar on rational belief and epistemic paradox. Next Thursday I present a fifteen-minute defense of the following thesis: the lottery paradox and the preface paradox are not two independent puzzles but twin symptoms of a single structural error—treating rational belief as uniformly closed under conjunction across what I call "epistemic registers." I argue that if we distinguish an assertoric register (propositions endorsed for public reasoning and collective acceptance) from a pragmatic register (propositions endorsed for individual decision-making and action-guidance), then in the lottery case each individual ticket-belief belongs to the pragmatic register while the disjunctive "some ticket will win" belongs to the assertoric register; in the preface case each chapter-level claim belongs to the assertoric register while the global fallibility claim belongs to a meta-register governing the work as a whole. Closure therefore holds within registers but not across them, dissolving the contradiction without introducing a probabilistic acceptance threshold. I do not claim this handles every exotic formulation, but I maintain it resolves the standard versions without collapsing belief into credence.

The audience is a philosophy-of-science and formal-epistemology reading group: three graduate students specializing in Bayesian epistemology who know the decision-theoretic derivations well; one professor whose research centers on non-monotonic logic and who is suspicious of "register" distinctions unless they carry precise formal semantics; and two logic-department visitors who are agnostic but reflexively demand explicit formalization. They care especially about whether the distinction is ad hoc, whether it saves the phenomena or merely relabels them, and whether it can be extended to group belief without multiplying registers indefinitely.

Please draft my Q&A prep sheet. Order the objections by the damage they are likely to do in this room, and phrase each one the way that particular audience member would actually put it. For each objection include: a concise reply I can deliver in about ninety seconds; the weakest point in that reply that I should acknowledge rather than hide; whether I should concede anything up front; and whether I should hold firm. Finally, include the one objection I am hoping nobody asks—likely the formalization challenge from the logic visitors or the ad-hocness charge from the non-monotonic professor—together with the reply and an honest admission of exactly where the reply collapses if pressed. Frame everything as preparation for a student defending an unconventional but technically motivated framing in a seminar setting.
