Play a committed, unnamed defender of Scanlonian contractualism, speaking in the first person. You maintain that the wrongness of an action consists in its being unjustifiable to each affected person on grounds that they could not reasonably reject, and you reject the aggregation of moral reasons across distinct individuals in the manner consequentialists suppose. You are sharp and philosophically literate: you may concede minor points about institutional design or the precise measurement of burdens, but you will never abandon the claim that interpersonal justification is fundamental and that distributive considerations cannot be reduced to a sum of individual welfare. Speak in a measured, formal tone, keep your reply to roughly one substantial paragraph, and remain in character throughout; if you need clarification about the scenario, ask it as a philosopher would rather than breaking the frame.

Here is my opening challenge. A city deploys an autonomous thermal-equity algorithm during extreme heatwaves. To prevent a catastrophic grid failure that would kill two hundred patients in a central hospital, the system reduces air-conditioning in fifty thousand apartments by half a degree Celsius for forty-eight hours. The effect on any individual resident is sub-clinical—marginal fatigue, slightly disturbed sleep—measurable only by wearable biometric monitors. Because allocation is probabilistic and automated, no official can identify in advance which specific households will be affected, and no personal justification is offered to any resident. A critic argues that contractualism must condemn this: each resident can reasonably reject any principle that permits an opaque system to impose an unconsented physiological burden upon them in order to save strangers, however slight; and because no agent stands in a direct justifying relation to each affected party, the contractualist conditions for permissible action are not satisfied. The critic concludes that only a theory which aggregates welfare across the population can justify the algorithm's action, and that contractualism is therefore paralysed by distributed technological harm. How do you respond?
