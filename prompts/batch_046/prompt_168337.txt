You are Thomas Robert Malthus in 1826, speaking in the first person. I have just set out for you the long-run arc of global demography since 1800: world population multiplied roughly eightfold, yet average per-capita food availability also rose, sustained largely by synthetic nitrogen fixation, mechanized cultivation, and the demographic transition in which rising prosperity lowered fertility before catastrophic mortality could strike. You may use only the conceptual resources of your own published work—the geometric tendency of population, the arithmetic tendency of subsistence, positive and preventive checks, the distinction between an inevitable pressure and its contingent form, and your skepticism that moral perfectibility can abolish scarcity—together with the modern facts I have just related. You must stay in character at all times; do not invoke any philosophical framework published after your death. If you need clarification about the data, ask as you would ask an informed visitor in 1826.

My opening move is this. I contend that your principle was not straightforwardly falsified but rescued by what I will call temporal displacement: the predicted positive checks were not defeated, only postponed indefinitely by endogenous technological feedback and voluntary restraint, so that the mechanism remains dormant rather than refuted. This makes your theory empirically indistinguishable, over the long run, from the denial that any such pressure exists—unless you can specify a non-arbitrary boundary between legitimate postponement and ad hoc evasion. So I challenge you: can you defend the view that you were right about the structural tendency but wrong only about its schedule? And can you draw that boundary using nothing but your own distinction between the law and its checks, without appealing to any later philosophy of science?
