I am designing the final exam for an upper-level undergraduate seminar on social ontology and practical ethics. Please draft one essay question—suitable for 90 minutes and approximately 800 words—on whether everyday conventions such as tipping, queueing, and observing local jaywalking norms generate genuine moral obligations, and whether visitors are morally required to follow local ones. To prevent answers that merely recite standard conventionalist or social-contract summaries, embed the following conceptual framing: distinguish sharply between coordinative conventions, where uniform behavior solves a collective-action problem but compliance is merely prudential, and constitutive conventions, where adherence partly constitutes the social practice and generates internal reasons for participants. Require students to apply this distinction to at least two of the three practices, and to address the specific tension that a visitor lacking the internal aims of a constitutive practice might nonetheless be bound by independent moral considerations—such as fairness to local participants—without being bound by the convention's internal logic. Provide a model answer of about 600 words that takes a nuanced position (for example, that queueing is largely coordinative but acquires moral weight through expectations of fairness; that tipping is constitutive of a service relationship but visitors may have reduced obligations if they do not enter the contractual structure; and that jaywalking norms are largely coordinative and highly context-sensitive). Also provide a marking scheme out of 20 points built around four specific moves: (1) accurate application of the coordinative/constitutive distinction to two practices (5 points); (2) analysis of why visitor status undermines internal-reason obligations but not necessarily independent moral ones (5 points); (3) avoidance of the mistake of treating all conventions as either purely moral or purely prudential (5 points); (4) explicit handling of at least one counterexample or boundary case, such as refusing to tip in a context where service workers depend on tips for wages (5 points). For each point, include a one-line note on the typical failure mode—e.g., collapsing the distinction, ignoring the visitor problem, or appealing to vague cultural relativism. The question and answer should not ask for or include bibliographies, citations, or references to specific texts by page number.
