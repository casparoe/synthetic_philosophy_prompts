I am trying to refine my own understanding of moral progress, particularly through the lens of what is sometimes called the expanding circle, and I find that my intuitive verdicts across a range of cases do not line up neatly with either a purely outcome-based or a purely intention-based account. I regard the abolition of chattel slavery and the extension of legal personhood to previously disenfranchised groups as clear instances of progress: the boundary of moral consideration was redrawn because the excluded group was recognised as falling within it. I feel similarly about the historical shift from viewing children as economic assets to viewing them as bearers of rights that constrain adult behaviour. By contrast, I do not think that granting legal standing to rivers or ecosystems constitutes moral progress; it strikes me as a legal fiction that mistakes instrumental value for moral status, and I would say the same about proposals to extend citizenship rights to artificial systems solely on the grounds that their decision-making requires regulatory coherence. Where I become uncertain is with cases in which harmful practices disappear without any corresponding normative shift. If factory-farming conditions improve solely because market efficiency demands leaner processes, with producers continuing to regard animals as units of production, I hesitate to call this moral progress, even though the circle of protected beings has widened in practice. Even more puzzling to me is the pair of cases involving the abandonment of foot-binding: in one scenario, the practice ends because a society genuinely comes to see women as possessing equal bodily autonomy and moral standing; in the other, it ends because industrial labour markets make bound feet an economic liability, and the taboo persists through social imitation long after the economic cause has disappeared. I judge the first to be genuine progress and the second to be mere cultural drift, yet both produce identical expansions in the protection of women, and I struggle to articulate what distinguishes them without smuggling in an implausible requirement that every moral improvement must be accompanied by explicit philosophical reasoning.

I would like a precise principle — or a small family of competing principles — that captures as many of these verdicts as possible. For each clause, please indicate which case motivates it, and then apply the principle to two further situations I have not mentioned: first, a future society that eliminates predation in wild ecosystems in order to reduce suffering, and second, a corporation that adopts stringent ethical supply-chain standards purely as a risk-management strategy. I also want an honest assessment of which of my verdicts the principle must reject if it is to remain coherent and not ad hoc — in particular, whether my distinction between the two foot-binding scenarios can be sustained, or whether it collapses once we separate normative recognition from functional outcomes. Finally, please state clearly what your account predicts about whether moral progress can occur when the expansion of protection is driven entirely by technological or economic change that later becomes internalised as taboo, without any intermediate stage of explicit moral recognition.
