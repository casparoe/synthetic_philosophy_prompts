I have noticed a recurring configuration in highly secular, educated moral communities and I am unsure whether it already has a precise name. The pattern is as follows: the group retains practices that are structurally identical to religious disciplines — regular communal examination of conscience, ritualised acknowledgment of moral failure, canonical study of ethical texts, and intergenerational transmission of moral narratives — yet explicitly rejects any supernatural or transcendent justification, framing them instead as social technologies for sustaining trust or psychological health. What distinguishes the configuration is its response to failure. When participants show no measurable improvement in prosocial behaviour, or when the practices generate factionalism rather than cohesion, the community does not abandon the framework. Rather, it introduces auxiliary adjustments — improved facilitation, updated curricula, more inclusive canon selection — and treats each revision as progressive refinement rather than as an ad hoc rescue. The practitioners insist they are engaged in nothing like religion, but both the functional architecture (discipline, confession, canon, ritual) and the immunisation strategy against counterevidence remain intact. Is there an established concept that captures precisely this combination — the preservation of religious-form practices under purely secular justification, coupled with a research-programme-style defence against disconfirmation? I know of "civil religion" and "secular ritualisation," but those appear to cover only the structural resemblance, not the specific epistemic posture toward failure. If no precise term exists, how should one delineate this phenomenon from ordinary institutional self-correction on the one hand and from implicit theology on the other? Please respond in British English.
