I hold a two-layer reading of Maimonides on animal sacrifice: on the surface he treats it as a divine concession to an Israelite population unable to abandon idolatrous habits, but beneath that he assigns sacrifice an irreducible pedagogical-cognitive function—coordinating embodied action, communal intention, and symbolic representation in a way that purely internal worship cannot replicate. On this view, sacrifice is not merely tolerated but structurally necessary for a fully realized human-divine relation, even in an ideal society freed from idolatry. What would count against this dual-layer view? I want an honest map of its exposure: which passages in the Guide or in Maimonides’ legal writings would have to be read differently, which assumptions about his Aristotelian teleology would need to fail, and whether the view risks being insulated by ad hoc distinctions. Rank the defeaters from fatal—for instance, a clear textual claim that prayer supersedes sacrifice in every respect—to merely awkward—for instance, historical evidence that Second Temple practices were more varied than the concession narrative allows. Also say whether defenders of the standard “pure concession” reading would themselves concede any of these points, or whether the dual-layer view is mainly vulnerable to charges of over-systematization. Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then the reasoning, with no preamble and no restatement of the question.
