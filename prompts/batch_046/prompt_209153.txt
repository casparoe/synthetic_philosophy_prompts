Wittgenstein's private language argument (the sections of the Philosophical Investigations commonly read together beginning around §243) is often summarized as showing that a language describing one's own inner sensations and intelligible only to oneself is impossible. Explain the structure of this argument, with particular attention to (a) whether its target is the epistemic privacy of mental states or the grammatical conditions under which a speaker can follow a rule for applying a psychological term; and (b) how the beetle-in-the-box scenario relates to this distinction. In your answer, clearly distinguish Wittgenstein's position from both Cartesian substance dualism and logical behaviorism.

Student answer:

Wittgenstein shows that a private language is impossible because if only I can access my sensations, there is no external check on whether I apply my private term consistently. He introduces the beetle-in-the-box scenario to show that if each speaker had a private object inside an inaccessible box, that object would play no role in the public language-game; we learn and use the word beetle through shared criteria, not through private inspection. From this he concludes that psychological terms such as pain are defined entirely by their behavioral and contextual manifestations—so to be in pain just is to display pain-behavior within a form of life. Thus he does not deny that people have inner experiences, but he shows these experiences are causally and grammatically inert: they exist, but they contribute nothing to the meaning of mental-language. The argument therefore targets epistemic privacy—without other people to correct my usage, I cannot maintain a stable rule—making the problem one of verification rather than ontology. Cartesian dualism fails on this view not because it misdescribes the grammar of mind-talk, but because it wrongly treats these inert private objects as the foundational referents of psychological vocabulary.
