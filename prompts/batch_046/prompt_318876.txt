I teach a seminar on Rousseau and public life — about 8 grad students, 90 minutes, not all Rousseau specialists. I want a simulation that makes them feel why he thinks theater corrupts a republic, but pushed past the usual "vanity and luxury" reading into the weirder parallel he draws between theatrical spectatorship and representative politics: once citizens start performing virtue for an audience, they become actors rather than sovereign agents, just as representative government turns active people into passive watchers. So split them into two groups of four. Group A solves a collective budget problem (fake points across three projects) behind closed doors, unanimous consent, no audience, no notes. Group B solves the exact same problem in front of Group A, explaining each step out loud, while I play a "chronicler" writing down quotable lines on a board they can see. After 25 mins we compare not just outcomes but how each group experienced ownership and agreement. Round two: Group B votes whether to invite the audience into the room for round three, but now the audience can visibly react — nodding, frowning, leaning in — without speaking. The point is they should feel the feedback loop where being seen doesn't just observe but reshapes thier deliberation. Include rules, the fake-point payoff scheme, a timeline (maybe 5 min intro, 25 min round one, 10 min comparison, 15 min round two with vote, 25 min debrief, 10 min buffer), and a debrief that maps "performing for spectators" onto the theater critique and "delegated visibility" onto the political representation worry. Also tell me what's predictably going to go wrong — I bet Group B dominates and actually enjoys the performance, while Group A feels resentful and checks out. Be frank about which feature of Rousseau's argument this can't reproduce: probably the economic/luxury corruption part where theater drains labor and money into spectacle. No lecture up front, and they shouldn't hear the parallel named until the debrief. Oh, and can you build in a twist where in round three the chronicler gets to read one line back at them before they finalize? I think that captures the alienation effect.
