I am not a historian, but I have been following the long argument over whether Germany bears responsibility for the First World War, especially the controversy that followed Fritz Fischer's claim that German leadership deliberately sought a continental war. In reading popular and scholarly accounts, I keep encountering the same argumentative structure, and I want to know whether it has a recognized name or form. Someone begins by saying that Germany caused the war, meaning that specific chancellors and generals made reckless, blameworthy decisions—say, issuing the blank check to Austria-Hungary in 1914. When a critic points out that other powers also made aggressive choices, the speaker shifts ground and says that Germany's institutional culture—its military planning, its bureaucratic rigidity—made conflict structurally probable regardless of individual intent. If that too is contested, they retreat further and speak of widespread German public enthusiasm for war, as if the nation's collective mood itself constituted a culpable agent. Each layer is individually plausible, but they are used interchangeably so that the accusation of national responsibility never settles on one specific claim that could be tested or refuted. I have taken to calling this pattern "layered attribution," though I suspect I am reinventing something philosophers or historians already describe. Is there an established term for this kind of distributed, level-shifting blame? How should one distinguish it from a genuinely multi-causal historical explanation, and from the legal concept of collective responsibility? If no precise term exists, what boundaries would you place around the phenomenon I am describing, and what would you call it?
