I lead a postgraduate philosophy-of-mind seminar of roughly sixteen students, meeting for a single ninety-minute session; they are conversant with the basic vocabulary of predictive processing but have not worked through the formal free-energy literature. Design an in-class simulation — with explicit rules, minimal materials, and a non-monetary payoff or reputation scheme — that makes the participants feel the force of the claim that perception and action are both just prediction-error minimisation, specifically through the mechanism of precision weighting: an agent’s relative confidence in its prior beliefs versus its sensory evidence determines whether it updates its internal model or acts upon the world. To avoid a standard rehearsal of active inference, inject a conceptual twist: arrange participants in pairs or triads assigned hidden, individually distinct precision profiles — for example, some with rigid, high-precision priors and others with high sensory precision — and task them with coordinating a shared navigation or categorisation problem under genuinely ambiguous conditions. The rules should be structured so that the group’s collective failure to coordinate arises not from disagreement about the content of the world, but from mismatched allocations of precision: one partner acts to confirm a strong prior (active inference) while another radically updates in response to error (perceptual inference), and neither can detect the other’s inference strategy from behaviour alone. The debrief should map specific breakdown moments onto the formal distinction between perceptual and active inference, explain how intersubjective or distributed precision weighting produces a collective free-energy landscape that is not reducible to individual errors, and say exactly what will predictably go wrong the first time the activity is run and how to handle it. Be frank about which feature of the free-energy framework — for instance, the continuous, hierarchical modulation of precision across nested timescales — a discrete, turn-based simulation with static roles cannot reproduce and must therefore be supplied by the facilitator in closing discussion. Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citations; the prompt should be self-contained and rely only on concepts that a well-informed model could engage with without external lookup.
