I have been trying to formalise the relationship between path dependence and counterfactual convergence for a seminar on institutional history, and I have constructed an argument that now strikes me as proving far too much. (1) Path dependence holds that, once a particular trajectory is selected, self-reinforcing mechanisms render deviation prohibitively costly and eventually make the trajectory irreversible. (2) If a trajectory is genuinely irreversible, then in any counterfactual history branching off before the point of lock-in, convergence back to the actual-world outcome is impossible, because such convergence would require reversing onto a path that has been closed off. (3) So, if path dependence applies to an outcome E, then in all nearby possible worlds with a different initial branch, E never occurs—not even eventually, and not via an alternative route. (4) Take the standard example of the QWERTY keyboard layout: economists often say it is path-dependent, adopted for contingent mechanical reasons in the 1870s and then locked in by network effects. By (3), in any world where an alternative layout won the initial patent competition, QWERTY can never subsequently dominate, regardless of later technological change or social coordination. (5) Yet it seems perfectly coherent—indeed, common in historical reasoning—to claim that network effects are so powerful that some standardised layout would eventually have achieved dominance, or even that QWERTY itself might have been adopted later through a different institutional route. (6) More broadly, if (3) is correct, then historians can never legitimately employ counterfactual convergence claims of the form that the same structural result would have arrived, just by another path. (7) But such claims are standard explanatory tools, and we also observe long-run institutional stability that genuinely looks like lock-in. So we face a dilemma: either reject (3) and allow convergence within path-dependent domains, which seems to weaken the very notion of irreversibility, or accept (3) and abandon either counterfactual reasoning or the reality of path dependence altogether. (8) Abandoning path dependence entirely, however, erases the distinction between genuinely contingent branching points and structurally inevitable outcomes, which feels like an excessive price.

Which step in this chain should I reject, and what exactly does rejecting it commit me to? I would prefer to preserve both the idea that some historical outcomes are genuinely locked in, in the spirit of (1), and the idea that counterfactual convergence is sometimes conceptually coherent, in the spirit of (5), but I cannot see how to hold both without contradiction. Could you diagnose where the slip occurs—whether it is an equivocation between irreversibility and non-convergence, a scope error in applying path dependence across modal space, or something else—and explain what each available repair costs?
