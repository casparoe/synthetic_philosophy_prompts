I recently began advising a long-horizon institutional forecasting project that attempts to model state fragility across multi-decade horizons, and I have been drawn into the long-running dispute over whether macrohistory can be genuinely quantitative—specifically, the debate surrounding cliodynamics and its claim that agrarian societies exhibit recurring secular cycles of integration and disintegration driven by coupled mechanisms such as elite overproduction and fiscal strain. On one side, quantitative macrohistorians treat these cycles as detectable dynamical patterns across societies and centuries, refining structural equations and claiming predictive leverage over crisis phases. On the other, critics—mostly narrative historians and some comparative sociologists—argue that the patterns are artifacts of selective case choice, that the mechanisms are too underspecified to generate falsifiable out-of-sample forecasts, and that apparent confirmations amount to post-hoc curve-fitting. Despite decades of exchange, neither camp has moved: each new dataset is read as validation by one side and as overfitting by the other.

My suspicion is that the stalemate is not primarily empirical but conceptual and possibly ontological. The quantitative camp appears to treat a “cycle” as a trajectory in a state-space defined by coupled relations among fiscal health, elite density, and popular immiseration; the critics appear to treat it as a narrative template that imposes retrospective coherence on contingent sequences of events. But I am unsure whether this captures the real fracture, or whether a hidden disagreement about evidentiary standards is doing the work—what could count as a “prediction” in a domain without experiments, where counterfactuals are irreducibly speculative—or even a normative divergence about whether reducing political history to structural dynamics eliminates the kind of human agency that makes the study of history worthwhile rather than merely technical.

Can you diagnose what kind of disagreement this actually is? Is it verbal (conflicting meanings of “prediction,” “cycle,” or “explanation”), substantive but underdetermined by available data, or rooted in bedrock commitments about the aims of historical inquiry? And given your diagnosis, what—an explicit formal distinction, a shared methodological benchmark for out-of-sample evaluation, an ontological clarification about what entities the models actually track—could plausibly move the debate forward, or should we conclude that the two projects are answering different questions and ought to be separated rather than reconciled?
