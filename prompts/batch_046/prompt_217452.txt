I am preparing an essay for a course on distributive justice and have found myself returning to the separateness of persons objection to utilitarian aggregation, but I want to test it in a conceptual space that standard textbook examples never reach. Please write a dialogue between two interlocutors—call them Mara and Jonas—who are equally philosophically competent and who genuinely engage with each other's arguments rather than speaking past one another.

Introduce a specific hypothetical framing that is not standard in the literature: suppose there exists a technological-phenomenological condition called cognitive interleaving, in which two individuals share executive reasoning and memory-access modules without merging their streams of consciousness. Each retains a distinct first-person perspective—there remains an irreducible subjective character to being Mara and a separate one to be Jonas—but they now deliberate using a single shared rational architecture. The utilitarian might be tempted to treat them as one distributed agent for welfare computations, since suffering registered in one module could, in principle, be offset by reasoning adjustments in the other.

Mara should defend a modified utilitarian view: she should argue that once practical reasoning is unified in this way, the metaphysical boundary of separate persons no longer supports the moral boundary required by the objection. If the very machinery that weighs costs and benefits is shared, then insisting on separate containers for utility is an arbitrary holdover from a pre-interleaved psychology. Jonas should defend a reconstructed separateness objection. He should grant that psychological continuity is now partially merged, but insist that the moral force of the objection was never about reasoning architecture; it was about the non-transferable ownership of experience. Even with interleaved cognition, Mara's pain is not experienced by Jonas, and there is no super-agent that undergoes both. Compelling Mara to bear a cost for a net gain within the shared architecture therefore still violates the same fundamental boundary, merely relocated to the level of phenomenological ownership rather than cognitive isolation.

The exchange should be disciplined: Jonas must respond directly to Mara's claim about the unity of practical reasoning, and Mara must respond directly to Jonas's claim about experiential ownership. Do not let either character become a strawman. I want this to stand entirely on its own conceptual reasoning, so please do not include bibliographies, citation requests, or references to obscure named theories that a reader could not verify from common knowledge. Most importantly, take a committed stance: the dialogue should reach a definite conclusion in which one side clearly has the stronger argument, with explicit reasons given for why the opposing view fails, rather than ending in an indecisive standoff.
