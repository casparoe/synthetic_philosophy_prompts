As a novelist drafting a story about two siblings—one born with a congenital condition that severely limits her life prospects, the other healthy but chronically reckless—I need to understand what justice demands between them before their relationship can be written with philosophical coherence. Write the desiderata that any adequate theory of distributive justice grounded in the distinction between luck and choice must satisfy. Which cases must it adjudicate correctly, such as congenital disability versus voluntarily assumed risk? Which distinctions must it preserve—not only the familiar division between brute luck and option luck, but also whether a further dimension, which I term narrative luck (the moral weight a person assigns to her own misfortune through the interpretive story she tells about it), must be integrated? What puzzles must it dissolve, including whether full compensation for brute luck eliminates the very conditions that make option luck meaningful, and whether the state can distinguish legitimate from illegitimate claims without intruding upon personal autonomy? Identify any pair of desiderata that seem jointly unsatisfiable—for instance, if complete neutralization of brute luck conflicts with the preservation of meaningful personal responsibility. Then, briefly, assess how standard luck-egalitarian accounts, a pure responsibility-sensitive view, and a hybrid capability approach fare against this list, keeping the weight on the criteria and their motivating cases rather than on the scoring.
