A podcast on rational belief summarized Kyburg's lottery paradox and Makinson's preface paradox as two manifestations of a single failure: rational belief violates deductive closure. According to the host, Kyburg demonstrated that if one accepts a high-probability threshold for rational belief—say, 0.99—then in a fair 1000-ticket lottery one is compelled to believe of each ticket that it will not win. The conjunction of these beliefs entails that no ticket wins, which contradicts the known fact that one must. The host then presented Makinson's preface case as structurally identical: a careful author can rationally believe each individual claim in her book while also rationally believing, in light of past experience, that the book contains at least one error. The host concluded that contemporary epistemology has largely settled the matter by abandoning closure: rational agents are not required to combine their beliefs into logically consistent sets, and the paradoxes thereby reveal that classical logic is an inappropriate model for human reasoning. He mentioned, only briefly, that some philosophers resist this move. I am inclined to think the summary captures the mainstream position, but I am unsure whether it overstates the agreement or conflates distinct problems. Is this a fair characterization?
