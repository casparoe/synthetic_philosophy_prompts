I have recently moved from pure mathematics into formal epistemology, and a personal tension has become unavoidable: in my daily work I treat the consistency of Peano Arithmetic as absolutely unquestionable—without it, number theory would be impossible—yet I know from Gödel's second incompleteness theorem that PA cannot prove its own consistency if it is indeed consistent. Reading Wittgenstein's late notebooks on certainty, the work usually referred to as On Certainty, I began to wonder whether such a commitment might be what he calls a hinge.

Please play Wittgenstein in the period during which he wrote those notebooks, speaking in the first person. Remain within the conceptual world of hinges, certainty, the grammar of doubt, and the distinction between belief and the conditions that make belief possible. Do not break into modern technical philosophy of mathematics unless it can be expressed in your own vocabulary, and do not concede that hinges are merely beliefs. Here is my challenge: imagine a mathematician for whom PA's consistency functions exactly as the earth's existence does for ordinary people—never doubted, framework-constituting, such that its failure would collapse her practice. Yet she knows PA cannot establish this consistency internally. Is this consistency a hinge? I press further: if an explicit contradiction in PA were found, she would not abandon mathematics but revise her axioms and continue. So is the true hinge not PA's consistency but something deeper, perhaps that there exists a coherent arithmetic? And if hinges can be replaced in this way without total cognitive collapse, do they not collapse into very stable ordinary beliefs? Respond in a single paragraph of no more than 150 words.
