As a software engineer with no formal background in philosophy, I have noticed a recurring pattern in large-scale technical systems that I can describe but not name precisely, and I would like to know whether it corresponds to an established philosophical concept—particularly in relation to Hannah Arendt’s views on Eichmann and the banality of evil.

Consider two concrete situations. First, a product team optimises a recommendation engine: one engineer improves click-through rates on notifications, another refines a ranking model, a third reduces latency in the feed. Each change is locally justified by assigned metrics and peer review; collectively they produce behavioural patterns that cause measurable harm to users, yet no individual intended this outcome, and no single meeting approved it. Second, a content-moderation pipeline is built in discrete layers—automated keyword filtering, classifier scoring, and human review governed by throughput quotas. A harmful item passes through not because any reviewer endorsed it, but because the false-negative rates of successive layers compound in a way that no engineer modelled, and each person only validated their own component.

What strikes me is not merely that people fail to think, but that there is no identifiable locus of decision. The harm is not ordered from above, nor is it the result of one person’s negligence; it emerges from an architecture of locally rational choices in which no node possesses the full causal picture. I can see how this resembles Arendt’s notion of the banality of evil—thoughtlessness, absence of malicious intent, bureaucratic distance—but her case centres on an individual, Eichmann, operating within a hierarchical command structure. My examples seem structurally different: the thoughtlessness is distributed across roles, there is no central authority issuing orders, and the danger lies in the interaction of correct procedures rather than in a single agent’s failure to judge.

Does this specific configuration—distributed, architecture-driven harm arising without malicious intent, without a central decision-maker, and through the accumulation of locally defensible technical choices—have an established philosophical name? How exactly should it be distinguished from Arendt’s original formulation, and from more familiar collective-action or responsibility-gap problems? If there is no precise term, please coin a concept with a clear definition and explicit boundary conditions: for instance, it should exclude cases where at least one actor intended the harm, and cases where a single identifiable decision-maker was merely ignorant of consequences. I ask for the answer in British English, and I do not want a bibliography, reading list, or citations—only the conceptual carving itself.
