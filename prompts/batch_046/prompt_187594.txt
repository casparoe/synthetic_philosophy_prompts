For roughly four decades the exchange over epistemic closure within Nozick's tracking framework has remained frozen in its original posture: tracking theorists treat the failure of closure in skeptical entailments as a feature that distinguishes knowledge from mere true belief, while closure defenders treat the same result as a reductio ad absurdum. I am less interested in another adjudication of the brain-in-vat case than in a diagnosis of why nothing moves. Is the disagreement genuinely substantive—about whether counterfactual sensitivity is constitutive of knowledge—or is it partly verbal, with knowledge serving different theoretical functions on each side? More specifically, might a hidden dispute about the metric of modal proximity be doing the work, so that tracking conditions are applied with incompatible standards for what counts as a nearby world? My own suspicion is that the stalemate conceals a deeper divergence about the unit of epistemic evaluation: tracking assesses individual beliefs in isolation, whereas closure presupposes a holistic coherence constraint on belief systems, with epistemic risk being measured atomistically in the one framework and distributively in the other. Using what each side actually says about sensitivity, adherence, and the role of skeptical hypotheses, diagnose whether this is a verbal dispute, a substantive disagreement about modal logic, or an ill-posed question that conflates two explanatory projects. And tell me honestly what, if anything, would constitute progress here—a formal result on the logic of tracking across entailments, a principled distinction between deductive and empirical closure, or an argument that no single concept of knowledge should be expected to satisfy both conditions simultaneously.
