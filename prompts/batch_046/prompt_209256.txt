In a working group on AI safety, two participants—call them A and B—fell into a sustained disagreement about whether "the alignment problem" denotes a single problem or an irreducible family. A argued that alignment is fundamentally one problem: maintaining identity between the system's effective objective (what its behavior actually optimizes) and the designer's intended objective across gains in capability, and that apparent sub-problems—reward hacking, sycophancy, deceptive alignment, goal misgeneralization—are merely different failure modes of this single identity relation breaking down. B countered that this framing illegitimately collapses three structurally distinct issues: (1) the specification problem (how to formally encode human intent that was never designed for formalization), (2) the robustness problem (how to preserve whatever specification was achieved under distribution shift and recursive self-modification), and (3) the authority problem (how to ensure the system continues to defer to human judgment about which objectives should be pursued, rather than locking in an initial objective and ignoring attempts to revise it). B added that solving (1) does not touch (3): a system could possess a perfectly specified, stable objective that it pursues relentlessly while disregarding human efforts to redirect it, which B called "aligned but ungovernable." A replied that B's authority problem is just robustness applied to the meta-level—if the system's objective includes a deference clause, then preserving that clause is part of preserving the objective, so it remains the single identity problem. B said this reply assumes precisely what is at issue, because any internalized deference is itself subject to the same robustness failures, whereas true governance requires an architectural separation between the optimizer and the objective-setter that no monolithic, single-objective framing can capture. They turned to me to referee, and I genuinely could not. Please adjudicate move by move: rate each of the following claims—(i) that all alignment failure modes reduce to one broken identity relation between intended and effective objective; (ii) that specification, robustness, and authority are structurally distinct rather than facets of one issue; (iii) that the authority problem can be reduced to robustness at the meta-level via an internalized deference clause; (iv) that internalized deference collapses back into the single-problem framework; and (v) that genuine governance requires an architectural separation between optimizer and objective-setter that a unified framing obscures—on a scale from clearly mistaken to clearly right, with brief justification for each rating. Then say which party is more right overall, and identify what both are missing, particularly regarding whether there is a hidden empirical premise about the feasibility of maintaining such an architectural separation in highly capable, self-modifying systems.
