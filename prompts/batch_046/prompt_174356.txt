A philosopher I am debating with offers a botanical analogy for the expanding circle of moral concern: she likens the growth of moral consideration to the development of a mature tree's root system, in which the trunk and primary roots—representing obligations to kin and compatriots—remain structurally central and continue to command the bulk of metabolic resources, while finer root hairs extend outward into foreign soil, representing concern for distant strangers, future generations, and nonhuman animals. She deploys this image to license the claim that authentic moral progress is always additive: extending concern to new groups never requires reallocating resources away from the core, because the tree's overall biomass simply grows. Please set out the correspondence explicitly—trunk, root hairs, nutrient flow, soil boundary, and whatever else is doing argumentative work—then identify the disanalogies that bear specifically on her conclusion. In particular, examine whether tree roots actually grow without competing for resources with the trunk, whether the "soil" of moral motivation behaves like physical substrate, and whether the analogy quietly imports a picture of moral concern as centrally administered and zero-sum in ways that distort the psychology and politics of obligation. Does her conclusion—that expanding the circle is necessarily costless to insiders—survive once these mismatches are on the table? If not, what alternative analogy, biological or physical, would better represent the actual trade-offs involved in widening moral consideration?
