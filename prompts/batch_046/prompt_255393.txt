I have been working through a long-running disagreement in my philosophy of science reading group about what are sometimes called Darwin's and Turing's strange inversions of reasoning — the move by which Darwin explains the appearance of biological design through blind natural selection, and Turing explains the appearance of intelligent behavior through mechanistic computation, in each case apparently dissolving a need for comprehension or understanding in the mechanism that produces the competence. For months we have stalled over whether these two inversions are genuinely structurally parallel or whether comparing them hides a category error. One camp holds that both are the same explanatory strategy: they preserve the phenomenon — design, intelligence — while offering a deflationary account of its origin, showing that competence can exist without comprehension in the producing system. The opposing camp argues that the cases diverge sharply because natural selection involves no representational rules at all, whereas computation is constituted by syntactic rules that require an interpreting agent, so Darwin genuinely eliminates teleology while Turing only relocates it into the programmer and the machine's syntax. Both sides agree on the historical details and on the formal structures involved; neither has persuaded the other.

My working hypothesis is that this is not a factual disagreement about biology or computer science, but a hidden dispute about what counts as a genuine explanation — specifically, whether an explanation must eliminate teleological vocabulary entirely or merely reduce it to a lower-level mechanism. But I am unsure whether we are instead asking different questions under one heading — one about whether intelligence is a natural kind susceptible to mechanistic reduction, the other about whether evolutionary processes are genuinely rule-governed — or whether the impasse reveals that the very framework of competence without comprehension is ill-posed. I want a diagnosis supported by what each side actually commits to: is the disagreement verbal, substantive, or a collision of unstated intuitions about where understanding must reside? And given that diagnosis, what would actually constitute progress — a formal distinction that separates the biological from the computational case, a conceptual clarification of explanation, or an admission that no single answer applies to both?
