Consider a modified version of Glaucon's challenge: suppose an agent acquires a mechanism analogous to the ring of Gyges, but instead of uniform invisibility it produces an epistemic asymmetry—the agent can observe every hidden moral failure of others, yet remains imperceptible to them in ninety-nine percent of interactions, with only a one-percent tail risk of total exposure and catastrophic social ruin. I want your credence, on a scale from 0 to 1, that such an agent still has decisive rational grounds to adhere to moral constraints rather than defect systematically. Decompose this into at least three independent components: first, the probability that moral norms carry non-instrumental rational weight for an agent irrespective of detection probability; second, the probability that standard evolutionary and game-theoretic reputation mechanisms actually fail under conditions of near-complete but not absolute invisibility, once internal self-monitoring and the tail-risk of exposure are factored in; third, the probability that the concept of rationality is sufficiently determinate in this scenario that there is a fact of the matter about the correct choice. Assign each component a rough numerical value with a one-line justification, state your aggregation rule, combine them into an overall credence, and identify which component is doing the most work in your final number. Finally, specify what kind of empirical result in moral psychology or formal result in decision theory would most substantially shift your view.
