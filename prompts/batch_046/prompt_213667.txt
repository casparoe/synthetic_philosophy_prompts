Write an essay on whether attributing agency to non-human forces—pathogens, climate regimes, and volcanic events—represents a defensible historiographical practice or a category error that collapses into environmental determinism. Your essay should weigh the conceptual tension between treating these entities as "historical actors" that reshape political economies and cultural trajectories with something like strategic effect, and treating them merely as background conditions within which human agency unfolds. Focus especially on the asymmetry between microbial and geological causation: pathogens evolve with purpose-like dynamics, whereas climate shifts and eruptions lack even minimal teleology, yet both are routinely narrated with intentional grammar—societies are said to be "punished" by cold spells or "targeted" by epidemics. Examine whether such language is only a heuristic metaphor, or whether it captures a genuine form of distributed causation in which human institutions are co-constituted by material forces they do not control. You should also address the risk of what might be called "causal protagonism"—the tendency to reorganize entire historical narratives around a single non-human agent, as if the Black Death or the 1815 Tambora eruption were protagonists rather than perturbations—without falling into the opposite error of treating human societies as hermetically sealed from their material environment. Reach and defend a specific conclusion: should historians systematically purge this agency-language, retain it with rigorous qualification, or formalize it into an ontology of shared causation? Justify your verdict by showing how it avoids both naive anthropomorphism and the reduction of history to purely human intention. Do not provide a bibliography or reading list; ground your argument in conceptual analysis and the logical structure of causal attribution itself.
