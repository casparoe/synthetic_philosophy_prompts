Please adopt the persona of a senior decision-theorist and philosopher of rationality advising a practical institution. Before constructing the deliverable, begin with a brief brainstorming section in which you identify the central tension between translating a mathematical betting principle into bureaucratic language and preserving its normative force, and flag the specific junctures—epistemic, ethical, and institutional—where judgement cannot be replaced by calculation.

You advise the quarterly allocation panel of a regional public-health preparedness authority. Every quarter the panel receives between three and five competing proposals for preemptive expenditure—vaccination stockpiles, surveillance upgrades, infrastructure hardening, or rapid-response protocols—each accompanied by an estimated probability that the threat will materialise within the quarter, a quantified welfare payoff (expressed in quality-adjusted life-years or equivalent protective value) should the threat occur, and a direct cost that depletes the authority's reserve fund. The panel must reach a decision on each proposal within thirty minutes, using only arithmetic and percentage calculations, with no access to external literature or computational tools. Any commitment exceeding twenty per cent of the quarterly reserve requires explicit sign-off by the panel chair. The members are experienced epidemiologists and senior administrators, not mathematicians.

The authority wishes to ground its allocations in the logic of Kelly betting combined with geometric rationality. That is, each quarterly decision should be treated not as an isolated exercise in expected-value maximisation, but as one round in a sequential betting game in which the authority's "bankroll" is its cumulative capacity to protect welfare over successive quarters. The rational provisional allocation should approximate the Kelly fraction—edge divided by odds—adjusted for partial information loss and for correlations between simultaneous proposals, with the explicit objective of maximising the geometric mean of long-run welfare outcomes and avoiding catastrophic depletion of the reserve. Where probabilities are ambiguous or payoffs incommensurable, the procedure must specify whether to halt, reduce the fraction, or hand the decision to values-based deliberation.

Your task is to design a reusable decision procedure—presented as an ordered checklist with explicit decision nodes, stopping conditions, arithmetic rules, and hand-off points—that any panel member could apply within the thirty-minute constraint. The procedure must include:

1. The ordered sequence of questions to ask of any proposal (probability estimate, payoff magnitude, direct cost, correlation with other active commitments, effect on reserve if the threat does not materialise).
2. A simplified arithmetic formula for computing the provisional Kelly fraction, expressed in terms the panel can calculate by hand.
3. Clear stopping conditions that return "do not fund" or "fund at minimal monitoring level only".
4. The condition that triggers the chair's sign-off (commitment exceeding twenty per cent of reserve) and the intermediate check for ruin risk (whether the allocation, combined with existing commitments, threatens to reduce the reserve below the survival threshold for other essential measures).
5. Explicit hand-off points marked "values judgement required" rather than "calculate further"—particularly where treating cumulative lives protected as a multiplicative bankroll conflicts with deontological or distributive concerns, or where correlation between proposals makes independent Kelly fractions collectively irrational.
6. A specific rule for handling correlated proposals (e.g., two measures targeting the same pathogen or watershed).

Make the procedure honest about its misfires: for instance, when probability inputs are speculative, when payoffs mix lives and monetary costs without a common metric, or when geometric optimisation recommends crowding out all other measures.

Then apply the procedure to three concrete proposals:

A. A rapid vaccination campaign against a novel respiratory pathogen: estimated 15 per cent outbreak probability; success prevents approximately 400 quality-adjusted life-years of harm; cost equals 12 per cent of reserve; no correlation with other active commitments.

B. A water-treatment surveillance expansion for a regional watershed: 60 per cent probability of detecting contamination before serious harm; success saves approximately 50 quality-adjusted life-years and avoids a substantial regulatory penalty; cost equals 25 per cent of reserve; highly correlated with an existing monitoring programme already covering the same watershed.

C. A stockpile of antiviral drugs for a low-probability (5 per cent), high-mortality emerging pathogen: the Kelly calculation yields a provisional fraction of 35 per cent of reserve because the edge-to-odds ratio is extreme; however, committing at this level would exhaust the reserve and preclude all other allocations for two quarters, and several panel members object that framing potential lives as units in a geometric growth function is morally unacceptable. Show exactly what the procedure recommends, identify the point at which it hands to values judgement, and present the answer that the chair is likely to reject—without resolving the conflict in the procedure's favour.

Finally, conclude with a precise statement of what this procedure cannot do: whether it can substitute for democratic deliberation, resolve moral objections to quantifying welfare multiplicatively, or produce meaningful fractions when all probability inputs are little more than informed guesswork. Do not provide bibliographies, citations, or reading lists. Write in clear, formal British English.
