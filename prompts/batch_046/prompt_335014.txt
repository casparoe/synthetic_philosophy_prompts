I am delivering the opening remarks this Friday at the retirement symposium for Dr. Elena Vasquez, who spent forty years at the intersection of philosophy of action and behavioral public health. The room is split: her colleagues in decision theory and clinical psychology know the literature on intertemporal choice inside out, while her family, former patients from recovery programs, and university administrators are present and have no technical background. The symposium theme is "The Divided Agent."

Recently, after a minor health scare, I found myself unable to adhere to a physician's straightforward dietary protocol despite fully endorsing it—an experience that made Aristotle's problem of akrasia and George Ainslie's picoeconomic framing of hyperbolic discounting suddenly concrete rather than abstract. I want a ten-minute address that uses this lived tension—between long-run endorsement and steep present-biased preference, between knowing and doing—as its structural spine. The speech must honor Vasquez's insistence that weakness of will is a structural feature of intertemporal bargaining, not an informational deficit that better data can cure; her family should grasp why that distinction mattered for her public-health work, and her colleagues should recognize that I understand the technical terrain (present-biased preferences, intertemporal bargaining, the boundary between weakness of will and compulsion) without hearing a lecture or a bibliography. Please write this in American English, keep it accessible to the non-specialists without simplifying away the philosophy, and do not include citations, reading lists, or references to specific papers by title or date.
