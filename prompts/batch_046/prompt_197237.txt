Question: Some historians argue presentism is unavoidable because we can't drop our own moral vocabulary. Others say historical empathy lets us judge past actors strictly on their own terms. Is there a stable middle position—say, calibrating our normative standards to the epistemic conditions available at the time rather than importing modern morality? Use an example.

Student answer: There's a workable middle ground, sometimes called calibrated presentism. You keep your normative framework but restrict its application to the information environment the actor actually inhabited. Take a 19th-century factory owner who kept workers in dangerous conditions. You don't excuse him just because safety regulations didn't exist—he had eyes and could see injuries. But you do suspend judgments that depend on concepts he couldn't possess, like systemic structural violence or unconscious bias. Historical empathy isn't evaluation; it's reconstructing the actor's reasoning from inside, so it's separate from judgment. So the historian applies modern values but only through the filter of what was epistemically available—like using the same map with roads that hadn't been built yet erased. This avoids pure presentism because we aren't importing modern ethics, only adjusting the resolution.
