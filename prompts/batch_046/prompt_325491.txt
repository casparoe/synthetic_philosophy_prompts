Begin with the shared premise that these animals possess nociceptive capacity, then branch through four concrete questions: first, whether moral status requires phenomenal consciousness or can rest on integrated stress-physiology alone; second, whether welfare duties scale continuously with cognitive complexity or operate as a binary threshold; third, whether economic feasibility and food-security demands can diminish, though not eliminate, welfare obligations; and fourth, whether preventing species-typical behaviors—burrowing, social hierarchy formation, shelter-seeking—counts as a distinct harm even when nociception is minimized. Note any pair of leaves that your tree cannot separate for practical policy purposes, and flag at least one logically possible branch combination that seems unoccupied—diagnosing whether it is incoherent, deeply unattractive, or merely unexplored. The goal is to classify people by what they actually believe about crustacean welfare rather than by which school they cite.
