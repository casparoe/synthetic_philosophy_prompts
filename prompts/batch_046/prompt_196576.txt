I've been drafting a short essay for a rationalist forum on what I'm calling the cascade problem in deontological ethics—specifically, whether Ross's paradox (the disjunctive-obligation derivation: if one ought to mail a letter, it seems one ought to mail it or burn it, which then licenses burning it if one fails to mail) is best read as a logical glitch or as an unintended feature of how prohibitions propagate through hierarchical decision structures. The niche injection I want to test is modeling this through an analogy from safety engineering: treating moral constraints like non-depletable risk budgets that, when breached at one node, cascade downward and generate derived prohibitions faster than actions can be evaluated. I argue that this makes deontology look less like a list of atomic rules and more like a distributed system with emergent lockouts, which might explain why deontologists resist consequentialist aggregation without being able to articulate the mechanism.

The outline I'm working from runs like this:

1. Reconstruct Ross's paradox precisely, distinguishing the formal derivation (from O(A) to O(A ∨ B)) from the intuitive recoil.
2. Introduce the cascade model: a constraint C at level L prohibits not only actions that violate C directly, but also any action that makes C's violation unavoidable downstream, with the cascade stopping only at actions genuinely independent of C.
3. Apply this to a concrete case: a clinician with a deontological prohibition on killing who must choose between withholding a lethal dose (allowing death) and administering it (causing death). The cascade model predicts that both paths eventually trigger derived prohibitions, leaving the clinician in a deontic deadlock that mirrors the paradox.
4. Note the cost: the model saves deontology from being trivially falsified by catastrophic outcomes, but only by making the theory computationally intractable for any agent with bounded reasoning.
5. Conclude tentatively: the paradox of deontology is not that it permits too much, but that its internal consistency requires a form of logical omniscience that real agents—human or artificial—cannot possess.

I'm asking for targeted comments on three points:

- Is the cascade analogy to engineering safety budgets a legitimate conceptual translation, or does it smuggle in consequentialist reasoning about risk trade-offs that deontologists explicitly reject?
- In the clinical case, does the model misrepresent the standard deontological distinction between killing and letting die, or does it correctly reveal that the distinction collapses under cascade pressure?
- The essay deliberately avoids citing any particular formal deontic logic (no neighborhood semantics, no explicit modal operators). Is that omission a strength for accessibility, or does it leave the argument too vague to be evaluated?

I'd particularly value pushback from anyone who's tried to formalize deontic reasoning for AI alignment or institutional decision theory. The intended audience is people comfortable with analytic philosophy but who also think about bounded rationality and system design. I don't need citations, a bibliography, or reading suggestions—I want to know whether the conceptual architecture holds together, where it collapses, and whether the cascade framing actually illuminates the paradox or just relocates the mystery. If you see a cleaner way to distinguish the formal derivation from its intuitive rejection without importing hidden premises, I'd like to hear it.
