If hyperbolic discounting describes intertemporal preference reversal not as an error but as locally rational updating—so that the discount weights at t=1 genuinely represent the agent's best evaluation given her temporal proximity to the reward—then in what sense can an agent at t=1 still be said to suffer from Aristotelian akrasia? The standard philosophical analysis requires that the agent act against a judgment she continues to endorse as best; yet if the planning-stage judgment at t=0 has been superseded by an equally authoritative present evaluation, there appears to be no stable cross-temporal standard of practical reason against which to measure failure. Does this mean that every case of hyperbolic reversal dissolves weakness of will into diachronic preference change, leaving the normative concept of akrasia applicable only to synchronic conflicts that hyperbolic models do not capture? Or is there a defensible account of diachronic authority—perhaps drawing on the planner-doer framework—under which the long-range judgment retains normative priority even when the agent's present discount rate treats it as irrelevant?
