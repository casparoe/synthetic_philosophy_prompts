I am moderating a colloquium Q&A with a philosopher who defends the view that certain ant colonies can possess a genuinely collective mind, but only when their behavioral dynamics instantiate what she calls the Ant Fugue: a contrapuntal, non-hierarchical organization of task-switching in which no individual ant carries the thematic material, yet the colony-level pattern exhibits recursive self-modification through pheromonal feedback loops that she takes to be functionally equivalent to self-reference. Central to her position is the Aunt Hillary thought experiment: an idealized observer positioned at the colony boundary who interacts exclusively with emergent colony-level outputs—foraging geometries, defensive formations, nest restructuring—and who, lacking any access to internal ant-level states, rationally attributes unitary beliefs and intentions to the colony as a single agent. The defender claims this attribution is not merely pragmatic but epistemically warranted, provided the colony’s internal dynamics meet the integration conditions specified by the fugue structure.

Please draft eight to ten interview questions that go well beyond asking her to restate the Ant Fugue thesis or summarize Aunt Hillary. I want at least one question on each of the following: (i) whether the contrapuntal organization requires a specific temporal scale or population density, and what happens to the mind-attribution if the colony is disrupted and reassembled with different individuals within that scale; (ii) whether Aunt Hillary’s inability to access internal states makes her attribution structurally analogous to other-minds skepticism, and if so, whether the integration conditions are meant to solve that problem or merely sidestep it; (iii) how her view differs from standard distributed-cognition or group-mind accounts, and specifically what she must deny about functionalist criteria for mentality to maintain that fugue structure alone is sufficient; and (iv) the moral-status implication—if a colony meeting the fugue conditions genuinely has beliefs, does it also have interests generating direct moral obligations, or is mental attribution compatible with treating the colony as instrumentally valuable but non-moral?

For each of these four, include a follow-up keyed to the answer she is most likely to give: for instance, if she answers that temporal scale is necessary but population identity is not, press her on whether that commits her to substrate-independent persistence that conflicts with biological intuition; if she answers that Aunt Hillary solves other-minds skepticism by substituting functional integration for internal access, ask what evidence could in principle defeat her attribution; if she distinguishes her view by rejecting functionalism about individual neurons, ask whether that rejection generalizes to reject functionalism about individual ants; and if she denies direct moral obligations, ask whether her denial relies on a distinction between cognitive integration and sentience that her own criteria do not clearly support. Conclude with one final question that forces her to specify what empirical or conceptual result would cause her to abandon the claim that an ant colony can be a mind, rather than merely to refine the conditions of the Ant Fugue.
