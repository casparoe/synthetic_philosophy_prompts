As a novelist preparing a short essay for a literary quarterly, I have been working through an analogy between the ontology of fictional characters and debates in philosophy of science concerning emergence and reduction. My argument runs as follows. A character’s enduring moral temperament—say, a particular, irreducible quality of regret—supervenes on the totality of sentences in which she appears, yet it is not reducible to any single description, nor to their mere conjunction considered apart from narrative context. I then press what I call the problem of negative space: the scenes that are implied but never written appear to function as a form of downward causation, constraining which actions in the written text remain coherent with the whole. I compare this to scientific debates over whether macro-level properties—biological, mental, or social—can causally influence the micro-level constituents on which they depend. I acknowledge the obvious disanalogy, that in fiction the macro-level structure is itself authored, but I ask whether that disanalogy undermines the comparison or merely shows that downward causation takes a different form in constructed worlds. Before I submit the draft, I would be grateful for your comments—specifically, on whether I have mischaracterized the scientific debate about downward causation; on whether treating narrative gaps as causally active is anything more than an extended metaphor; and on whether the essay’s philosophical claims remain legible and interesting to a reader who cares more about literature than about supervenience.
