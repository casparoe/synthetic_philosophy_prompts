As a retired experimental physicist who spent three decades studying phase transitions in condensed matter, I have recently begun auditing graduate philosophy seminars to remedy the unsystematic reading I pursued in my younger years. This semester I am sitting in on a metaethics course, and because my scientific training leaves me attentive to logical structure though still unfamiliar with the finer dialectical distinctions, the instructor has asked me to help grade one written examination. I need a concrete point-by-point assessment rather than a general impression—specifically, a grade together with precise comments justifying it—without any bibliography, reading list, or request for citations.

The examination asked students to explain Jonathan Dancy's claim that moral reasons are holistic, to contrast particularism with the generalist position associated with philosophers such as McKeever and Ridge, and to assess whether particularism necessarily denies that cruelty has an invariant moral valence. A student submitted the following answer: "Moral particularism, as Jonathan Dancy develops it, rests on the holism of reasons: whether a feature counts as a reason for or against an action depends entirely on the context in which it appears. For instance, the fact that an experience is pleasant might support performing an action in one case, yet oppose it in another, if other contextual features alter its moral significance. From this, particularists deny that there are true moral principles of the form 'If feature F is present, then action A is required,' since no feature carries a fixed moral weight across all situations. This stands in sharp contrast to moral generalism, which holds that moral reasoning must be governed by universal, exceptionless principles that apply identically in every possible case; defenders such as McKeever and Ridge maintain that without such absolute rules, moral judgment collapses into arbitrariness. Because particularists reject all universal principles, they are also committed to denying that any feature has an invariant moral valence. Cruelty, for example, cannot consistently be said to be always wrong, since in some conceivable context—perhaps one in which an act of cruelty prevents a far greater cruelty—the very feature of being cruel might function as a reason in favor of the action. Consequently, particularism slides into a form of moral relativism in which there are no cross-contextual moral truths, and disagreement about cruelty amounts merely to a difference in contextual framing rather than to a substantive moral error."

Please evaluate this answer with a specific grade and point-by-point comments. In particular, check whether the account of holism is accurate; whether the contrast with generalism misrepresents the opponent by treating defeasible, prima facie principles as exceptionless absolute rules; whether the claim that particularists must deny any invariant negative valence to cruelty actually follows from the rejection of moral principles; and whether the inference from context-dependency to moral relativism is valid. Deliver the assessment as an anonymous grader would, without signaling your own prior opinion of the answer, and avoid requesting any further sources or citations.
