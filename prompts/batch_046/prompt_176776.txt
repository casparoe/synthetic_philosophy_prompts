I've been reading Browning and Goldhagen for a forum thread about moral psychology and I keep circling back to one concrete case that feels like it should adjudicate between them, or at least complicate teh picture. The episode is Reserve Police Battalion 101 at Józefów in July 1942: middle-aged German policemen, mostly working-class and not SS fanatics, ordered to round up and shoot roughly 1,500 Jewish women, children, and elderly in a Polish village. A small minority stepped out and refused without career punishment; the rest complied, then over the following months the same unit participated in killings numbering in the tens of thousands and in large-scale deportations, with less direct coercion and apparently more routine efficiency. I'm trying to map this onto Browning's situationist account — peer pressure, authority, incremental brutalization, careerist self-interest turning ordinary men into killers — versus Goldhagen's cultural account of deep, specifically German eliminationist antisemitism that made them willing executioners who needed little pushing.

But I want to inject something that isn't just the standard debate replayed. There's this organizational-psychology idea about institutional normalization of deviance: once a group crosses a moral threshold without external punishment, internal norms shift so that the deviant behavior becomes the new baseline, which lowers the cognitive cost of repeating it and makes refusal look like social deviance rather than moral virtue. Can you analyze the battalion's trajectory — especially the shift from coerced participation in Józefów to more self-directed violence later — through both Browning and Goldhagen, but specifically using that normalization lens? For each framework, say what it predicts about who had standing to resist, whether refusal should have gotten easier or harder over time, and what counts as the key evidence. Then tell me whether the normalization pattern fits one framework better, reveals an anomaly for both, or points to a gap — maybe that situationist pressure created an initial compliance culture that then became self-sustaining in ways neither author fully captures. Keep the mapping disciplined: identify the players, the options, and what the theory actually says versus where it's silent.
