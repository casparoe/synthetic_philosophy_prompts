I'm a doctoral student in philosophy of history working at the intersection of genocide studies and the philosophy of modernity, and I'm trying to fix a three-year research agenda. I need to decide where the conceptual labor is actually unfinished rather than where debates have become repetitive.

The terrain I'm navigating has three established layers. First, the legacy of the Historikerstreit: the dispute over whether the Holocaust is historically unique, usually framed between those who treat its combination of industrial method, total ideological intent, and bureaucratic administration as constituting a singular event, and those who see uniqueness-claims as barriers to comparative historiography. Second, Zygmunt Bauman's thesis that the Holocaust was not a regression from modern rationality but one of its possible outputs, enabled by bureaucratic distance, functional division of labor, and instrumental reason. Third, I'm developing a framing I call the bureaucratic-culpability extension problem: if Bauman is right that modern administrative structures enabled the Holocaust, then comparative study of other state-administered mass harms—colonial famine regimes, forced-labor architectures, engineered displacement—does not diminish the Holocaust's significance but extends the indictment of modern rational-bureaucratic order. The open issue is whether this extension collapses the moral distinction between genocide and other modern catastrophes, or instead reveals a hidden structural continuity that comparative analysis should expose.

Within this terrain, four questions are competing for the center of my dissertation:

1. Can Bauman's modernity thesis survive rigorous historical counter-testing—identifying modern bureaucratic societies that possessed the structural conditions he identifies but did not produce intentional group destruction—without reducing the thesis to a trivial claim about organizational capacity?

2. Is the concept of uniqueness in the Historikerstreit best analyzed as a metaphysical claim about event ontology, a methodological claim about the limits of comparison, or a normative claim about exceptional moral status—and can these dimensions be separated cleanly?

3. Is the bureaucratic-culpability extension problem genuinely a new conceptual problem, or is it merely a vocabulary shift of the old uniqueness-versus-comparability debate? Crucially, does resolving it alter how comparative genocide law should be designed, or does it leave legal categories untouched?

4. Which empirical-historical cases outside the Holocaust serve as the strongest tests for whether modern bureaucratic rationalization is a necessary or merely contingent feature of large-scale intentional group destruction?

Please rank these four in terms of which should occupy the core years of a dissertation in this area. Defend the ranking with explicit criteria: tractability given philosophical-historical methods; how many other open questions depend on resolving this one first; whether the question is genuinely unsettled or largely settled with only rhetorical disagreement remaining; and what theoretical or practical payoff justifies the investment. Identify the question you consider most overrated in current literature, and specify what finding or argument would change your ranking. I am not asking for a bibliography, citation list, or survey; I want a defended agenda for where the unfinished conceptual labor actually lies.
