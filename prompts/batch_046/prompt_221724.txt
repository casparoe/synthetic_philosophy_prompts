In my advanced high-school seminar on international ethics, we have spent the last two months debating whether humanitarian intervention that results in regime change—specifically Kosovo in 1999, Iraq in 2003, and Libya in 2011—can ever be morally justified when the initial mandate is civilian protection rather than government replacement. The discussion has stalled completely. One group argues that Kosovo and Libya show regime change can be an unintended but acceptable consequence of stopping mass atrocities, provided the intervention is multilateral and the post-conflict transition eventually yields representative institutions. The opposing group insists that Iraq reveals an irreparable structural pattern: once regime change occurs, the intervening powers acquire long-term incentives to maintain influence through security-sector dependency and economic conditionalities, which undermines the very self-determination that was supposed to justify the action in the first place. Both sides cite the same three cases, both agree that civilian suffering should be minimized, and neither has shifted position since October.

My working hypothesis is that the disagreement is not mainly empirical—since we have all read the same post-conflict assessments—but rather turns on a hidden normative distinction I am calling retroactive legitimation: whether an intervention can become morally sound only if the affected population, after the fact, consolidates democratic institutions capable of having authorized the intervention ex ante, and whether the presence of open-ended security and economic dependencies makes such consolidation impossible by definition. But I am unsure whether this framing actually captures the stall, or whether the two sides are simply answering different questions—one about the permissibility of entering, the other about the obligations of exiting—while using identical vocabulary.

Can you diagnose the actual structure of this disagreement? Is it verbal (centered on the meaning of humanitarian), substantive but obscured (about the moral weight of sovereignty versus suffering), or a case of two groups using the same case studies to pursue different theoretical projects?
