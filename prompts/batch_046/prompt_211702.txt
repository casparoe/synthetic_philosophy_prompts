I work as a software engineer and have no formal background in philosophy. In debates about whether monuments to figures such as Jefferson, Churchill, or Columbus ought to remain, I repeatedly hear the refrain: 'You cannot judge a man by the standards of a different era.' It is deployed both to dismiss moral criticism of slave-owning or imperial conduct and to insist that historical complexity must be preserved. Please separate the precise claims compressed into that sentence. Distinguish: (a) the descriptive observation that normative beliefs change over time; (b) the normative claim that present-day observers lack legitimate standing to condemn past actors; (c) the causal thesis that these individuals could not have behaved differently given their socialisation; and (d) the meta-ethical thesis that moral truths are themselves relative to historical epochs. For each, indicate whether it amounts to a platitude, a defensible substantive claim, or an outright falsehood. Furthermore, analogising to how we treat deprecated software APIs—acknowledging that a legacy protocol had vulnerabilities unknown to its designers, without thereby licensing its present use—clarify which of the above readings actually supplies a normative shield against removing monuments or revising judgement, and which merely describes historical context. Lay out the interpretations as a menu with verdicts rather than selecting a single one, and specify which reading is typically being invoked when the phrase is used to terminate an argument.
