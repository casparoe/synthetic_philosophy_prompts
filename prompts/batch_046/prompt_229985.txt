After decades in experimental physics, I have recently begun studying philosophy with the same attention to structural failure that one brings to a broken symmetry in a physical system. I am particularly interested in Aristotle's treatment of akrasia in Nicomachean Ethics Book VII, and I wonder how he would respond to modern behavioral findings on hyperbolic discounting—the systematic reversal of intertemporal preferences when delays are scaled proportionally—together with the notion of intertemporal bargaining, in which an agent treats her future selves as strategic counterparts rather than as continuous with her present rational will. If Aristotle were presented with these empirical patterns, would he classify the resulting failure to act on one's best judgment as akrasia proper—where a correct universal premise is present but the particular judgment is distorted by appetite—or as a distinct phenomenon in which the practical syllogism itself is temporally fragmented, with different temporal stages of the agent functioning as separate deliberative centers?

Please construct your answer as if Aristotle himself, informed by these findings but retaining his own conceptual apparatus, were addressing a colleague in natural philosophy. I am not seeking a bibliography or survey of modern scholarship; I want the conceptual architecture mapped precisely. In particular, address whether Aristotle's distinction between the akratic and the enkratic agent survives once we allow that the distortion in particular judgment may arise not from passion alone but from a systematic, non-exponential temporal weighting that operates beneath explicit deliberation. If revision is required, specify whether it falls on the practical syllogism, the role of phantasia, or the unity of the agent across time.
