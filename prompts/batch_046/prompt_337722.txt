I've been trying to work out for myself when it is rational to hold a belief even though I know that, taken together, my beliefs cannot all be correct. I have no formal training in philosophy, so I am going purely by my gut on concrete cases, and I am hoping there is a coherent principle behind them.

Here are my verdicts. First, the lottery: if I hold one ticket in a fair draw of five hundred thousand, I think it is perfectly rational for me to believe I will not win, and I do not think I am required to suspend judgment.

Second, the preface: I have written a long report containing about one hundred and fifty specific factual claims, each of which I have checked carefully. I think it is rational for me to believe each claim is true. At the same time, I think it is also rational for me to believe that there is very likely at least one mistake hidden somewhere in the report. These two attitudes do not feel contradictory to me.

Third, a case that mixes the two structures. Imagine a diagnostic machine that detects a rare condition with 99.9 percent accuracy. It tests one thousand people, all negative. For each individual, I think it is rational to believe they are healthy. But I also think it is rational to believe that about one person in the group actually has the condition, despite the negative result. Now change the setting: the machine tests only me, and it is negative. I think I should believe I am healthy. But I also know that this machine produces false negatives in roughly 0.1 percent of cases, and I have used it on many different people over the years. Should my belief about this specific result be weaker because I know errors have probably occurred elsewhere in the machine's history? My gut says no—this result stands on its own.

Fourth, a pair that is harder to reconcile. I read a long argument with twenty steps. I understand and accept each step individually. But I also think that in arguments of that length, people usually make at least one subtle mistake. So I accept the conclusion, yet I also think there is probably a hidden flaw somewhere. This feels acceptable. But now suppose I must place a bet: I can bet that step seven is valid, or I can bet that there is a flaw somewhere in the argument. I feel I should take the first bet, even though that seems to commit me to denying the second. Is there a principle that allows me to hold both?

Fifth, a social version. In a town of ten thousand residents, a census predicts that exactly 9,999 are citizens and one is not, but it does not identify which one. Each resident knows the statistics and knows they were registered normally. Each believes they are a citizen. That seems rational. But should the mayor believe that all ten thousand are citizens? Clearly not. So the same statistical fact supports different beliefs depending on one's position. Now apply this to belief about belief: I know that human judgment is about ninety percent accurate on hard moral questions. I have formed one hundred moral judgments. I think each one is right, but I also think about ten of them are wrong. Should I revise ten of them downward? My gut says no—revising them all equally feels like abandoning reasoning—but I cannot point to which ten are the mistakes.

Is there a single principle—perhaps about the level at which error is tolerated, or about practical acceptance versus pure belief—that captures all of these judgments? Or do I have to drop some of them? In particular, can I keep both the lottery belief and the preface belief without contradiction, and what should the principle say about the case I keep returning to: a pilot who knows her navigation system is 99.99 percent reliable per flight, has completed 9,999 successful flights, and is now on flight ten thousand—should she trust the system on this flight exactly as much as she did on flight one?
