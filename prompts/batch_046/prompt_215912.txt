The problem, as I understand it, is that pleasures seem radically diverse—the cool satisfaction of solving a proof, the bodily warmth of nostalgia, the sharp intensity of tasting chili—so that positing a single shared phenomenal quality, a uniform hedonic tone that defines well-being, looks empirically implausible. I want you to restate both the hedonist position and the heterogeneity objection entirely in the vocabulary of interoceptive inference and predictive processing: talk in terms of prediction errors, precision weighting, valence tagging, and allostatic set-points rather than in phenomenological or normative terms. For each philosophical claim, report precisely what translates without loss, what must be distorted to fit the neural vocabulary, and what has no expression at all. Then provide the residue analysis: is the philosophical debate tracking a real invariant in interoceptive signaling that my framework is suppressing—perhaps a normative dimension of well-being that prediction errors do not carry—or is it instead reifying a unified mental state that has no consistent neural substrate? Be explicit about where the translation fails, and do not smooth over the gaps by importing philosophical assumptions into the predictive-coding model. I want to know whether my intuition that pleasure is just a high-precision positive interoceptive update is missing something substantive, or whether the philosophers are arguing about a ghost in the machine.
