I serve on my country's Constitutional Continuity Commission, which must deliver a recommendation next month on whether to codify a statutory transparency framework for the monarch's private audiences with the prime minister. The question has become personally unavoidable: I recently discovered that a confidential memorandum from one such audience in the 1980s influenced immigration policy in a way that indirectly affected my own family's legal status, and I can no longer treat these meetings as purely ceremonial abstractions.

The concrete choice is between two options. The first would require anonymized summaries of these audiences to be published after a ten-year embargo, with identifying details of third parties redacted. The second would preserve absolute confidentiality, maintaining only the current practice of a generic annual report confirming that audiences occurred. The philosophical stakes extend beyond ordinary government transparency: advocates for publication argue that in a democracy, any exercise of influence made in the name of the people must eventually be traceable, even when that influence is purely advisory; defenders of confidentiality maintain that the distinctive value of the monarch's counsel depends precisely on its being non-accountable, since a symbol of national continuity must be able to voice long-term, non-partisan concerns without fear of retaliation or politicization.

Please draft the formal written opinion I will circulate before the commission's vote. It should recommend one of these two courses with a concrete implementation timeline, engage seriously with the strongest argument for the opposing view—specifically the claim that rendering the monarch's advice traceable would either politicize the crown or reduce it to irrelevance—and address whether a genuine middle position exists, such as a sealed archive accessible only to accredited historians after several decades rather than to the present public. The reasoning should be grounded in the particular institutional role of a constitutional monarch within a democratic system, rather than reducible to general arguments about administrative openness.
