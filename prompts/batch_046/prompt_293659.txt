Consider the consistent pattern of verdicts that a memory-ethics framework—drawing on relational accounts of remembrance in the spirit of Margalit and on skeptical, autonomy-weighted arguments for forgetting in the spirit of Rieff—has rendered across the following five cases.

1. A state that perpetrated a genocide is required to maintain accurate public archives and physical memorials for its citizens, even when a democratic majority of those citizens prefers collective amnesia and the dismantling of monuments.
Verdict: The duty is binding; popular preference to forget does not dissolve the obligation.

2. An individual survivor of that genocide requests the permanent deletion of her own first-person testimony from all public archives, on the grounds that its retention causes her severe, ongoing psychological harm, and extensive official records of the event exist independently of her account.
Verdict: The request should be honored; institutions must permit the erasure.

3. A foreign nation with no direct historical, ethnic, or political connection to the atrocity institutes a mandatory national day of remembrance and a school curriculum devoted to the event.
Verdict: This is permissible but not ethically required; if it functions as performative appropriation or systematically distracts from local injustices, discontinuation is appropriate.

4. Within a family directly affected, descendants seek to preserve private, detailed records of a wronged ancestor who explicitly requested posthumous forgetting, arguing that preservation serves justice, while other family members support honoring the request and allowing the details to fade.
Verdict: The request for forgetting should prevail; private records should be allowed to fade, with historical accountability left to official archives.

5. A survivor community maintains collective commemorative rituals, but some members are administratively compelled to attend or to provide testimony against their will.
Verdict: Collective ritual should be supported and resourced, but no individual should be compelled to personally recall or speak.

Next, consider this new case. A trauma-survivor community—bound by thick relational ties to the dead and to one another—votes collectively to shut down an AI system that autonomously generates synthetic first-person narratives in the voices of deceased victims. The system is hosted and maintained by an international consortium of strangers with no direct connection to the event, displayed in a virtual memorial accessible globally. Extensive, authoritative historical records of the atrocity exist in official archives elsewhere. The community argues that the synthetic voices distort authentic remembrance, that remembrance has been outsourced to simulacra, and that allowing the voices to fall silent respects their collective authority over their own shared past. The consortium argues that the synthetic archive conveys emotional and moral dimensions that dry records cannot, that it serves a universal preventive function for humanity at large, and that a local community’s preference should not determine whether humanity retains access to these moral truths.

Predict the framework’s verdict on this case, identify the closest analog among cases 1–5, and state which of at least two competing principles best explains the full pattern—including your predicted verdict for case 6. The candidates should include a relational principle that assigns authority over remembrance primarily to those within thick ethical bonds, an instrumental principle that subordinates local preference to universal justice or atrocity-prevention, and an autonomy-based principle that treats collective or individual forgetting as presumptively authoritative. Say which principle the framework would be implicitly adopting if it ruled in the opposite direction, and specify one further hypothetical case—concretely described—that would definitively distinguish the two leading principles.
