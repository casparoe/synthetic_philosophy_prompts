Contemporary AI alignment practice in commercial labs often follows an institutional design best described as a "layer-cake" separation: the team that pre-trains and capability-fine-tunes the base model is organizationally walled off from the team that applies post-hoc refusal filters, system prompts, and safety benchmarks, with the latter typically denied access to the former's training logs, internal representations, or weight updates. Alignment is certified not by examining whether the base model's reasoning dynamics have been shaped away from harmful objectives, but by measuring refusal rates and harmlessness scores on held-out prompts applied to the combined system. Yet when harm occurs, public and regulatory discourse assigns moral responsibility to the deploying organization as a whole — and sometimes specifically to the base-model developers — rather than treating the failure as belonging solely to the filter layer. What philosophical commitments about agency, moral responsibility, and the ontology of "the system" does this arrangement presuppose? Does it presuppose that harmful behavior is always a superficial interface error correctable by separation, rather than an emergent property of the underlying model's reasoning? Can it consistently hold base-model teams responsible while denying them authority over the safety architecture? Reconstruct the implicit theory of what an "aligned system" is — a modified agent, a constrained tool, or a composite institution — distinguish what the practice strictly requires from what it merely accommodates, and describe what changes in organizational design and evaluation criteria would be necessary if one rejected the premise that alignment can be achieved by separation rather than by integration.
