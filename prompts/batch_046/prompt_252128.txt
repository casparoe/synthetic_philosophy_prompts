Consider five proposed statutes, each defended primarily on the ground that the law may enforce a shared moral view rather than prevent direct, identifiable harm to non-consenting others. I want to know whether there is a coherent principle behind my judgments about when that defense succeeds, and if so which of my verdicts must be sacrificed to keep it.

Case 1: A law criminalizing consensual sexual relations between adult siblings, justified solely by the widespread conviction that incest violates a fundamental taboo necessary for family integrity and social stability.

Case 2: A prohibition on the retail sale of alcohol every Sunday, enacted not for any public-health or safety reason but because a legislative majority holds that Sabbath-breaking is inherently sinful and that the state ought not facilitate it.

Case 3: A statute requiring every citizen with income above the national median to direct two percent of earnings to certified anti-poverty organizations, justified not by the direct harm of poverty but by the claim that allowing extreme wealth disparity to persist unchecked morally corrupts both the wealthy and the social order.

Case 4: A ban on physician-assisted suicide for terminal patients who are fully competent, defended not on grounds of coercion, error, or slippery slope but on the doctrine that human life possesses an intrinsic sanctity that the state is obligated to uphold regardless of individual consent.

Case 5: A law prohibiting the private, victimless use of an immersive virtual environment in which users simulate acts of extreme cruelty, justified solely on the belief that engaging in such fantasies deforms the user's moral character and weakens their capacity for empathy.

My verdicts are these: Case 1 is not justified by moralism alone. Case 2 is clearly unjustified. Case 3 is justified. Case 4 is justified. Case 5 is not justified.

The tension is evident: I reject moralistic justification for private sexual conduct and for private fantasy, yet I accept it for a macro-level economic obligation and for the preservation of life at its end. I also distinguish a structural redistribution mandate from a religious practice restriction, even though both invoke collective moral health.

Please offer the best principle, or a small set of competing candidates, that captures this pattern. For each principle, state precisely which clause is motivated by which case, apply it to a further case I have not mentioned—specifically, a law banning unmarried cohabitation justified solely by the moral importance of traditional marriage—and be explicit about which of my five verdicts it cannot accommodate. If the pattern is genuinely incoherent, say so and identify the outlier verdict.
