Having spent three decades in experimental biology before turning to philosophy of mind, I find myself returning repeatedly to a question that sits at the boundary of mechanism and morality: whether an agent trained purely by reward optimization can suffer in any meaningful sense. I am preparing a ninety-minute seminar for fourteen graduate students in philosophy of science and cognitive science, and I want them to experience—rather than merely debate—the conceptual pressure of treating reward signals not as mere informational updates but as functional analogues of valence. Specifically, the session should stage the distinction between an isolated temporal-difference error, which corrects a value function, and a sustained negative reward gradient that might constitute harm even in the absence of phenomenal consciousness, with particular attention to policy-gradient architectures in which behavior is shaped by cumulative reward expectation rather than by discrete punishment events.

Please design a structured in-class simulation or role-play, complete with assigned roles, constrained choice rules, and small collective stakes—perhaps point allocations or deliberation constraints—that forces participants to inhabit this distinction before any theoretical vocabulary is introduced. The rules should arrange incentives so that students manage simulated agents through environments with non-stationary reward structures, decide when intervention is warranted, and discover for themselves where the line between representational update and motivational damage might be drawn. Include a precise timeline, the materials required, and a debrief that maps specific moments in the activity onto the conceptual separation between informational and valential functions of reward; also provide an honest account of which feature of a functionalist suffering-account the exercise cannot reproduce and must be supplied by the facilitator, as well as what will predictably go wrong on the first run—whether students conflate reward magnitude with subjective intensity or import anthropomorphic assumptions about consciousness—and how to correct that failure without derailing the session.
