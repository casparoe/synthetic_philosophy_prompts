I've been thinking about Ubuntu and relational conceptions of personhood outside any formal study—mostly from following debates about New Zealand's river-personhood rulings and South African jurisprudence that invokes communal dignity. What strikes me is that the idea is usually pushed in two directions that don't meet: either deep metaphysics about whether "I am because we are" defines human being, or direct political advocacy for collective rights. The middle ground—conceptual work that could actually guide local institutions—seems underdeveloped, and I want to know where effort should go.

On my own, I've noticed three tensions. First, if personhood is constituted by relation, how should institutions handle obligations to beings that cannot reciprocate—future generations, ecosystems, ancestors—without importing a hidden hierarchy that treats some relations as metaphorical and others as binding? Second, when relational ideas enter law or governance, they tend to be translated back into either group rights or individual rights mediated by community; is there a genuinely distinct institutional form, or is that translation unavoidable? Third, if my identity is partly constituted by relationships that include people with opposing visions of the good, what does it mean to resolve conflict "within" the relation rather than by appealing to a neutral external principle?

If a small working group—designing, say, community mental-health protocols or local environmental co-management boards—wants to take relational personhood seriously without just overlaying Western liberal frameworks onto it, which of these problems (or others I've missed) should they treat as the priority? I'm asking for a defended agenda, not a survey: rank the lines of work by importance and tractability for non-specialists, identify which ones other questions depend on, and state the criteria you're using—practical institutional impact, risk of conceptual co-optation, coherence with Ubuntu's internal logic, or something else. Also say whether the ranking changes if the goal is legal reform versus small-scale local design, and name the one further case or distinction that would most clearly test whether your top priority is the right one.
