I have recently begun supervising a small AI research group, and I have noticed that my colleagues routinely speak as if the language models we deploy hold beliefs—referring, for instance, to a system believing that a prompt is adversarial. This casual practice has made the intentional stance suddenly personal for me, and I want to map precisely where I stand. Please construct a decision tree that takes a respondent from an initial branching question—Is attributing beliefs to an AI ever literally true, or always a useful fiction?—through three subsequent nodes: whether functional equivalence to human belief-fixing mechanisms is sufficient on its own; whether the absence of phenomenal consciousness is an absolute disqualifier regardless of functional profile; and whether such attribution carries independent moral or epistemic weight, or is purely predictive. Each leaf should carry the name of the position (or be marked unnamed) and state its most awkward commitment in one sentence, with particular attention to the leaf that permits full literal belief-attribution to present LLMs despite their lacking subjective experience. Flag any pair of leaves your tree cannot cleanly separate. In addition, include a branch that tests whether defining belief purely as hierarchical prediction-error minimization collapses the distinction between human and artificial belief, and indicate whether that branch lands on an occupied or unoccupied position. The tree should be usable by someone familiar with Dennett’s basic idea but not with the specialist literature, and it must not depend on citation of specific texts.
