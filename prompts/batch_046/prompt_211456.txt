Two interpretive camps have spent a year debating the philosophical significance of Gongsun Long’s “White Horse Is Not Horse” dialogue and its relation to the School of Names (Mingjia). The Separability Interpreter reads the dialogue as defending a metaphysical thesis: the form (xing) “horse” is ontologically and logically distinct from the color-determination “white,” so that “white horse” denotes a compound reality rather than the pure kind “horse.” The Contextual-Predication Interpreter reads the same dialogue as an analysis of how kind-terms behave under modification: “horse” in isolation denotes an unqualified kind, but in the phrase “white horse” it operates within a restricted extension shaped by the color term, with no commitment to separated universals.

Write the adversarial collaboration document they should have produced. Structure it as follows: (1) a numbered section of substantive claims both sides can sign—covering the logical steps visible in the dialogue (e.g., that color is not identical to horse, and that horse does not include whiteness), the broader Mingjia concern with rectifying names (zhengming) and the relationship between names and realities (ming-shi), and the dialogue’s connection to Hui Shi’s doctrine of “contracting differences” (he tong yi); (2) a disagreements table stating each camp’s position in wording the other accepts as accurate, addressing at least whether the argument commits a fallacy of division or instead draws a valid predicative distinction, whether “horse” functions like an intersective kind term or like a term susceptible to subsective modification (analogous to modern distinctions between “small elephant” and compound breed-terms), and whether the School of Names supports a realist ontology of separated forms or a conventionalist account of linguistic categorization; (3) the cruxes—specifically, whether parallel arguments in the dialogue about “yellow horse” and “black horse” indicate a systematic ontological separation of color and form or merely illustrate context-sensitive predication, and whether the Mingjia texts on “names and realities” treat kind-terms as mapping onto pre-linguistic forms or as tools for practical discrimination; (4) for each crux, a concrete textual case or structural feature both agree is relevant—such as the repeated pattern of substituting colors and observing the failure of identity—plus each side’s explicit, advance prediction of what that feature demonstrates.

Keep every formulation neutral: do not ask which interpretation is correct. Ensure the agreed claims are specific enough to be meaningful and that the disagreement wording is precise enough that each party recognizes its own view without distortion. Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or requests for citation.
