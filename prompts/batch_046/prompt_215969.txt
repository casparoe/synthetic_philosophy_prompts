In my school's philosophy seminar, two classmates—Jordan and Riley—spent the better part of an hour arguing about whether human free will is best understood as the negation of sphexishness, using the sphex wasp's mechanically rigid burrow-checking behavior as the baseline case. I followed the exchange closely but could not determine who was landing the stronger points, and I would like a careful, move-by-move adjudication.

Jordan began by observing that the sphex wasp exhibits pure mechanical rigidity: it drags prey to its burrow, checks inside, and if the prey has been removed, repeats the check in an endless loop rather than revising its plan. Jordan argued that human free will is precisely our capacity to break such loops—to recognize that a step has become pointless and abandon it—so free will is real and is defined by being genuinely non-sphexish.

Riley replied that this sets up a false dichotomy. Human neural processes are equally deterministic and mechanistic; we simply possess more intermediate variables and feedback loops. Calling our complex determinism free will is like claiming that a computer with more transistors is less mechanical. Riley contended that the sphex comparison actually undermines free will, because it demonstrates that fully mechanistic behavior can appear goal-directed without being genuinely free.

Jordan answered with a claim about qualitative difference: humans can respond to reasons, revise intentions, and recognize futility in a way the wasp cannot. The wasp does not understand that its loop is futile; we do. This reason-responsiveness is not merely an increase in variables but a different kind of flexibility, one sufficient to distinguish free will from sphexishness.

Riley countered that reason-responsiveness is still mechanistic—our brains compute reasons in much the same way the wasp computes prey-location. The capacity to recognize pointlessness is just a feedback loop with more nodes. If free will is defined only as mechanism with sufficient nodes, then it is compatible with determinism but metaphysically hollow. Riley insisted that Jordan had not shown why adding nodes creates a new ontological category rather than merely scaling the old one.

Jordan then introduced a further distinction between first-order flexibility (possessing many behavioral options) and second-order flexibility (being able to revise the rules by which one selects among options). The sphex lacks both. Humans clearly have first-order flexibility, but Jordan questioned whether we truly possess second-order flexibility. If our values and reasoning rules are themselves fixed by biology, upbringing, and prior causal conditions—if even our revisions are just the deterministic outputs of earlier states—then we are merely sphexes with a larger menu. Jordan called this possibility meta-sphexishness: the threat that our flexibility itself is rigidly determined at a higher level.

Riley responded that this meta-sphexish standard proves too much. If second-order flexibility requires that our values not be causally determined, then no physical being could ever satisfy it. Yet people genuinely change religions, ethical commitments, and life goals. The fact that those revisions have causes does not render them illusory. Riley noted that the sphex never revises its burrow-checking rule, whereas humans revise their decision-rules constantly. That difference is real and sufficient, Riley argued, without requiring an impossible, uncaused freedom.

Please adjudicate the exchange move by move: rate each of the six moves above from clearly mistaken to clearly right, with brief justification for each rating. Then state specifically who is right about whether meta-sphexishness poses a genuine threat to free will, and about whether reason-responsiveness requires a non-mechanistic explanation. Finally, identify what both Jordan and Riley are missing—particularly any unstated assumption about the relationship between determinism and the normative authority of reasons that neither party has questioned.
