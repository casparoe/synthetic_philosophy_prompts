I'm serving as a grader for the final exam in my department's graduate seminar on Kant's practical philosophy. The exam posed the following question: "In his reply to Benjamin Constant's murderer-at-the-door scenario, Kant refuses to sanction even a benevolent lie. Analyze whether this refusal is best justified by the Formula of Universal Law, the Formula of Humanity, or the Doctrine of Virtue's distinction between perfect and imperfect duties. Then evaluate whether the position coheres with Kant's broader account of moral worth and casuistry."

One student submitted an answer that runs roughly as follows. They correctly identify truthfulness as a perfect duty that admits no exceptions, and they invoke the Formula of Humanity to argue that lying manipulates the murderer's rational agency by treating him merely as a means. However, they claim that Kant's actual reasoning in the reply is "essentially consequentialist," resting mainly on the worry that permitting deception would undermine social contracts and render promises meaningless. They go on to assert that the Doctrine of Virtue permits "casuistical exceptions" to perfect duties whenever benevolence demands it, and that Kant's rigid refusal in the essay therefore contradicts the more flexible framework developed in the later work. Finally, they mischaracterize Constant's challenge as the unconditional claim that lying is always permissible, rather than as the conditional claim that one owes the truth only to interlocutors who have not forfeited their right to it.

Please assign this answer a specific letter grade and provide a point-by-point justification. I want to know which parts demonstrate real comprehension of the coherence problem between the essay and the Doctrine of Virtue, which mistakes are merely sloppy as opposed to conceptually serious, and whether the misreading of Constant's position fatally undermines the student's evaluation of Kant's reply. Be precise about what a correct account of the relationship between perfect duties and casuistry should look like, and indicate whether the consequentialist interpretation of the essay, though clearly incomplete, is entirely indefensible or captures something Kant genuinely emphasizes.
