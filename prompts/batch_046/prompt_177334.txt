You have been asked to draft a design brief for a residents' committee in an ageing apartment block that has commissioned a learning-based heating controller. The device adjusts boiler settings by observing thermostat changes, verbal complaints, and seasonal patterns, then proposes new default schedules. The committee is divided. One faction insists the engineers should hard-code explicit fairness constraints—say, an equal thermal budget per flat—so the system never violates distributive justice. Another faction argues the controller should instead learn each resident's revealed comfort preferences and maximise some aggregate welfare function, trusting that accurate preference satisfaction is the only legitimate goal. A third faction warns that if the system becomes too adept at predicting and pre-empting complaints, it might gradually reshape residents' expectations—lowering their sense of entitlement to warmth—without anyone noticing, thereby achieving high satisfaction scores while undermining their autonomy.

You feel the disagreement runs deeper than engineering trade-offs. You suspect the committee members may not be proposing different solutions to a single problem, but rather describing altogether different problems: one concerning the correct specification of values, another concerning the robustness of learned objectives, and another concerning who retains authority over the norms that govern daily life. You also worry that treating these as entirely separate might miss a unifying question about what it genuinely means for an automated system to be responsive to the people it serves, rather than merely optimising over them.

Please advise, in British English, on whether you should frame your brief around one unified philosophical problem or several distinct ones. Identify the precise structure of each problem at stake, apply relevant theoretical distinctions—such as that between outcome-based and process-based responsiveness, or between preference satisfaction and the preservation of normative authority—and avoid generic project-management guidance. Do not provide a bibliography, reading list, or citations.
