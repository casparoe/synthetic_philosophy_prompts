Consider the institutional norm—common in university honor councils, workplace ethics boards, and public moral discourse—that hypocrisy specifically disqualifies a person from blaming others for an offense, while other moral shortcomings do not. Under this norm, an individual who has recently committed act X and now condemns others for X is routinely dismissed as lacking standing, even if they have genuinely reformed; by contrast, an individual who has never committed X but is also wholly unaffected by it—say, through disability, institutional exemption, or simple indifference—faces no comparable challenge to their authority to blame. A third case complicates the picture further: a victim of X is generally granted full standing to blame the perpetrator, regardless of whether the victim has committed unrelated moral offenses or even past instances of X in different circumstances. The result is a selective pattern: hypocrisy undermines standing, but non-involvement and victimhood protect or restore it, irrespective of broader moral consistency.

What philosophical theory of blame and standing does this pattern presuppose? Reconstruct the implicit account: does the norm treat standing as contingent on a reciprocity condition that the blamer must satisfy with respect to the specific norm violated, on an epistemic authority that requires lived conformity, or on a purely strategic concern that hypocritical blame is likely insincere? Carefully distinguish what the practice strictly requires—must the disqualifying hypocrisy involve the very same wrong being blamed, or merely a relevantly similar failure?—from what it merely happens to accommodate. Assess whether the practice is internally consistent: can it explain why unrelated moral failings do not diminish a victim's standing, and why inability or exemption does not diminish the standing of an unaffected observer? Finally, specify what would have to change in these institutional procedures if standing were instead grounded solely in the wrongness of the act condemned and in the blamer's non-instrumental concern that the wrong cease, with no further conditions on the blamer's personal history.
