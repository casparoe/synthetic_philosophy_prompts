I have been teaching a seminar on the limits of moral obligation, and my students are divided on whether supererogation—action that is morally good but not strictly required—names a genuine category or merely romanticizes ordinary duty. To test the question against a work that lives and breathes in that gray zone, I would like you to read Victor Hugo’s Les Misérables through the philosophical lens of supererogation, but not in the usual broad-strokes way. I am particularly interested in pressing two somewhat under-discussed distinctions into the text: first, the difference between cost-based accounts (which locate the supererogatory quality in the agent’s substantial personal sacrifice) and status-based accounts (which treat it as possessing a special moral status regardless of cost); second, Susan Wolf’s critique that relentless self-sacrifice produces not admirable saints but one-dimensional, unattractive figures who have surrendered every personal project to morality.

Please focus your reading on three well-known scenes: the Bishop of Digne giving his silver candlesticks to Jean Valjean; Valjean’s rescue of Marius from the sewers, which costs him his concealed identity and his future with Cosette; and Valjean’s release of Javert at the barricade. For each scene, ask whether Hugo presents the act as genuinely optional, whether the narrative rewards it with social recognition or punishes it with isolation and misunderstanding, and whether Valjean’s fierce attachment to Cosette operates as a personal project that saves him from Wolf’s charge of moral sainthood—or whether it merely disguises a total subordination of self to duty. You might also consider Javert’s suicide: does it represent a failure to understand that mercy can be supererogatory rather than a dereliction of justice?

I want the reading to be honest in both directions. Where does the philosophical framework illuminate Hugo’s dramaturgy—perhaps in showing that Valjean’s acts only become meaningful as supererogatory when witnessed and interpreted by others, not merely performed? Where does it distort the novel’s religious framing, which may treat charity not as beyond duty but as the fulfillment of a Christian vocation that collapses the distinction entirely? And finally, what does the novel add to the philosophical debate that conceptual analysis alone cannot provide—maybe something about the narrative conditions required for an audience to recognize an act as going beyond what can be demanded, without reducing that recognition to mere sentiment?
