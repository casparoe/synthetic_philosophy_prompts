In constitutional theory and rational-choice discussions of democratic self-binding, the episode of Ulysses and the Sirens is often treated as a direct model for constitutional entrenchment: present citizens are Ulysses, rigid amendment rules and judicial review are the ropes and the mast, the crew that refuses to untie him despite his pleading represents courts and procedural barriers, and the Sirens stand for the predictable but destructive temptations of future majorities—populism, rights violations, or short-termist policy. The analogy is typically invoked to justify strong precommitment mechanisms, on the grounds that a collective agent, like an individual, can rationally bind its future self against foreseeable failures of will.

Audit this analogy systematically. First, provide an explicit correspondence table: what plays the role of Ulysses, the mast, the ropes, the crew, and the Sirens in the constitutional setting, and what, if anything, corresponds to the ship itself? Second, examine the disanalogies that bear on the normative conclusion. In particular, address the problem of collective identity over time: Ulysses is one continuous agent, whereas a democratic people is a succession of different individuals, so binding “the people” across generations raises a consent problem that has no individual analog. Consider also the asymmetry of foresight: Ulysses knows exactly what the Sirens will do and that his future irrationality will be temporary and specific, but constitutional drafters face open-ended uncertainty about future democratic failures. Does the analogy smuggle in an unjustified epistemic privilege? Third, examine the “crew” correspondence more closely: Ulysses issues direct instructions to specific agents who owe him obedience, whereas constitutional enforcers derive authority from the document they protect, which raises a circularity concern absent from the original episode.

Finally, evaluate whether the conclusion—that strong constitutional precommitment is rational and legitimate—survives once these disanalogies are fully explicit, or whether the analogy was carrying the normative load all along. Is there a more precise framework—perhaps one that distinguishes strategic commitment devices, intertemporal collective-choice mechanisms, and democratic self-governance—that captures what the Ulysses image genuinely illuminates about binding future majorities, without importing the misleading baggage of individual self-control?
