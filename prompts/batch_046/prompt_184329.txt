I am preparing the final exam for an upper-level philosophy of mind seminar that spent several weeks on Dennett's account of real patterns and his taxonomy of explanatory stances—physical, design, and intentional. I want a question that cannot be answered by simply listing the three stances and giving the standard examples; instead, students should have to navigate a case where the boundaries genuinely blur, so I have constructed a somewhat contrived technological scenario that forces adjudication rather than summary.

Please draft a single essay question appropriate for a 75-minute exam period, with an expected answer of roughly 900–1000 words. The prompt should present the following situation: a city-wide distributed sensor and actuation network—installed for traffic and energy management—begins to generate predictive outputs that are too systematic to be noise and too context-sensitive to be derived from any documented algorithm or engineer's design specification. The predictions can be improved only by attributing to the network beliefs about local urban dynamics and desires to maintain equilibrium, yet the system lacks any consciousness, subjective experience, or biological substrate. Ask students to determine which stance (physical, design, intentional, or some hybrid) is appropriate for explaining its predictive success; to assess whether this predictive success establishes that the pattern is real in Dennett's sense; and to evaluate whether adopting the intentional stance here forces an ontological commitment stronger than Dennett's instrumentalism allows—particularly in light of the fact that the design stance seems partially adequate but systematically incomplete.

Along with the question, please provide a model answer of about 700–800 words that makes the specific conceptual moves a top response requires—such as distinguishing predictive utility from ontological commitment, explaining why the design stance fails precisely where context-sensitive novelty outstrips programmed parameters, and addressing the mere instrumentalism objection—rather than summarizing Dennett's general view. Then provide a marking scheme out of 25 points that distinguishes an A answer from a B answer by naming the exact moves each must contain (for instance, recognizing that real-pattern status is stance-relative but not arbitrarily so), and that lists the specific errors that should cost marks, such as conflating functional analysis with the design stance or assuming that intentional-stance adoption requires phenomenal consciousness.

Finally, please include in both the model answer and the marking scheme a brief meta-critical reflection: identify the weakest point in the analysis—specifically, the assumption that sustained predictive success is sufficient for real-pattern status without addressing whether counterfactual intervention on the attributed beliefs would disrupt the predictions—and state clearly what kind of finding or argument (for example, evidence that the predictions remain stable even when the belief-attributions are systematically varied) would cause you to revise the verdict about which stance is appropriate.
