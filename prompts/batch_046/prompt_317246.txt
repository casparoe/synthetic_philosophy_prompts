okay so i've been thinking about transitional justice mechanisms and there's something that bugs me conceptually. in south africa's TRC you could get amnesty if you fully disclosed your politically motivated crimes, but the law never made remorse or apologizing a formal condition - disclosure was basically the only currency. meanwhile in rwanda's gacaca courts, confessing and asking the community for forgiveness actually reduced your sentence, so remorse and reintegration were literally part of the penal calculus. and then there's argentina where they initially went for prosecution of high-ranking officers but left lower-level people alone mostly because they lacked evidence, not because of any moral theory about who deserves amnesty.

so here's the thing: these three practices seem to treat truth, remorse, and punishment as if they're interchangable tokens in some moral economy, but they assign them totally different exchange rates. what implicit theory of moral repair makes sense of treating disclosure as sufficient for immunity in one system, but requiring communal forgiveness in another, and using evidentiary failure as a de facto amnesty in the third?

does the south african model presuppose that the state's primary duty is epistemic - that establishing a public record is the core of justice - and that punishment is just an instrument for that? or does it assume victims and perpetrators are in a kind of transactional relation where information is traded for freedom? and if justice actually requires recognizing the victim's dignity rather than just knowing the facts, wouldn't that force us to redesign amnesty conditions entirely?

i'm not asking for a history of these commissions. i want to know what philosophy of moral responsibility or restorative justice makes these different 'currencies' coherent, whether each practice strictly needs its implicit theory or could work with rival ones, and what a policy built on victim dignity rather than informational repair would actually have to change in practice.
