hey — i'm working on a novel where the main character literally mails her own passport to a future address so she can't flee the country when she panics. classic Ulysses thing, but it got me thinking about constitutional pre-commitment, and now i've built this argument that feels like a trap.

(1) A Ulysses contract only makes sense if the future self you're binding is genuinely other — not just you-with-different-moods, but lacking the standing to undo what you did.

(2) Constitutional entrenchment is just the collective version: founders write hard amendment rules to bind citizens who come later.

(3) So if entrenchment is rational, those later citizens must be genuinely other agents, not the same collective will across time.

(4) But if they are genuinely other agents, then the founders have no authority over them — you can't contract for strangers. authority needs continuity of agency.

(5) So either later citizens are the same agent (which makes entrenchment redundant, like locking your own door from inside and changing it's mind) or they're different agents (which makes it illegitimate).

(6) Therefore constitutional precommitment is either pointless or tyrannical, and there's no middle ground.

i think there's a slide between 'other in preferences' and 'other in identity' hiding in step (3), but i'm not sure. which step should i reject? and if i keep (4) — that you can't bind strangers — what does that do to democratic majorities that want to change old rules? also, is there any room for a 'fictional collective agent' that survives generations without being literally the same people?

this is probably over my head but i'd like to know where the argument breaks, and what rejecting that break costs.
