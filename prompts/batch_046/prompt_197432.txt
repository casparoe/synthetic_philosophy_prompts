I have been trying to isolate a particular defensive structure that recurs in moral reasoning about deep commitments, and I want to know whether philosophy already has a name for it or a literature that treats it as distinct. The pattern concerns an agent whose identity is bound up with a ground project—say, a decades-long scientific inquiry or an intimate loyalty. The agent can competently apply an impartial calculus to other people's parallel situations and can even acknowledge, in the abstract, that such a calculus might recommend sacrificing her own commitment for a greater good. Yet when the reasoning turns to her own case, she does not merely outweigh the impartial demand; she blocks it from reaching a first-person conclusion. She refuses to form the belief that she ought to abandon the commitment, not because she can cite a specific countervailing reason, but because reaching that belief would require her to have already adopted the weighing perspective that her commitment is meant to resist. She maintains something like an epistemic firewall: the impartial argument is permitted to exist at a distance, but it is not allowed to issue in an ought-judgment directed at her. Two concrete instances may help. First, a researcher whose work has begun to cause serious harm recognizes that anyone in a structurally similar position should probably stop, yet she finds herself unable to conclude that she herself ought to stop; she reports that to reach that conclusion she would have to become someone for whom the work was already just one factor among others. Second, a person asked to betray a close friend's confidence for a clear public benefit does not claim that loyalty always trumps the benefit; instead, he says that if he were able to see betrayal as permissible for him, he would no longer be the kind of friend he takes himself to be, and so he refuses to complete the reasoning that would license it. This is clearly in the neighborhood of the well-known integrity objection and the familiar spouse-rescue worry about an alienating extra thought in practical justification. But the standard integrity objection seems directed primarily at action—doing what alienates one from one's ground projects—whereas what I am tracking is directed at cognition or inference: the agent protects her commitment by refusing to draw the concluding belief, not merely by refusing to act on it. Is there an established concept for this specific configuration—the protection of a ground project at the level of belief-formation or deliberative framing rather than at the level of action? How does it differ from ordinary agent-relative reasons, and from the standard integrity-based refusal? If the literature does not already distinguish it, can you coin a precise term, provide a definition with boundary conditions, and explain what it is not—distinguishing it carefully from simple self-deception, from moral particularism, and from the standard case of an alienating extra thought in practical justification?
