The interdisciplinary debate over formalizing the Anthropocene as a geological epoch has stalled despite more than a decade of working-group proposals: stratigraphers advocating formal epoch-status press for a mid-twentieth-century boundary marked by plutonium fallout, plastic stratigraphy, and sharp nitrogen-phosphorus perturbations, while critics—ranging from Quaternary geologists to environmental historians—insist on an earlier industrial origin, invoke the diachronous and regionally heterogeneous character of human impact, or reject epoch-status altogether in favor of treating the Anthropocene as an event or a cultural concept. The disagreement is presented as empirical—sediment cores, isotopic signals, and global correlation—yet the opposing camps rarely converge on shared criteria for what counts as decisive evidence, and formal ratification by stratigraphic authorities remains elusive. Is this stalemate actually a hidden empirical dispute about global synchroneity and irreversibility, or is it a disagreement about the ontology of geological time—specifically, whether an epoch must be readable in a Global Boundary Stratotype Section and Point for future geologists, or whether it can be defined by a causal-regime shift in Earth-system dynamics that historians and climate scientists recognize? Might part of the conflict be verbal, with 'Anthropocene' functioning as a chronostratigraphic unit for some, a normative historical periodization for others, and an ontological thesis about human-nature entanglement for a third? Diagnose the disagreement by engaging what each side actually treats as authoritative—rock layers, model outputs, or disciplinary jurisdiction—and determine whether the sides differ about standards of evidence, about which sciences have definitional authority, or about bedrock intuitions that stratigraphy should remain insulated from recent cultural history. Given your diagnosis—whether the impasse is empirical, conceptual, normative, or a misalignment of questions under one name—what would genuinely move it: a newly proposed globally synchronous stratigraphic marker, a formal philosophical distinction between chronostratigraphic and geohistorical periodization, or an admission that the debate conflates incompatible ontologies of deep time and should be dissolved into separate inquiries?
