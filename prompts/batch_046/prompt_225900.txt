I had a long argument with a friend about whether Bostrom-style simulation reasoning should temper the extreme longtermist commitments that treat the welfare of astronomical numbers of distant future beings as lexically dominant over present concerns. At a critical moment he kept falling back on the line: "If we're simulated, the far future is just rendered pixels, so treating it as sacred is a category mistake." He used this to dismiss the inference from "existential risk reduction matters" to "we should restructure present priorities around protecting trillions of simulated lives," but he never said exactly what kind of mistake he thought was being made. I want to separate the precise claims that could be hiding inside that slogan. Is it an ontological thesis that future simulated beings lack the kind of existence required for moral patienthood? An epistemic claim that being in a simulation undermines our confidence in long-run predictions about those beings? A normative restriction of moral concern to entities in "base reality"? Or a causal thesis that actions inside the simulation are impotent with respect to anything that genuinely matters? Please lay these out as a menu—mark which are platitudinous restatements of the simulation premise, which are substantive and defensible, which are false, and which actually do the work of undermining far-future fanaticism rather than merely sounding dramatic. In particular, address whether the reasoning changes if we allow nested simulations, or if simulated suffering might still matter instrumentally to the simulators; and tell me which reading my friend was most likely invoking when he used the line to block discussion of whether present-day resource allocation should be dominated by distant-future considerations.
