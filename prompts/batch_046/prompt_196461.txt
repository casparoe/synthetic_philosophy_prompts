I am trying to determine whether genuine moral dilemmas are genuinely possible, or whether every apparent dilemma can be resolved by showing that one obligation is overridden rather than violated. I am testing this by reflecting on my own verdicts across cases.

Case one: a parent must choose which of two children can be saved from a burning building when only one can be carried out. I judge this a genuine moral dilemma. The parent does wrong either way—leaving one child is a serious moral failure that is not erased by saving the other—and enduring agent-regret is appropriate, not merely psychological trauma.

Case two: a triage physician with one remaining ventilator and two patients with identical survival probabilities assigns it by a fair lottery. I judge this not a genuine moral dilemma. The physician applies a correct decision procedure; the death of the loser is a tragedy, not a wrong committed by the physician, and moral residue is inappropriate.

Case three: a procurement director for a public water authority discovers that fulfilling a long-term supply contract requires continuing a filtration process that releases a harmful byproduct. Terminating the contract would breach a valid agreement and likely bankrupt the small supplier, destroying its workers' livelihoods; maintaining it continues the environmental harm. I judge this a genuine moral dilemma. Both honoring and breaking the contract involve serious, un-canceled moral wrongs, and the director bears residue regardless of her choice.

Case four: lying to an armed intruder about the location of a hidden family member. This is clearly not a dilemma. The obligation to be truthful does not apply in this way, and deception carries no moral remainder.

Case five: a hospice nurse, at a dying patient's explicit request, affirms that an estranged sibling sent a message of reconciliation, though the sibling in fact refused all contact. The nurse feels torn between compassion and respect for autonomy, and I go back and forth on whether this is a genuine dilemma or a hard case with one right answer.

My tentative principle is that a genuine dilemma occurs only when an agent faces two obligations that are both perfect—not merely weighty—and neither is overridden, only outweighed, leaving moral remainder. But this seems to misclassify case three, where the obligations might be imperfect, and it struggles with case five, where I am unsure whether the obligations are perfect or imperfect.

What I want is a precise principle, or a small set of candidates, that delivers my verdicts on cases one through four, addresses case five consistently, predicts the result for further cases—such as a civil servant who must enforce an unjust but democratically enacted statute, or a researcher who must allocate a single experimental therapy between two equally deserving patients—and honestly states which of my five verdicts must be overturned for coherence. I am particularly interested in whether the correct account tracks direct causal agency, the incommensurability of the values at stake, or the perfect versus imperfect nature of the obligations involved, but I want the answer to show its work: which clause of the principle handles which case, what it predicts for an unconsidered scenario, and which verdict is the price of simplicity.
