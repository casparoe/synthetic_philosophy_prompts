I have been thinking a lot about what it actually means to be rational in what we believe, and I keep coming back to the idea that beliefs should aim to be accurate—to match reality as closely as possible—rather than just avoiding obvious mistakes. This accuracy-first view seems simple, but I am struggling to see what it implies for situations where the world around us is set up to reward being wrong in specific ways. For instance, imagine an information environment, like a prediction market or an algorithm-driven news feed, where people or systems gain an advantage when beliefs are skewed in a particular direction, because that skew generates more trading or engagement. In such a setting, being precisely calibrated to the truth might actually put you at a disadvantage.

Please describe the most important implications of taking an accuracy-first approach—where the fundamental goal of belief is to minimize the overall gap between what we think and what is actually true—for how we should revise our beliefs in these kinds of adversarial or strategically distorted environments. Specifically, I want to know whether this framework tells us to treat the distortion as just another piece of evidence, or whether it requires us to build corrections directly into the rules by which we update our beliefs. I am also interested in how we should handle the conflict between getting each individual belief right, which pulls us toward correcting local errors, and preserving the overall coherence of our belief system, which might require allowing some local inaccuracies if they prevent a larger global miscalibration. Is there a clear principle that tells us when to make small adjustments to individual beliefs versus when to restructure our beliefs more broadly, and does that principle change depending on whether we are calibrating to the world itself or to the strategic environment we find ourselves in?
