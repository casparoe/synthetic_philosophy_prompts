As a journalist who covers neuroscience and philosophy of mind, I serve on the interdisciplinary ethics and funding review panel of the Meridian Institute for Cognitive Health. We vote this Thursday on whether to grant Phase-I endorsement to the Mirror-Interface Protocol, a proposed clinical trial for severe, treatment-resistant depersonalization-derealization disorder. The protocol is built on a specific theoretical premise: that phenomenal consciousness operates as a predictive user-illusion, a constructed interface rather than a direct revelation of an enduring mental substance. Rather than attempting to restore conventional self-continuity, the intervention uses targeted neurofeedback combined with a low-dose dissociative adjunct to deliberately thin the self-model interface, on the hypothesis that these patients suffer not from broken contact with reality but from an overly rigid, maladaptive user-model that misrepresents transient experiential discontinuities as catastrophic loss of identity.

The strongest objection, advanced by the patient-advocacy coalition Presence First, holds that if subjective selfhood is genuinely illusory in this technical sense, then deliberately attenuating the interface does not treat a disorder but dismantles the only reality available to the patient—effectively erasing personhood under the guise of repair. They argue that any therapeutic framework treating consciousness as an interface must be held to a stricter normative standard: it should restore functional coherence of experience, not redesign the architecture of experience itself.

Please draft the formal written opinion I will circulate before Thursday’s session. It should recommend a concrete course of action—approval with binding safeguards, conditional deferral pending a revised endpoint framework, or rejection—together with an explicit timeline for implementation or reconsideration. The reasoning should take the user-illusion framework seriously on its own terms rather than dismissing it, yet it must also engage the Presence First objection at full strength, explaining precisely why that consideration does or does not outweigh the proposed clinical benefit. Structure the document for institutional action: a concise bottom-line recommendation up front, followed by an analysis of what is conceptually at stake when therapy targets the interface itself rather than its contents, and a clear statement of the monitoring conditions, ethical limits, or procedural checks that should accompany whichever decision you advise.
