I am taking a graduate seminar on democratic theory, and during our session on the boundary problem—who rightfully belongs to the demos—one of my classmates offered a summary of what she presented as the mainstream "stakeholder" resolution. She argued that the fundamental difficulty is not merely practical but logical: any democratic rule for including members presupposes a prior demos that can vote on the rule, which generates an infinite regress she called the "recursive closure problem." To break the regress, she said, stakeholder theorists rely on a principle she named "institutional echoing," according to which entities outside the formal boundary—future generations, foreign nationals affected by climate policy, or non-citizen residents—are not admitted to direct membership, but their interests are structurally mirrored within the demos through representational safeguards and impact assessments, so that exclusion operates as a form of "negative representation" rather than domination. From this, she drew two further conclusions: first, that the familiar distinction between the all-affected-interests principle and the all-subjected-to-coercion principle collapses, since both are merely descriptive variants of the same echo mechanism; and second, that the classic objection—that stakeholder democracy cannot draw a stable, non-arbitrary border—is therefore dissolved, because the boundary is not a binary membership line but a continuous gradient of echoed influence.

I found her account elegant, but I suspect she has blended a genuine logical puzzle with an original conceptual gadget and then presented the mixture as the standard view. Could you examine her summary point by point? In particular, I would like to know: whether the recursive closure problem is genuinely treated by stakeholder theorists in the way she describes; whether "institutional echoing" accurately captures any recognized mechanism in the literature, or whether it is a mischaracterization of how affected interests are handled; whether the collapse of the all-affected and all-subjected distinctions is justified or a conflation; and whether her claim that the boundary objection is dissolved—rather than merely relocated—faithfully represents the actual defenses offered by proponents of expansive democratic inclusion, or whether it overstates their confidence.
