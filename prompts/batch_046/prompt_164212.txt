Assume one takes seriously Schrödinger's Vedanta-influenced monism — that the appearance of many separate minds is the self-differentiation of one universal consciousness rather than an ontological plurality. Within philosophy of mind and cognitive science, which open conceptual problems does this view actually dissolve, which does it sharpen, and which remain untouched? Prioritize three specific issues: the neural binding problem for distributed representations, the indexicality of first-person reports, and the explanatory gap between physical processes and phenomenology. For each, defend whether resolving it genuinely requires mental plurality, whether the monist position relocates rather than solves the difficulty, and how tractable the problem is for philosophers working without empirical neuroscience. Frame this as a three-year research agenda for a small philosophy group with limited resources: which question is genuinely upstream of the others, which should be deprioritized because its answer changes little downstream, and what purely conceptual result over the next few years would most alter your ranking. Make your criteria explicit — dependency relations among the problems, tractability for non-scientists, and practical consequence for theories of personal identity — and deliver a defended ordering rather than a survey.
