As a journalist who covers science and ideas, I have been working through Daniel Dennett’s critique of the Cartesian theater for a long-form piece on consciousness, and I have constructed an argument that now strikes me as suspiciously overreaching—its conclusion, that denying the theater dissolves the very subject of experience, feels like a reductio, yet each step seems defensible on its own. I want to know where the slip occurs, what rejecting that step costs, and whether I am forced to choose between keeping my commitment to (1) and my commitment to (10). Here is the chain: (1) If consciousness lacks a Cartesian theater, then there is no single spatiotemporal location in which all phenomenal contents are jointly presented to one subject. (2) The multiple drafts model holds precisely this: experiential contents are distributed across parallel, competing narratives with no canonical version. (3) But if there is no joint presentation, then the relation “experienced together with” is not intrinsic to the contents themselves; it must be imposed by some integrative mechanism. (4) Any such mechanism, however, operates on subpersonal representations rather than on phenomenal properties directly—otherwise it would itself constitute part of the theater. (5) So on this model, whether two contents belong to the same experience depends entirely on whether the mechanism binds them, not on whether a subject directly apprehends them as unified. (6) Now imagine a case of cross-draft interference: mechanism M binds draft D1 (representing red) with draft D2 (representing green) into “I see a reddish-green field,” while mechanism M’—running with equal neural authority—binds D1 with D3 (representing blue) into “I see a reddish-blue field.” (7) Because there is no theater to adjudicate between M and M’, both narratives have equal claim to being the subject’s experience. (8) Therefore a single subject can determinately experience contradictory color-properties in the same visual field, with no possible error—since there is no “real” experience against which either draft can be measured. (9) But if contradictory phenomenal states can both be determinately true of one subject without error, then phenomenal concepts lose their first-person authority: I cannot know what I experience merely by introspecting, because introspection accesses only one unprivileged draft. (10) And if first-person authority is lost in this way, the distinction between seeming and being collapses for consciousness itself, undermining the very phenomenological data the model was meant to accommodate. (11) So denying the Cartesian theater proves too much: it eliminates the mythical stage only by dissolving the subject whose experience we set out to explain.
