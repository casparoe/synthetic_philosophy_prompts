I serve on the board of trustees for the National Museum of Civic Memory, and we face a vote this Thursday on whether to approve the curatorial team’s redesign of the permanent “Postwar Reconstruction” gallery. The proposal centers on a single immersive installation—tentatively titled The Unfinished Ledger—that structures visitor movement through four sequential chambers corresponding to Karl Jaspers’ postwar taxonomy of guilt: criminal, political, moral, and metaphysical. The first three rooms present documentary evidence of state-led atrocities, bureaucratic complicity, and individual acts of resistance; the fourth is an empty, mirrored space intended to evoke metaphysical guilt, with wall text asking whether citizens born decades after the events share in a collective moral condition simply by belonging to the nation-state that perpetrated them. A coalition of board members representing survivor-descendant organizations strongly supports the installation, arguing that without the fourth chamber the exhibition reduces systemic violence to a catalog of individual crimes. Conversely, several trustees—backed by a public letter from regional historians—contend that attributing metaphysical guilt to present-day citizens collapses the distinction between moral agency and inherited identity, risks instrumentalizing historical trauma for emotional spectacle, and violates the museum’s charter mandate to educate rather than to assign collective culpability.

Please draft the formal decision memo I will circulate before Thursday’s meeting. The memo should recommend a concrete course of action—approve the installation as designed, reject the fourth chamber specifically, approve it only with substantial revisions, or table the decision pending further review—with clear reasoning that the board can act upon. In your reasoning, apply Jaspers’ distinctions carefully: treat political guilt (borne by citizens for state actions they did not personally commit) and metaphysical guilt (rooted in universal human solidarity rather than legal or moral fault) as genuinely different categories, and explain whether either can be meaningfully assigned across generations within a museum’s public-educational role. You must also engage seriously with the strongest argument on the opposing side: if you recommend retaining the mirrored chamber, address the concern that it converts philosophical reflection into collective accusation without individual consent; if you recommend removing or modifying it, address the concern that excising metaphysical guilt sanitizes the past and abandons the museum’s duty to confront the enduring moral weight of history. The memo should conclude with an actionable bottom line—specific conditions, a timeline for implementation or revision, and criteria by which the board can evaluate compliance—rather than open-ended philosophical commentary.
