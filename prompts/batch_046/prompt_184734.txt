Question: In Hofstadter's Platonia, every inhabitant is perfectly rational and shares the exact same reasoning process, knowing this about all others. The Luring Lottery promises an enormous payout, but only if exactly one person enters; if two or more enter, the prize is destroyed. First, lay out the paradox a Platonic agent faces when deciding whether to enter. Second, evaluate a proposed fix called "epistemic humility weighting," where each agent assigns a tiny credence ε that they are uniquely capable of reasoning about the lottery, then enters with probability proportional to ε. Does this preserve superrational symmetry, or does it collapse back into the original problem?

Student answer:
The paradox is that since everyone in Platonia reasons identically, whatever conclusion I reach about entering, every other resident reaches too. So if I decide entering is rational, then everyone enters and the prize is destroyed, which makes entering irrational. But if I decide not to enter, then everyone also decides not to enter, which means I could have entered alone and won. So there's no stable rational choice—its basically the prisoner's dilema but with only one winner.

As for the epistemic humility fix, this is essentially Hofstadter's idea of superrational cooperation. By giving yourself a small chance that you're special, you break the symmetry just enough to randomize entry. Since ε is small, only about one person in expectation applies, which solves the coordination failure. This doesn't violate rationality because its analogous to mixing strategies in game theory, where rational agents randomize to avoid being predicted. So yes, it preserves symmetry in expectation and resolves the lottery problem.
