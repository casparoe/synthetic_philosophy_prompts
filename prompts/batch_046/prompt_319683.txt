The situationist critique in virtue ethics—associated with claims that behavior is more strongly predicted by situational variables than by global character traits, and that classic obedience and bystander-intervention studies undermine the empirical basis for virtues like courage or compassion—has been running for decades. Virtue ethicists have responded by reconceiving traits as situation-sensitive dispositions, questioning the ecological validity of the experiments, and noting that replication crises in social psychology weaken the critique's empirical footing. Yet the exchange feels circular: situationists say virtue ethics lacks predictive power; virtue ethicists say prediction was never the point, or that traits manifest differently across contexts.

I want a structural diagnosis, not a literature review. Is this impasse verbal—hinging on different uses of trait and prediction—or substantive? Specifically, are the sides divided over an empirical standard, such as whether cross-situational correlations below r ≈ .30 falsify trait talk, or whether such correlations are compatible with virtue if traits are conceived as dynamic patterns rather than static dispositions? Or is the real disagreement normative: whether moral education requires the kind of behavioral stability that situationists claim is absent?

Tell me which kind of disagreement dominates, defend your diagnosis with reference to the actual moves each side makes, and say whether anything—a formal model of trait dynamics, a distinction between predictive and normative psychology, or a shift in what counts as evidence—could actually move the debate. Or argue that it is bedrock: competing intuitions about whether ethical character must be robust across contexts, with no shared criterion to adjudicate them.

One caveat: if this framing mischaracterizes the debate—say, by treating a resolved empirical issue as open, or by conflating distinct strands of situationism—say so plainly rather than proceeding within a confused premise.
