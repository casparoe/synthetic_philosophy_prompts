Write the opening remarks for the retirement symposium honoring Dr. Elena Voss, delivered in a university auditorium before an audience split evenly between analytic philosophers of mind and her clinical psychology colleagues, students, and family members who have no philosophical training. Dr. Voss spent forty years developing a therapeutic practice centered on what she called "epistemic camouflage" — a form of self-misrecognition in which an agent does not secrete an unwelcome belief in some hidden mental compartment, but instead dissolves the boundary between belief and pretense so thoroughly that there is no intact "true" belief left for a deceiver to conceal. Your remarks must welcome both halves of the room, honor her career without patronizing anyone, and allow the philosophical problem to do real structural work: if self-deception requires both a deceiver and a deceived, and if belief is in some sense transparent to the agent who holds it, then epistemic camouflage may not be deception at all but rather a collapse of doxastic architecture; yet if it is not deception, then the clinical aim of "restoring" a patient to self-knowledge risks imposing a false clarity rather than recovering one. Consequently, you must avoid praising her for "uncovering hidden truths" or "exposing lies patients told themselves," since that framing misrepresents her actual method, which aimed not at excavation but at rebuilding the very conditions under which belief and pretense can be distinguished. The speech should remain spoken in the first person, should not include bibliographies, citations, or reading lists, should explain the conceptual tension clearly enough that the family members can follow its stakes, and should land with genuine warmth that does not resolve the paradox with false comfort. Around 600 words.
