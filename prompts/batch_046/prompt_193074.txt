Analyze the Classic Maya lowland transformation (roughly 800–950 CE) through the conceptual lens of societal phase transition rather than civilizational failure. Treat the abandonment of southern urban centers not as a collapse of Maya society but as a rapid reconfiguration of political authority away from divine-kingship nodes—where ajaw legitimacy was tied to monument construction and calendrical performance—toward distributed, post-monumental settlement patterns in the northern Yucatán and highlands. Map the units precisely: the Classic-period court elites, the agricultural hinterland communities that persisted, and the post-Classic noble houses that reorganized authority without centralized divine kingship. Identify what the "merely change" framework explains well—continuity in spoken language, ceramic traditions, and calendrical knowledge—and where it strains credibility, particularly regarding the abrupt cessation of dated monuments, the loss of long-form literacy in the southern lowlands, and the demographic contraction of major centers. Then turn the analysis back on the concept: does the speed and geographic scale of the transformation justify retaining "collapse" as an ontologically useful category, or is it a historiographical convenience that conflates political decentralization with societal disappearance? Be disciplined about where the theory is silent—especially regarding the interaction of prolonged drought stress and intensified warfare as accelerants rather than root causes—and do not force the framework to account for archaeological details it was not designed to address. What does the episode ultimately suggest about whether societies "fail" or only change their mode of institutional being?
