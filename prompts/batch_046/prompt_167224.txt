I have constructed a modal version of the omnipotence paradox that seems to show not merely that omnipotence is impossible, but that any property defined as universal ability over logically compatible tasks is necessarily empty. The argument runs as follows.

(1) Define omnipotence O: for every task-type T, if performing T is logically consistent with the being having O, then the being can perform T.

(2) Consider the task-type L: bring about a state S in which the being lacks O.

(3) L is logically possible, since there are beings that lack O and nothing in the concept of self-limitation is contradictory.

(4) So, if the being has O, then performing L is consistent with having O, and therefore by (1) the being can perform L.

(5) If the being performs L, then in S the being lacks O.

(6) Now consider that O, if it is a maximal modal property, must be either essential or contingent. If essential, then (5) is impossible, which contradicts (4). Hence O is contingent.

(7) Because O is contingent, there is a world (or state) in which the being has O and a world in which it lacks O.

(8) Define the task-type M: actualize, from a world in which one has O, a world in which O is false of oneself.

(9) M is logically possible: worlds where O is false exist, and actualizing a world is a task-type.

(10) By (1) applied in the world where the being has O, the being can perform M.

(11) Performing M makes O false of the being.

(12) Therefore, if (1) is true, then exercising it produces a state in which (1) is false.

(13) This is not a direct contradiction but a self-undermining loop: the condition that licenses applying (1) is destroyed by the very exercise of (1).

(14) Generalizing, for any property P with the form "can do all tasks compatible with P," the task "destroy P" is initially compatible (since P is contingent), hence included in P's scope; but performing it removes P, hence the task was never compatible after all, so P's scope is empty.

(15) Thus no non-empty property of this form can exist; omnipotence is impossible not because of a stone, but because its defining quantifier collapses upon itself.

The conclusion strikes me as absurd—not just that an omnipotent being cannot exist, but that the conceptual structure of omnipotence is incoherent in the same way a universal set is incoherent. I want to keep (1) as the definition and I am reluctant to reject (3) or (6), since self-limitation and contingency seem straightforward. I suspect the error is in (8)–(10), where there may be a hidden scope shift between performing a task within a world and actualizing an entire world, or perhaps in (14), where I illegitimately move from "the task removes the condition" to "the task was never in the scope." Which step should I reject? And if I reject (10), does that mean an omnipotent being is modally isolated—unable to actualize worlds where it lacks its powers? Or if I reject (14), am I committed to allowing self-undermining properties after all?
