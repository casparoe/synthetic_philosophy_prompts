Next Thursday I am presenting my paper, “Belief as Grammatical Frame: Wittgenstein on Religion and the Analogy to Mathematics,” to the philosophy of religion colloquium at a mid-sized research university. Since my father’s terminal diagnosis three months ago, the question of whether religious belief is a form of life or a propositional claim has become personally urgent; I am defending this interpretation not merely as exegesis but as a conceptual framework I am trying to inhabit.

My thesis is that Wittgenstein’s scattered remarks on religious belief—particularly in the so-called Lectures on Religious Belief and in related notebook entries—should be read through the lens of his philosophy of mathematics rather than through the standard non-cognitivist interpretation. I argue that religious belief functions neither as an empirical hypothesis nor as a purely expressive attitude, but as a grammatical rule analogous to the “hardness” of logical and mathematical necessity: it makes certain judgments about suffering, death, and value possible without being justified by evidence, and it operates within a “form of life” in the precise sense that agreement in judgments is presupposed rather than argued for. This allows religious belief to be rational and truth-apt—capable of being correct or mistaken within its frame—without collapsing into evidentialist natural theology on one side or fideist non-cognitivism on the other.

The audience will include Dr. Sarah Chen, an analytic theologian who defends moderate evidentialism; Dr. Marcus Webb, a Wittgenstein specialist committed to the orthodox reading that religious belief is essentially a regulating picture with no truth-conditional content; Dr. Elena Vasquez, a phenomenologist of religion skeptical of “intellectualist” readings; and several metaphysics graduate students recently immersed in debates about the separateness of persons, who are primed to ask whether framework-relative belief dissolves genuine disagreement into incommensurability.
