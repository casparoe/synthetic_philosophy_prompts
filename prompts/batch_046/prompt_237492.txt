Next Thursday I will deliver the opening paper at an interdisciplinary workshop on Moral Psychology and Early Modern Europe, co-sponsored by the history and philosophy departments. My thesis is that Montaigne's essay on cannibals—commonly interpreted either as a proto-anthropological exercise in cultural relativism or as a skeptical rhetorical mirror—should instead be read through the conceptual frame I am calling institutional scaling of cruelty. I contend that Montaigne is not primarily comparing cultures in the abstract, but comparing the institutional architectures that contain or amplify violence: the Tupinambá ritual practices occur within tightly bounded, face-to-face social structures that severely limit the scale of harm, whereas European societies of the same era had developed bureaucratic, religious, and military institutions that allowed cruelty to expand well beyond individual moral intention. On this reading, Montaigne's juxtaposition of cannibal rituals with the religious violence of the Wars of Religion functions not as an invitation to suspend cross-cultural judgment, but as a comparative argument about how institutional complexity transforms the moral quality of shared human capacities. The framing is deliberately anachronistic—I am importing vocabulary from comparative institutional analysis into a sixteenth-century text—but I defend it as philosophical reconstruction rather than historical attribution.

The audience will be divided between senior historians of early modern France, who are deeply suspicious of projecting modern sociological categories onto Renaissance texts, and philosophers of culture and moral psychology, who are more open to conceptual reconstruction but will press me on whether Montaigne ever articulates anything resembling a theory of institutional causation. Several attendees have published specifically on Montaigne's use of the New World as a mirror for European self-critique, and the room cares intensely about source fidelity, rhetorical context, and whether philosophical readings distort the skeptical aims of the Essays.
