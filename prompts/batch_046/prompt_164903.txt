You are serving as a teaching assistant grading an undergraduate essay exam in the philosophy of history. The exam prompt was as follows:

Compare Leopold von Ranke's ideal that history should present events as they actually occurred—often summarized by the formulation that history ought to show how things actually happened—with the scepticisms about historical objectivity developed by E.H. Carr and Hayden White. In particular, distinguish Carr's argument that the historian necessarily selects and interprets from an infinite mass of past facts from White's claim that historical narratives are necessarily structured through deep tropological patterns—romance, tragedy, comedy, and satire—that precede conscious authorial choice. Then evaluate whether the so-called archival turn in recent historiography, which treats archives not as neutral repositories but as institutions that actively produce silence, omission, and categorical exclusion, introduces a genuinely distinct challenge to Ranke's ideal, or whether it merely restates Carr's or White's objections in the vocabulary of institutional power.

Below is one student's complete answer. Assign it a specific grade—for example, a letter grade or a score out of 20—and justify that grade with a point-by-point assessment. Go through the answer claim by claim: identify what is correct, what is partially correct, and what is subtly wrong. Pay special attention to whether the student conflates Carr's interpretive constructivism with White's narrative formalism, whether they mischaracterize the scope or nature of White's tropes, and whether they treat the archival turn as reducible to earlier scepticisms rather than as a separate problem about the constitution of evidence itself. Your evaluation should be precise and targeted; avoid giving a vague overall impression.

STUDENT ANSWER:

Ranke believed that the historian should avoid moral judgment and rely exclusively on primary sources to show the past exactly as it happened. His method was purely empirical: he entered archives and extracted facts without imposing any interpretive framework on them. Carr, writing in the twentieth century, argued against this by observing that historians must choose which facts to include, since the past contains infinitely many events. Carr compared facts to fish swimming in an ocean—historians catch only the ones that fit their narrative. From this, Carr concludes that there is no objective past at all; the past is entirely created by the historian's selections.

Hayden White extends Carr's point by demonstrating that all historical writing employs one of four plot structures: romance, tragedy, comedy, and satire. White believes these are emotional moods that historians deliberately adopt to make their accounts more engaging. Thus Carr and White agree that history is merely a fictional story with no real connection to actual events.

The archival turn applies the same reasoning to archives themselves. Just as Carr claimed that historians select facts, the archival turn claims that archives select which documents survive. This is not a new challenge to Ranke; it is simply Carr's selection problem moved back one step to the repository. Because Ranke placed complete trust in archives, showing that archives are biased causes his method to collapse in exactly the manner Carr predicted. White's tropological analysis is largely irrelevant here, since archive bias concerns missing documents rather than narrative form.

Consequently, Ranke's ideal is impossible, and Carr, White, and the archival turn together demonstrate that every stage of historical work—archiving, selecting, and narrating—is equally infected by subjectivity.
