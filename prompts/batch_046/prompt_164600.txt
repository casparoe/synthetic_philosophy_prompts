I have recently been tasked with auditing evaluation pipelines in a research setting, and this has made me fixate on a peculiar inversion that seems to combine Darwinian and Turing-style reasoning. Darwin showed that adaptive design can emerge through selection without any comprehending agent; Turing showed that correct computation can occur through formal rules without semantic understanding. Both are classic cases of competence without comprehension. But I keep encountering a further twist: when an organization deploys an optimization or selection mechanism—say, an iterative filtering system or a learning procedure that adjusts parameters without access to meaning—the success of the output often leads observers to infer that some hidden reasoning or intentionality must have guided the process. Rather than accepting competence without comprehension, they treat the very adaptiveness of the result as proof that comprehension was present all along, just concealed. This feels like a second-order inversion: not merely achieving results without understanding, but using the achievement of results to retroactively deny the absence of understanding. Does this specific pattern—the reflexive attribution of hidden comprehension to a blindly competent mechanism—have an established name in philosophy of science or philosophy of mind? If there is no precise term, what distinguishes it from the intentional stance, the argument from design, or ordinary black-box reliance?
