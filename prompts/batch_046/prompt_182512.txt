Consider the disjunctivist thesis that in veridical perception the phenomenal character of an experience is constituted by the actual presence of the perceived object and its properties, whereas in a matching hallucination or illusion there is no such constituent—so that the two states share no mental kind in common, despite being subjectively indistinguishable. Defenders often treat the argument from illusion as neutralized by rejecting the common-factor inference, sometimes analogizing the case to a genuine banknote and a perfect counterfeit that feel identical but differ in kind. Play the position straight: what exactly would have to obtain for disjunctivism to be false? For each of the view's core commitments—say, the claim that introspective access does not reveal a shared internal vehicle, the claim that phenomenal properties are not supervenient on brain states alone, and the claim that perceptual knowledge can be direct rather than inferential—specify what kind of evidence or argument would count as a genuine defeater rather than a mere anomaly that can be absorbed by qualification. Would a neuroscientific result identifying a single necessary-and-sufficient neural pattern underlying both veridical and hallucinatory reports of the same content be decisive, or could the disjunctivist redescribe it? Would a formal demonstration that any theory denying a common factor must either collapse into adverbialism or fail to explain the structure of comparative judgments about what an experience is like be fatal? What risky predictions does disjunctivism make—perhaps about the transparency of experience, about the impossibility of identifying a hallucination from the first-person perspective alone, or about the explanatory redundancy of representational content—that its rivals do not share, and are these predictions empirically exposed? Finally, assess whether the view is well insulated: are its defensive moves, such as reinterpreting neural data, restricting the scope of introspection, or denying that subjective matching requires mental matching, principled adjustments or ad hoc protections? Conclude with an honest judgment about how exposed the standard disjunctivist position actually is.
