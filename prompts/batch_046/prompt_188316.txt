Consider specifically a nested-simulation variant, in which posthuman civilizations routinely run ancestor simulations that themselves reach posthumanity and spawn further simulations. Does this nesting genuinely weaken the moral case for reducing existential risk in our present world? In your answer, address (i) what Bostrom's original trilemma actually establishes regarding our epistemic position, (ii) whether causal isolation between nesting levels implies moral irrelevance for actions taken at our level, and (iii) one reason that expected-value reasoning may fail to transfer across an indefinite hierarchy of simulations.

Student answer:
Bostrom's trilemma states that either humanity goes extinct before reaching posthumanity, posthuman civilizations choose never to run simulations, or we are almost certainly living in a simulation. If we apply this reasoning recursively, then every simulated civilization that achieves posthuman status will generate its own nested simulations, producing an infinite hierarchy in which each level contains far more minds than the level above it. Because of this, the expected moral value of preserving any single world—including ours—is divided across all nested levels, which makes longtermist projects to prevent extinction statistically negligible unless we can prove we occupy the base reality, something Bostrom demonstrates is impossible. In addition, Bostrom maintains that simulated beings have lower moral weight than base-reality beings, so even a successful intervention in our world would likely only protect copies of limited ethical significance. Therefore, the nested-simulation variant shows that intense concern for our distant future is both epistemically unsupported and morally disproportionate.
