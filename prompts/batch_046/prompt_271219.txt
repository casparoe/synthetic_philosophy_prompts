I'm in the middle of writing a paper for my comparative ethics semniar and I'm stuck on whether brain size does any actual normative work, or if it's just a noisy proxy I'm smuggling in. Going by gut: an average human brain is about 1.3kg, a sperm whale's is around 8kg, but I don't think the whale gets extra rights just for bulk. A human with severe hydrocephalus where the tissue is compressed to a fraction of normal volume is still fully in the moral community, and someone with macrocephaly doesn't recieve double status. So inside our species, absolute volume seems completely inert.

But then I compare across species and my intuitons get messier. A mouse brain weighs about half a gram, an elephant's is around five kilos, and I do think the elephant's richer mental life should count for something -- though maybe that's neuron architecture, not kilograms. Crows have tiny brains but I think they're closer to mammals cognitively than their weight suggests. The case I keep going back and forth on is this: imagine two non-human animals with identical behavior and suffering capacities, but one has had its brain artifically doubled with non-functional tissue. I think they have identical moral status, which means size isn't tracking anything real. Yet comparing that original small-brained creature to a whale, part of me wants to say the whale's larger, more complex brain supports a stronger claim. So is there a principle that saves both the intra-species irrelevance and the cross-species pull without just saying size matters except when it doesn't? And specifically, does the artificial-doubling case force me to drop the whale intuition, or can I save it by treating size as only an indirect marker for cognitive complexity? I'd also like to know what your principle predicts for a case I haven't listed -- say, a genetically engineered human with triple normal brain volume but identical consciousness.
