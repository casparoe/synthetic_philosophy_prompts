I've been running a reading group on philosophies of history and we've hit a wall that feels weirdly familiar. We started with the standard cyclical vs linear framing—Polybius's anacyclosis, Ibn Khaldun's asabiyya decay, Spengler's culture-morphology, Toynbee's challenge-response loops—versus the linear-progress story about cumulative tech and expanding moral circles. The problem is that for about a year now, every time someone brings a new case study—say, Qing recovery patterns, or the shift from Roman aqueduct maintanence to medieval reuse—both sides just redescribe it in their own vocabulary. The cyclical people call it institutional entropy recurring; the linear people call it local noise inside a longer directional trajectory. Nobody has changed their mind, but also nobody can point to an empirical finding that the other side would accept as falsifing. So I'm trying to figure out: is this actually a substantive disagreement about hidden facts (like whether cultural memory survives collapse, or whether tech knowledge is genuinely lost), or is it mostly verbal—just a dispute about what temporal scale counts as the right one? Or maybe it's bedrock: an unargued intuition that human agency can or can't break historical patterns, which neither side can defend without begging the question. If it's the scale issue, would a formal model of recurrence at different resolutions help, or is the whole framing ill-posed because "history" doesn't have a single scale? I want the diagnosis, not another round of examples.
