I'm a PhD student in aesthetics putting together the final for my graduate seminar on authenticity, reproduction, and value. I need one essay prompt—45 minutes, 40 percent of the exam—on fakes and forgeries that students cannot answer by reciting textbook summaries. Center the question on this constructed case: a museum installs a chemically and visually perfect digital projection of a lost Vermeer, displays it without labeling it as reproduction, and relies entirely on institutional authentication procedures (provenance records, curatorial authority) to establish its status. The question should require students to work through two frameworks: first, the autographic/allographic distinction, asking whether a digital forgery of a lost original counts as a forgery at all when no extant original exists to counterfeit, and whether syntactic density or institutional notation can substitute for physical uniqueness; second, the concept of aura and the degradation of presence through reproduction, asking whether aura requires an actually existing original or can be projected onto a perfect stand-in, and whether museum framing can restore or simulate that presence. The model answer should run about 650 words and must contain four specific moves: (1) a precise application of the autographic/allographic distinction to the lost-canvas scenario; (2) an argument about whether historical presence depends on material continuity or can be institutionally reconstructed; (3) an assessment of whether perfect visual and chemical similarity resolves the ethical problem of displaying the work unlabeled; and (4) a defended conclusion on whether the museum's practice is aesthetically or ethically problematic, with explicit reasoning rather than mere intuition. The marking scheme should separate an A from a B answer by naming concrete moves: an A must identify the tension between production-history and institutional authentication, must not treat the two frameworks as simply complementary, and must address the specific problem of the missing original; a B should be marked down for summarizing the theories separately without applying them to the digital-forgery case, for assuming visual identity automatically establishes value, or for failing to take a reasoned position on the museum's display. Do not ask students to provide a bibliography, citation list, or page references.
