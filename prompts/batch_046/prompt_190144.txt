Draft the joint statement that a defender of the Cartesian theater model of consciousness and a defender of the multiple drafts model could both sign after a good-faith adversarial collaboration. Treat the Cartesian theater camp as holding that conscious experience requires a singular, unified presentation—an integrated “show” delivered to a central functional audience, whether conceived as a Cartesian ego or as a unified integration point—while the multiple drafts camp holds that there is no such central screen; instead, parallel, continuously edited narrative streams compete, and content is fixed only in the particular modalities that happen to probe them.

Format the deliverable as a numbered agreed-claims section followed by a disagreements table. In the agreed-claims section, list propositions both sides can endorse about: (i) the phenomenological data that require explanation, including the apparent unity of consciousness, the prevalence of post-hoc confabulation, and the variability of verbal versus non-verbal report; (ii) the neuroscientific constraints both accept, specifically that cortical processing is massively parallel and that no single anatomical locus serves as a literal screen; and (iii) the logical architecture of the dispute, particularly whether the absence of a central anatomical locus necessarily entails the absence of a functional center of integration.

In the disagreements table, state each camp’s position in wording its own members would accept, not in the opponent’s framing. Include at least these cruxes: (A) whether the subjective impression of a unified stream is best explained by an actual integration mechanism or by the retrospective binding of separately edited drafts; (B) whether a split-modality arbitration-failure case—in which verbal report tracks one perceptual content while an immediate affective or motor response tracks a contradictory one, with no hierarchical resolution activated—counts as evidence against the theater model or merely as two drafts being probed independently; and (C) whether the notion of a “self” as the audience in the theater can be reconstructed in purely functional terms without reintroducing the very centralization that the drafts model rejects.

For each crux, identify a concrete case, argument, or predicted observation that both sides agree bears on it, and record in advance what each side predicts the correct analysis or outcome would be. One crux should turn on a niche, non-standard variant: an “interleaved-drafts” condition in which editing processes overlap in real time rather than proceeding sequentially, producing transient hybrid narratives that fit neither camp’s standard taxonomy, so that resolving the case requires more than repeating familiar objections.

Conclude with a brief section distinguishing parts of the dispute that are merely verbal—such as disagreements over whether “consciousness” analytically requires a single subject—from parts that are substantively empirical or conceptual, and specify exactly what finding or argument would cause at least one camp to revise its core commitment. Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citations; the document should stand on conceptual and argumentative grounds alone.
