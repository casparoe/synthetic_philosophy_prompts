Read Ursula K. Le Guin's short story The Ones Who Walk Away from Omelas through the lens of population axiology, focusing specifically on the repugnant conclusion and the asymmetry between creating lives with positive and negative welfare. The narrative depicts a flourishing society whose happiness depends on the perpetual suffering of one child confined in squalor; most citizens accept this arrangement, while a few choose to leave. First, ask whether the citizens' acceptance implicitly endorses a total-utilitarian aggregation that permits extreme suffering in exchange for large aggregate gains, and whether the walkers reject that calculus on critical-level grounds—treating the child's misery as lexically overriding any sum of happiness—or on person-affecting grounds that deny moral relevance to merely possible people. Then scale the scenario up: suppose preserving Omelas required not one agonized child but ten thousand children each enduring mild discomfort, or suppose it required bringing into existence a vast population of lives barely worth living whose collective welfare numerically outweighed the victim's pain. Would the moral urgency of walking away diminish, stay constant, or even invert? Does the story's horror depend on a kind of numerical particularism—the refusal to dissolve a concrete, visible victim into an impersonal aggregate—or does it instead expose a bias toward small, spatially proximate populations over diffuse ones? Consider also the phenomenology of complicity: the citizens feast in spaces whose floorboards literally cover the suffering, suggesting that spatial and relational proximity, not just welfare arithmetic, structures moral responsibility. Where does formal population ethics miss this dimension? Finally, adjudicate whether Le Guin dramatizes a genuine objection to totalist aggregation—perhaps showing that instrumentalizing one life for others corrupts the value of the happiness produced—or whether she smuggles in an unjustified preference for vivid, small-scale harm that would mislead us about real-world obligations to distant or numerous people. What, if anything, does the story add to the philosophical debate that axiological argument alone cannot capture?
