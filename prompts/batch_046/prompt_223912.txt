Consider the bifurcated institutional practice found in many European occupational-health and disability-compensation frameworks, whereby conditions such as pre-diabetes or pre-hypertension—defined purely by statistical deviation from population epidemiological baselines—are treated as fully legitimate clinical entities warranting medical intervention and, in some jurisdictions, workplace accommodation, even when the affected individual reports no symptoms and no functional impairment. By contrast, conditions defined chiefly by subjective experience—such as burnout, moral injury among healthcare workers, or chronic fatigue—are granted compensatory or clinical standing only when they can be translated into an established psychiatric taxonomy (for instance, major depressive disorder or PTSD) supported by identifiable biomarkers or demonstrable functional limitation. The result is a dual-track epistemology: physical states achieve disease status through naturalistic deviation alone, while psychological or psychosomatic states require an additional normative judgment about dysfunction, suffering, or social impairment.

What implicit philosophical theory of health and disease does this institutional design presuppose? Reconstruct the account it tacitly endorses, distinguishing carefully between what the practice strictly requires and what it merely accommodates. Does it implicitly adopt a biostatistical naturalism for bodily conditions while inconsistently demanding a normativist criterion—harm, dysfunction, or subjective suffering—for mental ones? Or does it presuppose a unified normativist framework applied selectively on grounds of institutional tractability rather than conceptual coherence? Assess whether the practice is philosophically underdetermined: could a consistently naturalist or consistently normativist theory license the same outcomes, and what would have to change in clinical assessment, compensation law, and surveillance policy if we eliminated the bifurcation?
