In philosophy of action and comparative cognition there has long been a stalemate over what is usually called “sphexishness”—the mechanically rigid, loop-like behavior of the sphex wasp, which executes a fixed sequence of actions even when the conditions that originally triggered them have been altered or removed, and which is often treated as the limiting case against which genuinely flexible, free human action is defined. One camp holds that freedom is essentially the capacity to break sphexish loops: to suspend habitual routines, respond to reasons rather than environmental triggers, and display counterfactual robustness, meaning that if circumstances or reasons had been different the agent would have acted differently without requiring an entirely different causal history. The opposing camp replies that apparent flexibility is merely layered sphexishness—more computationally complex deterministic patterns—so that the presence or absence of rigidity has no bearing on whether an action is free, and that debates about determinism should therefore be separated entirely from observations about behavioral rigidity. For years the exchange has cycled through the same examples—habit formation, expert automaticity, compulsive behavior, failures of cognitive control—and appealed to the same neuroscientific literature on prefrontal regulation, yet neither side has moved.

My own suspicion is that the impasse is not about determinism at all, but about two unacknowledged metrics for rigidity: one side measures it structurally, as the presence of fixed action-patterns regardless of internal complexity, while the other measures it modally, as the absence of counterfactual sensitivity to reasons regardless of causal determinism. If that is right, the sides may be answering different questions—one about the phenomenology and architecture of deliberation, one about causal openness—while using the same vocabulary of “flexibility” and “mechanical rigidity.” But I am also open to the possibility that a hidden empirical question is doing the work—perhaps about whether human prefrontal control introduces a genuinely different causal structure or merely adds computational depth to an otherwise deterministic loop—and that the conceptual disagreement is only a proxy for an unsettled scientific one. I want a diagnosis, not an adjudication: is this disagreement verbal, substantive but masked by ambiguous terms, a case of two different research programs running under one heading, or a sign that the framing of sphexishness versus freedom is itself ill-posed? Please engage what each side actually says about the wasp analogy and about human habit, rather than offering a generic survey of compatibilist positions. And given your diagnosis, what would count as progress—a sharper conceptual distinction between structural and modal rigidity, an empirical finding about cognitive control, a formal result about counterfactual reasoning in deterministic systems, or an argument that no progress should be expected and why?
