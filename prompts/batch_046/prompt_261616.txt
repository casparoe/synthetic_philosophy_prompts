You are delivering the opening address at the dedication of the Meridian Archive, a new repository that holds declassified government records and rigorously corrected survivor testimonies pertaining to three twentieth-century mass atrocities: the Holocaust, the Armenian genocide, and the Nanjing Massacre. The room is deliberately mixed: senior historians who know that archival discovery routinely revises specific figures, locations, and timelines; survivors and descendants for whom any alteration to the established narrative risks feeling like an erasure; and undergraduate students encountering these materials for the first time. The archive's inaugural publication contains a documented correction to a widely cited statistic and chronology for one of the three events—an adjustment that changes nothing about the moral reality or scale of the atrocity, but that denialist networks have already begun to quote out of context.

Write a ten-minute speech in which the conceptual distinction between legitimate historical revisionism and denialism functions as the structural backbone, not as decoration. Let that distinction determine exactly what you can and cannot say about the corrected record: show where careful revision ends and denial begins, but do not name either concept until the final third of the address, and never sound as though you are apologizing for the archive's work. The speech must be fully audible to non-specialists—no bibliographies, no appeals to authority by citation, no footnotes—while remaining precise enough that a historian in the audience recognizes you understand why a corrected date is not a canceled genocide. Include a direct address to the survivors present that acknowledges their specific fear of alteration without conceding to it, and close by articulating what obligation the living have to a record that can be both corrected and honored. The argument must stand entirely on its conceptual architecture.
