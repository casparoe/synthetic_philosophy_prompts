I am preparing an interview for my philosophy-of-science podcast, and next week’s guest is a philosopher who defends a specific form of ontic parsimony constrained by epistemic accessibility: she maintains that when two candidate ontologies are empirically equivalent but differ only in properties hidden behind a principled informational barrier—such as gauge-variant internal configurations or causally isolated modal profiles—we should not treat them as distinct entities, but rather as one ontology described in redundant ways. Before drafting anything, please adopt the role of an experienced philosophy interviewer with deep familiarity in metaphysics and philosophy of physics, and briefly brainstorm the three pressure points her view typically faces: first, the charge that it collapses into standard structural realism without genuine novelty; second, the objection that it illicitly conflates epistemology with ontology by letting ignorance dictate being; and third, the problem of ontological identification across theory change when the informational barriers themselves are not stable. Then draft eight to ten interview questions that move well beyond a restatement of her position. I want at least one question that presses her on how she distinguishes her view from orthodox structural realism and whether she retains any thinner notion of objecthood beyond pure structure; include a follow-up keyed to her likely reply that she does preserve a minimal objecthood anchored to accessible relational roles. I also want at least one question about the epistemology-ontology boundary—specifically, whether there are cases where we should positively affirm an entity’s existence precisely because its informational inaccessibility explains an observable asymmetry; include a follow-up anticipating her answer that explanatory indispensability can sometimes override accessibility constraints. Finally, include a question on shifting informational barriers across theoretical frameworks, with a follow-up addressing her probable appeal to invariant relational patterns that survive the shift. Keep the tone rigorous yet accessible to an informed non-specialist audience, avoid generic biographical or “how did you get into philosophy” questions, and do not request citations, bibliographies, or reading lists.
