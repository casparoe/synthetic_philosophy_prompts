At a recent study-group meeting, two members—Jordan and Sam—fell into a sustained argument about whether French laïcité and Anglo-American multiculturalism are fundamentally opposed or secretly convergent. They kept returning to the French prohibition on conspicuous religious symbols in state schools, enacted in 2004, which applies to Muslim headscarves, Sikh turbans, and large Christian crosses alike.

Jordan opened by saying the law is not anti-multicultural but multiculturalism applied rigorously. The point of accommodating difference, Jordan argued, is to protect inclusive shared institutions. If visible religious symbols in classrooms create coercive pressure—particularly on girls from conservative families—then banning them protects minority students from majority religious dominance. On this view, laïcité and multiculturalism share the same goal: a neutral space where citizens interact without sectarian hierarchy. The difference is only aesthetic—France excludes particularistic identity from the public sphere, while Anglo-American policy includes it with safeguards.

Sam replied that this misreads multiculturalism’s core commitment. Anglo-American multiculturalism, in both its Canadian and British forms, does not merely tolerate difference; it recognizes group-differentiated rights inside public institutions. Allowing a Sikh turban in a police uniform or accommodating Indigenous ceremonial practice in court is not a failure of neutrality but an acknowledgment that so-called neutral spaces are already structured by majority cultural norms. The 2004 law, Sam argued, forces observant Muslim girls to choose between education and religious obligation. Even if the statutory text applies to all symbols, the practical burden falls asymmetrically on one group. So the law functions as assimilation, not protection.

Jordan answered that Sam was conflating multicultural recognition with the demands of political liberalism. Democratic institutions, Jordan said, must be justified by reasons all citizens can share regardless of their comprehensive doctrines. Religious symbols in schools introduce exactly such doctrines into a space meant for shared citizenship. Jordan added that the law applies to large Christian crosses too, so it is not asymmetrical in principle, only in statistical frequency. If every symbol is permitted, Jordan continued, we do not achieve inclusive neutrality; we achieve what Jordan called symbolic pluralism, where schools become competitive marketplaces for group identity rather than spaces of common education. Jordan then pushed further: democratic solidarity requires what might be termed civic monism—a single, unmarked public identity—because the visibility of particularistic commitments fragments the shared civic bond.

Sam conceded that asymmetry in legal text is not identical to asymmetry in social impact, but insisted that impact is precisely what justice must track. A large cross is rarely worn as a daily obligation; a headscarf is. To treat them identically is formal equality that masks substantive inequality. Moreover, Sam argued, Jordan’s civic monism is not a neutral framework but a particular cultural formation—rooted in Catholic history, Enlightenment rationalism, and republican nationalism—that has succeeded in rendering itself invisible while marking minority practices as particularistic. Sam pointed to Canada and the UK, where allowing religious symbols in public roles has not undermined citizenship but strengthened institutional credibility among minorities. The unstated assumption in Jordan’s position, Sam concluded, is that minority visibility inherently threatens solidarity, which reveals that the universal secular norm is actually a specific identity disguised as transcendence.

Jordan responded that importing Canadian or British examples misses the conceptual architecture of French citizenship. In the republican tradition, citizenship is defined precisely by the transcendence of particularistic identities—religious, ethnic, or regional—not by their accommodation. Comparing practical outcomes ignores the different meaning assigned to citizenship in each tradition. What looks like assimilation from an Anglo-American framework may be emancipation within a republican framework.

Sam countered that transcendence is never actually achieved; it merely renders dominant norms invisible. The secular French classroom is not a culture-empty space but one filled with unacknowledged cultural content. By refusing to recognize Muslim identity as a legitimate part of public citizenship, laïcité denies recognition rather than providing neutrality. The disagreement, Sam said, ultimately hinges on whether one believes minority practices inherently threaten democratic solidarity—a belief that itself requires justification, not mere assertion.

Neither convinced the other. They asked me to referee.

Please adjudicate their exchange round by round. For each of the four main moves above, rate Jordan’s contribution, Sam’s contribution, or both as clearly mistaken, partially right, or clearly right, and briefly justify each rating. Then say who is more right overall, and identify at least one significant issue that both Jordan and Sam are missing—perhaps something about the practical institutional conditions under which either framework succeeds or fails, or about whether their conceptual disagreement actually determines policy outcomes in the way they assume.
