I'd like to read Oscar Wilde's The Picture of Dorian Gray through the lens of the wrong-kind-of-reasons problem—specifically the debate over whether self-interested, aesthetic, or fear-driven motives can ever supply the right kind of reason for valuing moral goods. In the novel, Dorian treats his preserved youth and the portrait's hidden corruption as aesthetic assets to be managed rather than as moral states demanding response; does Wilde dramatize Michael Smith's worry about moral fetishism, or does the portrait function more like a Scanlon-style buck-passing device that makes value visible without making it motivationally efficacious? I'm looking for a steel-man reading that takes both the gothic mechanics and the philosophy seriously: point to scenes where Dorian clearly acts on wrong-kind reasons (the initial wish, the murder of Basil, the final knife), but also say where the novel resists reduction to a philosophical illustration—perhaps by blurring the line between aesthetic and moral reasons in ways that challenge the very distinction. If you were guiding a rationalist-adjacent book club, what is the one insight about moral motivation this novel delivers that a straight conceptual argument about wrong-kind reasons tends to miss?
