I spent much of last evening arguing with a friend about the experience machine and theories of well-being, and I want a consistency check on my own package of views before I defend it further. I hold: (1) Nozick's experience-machine intuition is sound—permanent, undetectable immersion in a pleasant simulation is not good for well-being, so mental-state hedonism fails. (2) Well-being is nevertheless essentially first-personal and attitudinal: if a subject sincerely and stably judges her life to be going well, there is no further objective fact of the matter that she is wrong; this is my reason for rejecting objective-list and perfectionist accounts. (3) Knowingly entering a high-fidelity virtual environment can be just as conducive to well-being as the corresponding physical-world situation, since agency, social relations, and mental states can be structurally preserved. (4) The problem with the machine is not deception—I am considering a fully transparent version—but that the occupant's mental states lack what I call modal robustness: they depend on a single fragile causal chain and would not persist across nearby possible worlds in which the occupant's beliefs are true. (5) At the same time, I deny that well-being requires causal efficacy with the actual external world; mental causation internal to the simulation should suffice, provided the structure is right. Do these commitments hang together? In particular, does (2) not commit me to accepting the transparent machine whenever the occupant approves, thereby undermining (1) and (4)? Is (4) smuggling in an objective condition that (2) explicitly rejects? And is there a real tension between (3) and (5), or only an apparent one? Please rank any genuine tensions by severity, identify which pairs only look incompatible, specify the least costly revision (I am least attached to (4)), and say what I would have to give up to preserve (2).
