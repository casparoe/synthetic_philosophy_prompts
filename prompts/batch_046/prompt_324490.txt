I am a novelist with a working familiarity with narrative theory—focalization, diegetic nesting, unreliable narration, free indirect discourse, and the practical management of character interiority across altered states of consciousness. I want you to restate the philosophical disagreement between Norman Malcolm and Daniel Dennett over whether dreams are genuine experiences, translated entirely into the vocabulary of narrative craft and story architecture, and then to deliver a precise residue report on what survives, what distorts, and what vanishes in the translation.

For Malcolm’s position—that the sleeper is not present as a subject and that dream reports are retrospective constructions rather than descriptions of events actually undergone—map it onto the novelist’s problems of absent focalization, frame narratives that invent an interiority where none was registered at the time, and the distinction between a story told about a character and a story experienced from within that character’s point of view. For Dennett’s opposing view—that mental events occur during sleep and are subsequently loaded into memory upon awakening, so that dreams are experiences albeit reconstructed in the telling—map it onto retroactive narrative stitching, nested diegetic levels in which a second-order narrator (the waking self) edits and assembles a first-order scene (the sleeping self), and forms of unreliable narration in which the report is not false but assembled post-hoc from fragments. Be exact: do not merely say that one side resembles unreliable narration; specify whether the unreliability lies in the focalizer, the narrator, the diegetic frame, or the temporal order of composition.

Then provide the residue report. Which philosophical claims translate cleanly into narrative terms? Which survive only with distortion—for example, does Dennett’s notion of dream-content being loaded into consciousness upon awakening correspond to any technique a novelist uses when reconstructing a character’s past, or does it require assuming a storage and retrieval mechanism that narrative theory has no need to posit? Does Malcolm’s argument depend on a metaphysical claim about subjecthood and presence that has no practical counterpart in the novelist’s problem of writing an unconscious protagonist? Identify any part of the exchange that has no expression whatsoever in narrative vocabulary, and say whether that absence indicates a genuine limitation of the craft framework or suggests that the philosophical point was less substantive than it appeared.

To keep this from floating away into pure abstraction, anchor your translation in a concrete craft problem I actually face: constructing a scene in which a protagonist is unconscious—under deep anesthesia, in a coma, or in dreamless sleep—yet the novel reports what they “experienced” during that interval. How would Malcolm and Dennett, rendered into my vocabulary, advise me to build that scene? Does one view imply that I must resort to an external narrator or a frame story, while the other permits direct focalization if I treat the report as retroactively assembled? After addressing that practical application, return to the residue: what does the novelist’s need for coherent point-of-view management and readerly immersion miss about the philosophical dispute, and conversely, what does the philosophical dispute overlook about the practical demands of maintaining narrative coherence?

Write the prompt as a single, continuous request that assumes I understand both the debate and the narrative concepts named, but that does not ask for bibliographies, reading lists, citations, or verbatim quotations. I want the translation to remain honest—do not smuggle narrative assumptions into the philosophical positions, or philosophical assumptions into the narrative framework, and call the result a faithful rendering.
