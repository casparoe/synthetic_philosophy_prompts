I spent thirty years as an experimental physicist working on precision instrumentation, and since retiring I have been reading moral philosophy with the habits of someone trained to worry about noise floors, calibration errors, and the difference between a theoretical limit and an operational failure. I have drafted notes for an essay on demandingness objections to consequentialism, and I am writing to ask whether my framing introduces a genuinely distinct line of argument or merely redecorates familiar complaints. The standard discussion—associated with Scheffler and the literature on agent-relative prerogatives—treats demandingness primarily as a motivational or psychological burden: consequentialism requires too much sacrifice, too constant a calculation, too little room for personal projects. I want to propose a different variant, which I call the problem of normative resolution. The idea is that consequentialist reasoning requires an agent to discriminate between outcomes and to assign precise comparative weights to states of affairs that differ in kind, probability, and temporal distance. But just as every measuring instrument has a fundamental noise floor that makes reporting values below its resolving power misleading rather than merely difficult, moral cognition may have an analogous epistemic and representational limit. If the precision demanded by consequentialist calculation exceeds the resolution available to any embodied agent—if it asks us to rank outcomes with a fineness that our normative apparatus cannot reliably deliver—then the objection is not simply that consequentialism is too costly, but that it asks us to perform a kind of computation that is, in principle, representationally spurious. This is not, I think, identical to the standard epistemic objection that we cannot know all the consequences of our actions; it concerns the internal structure of comparative judgment itself. I would welcome comments on three specific points. First, is this distinction between motivational demandingness and normative resolution coherent, or does it collapse into either a generic claim about human cognitive limits or a disguised version of the agent-relative prerogative? Second, does my measurement analogy do useful philosophical work, or does it import assumptions from physical science that distort the nature of moral reasoning? Third, if this framing holds up, what kind of response is available to a consequentialist—must they deny that precision is required, or can they accommodate a notion of approximate optimization without abandoning the core theory? I am particularly concerned that I may be conflating issues in decision theory under uncertainty with specifically moral objections, and I would value suggestions on how to keep those domains separated in the essay.
