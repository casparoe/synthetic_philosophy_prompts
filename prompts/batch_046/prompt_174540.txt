I've been working through methodological debates in social history for a seminar on historiography, and I've become convinced of a strong claim: that microhistory and history from below are not merely correctives to elite-focused narratives but are epistemically prior to them. The position is that any macro-level causal claim about states, institutions, or class structures—for instance, that the fiscal-military state extracted surplus through a particular tax regime—necessarily risks an ecological fallacy unless every step of the causal chain has been reconstructed from granular, bottom-up documentation of individual transactions, household budgets, or local disputes. On this view, macrohistory does not simply lack narrative color without microhistory; it lacks independent justification. State-centered explanations are legitimate only as syntheses of already-verified micro-level patterns, not as autonomous frameworks that can be confirmed through aggregate data, institutional archives, or elite correspondence alone. I want to know what would genuinely count against this—not the familiar objections that microhistory is anecdotal or unrepresentative, which I think miss the epistemic point, but the defeaters that strike at the heart of the claim.

Specifically, I am asking for cases where macro-level explanations have been vindicated despite lacking micro-level reconstruction; for arguments that the ecological fallacy does not apply to historical causation in the same way it applies to statistical correlation; and for theoretical reasons why individual-level documentation might systematically mislead about structural dynamics, perhaps by obscuring the very mechanisms—coercion, bureaucratic routine, or fiscal extraction—that operate precisely because they are invisible to the individuals experiencing them. I also want an assessment of whether defenders of this epistemic-priority view can absorb these objections through qualification—say, by restricting the claim to certain kinds of causal mechanisms or to pre-statistical eras—without rendering the position unfalsifiable or trivially true. Rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, distinguish what defenders of the view should themselves concede as counting against it from what critics allege but proponents can dismiss, and be honest about whether the position, as stated, risks being insulated into a methodological dogma that no amount of successful macrohistory could dislodge. If the view survives only by retreating to an unfalsifiable core, I want that conclusion drawn explicitly.
