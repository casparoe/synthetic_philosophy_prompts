I'm taking a course on philosophy and literature this semester and am drafting a paper on justice and narrative form, so I'd like a careful philosophical reading of Dumas's The Count of Monte Cristo through the lens of karma as a theory of moral desert—specifically, the view that suffering or flourishing is distributed by an impersonal, non-agentive cosmic mechanism rather than through deliberate punishment. The novel's plot turns on Edmond Dantès's unjust imprisonment, his hidden acquisition of knowledge and wealth, and his subsequent engineering of Fernand's, Danglars's, and Villefort's ruin, often by arranging conditions in which their own vices destroy them rather than by direct execution. Does the novel present these outcomes as a kind of karmic settlement—an automatic balancing of moral accounts—or as an intentional, human project of retribution? Focus especially on the mechanics of the three downfalls: in each case Dantès sets the stage but does not personally deliver the harm. Does this indirectness make the ruin look like cosmic justice rather than personal vengeance? And does the novel's final movement—Dantès's isolation, his ambiguous relationship with Mercédès, and his departure with Haydée—suggest a philosophical limit to karma-style accounting, namely that the agent who administers the balance becomes morally entangled? I want a reading that takes both the plot's architecture and the philosophical distinction between agentive punishment and impersonal moral desert seriously, and that says whether Dumas ultimately endorses, problematizes, or remains neutral about karma as an account of justice.
