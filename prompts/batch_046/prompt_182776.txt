I am preparing material for an interdisciplinary seminar that treats societal collapse not as a self-evident catastrophe but as a contested historiographical frame. Please write a short story—no more than about eight hundred words—that dramatises this conceptual tension through the experience of a fictional archivist in the late twenty-first century who is curating fragmentary remains from three contexts: the Late Bronze Age eastern Mediterranean, the Classic-period Maya lowlands, and Rapa Nui. Rather than presenting these as parallel disasters, the narrative should unfold through an intimate, specific scene—perhaps the examination of a single artefact whose provenance is disputed, or a conversation with a local interlocutor who refuses the vocabulary of failure altogether—so that the protagonist gradually recognises that what one tradition records as the cessation of monument-building or long-distance exchange, another registers as adaptive simplification or deliberate transformation. I want the story to avoid clichéd apocalyptic imagery; instead, it should explore the ambiguity of whether societies truly fail or merely change form, using sensory and emotional detail rather than expository history.

Please compose the story in British English. After the narrative, append a brief reflective note stating how confident you are in the historical and interpretive claims embedded within the fiction, explicitly identifying the one point—whether archaeological, conceptual, or narrative—about which you are least certain, and indicating where the story takes liberties with contested scholarly readings of societal change.
