The student writes: Schopenhauer’s aesthetics in The World as Will and Representation assigns music a singular status because it is not, like painting or poetry, a representation of Platonic Ideas. Painting copies the Idea of a tree or a storm as mediated through the perceiving subject, and poetry evokes Ideas through conceptual language; both remain within the principle of sufficient reason and its fourfold root. Music, however, is a direct copy of the Will itself, expressing the inner essence of the world without passing through the representational form of Ideas. This is why Schopenhauer describes it as the universal language of feeling and why he places it above all other arts in his hierarchy. With respect to structure, Schopenhauer compares the bass line to the lowest grades of the Will’s objectification—the inorganic forces such as gravity and chemical affinity—while the melody corresponds to the highest grade, the human will with its rapid emotional fluctuations. He intends this comparison mainly as a pedagogical analogy: the listener can visualize the layered structure of reality through the layered structure of harmony and melody, but he does not claim that acoustic frequencies literally mirror ontological grades. The bass provides a stable ground analogous to nature’s laws, and the melody moves freely above it, much as conscious striving moves within but is not determined by physical law. On the question of opera, Schopenhauer clearly favors purely instrumental music. Once a libretto is added, words and narrative concepts enter the work, pulling it back into the realm of Ideas and representation. Nevertheless, Schopenhauer recognizes that opera can serve a useful function: by attaching specific emotions and dramatic situations to the musical line, the text gives the listener a conceptual framework that clarifies what the music expresses about the Will. So while the libretto dilutes music’s metaphysical purity, it also makes its content more accessible, and Schopenhauer might concede that this trade-off is acceptable for audiences who need conceptual guidance. It is also important to see Schopenhauer’s view in context. He describes music as the Dionysian power that dissolves the principle of individuation, allowing the listener to experience the primordial unity of the Will beyond individual form. Although this language sounds like Nietzsche, it belongs to Schopenhauer’s own account of how music suspends the ordinary boundaries of the self. Nietzsche later adopted and modified this insight in The Birth of Tragedy, but the core idea—that music dissolves individuation—is already present in Schopenhauer’s text. Thus Schopenhauer’s metaphysics of music stands on its own as a theory of direct Will-objectification, not merely as a precursor to later aesthetic theories.
