student answer (typed quick on my laptop):

Butterfield in his 1931 book says Whig historians write England's past like its all leading up to modern democracy. They do this by judging old actors by outcomes they never saw coming. His big distinction is that its okay to study the past with reference to the present—like asking how the Civil War shaped modern Britain—but its not okay to study it with direct and perpetual reference, where every event is just a step in our moral story. He uses the Puritans as an example: Whig writers call them champions of religious freedom, but actually they wanted a theocracy and hated modern liberal ideas. So the distortion is moral and teleogical.

Butterfield thinks this only applies to abridged history, not technical history. Technical history studies everything without selecting for present relevance, so its safe. Abridged history is always risky because selection itself is present-centered. So if you ask present-centered questions youre already doing Whig history unless you switch to technical mode. Therefore avoiding Whig history means mostly giving up abridged narrative and sticking to exaustive reconstruction.
