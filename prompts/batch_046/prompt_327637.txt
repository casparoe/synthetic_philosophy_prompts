Question: Compare the Platonia dilemma (the Hofstadter thought experiment with 20 people and the telegram) and the Scientific American Luring Lottery as experiments in superrational reasoning. Explain (a) why symmetric randomization is defended as the superrational move in Platonia, (b) why the same logic doesn't stabilize the Lottery, and (c) whether either case actually demonstrates superrationality or just exposes a correlation failure under common knowledge of rationality.

Student answer:
In Platonia, 20 people are told that if exactly one sends a telegram they win a billion dollars, but if more or none do, nobody gets anything. Since they can't cooperate, the superrational agent realizes everyone is identical, so they should all randomize with p=1/20. This is basically a Nash equilibrium in mixed strategies: each player is indifferent between sending and not sending at that probability, so it's stable.

The Luring Lottery works differently. Readers send postcards with entry numbers, and the prize is one million divided by total entries. The magazine suggested rolling a die with sides equal to the expected number of responders—about five percent of the readership—and sending "1" only on a roll of 1. If everyone did this, exactly one postcard might arrive. But in reality people sent astronomically large numbers, causing a runaway effect. This happens because common knowledge of rationality breaks down: people don't trust others to roll fairly, so they over-enter. Thus the Lottery shows that superrationality requires not just identical reasoning but also trust that others will actually follow through.

In both cases, I think what's really going on is coordination failure, not some special form of rationality. In Platonia the 1/20 strategy only works if everyone correlates perfectly, which is just a coordination problem. In the Lottery, the payoff depends on aggregate behavior in a linear way, so there's no fixed point except infinite entries. So neither experiment proves superrationality exists; they just show that symmetric reasoning collapses when payoffs are coupled to group behavior.
