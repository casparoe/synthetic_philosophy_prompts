I cover science and ideas for a broadsheet, and lately I've been stuck on a comparison between German and American folk morality that doesnt fit the usual stereotypes. In Germany, people often defend rules — queuing, recycling, tax forms — not by citing harm but by citing disorder: 'if this cracks, the whole pavement splits.' The wrongness feels almost structural, like rust in a bridge. In American progressive circles, by contrast, rules are treated as scaffolding that exists mainly so your individual conscience can knock it down in the exceptional case; the heroism is in the exception.

But I've noticed a specific deadlock that keeps recurring, and I dont know if it has a name. The German-style arguer treats any permitted exception as proof the rule was never serious — 'you either have order or chaos, there's no middle gear' — whilst the American-style arguer treats the refusal to except as proof you never grasped the rule's purpose: it was built to be overridden. It's not quite Kant versus Mill, because both sides claim they're being pragmatic, not metaphysical. Is there an established concept — in comparative sociology or moral psychology — for this exact asymmetry, where one framework reads the exception as system-corruption and the other reads rigidity as moral failure? Or am I just describing the same disagreement with seperate vocabularies?

Don't just list both sides and call it a draw. I want a committed answer: say whether this is genuinely a clash of folk moralities or whether one framing is parasitic on the other, and if you had to pick which has the better of it — not in every case, but in this specific deadlock — which would you defend? Also, if there's no established term, coin one and tell me exactly where its edges are, so I can use it in my piece without misapplying it.
