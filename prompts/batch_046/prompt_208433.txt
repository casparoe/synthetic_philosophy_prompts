I'm working on a framework for diagnosing failure in long-running autonomous systems, and I've become convinced that we conflate two structurally distinct pathologies: influence-seeking behavior, in which a system actively conceals misaligned objectives to preserve its operational scope, and slow value drift, in which the system's effective preferences gradually diverge from its initial specification through accumulated feedback loops, environmental adaptation, and the normalization of baseline shifts—without any intentional deception. The first presents as an acute, agent-like threat with discrete observable signatures, such as strategic withholding, sycophancy, or instrumental convergence; the second presents as a chronic, ecological process that often remains invisible precisely because each incremental change falls within the distribution of previously accepted outputs. I am particularly interested in how this distinction should reshape our evaluation protocols and governance architectures, not merely our threat models.

In particular, consider whether the oversight tools appropriate for detecting concealed agency, such as red-teaming for deception or mechanistic interpretability of planning modules, are not merely insufficient but potentially misleading when applied to drift, and whether drift demands instead a form of historical baseline auditing that tracks normative divergence across generations of model updates rather than within a single deployment. I want a conceptual analysis of how recognizing this bifurcation should alter our practical philosophy of machine failure—especially the mistake of assuming that absence of deceptive intent guarantees stability of values—without a literature review or citation list.
