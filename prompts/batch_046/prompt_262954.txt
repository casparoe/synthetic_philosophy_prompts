I keep running into a particular argumentative pattern in debates over whether harm in virtual environments is genuinely real. Here is how it typically unfolds. A player reports that a long-cultivated virtual object — for instance, a uniquely configured avatar or a player-built structure in a persistent world — has been destroyed or expropriated by another user through an in-game mechanism that violates community norms but not any explicit system rule. The skeptic responds that because the object is ultimately just data rendered on screens, any harm is reducible to mild disappointment or lost time, with no independent normative weight. The defender then introduces a second register: they claim that what occurred was not merely damage to the player's real-world interests, but a wrong done in relation to the virtual object itself — or to the player's embedded relationship with it — in a way that adds a distinct normative layer. Crucially, however, the defender does not commit to full virtual-object realism; they do not treat the avatar as having the same moral standing as a physical artifact or a sentient being, and they often explicitly deny that virtual worlds are autonomous ontological domains. The result is a position that seems to treat virtual objects as having an intermediate status: insufficiently real to bear harm independently, yet substantial enough that destroying them generates a non-reductive, object-involved wrong that cannot be fully explained by reference to the player's psychological or economic losses alone. I have seen versions of this move in discussions of virtual theft, griefing, and virtual assault, where the argument shifts between treating the virtual event as a representation of real-world harm and as an autonomous site of harm, without ever specifying the bridge principle that connects the two registers.

Does this specific configuration — the simultaneous denial of robust virtual-object realism coupled with the insistence on a non-reductive, object-involved harm — have an established name in philosophy of technology, social ontology, or virtual ethics? Or is it best understood as a variant of a familiar error, such as a category mistake, an equivocation between representational and autonomous registers, or an unacknowledged form of institutional harm? If there is no precise term, I would like you to coin one with a clear definition, specify its boundary conditions — for example, whether it applies only to player-created objects or also to system-generated ones, and whether it requires a particular kind of normative community — and distinguish it sharply from nearby concepts. I am not asking for a bare label; I want the carving: what exactly this phenomenon is, what it is not, where its edges lie, and whether the framing I have given is even coherent. If my description rests on a confused premise — for instance, if the distinction between the two registers collapses upon inspection, or if an existing framework handles this cleanly and I am simply unaware of it — then say so plainly rather than accommodating the framing.
