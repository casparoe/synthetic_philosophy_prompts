Candidate's answer:

The "Death in Damascus" parable involves a man warned that he will meet Death in Damascus; he travels to Aleppo, yet Death is surprised to find him there and takes him. The story is traditionally read as supporting logical fatalism—the thesis that if an event will occur, then it is unavoidable regardless of intervention. This must be distinguished from causal determinism, which holds that prior physical states necessitate later ones. Richard Taylor's well-known analysis demonstrates that fatalism requires no causal chain; it requires only that future truths are fixed. In the Damascus case, the man's flight to Aleppo is causally efficacious—he genuinely reaches the city—but the outcome remains unavoidable because the prediction was true. Thus the paradox supports logical fatalism without entailing determinism.

However, the argument presupposes that the predictor's knowledge is infallible yet non-causal, which parallels the problem of divine foreknowledge. Consequently, the narrative is better interpreted as an instance of theological fatalism rather than logical fatalism, since its force depends on the authority of the oracle rather than on pure modal logic. Once this is recognised, the threat to human freedom disappears: causal determinism is false, and the oracle's knowledge does not constrain the agent's will. The introduction of probabilistic forecasting does not change this, because a 99 per cent confidence still leaves logical space for avoidance, confirming that only absolute inevitability poses a problem.
