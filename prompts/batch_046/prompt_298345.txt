Consider a philosopher—call her V—who has delivered explicit verdicts on five cases involving higher-order evidence and epistemic akrasia, published in a series of short seminar responses. Her rulings are as follows.

Case 1: A researcher believes that a drug is effective based on her own well-designed trial. She then learns that, across the literature, 40% of trials with this design in this subfield are false positives, but she identifies no specific error in her own protocol. V rules that the researcher should maintain her belief without reducing confidence, on the grounds that the replication base-rate concerns different protocols and does not undermine this particular study.

Case 2: The same researcher learns that her specific trial was conducted on a Tuesday, and that her lab’s Tuesday runs have a documented history of equipment calibration errors—though she has no direct reason to think this particular Tuesday was affected. V rules that she should suspend belief in the drug’s efficacy until she can rule out the Tuesday-specific risk.

Case 3: A mathematician derives a theorem and is highly confident. She then receives peer feedback that her proof relies on a lemma she has not fully verified. After careful review, she confirms the lemma is indeed correct. V rules that she should maintain full confidence; the peer’s challenge was first-order, not higher-order, and its resolution restores her epistemic standing.

Case 4: A juror forms a guilty verdict based on eyewitness identification. She then receives psychological evidence that she personally exhibits high suggestibility in lineup tasks, though she remembers the identification process clearly and sees no specific mistake in her reasoning. V rules that she should keep the guilty verdict but qualify her confidence downward somewhat; general self-doubt without a localized error warrants caution but not reversal.

Case 5: A climate scientist believes in a warming trend based on her data analysis. She then learns that she holds this belief with confidence 0.9, but also believes—on the basis of her general epistemic self-assessment—that she should only hold beliefs in this domain with confidence no higher than 0.7. V rules that she may rationally maintain the 0.9 belief while acknowledging the second-order judgment; epistemic akrasia of this global-versus-local kind is permissible.

Now consider a new case. Dr. L is a neuropsychologist who believes that a particular patient has condition C, based on a complex diagnostic battery she administered personally. She then acquires two pieces of higher-order information: (a) a meta-analysis shows that neuropsychologists using this exact battery, when they administer it after 10 p.m., produce false positives at twice the normal rate (Dr. L did administer it at 10:30 p.m., but she remembers the session clearly and detects no procedural slip); and (b) a senior colleague tells her that in this specific patient’s file, one subtest score was mis-transcribed by an assistant, though when Dr. L checks the original sheet herself, the transcription appears correct to her.

Predict V’s verdict: should Dr. L maintain, reduce, or suspend belief in diagnosis C? Identify the closest analog among Cases 1–5, specify the rule or rules that best explain V’s pattern across the five cases, and state what principle V would be adopting if she treats (a) as decisive versus if she treats (b) as decisive (or neither). If the precedents genuinely underdetermine the outcome, say so honestly and name the further case that would settle which rule V is running. Finally, indicate whether V’s framework permits Dr. L to hold the belief that the patient has C while simultaneously believing that she should not hold that belief.

End with a one-sentence summary beginning "Verdict:" stating the predicted ruling, followed immediately by \answer{maintain}, \answer{reduce}, or \answer{suspend}.
