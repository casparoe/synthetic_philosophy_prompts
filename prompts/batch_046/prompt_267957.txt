Following an extended argument with a friend over whether archaeologists ever have sufficient warrant to attribute institutional beliefs—rather than merely functional behaviors—to prehistoric societies on the basis of material remains alone, I am finalizing the assessment for my graduate seminar in the philosophy of archaeology. I need a rigorous final-exam essay question, answerable in roughly ninety minutes, that forces students past rote summaries of standard linking-argument frameworks and instead requires them to navigate the specific epistemic risk I will call the index-to-structure fallacy: the mistake of treating quantitative or spatial patterns in artifacts as direct, unmediated indices of social institutions without an independently justified chain of mediating beliefs. To ground the problem concretely, please construct the question around a hypothetical site designated K-7, where excavation reveals an abrupt stratigraphic transition from large, undifferentiated communal storage pits to smaller, individually marked burial caches containing functionally identical ritual objects arranged in consistent directional alignments, with no accompanying iconography, textual material, or representational art whatsoever.

The essay prompt should ask candidates to evaluate whether this assemblage can support an inference to a specific institutional transformation—say, from collective resource governance to a hereditary ritual specialist class—while explicitly requiring them to distinguish belief-attribution from institution-attribution, to construct at least one middle-range linking argument that does not presuppose the conclusion, and to explain why the absence of iconographic evidence functions as a structural constraint on the inference rather than a mere contingent gap. Along with the question, please provide a model answer of approximately 650 to 750 words and a marking scheme built around five must-hit analytical moves—such as correctly defining the index-to-structure fallacy, separating mental-state reconstruction from institutional reconstruction, identifying the directional-alignment pattern as ambiguous between cosmological and social-hierarchical readings, explaining the structural rather than contingent nature of the iconographic absence, and proposing a non-circular linking argument—each assigned specific marks totaling 25, plus a brief note on the characteristic failure mode for each point. The difficulty should lie in selecting the crux: students who have merely memorized the broad distinction between processual and post-processual approaches should be unable to score highly without actually performing the inference they are asked to evaluate.
