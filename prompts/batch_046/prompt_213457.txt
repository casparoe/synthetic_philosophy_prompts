Here are my judgements on whether particular episodes count as genuine joint action, and I should like to know whether a single principle can capture them all.

Case A: Two strangers in a lift both press the button for the fourth floor, unaware of each other. Each wants the lift to stop there. My verdict: not joint action; it is parallel individual action with a coincident goal.

Case B: Two hikers who have never spoken silently adjust their pace to stay abreast for an hour. Neither intended to walk together, yet each notices the synchrony and maintains it. My verdict: still not joint action; behavioural coupling without shared intention is not enough.

Case C: A surgeon and an anaesthetist perform a complex operation according to a shared plan. Unbeknownst to the surgeon, the anaesthetist is an advanced automaton lacking mental states, though it behaves exactly as a human colleague would. The surgeon intends “we perform the operation”. My verdict: joint action is present, though asymmetrical; the surgeon’s we-intention and the functional integration of roles suffice, and the partner’s absence of mentality does not dissolve the jointness from the surgeon’s side.

Case D: Three people in a queue each subtly shift position to block a line-cutter, with no eye contact, no verbal agreement, and no prior shared plan. Each acts individually to stop the intrusion, yet their movements interlock with precision. My verdict: this is collective action, but not joint action in the strict sense; it is emergent coordination without a shared we-intention.

Case E: Two musicians improvise a duet. Each intends “we are creating a piece together”. However, musician A believes they are playing in a major key for a joyful effect, while musician B believes they are playing in a minor mode for a melancholic effect; in fact they are playing the same ambiguous notes. They agree on every concrete movement but diverge on the collective goal’s meaning. My verdict: not genuine joint action, because shared intention must include agreement on what “we” are doing, not merely on the physical movements.

Case F: A research team divides a project. Each member explicitly intends “we produce a report”, but each privately interprets “report” differently—one as advocacy, one as neutral analysis, one as satire. They write sections that happen to fit together grammatically but argue for incompatible conclusions. My verdict: despite the explicit shared formula, this is not joint action; it is a fractured assemblage wearing the costume of collaboration.

These verdicts feel partly in tension. Case C permits joint action with a non-mental partner if roles integrate and one party holds a we-intention, whereas Case E denies it between mental partners who share movements but diverge on the collective goal’s content. Meanwhile Case D allows collective coordination with no we-intention at all. Is there one account—perhaps built on interlocking practical commitments, shared practical reasoning, or functional role-integration—that delivers all six verdicts? Or must I overturn one?

Please state the principle precisely, indicating which clause handles each case. Then apply it to two further situations I have not listed: first, a “delegated veto” in which two people can open a safe only by turning keys simultaneously, each knowing the mechanism but unsure whether the other intends to open it; second, a “ghostwriter” situation in which an author dictates a political manifesto to a scribe who believes they are writing fiction, yet both intend “we produce this text” and agree on every sentence. What does your principle predict for these?

Finally, be explicit about which of my original verdicts your principle cannot accommodate without strain, and whether simplicity or fidelity to intuition should win out.
