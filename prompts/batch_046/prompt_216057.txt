Here is an argument I have been working through, and I find it suspicious because the conclusion seems to condemn nearly every actual human decision-maker as deeply irrational.

(1) The canonical coherence argument shows that if an agent's preferences violate the axioms of expected utility—completeness, transitivity, continuity, and independence—she can be subjected to a Dutch book: a sequence of individually acceptable bets that collectively guarantee a net loss.

(2) Being vulnerable to a sure loss is a paradigmatic failure of practical rationality.

(3) Therefore, any agent whose preferences violate the EU axioms is practically irrational.

(4) Moreover, the representation theorem shows that if an agent's preferences do satisfy those axioms, there exists a utility function and a credence function such that she always chooses the option that maximizes expected utility relative to that single, globally stable pair.

(5) So coherence does not merely prohibit Dutch-book vulnerability; it positively requires that one's choices conform to EU maximization relative to one unified belief-desire framework.

(6) Now consider an agent with what I will call domain-modular preferences: within each local context—financial planning, interpersonal relationships, ethical decision-making—her preferences satisfy the EU axioms relative to a local utility function and belief state, but across contexts these local pairs are not jointly representable. She treats certain moral risks as incommensurable with monetary gains, updates beliefs via Jeffrey conditioning in one domain while using standard conditioning in another, and refuses to assign probabilities to outcomes she regards as categorically unacceptable.

(7) Because her global preference ordering fails completeness and independence across domains, a sufficiently clever bookmaker can construct bets that straddle contexts and guarantee her a loss.

(8) By step (3), she is therefore practically irrational.

(9) The same pressure applies to any agent with moral side-constraints: if she refuses to trade even an infinitesimal risk of being forced to betray a friend for any amount of benefit, she violates continuity and can be exploited.

(10) So coherence arguments entail that any departure from globally unified EU maximization—including context-sensitive reasoning, non-probabilistic belief revision, incommensurability across domains, and refusal to monetize certain outcomes—is not merely suboptimal but rationally defective in a deep sense.

(11) Yet this conclusion is absurd: it eliminates the conceptual space for bounded rationality, non-utilitarian ethics, and modular cognition, and it implies that virtually every actual human agent is systematically irrational.

(12) Therefore, the coherence argument proves too much.

Which step, or transition between steps, should be rejected? Is the error in identifying Dutch-book vulnerability with irrationality in step (3), in the representation theorem's global scope in step (5), or in the application to modular and morally constrained agents in steps (6) through (9)? If you want to preserve the idea that coherence has genuine normative force without accepting the overgeneralization, what is the least costly revision, and what does giving up that step commit you to?
