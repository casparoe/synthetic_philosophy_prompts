My intuitions about future-tensed truths extend well beyond Aristotle's classic sea-battle, and I'd like to know whether they cohere. Here are my verdicts: that a deterministic asteroid will strike in 2175 is already true now, given present orbital mechanics; that a genuinely open quantum decay event will occur in the next hour is not already determinately true or false; that I will freely choose tea over coffee tomorrow morning is not determinately true now, despite my strong habitual pattern; that in a branching-time framework, "it will rain" requires truth on all extending branches for modal necessity but not for simple futurity; and that if an omniscient being foreknows every future choice without causally determining it, the corresponding future propositions are true now in virtue of that knowledge rather than present causation. Meanwhile, I resist applying bivalence to the original sea-battle case when the outcome remains causally open. Is there a single principle—turning on determinism, causal closure, epistemic access, or tense semantics—that sorts all of these judgments? Or must I drop the verdict about divine foreknowledge, or the one about deterministic celestial mechanics, to keep the account consistent? And what does the surviving principle predict for a case I haven't listed—say, a computer simulation that deterministically outputs a random-seeming number in ten years?
