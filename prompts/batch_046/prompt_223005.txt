Global workspace theory treats consciousness as the global broadcasting of information to a network of consumer systems, yet philosophers routinely distinguish access consciousness—availability for reasoning, report, and control—from phenomenal consciousness, or subjective experience. One underexplored tension is that broadcasting appears graded: some representations reach many consumer systems, others only a subset, suggesting intermediate “micro-access” states that blur the boundary between full global availability and mere local processing. Suppose you are directing a three-year, single-grant project staffed by one philosopher of mind, one cognitive scientist, and one computational researcher, none of whom can search the literature online. How should they divide their limited effort among the following open lines of work? First, developing empirical markers that can separate phenomenal presence from graded access in clinical populations with dissociated reportability; second, testing whether artificial architectures with distributed but non-integrated broadcasting generate functional analogs of access without any corresponding phenomenal property; third, determining whether so-called phenomenal overflow is genuinely rich experience that exceeds working-memory capacity, or merely rapid decay of access traces; fourth, formalizing whether local recurrent dynamics can produce phenomenal properties independently of global broadcasting at all. Rank these four by which deserve the majority of the group’s resources, and defend the ordering with explicit criteria—tractability within the three-year window, how many other questions depend on the answer, and practical payoff for either consciousness science or AI evaluation. Which negative result—if one of these lines failed—would most destabilize the standard access-phenomenal distinction? And what specific empirical finding or conceptual argument would cause you to reorder the ranking?
