I have been discussing with a colleague the thought experiment discussed in Hofstadter and Dennett's The Mind's I, in which Einstein's brain is preserved and its functional architecture eventually transcribed into a massive printed volume. We have formed two opposing camps about whether such a book could ever be conscious. Camp One—the Substrate-Independent Functionalists—maintains that if every state-transition and causal relation of Einstein's neural dynamics is exactly replicated in the book's logical structure (for instance, via printed instructions that tell a reader precisely how to update symbolic states), then consciousness is present regardless of whether the substrate is neurons or paper and ink; they treat the book as a slow but genuinely implemented version of the same program. Camp Two—the Dynamic-Integrationists—maintains that consciousness requires not merely correct symbolic mappings but continuous, self-modifying causal interaction within a unified spatiotemporal system; they argue that a static printed object lacks the real-time feedback loops, temporal continuity, and causal power over its own substrate that neural processes possess, so even perfect functional replication in a book yields only a description of consciousness, not consciousness itself.

We want you to draft the joint statement that an adversarial collaboration between these two camps should produce. The document should contain: (1) a numbered section of claims both sides can genuinely sign, covering what is physically possible, what functional replication means in this context, and the present limits of our knowledge about neural correlates of consciousness; (2) a disagreements table with precise, neutrally worded statements of each camp's position on the book's potential consciousness, phrased so that each side accepts the wording of its own view; (3) the cruxes—sub-questions whose resolution would actually move at least one side—with explicit reasoning for why each counts as substantive rather than merely verbal; and (4) for each crux, a concrete scenario or empirical finding that both sides agree would bear on it, together with each camp's explicit prediction about what that scenario or finding would show.

To keep the exercise from collapsing into familiar rehearsals, inject the following niche variant into the collaboration: suppose the book is not read sequentially by one person, but is distributed across a network of ten thousand readers, each holding one page and updating their local state in real time via a synchronized protocol, so that the global system updates at roughly the speed of neural firing. Both camps must address whether this parallel-reader architecture alters their assessment, and if so, exactly which feature—causal integration, temporal continuity, substrate uniformity, or indexical self-location—remains deficient or is now satisfied. Please take a committed stance in your drafting: do not present the cruxes as an open menu. Identify which disagreements are genuinely about the nature of consciousness and which collapse into definitional disputes, state your reasoning for each identification, and record each camp's predictions with definite confidence rather than tentative qualification. The answer should stand on its own conceptual merits without invoking bibliographies, citation lists, or appeals to external authority.
