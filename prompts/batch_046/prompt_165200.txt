I am delivering the opening remarks at tomorrow's dedication of the Meridian Water Reclamation Facility, a plant engineered to operate for two centuries. I am a civil engineer by training and not a philosopher, but over the last year I have found myself returning, without any academic framework, to a problem that now feels inseparable from this concrete and steel: if human life continues indefinitely, then any risk we fail to eliminate today — however small — is multiplied across an infinite number of future people, making its expected weight infinite, while every cost we bear to prevent it remains stubbornly finite. That arithmetic seems to demand that we spare no expense, yet it also feels like there is a hidden error in the multiplication itself, some confusion between counting people and weighing harm. I want the remarks to hold that tension honestly rather than resolve it. The audience will include city council members who approved the budget, the plant workers who will maintain it, and their families. I need the speech to honor the practical decision they all made — to build something durable with finite funds — without pretending that the infinite shadow behind it is not real. It should speak to the engineers without jargon, acknowledge to the families that this structure is meant for descendants they will never meet, and avoid any false consolation. The paradox itself should structure what I can and cannot claim about why this matters.
