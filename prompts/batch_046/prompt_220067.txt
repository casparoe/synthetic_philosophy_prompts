Some philosophers argue that if you can genuinely conceive of a physical duplicate of yourself that lacks consciousness entirely — a philosophical zombie — then consciousness can't be identical to any physical state. Critics reply that conceivability isn't a reliable guide to metaphysical possibility, using necessary a posteriori identities like water being H2O. Now consider a slightly different case: imagine a being physically identical to you, with all the same sensory experiences, but with no higher-order representation that those experiences belong to 'it' — call it a shadow-self. Does the conceivability of a shadow-self strengthen the anti-physicalist argument, or does the water/H2O reply undercut it just as well? Give the best answer and explain where the analogy between shadow-self and zombie cases breaks, if it does.

the student answer:

Conceivability is fallible, but when we have a clear and distinct conception with no hidden essential nature revealed, it tracks metaphysical possibility. In the water case we conceive water without H2O only through superficial properties like liquidity, but science reveals H2O is essential. With a philosophical zombie we conceive a body with all physical properties but no phenomenal consciousness, and since consciousness has no hidden microstructure like water does, this conception is veridical. The shadow-self is basically the same argument applied to self-consciousness: we can clearly imagine being physically identical yet lacking the higher-order thought that 'I am experiencing this.' Since self-awareness also lacks a hidden essential nature, the necessary a posteriori reply fails here too. So physicalism about higher-order consciousness is false, though first-order phenomenal states might still be physical. The analogy to water breaks down precisely because mental properties are transparent, not like chemical kinds.
