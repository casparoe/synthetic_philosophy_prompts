I'm currently enrolled in a seminar comparing analytic metaphysics with classical Indian philosophy, and the question of personal identity has become unexpectedly personal since I began assisting in the care of my grandmother, whose dementia has gradually erased her memories and dispositions while leaving her physically present and occasionally responsive. This has made abstract debates about persistence feel less like puzzles and more like attempts to locate where, if anywhere, a self resides in the continuity of a life. Reading about the Buddhist doctrine of anatta—the denial of any permanent, substantial self underlying the five aggregates—alongside contemporary discussions of psychological and bodily continuity, I have developed a set of intuitive verdicts about identity that I cannot quite unify, and I want to know whether a coherent principle underlies them or whether some must be abandoned.

Here are my judgments. First, in the standard fission case, if a person's brain is split and both hemispheres generate psychologically continuous successors, neither successor is identical to the original; identity is not preserved, but survival is not the same thing as identity, and both may survive in some derivative sense. Second, if a human being's neurons are gradually replaced by synthetic equivalents that preserve all psychological functions and affective patterns, the person survives, but if the replacement eliminates the habitual patterns of craving and aversion—what Buddhist thought calls upadana, or clinging—then despite perfect memory and reasoning, something essential to that person's identity has been lost. Third, the classical Buddhist account of rebirth without a transmigrating soul strikes me as conceptually coherent: karmic continuity can link lives without requiring a substrate, provided we reject the intuition that identity needs a continuous bearer. Fourth, a corporation that maintains institutional memory and decision protocols across complete staff turnover does not constitute a persisting person, even if its functional profile remains constant, because there is no locus of phenomenal suffering or clinging to bind the sequence. Fifth, when a meditator in deep absorption reports the complete dissolution of self-boundaries and then returns to ordinary consciousness with intact memories and traits, I am genuinely uncertain whether the returning subject is strictly identical to the one who entered; my intuition oscillates between seeing it as a temporary interruption of the clinging-process and seeing it as a death and replacement. Sixth, if an artificial system were constructed to replicate every behavioral and cognitive pattern of a deceased individual but lacked any phenomenal consciousness or affective attachment, I would deny that the person survives, regardless of functional fidelity.

Is there a single principle—perhaps one that defines personal identity not through psychological or physical continuity alone but through the uninterrupted flow of a clinging-process tied to phenomenal experience—that captures all six verdicts? If so, please state it precisely, indicate which clause of the principle is motivated by which case, predict its verdict on a scenario I have not mentioned (for instance, a being whose clinging-patterns are gradually redirected onto a digital substrate while biological consciousness fades), and tell me honestly which of my six judgments the principle forces me to overturn. If no unified principle can accommodate them, present the two strongest competing accounts, identify the specific pair of cases they diverge on, and advise which verdict I should relinquish to preserve theoretical simplicity.
