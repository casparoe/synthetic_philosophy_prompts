In debates about democratic legitimacy I keep encountering the claim that a policy is justified because it expresses "the will of the people," usually invoked to dismiss procedural objections—say, that a court invalidated a referendum result or that minority protections were overridden. Rather than accepting or rejecting the phrase wholesale, I want its logical anatomy. Can you lay out the distinct precise theses that slogan could stand for—specifying, for each reading, what counts as "the people" (the present electorate? all affected parties? a transhistorical nation?), what mental or collective state "will" denotes (aggregated preferences? a counterfactual unanimous consent? a general will in the Rousseauian sense?), and what normative work the claim is doing (purely descriptive? legitimating? lexically overriding other values?). For each reading, say whether it is trivial, defensible, or false, and indicate which versions actually license bypassing institutional checks and which leave those checks fully intact. Please also state how confident you are in this taxonomy and single out the point about which you are least sure.
