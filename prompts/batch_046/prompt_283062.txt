I am a doctoral student in intellectual history and the history of science, and in a recent seminar I have been debating the ontology of macro-periodization with a senior colleague whose judgments about historical discontinuities strike me as systematically ambiguous rather than obviously principled. She holds that the “Middle Ages” is a pure retrospective invention, first constructed by Petrarchan humanists and later institutionalized by nineteenth-century medievalists with no basis in medieval self-understanding; she considers the “Renaissance” also an invention, arguing that despite some humanist self-rhetoric the causal and institutional threads run continuously from late-medieval scholasticism and urban culture without a genuine break. By contrast, she treats the “Scientific Revolution” as a real ontological discontinuity, not because of sudden content change but because the institutionalization of experimental protocols and mathematical physics created discontinuous epistemic practices; she regards the “Enlightenment” as an invention, essentially a Franco-German narrative projection onto a heterogeneous eighteenth-century republic of letters that never formed a coherent causal cluster; she accepts the “Industrial Revolution” as real, anchored in measurable discontinuities in energy capture, labor organization, and demographic growth; she dismisses the “Long Eighteenth Century” (1688–1815) as a purely historiographic convenience lacking internal coherence; and she rejects Karl Jaspers’s framing of an “Axial Age” (c. 800–200 BCE) as an artificial aggregation of causally unconnected local developments.

Given this track record, I want to anticipate her verdict on a more recent and somewhat contested framing: the so-called “Great Acceleration” (c. 1945–1970), proposed not merely as an economic episode but as a global structural period defined by exponential, synchronized discontinuities in population growth, energy consumption, nitrogen fixation, and atmospheric chemistry. Would she classify it as a real period or as a retrospective environmental narrative? I am particularly interested in the competing rules that could jointly accommodate her previous judgments—perhaps one privileging measurable material and systemic discontinuities regardless of actor awareness, another privileging self-conscious collective identity and institutional self-naming among historical agents, and perhaps a third turning on whether a proposed period can be delimited without arbitrary chronological bookends. For each rule, please say what it predicts about the Great Acceleration. Then identify which single further hypothetical case—say, a proposed “Cognitive Revolution” (c. 1950–1980) defined by the shift from behaviorism to information-processing models, marked by high self-awareness among its protagonists but lacking any obvious material or demographic discontinuity—would best distinguish these underlying principles. Finally, tell me whether any consistent principle exists that avoids rendering her acceptance of the Scientific Revolution and her simultaneous rejection of both the Renaissance and the Enlightenment arbitrary.
