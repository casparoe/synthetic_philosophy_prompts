I teach an upper-level philosophy seminar (ethics/metaphysics) and am drafting the final exam: 90 minutes, essay of about 800 words. Write one essay question on the nonidentity problem that students cannot answer by reciting the familiar textbook example of a teenager deciding whether to conceive now or wait. Present this case instead: in 2025 a government adopts a climate-adaptation policy that reshapes urban housing and labor markets; by 2100 the actual residents live lives well above a decent threshold, but the policy was a necessary condition for their existence, and they live somewhat worse lives than the different people who would have inhabited the same region absent the policy. A critic claims the policy wrongs future people by burdening them for present gain; a defender replies that since the policy is necessary for their existence, no particular person is harmed, and that morality is fundamentally person-affecting.

The question should require students to do three things: (1) explain why the defender's reply depends on a specific interpretation of the nonidentity problem—distinguishing the 'no harm to identifiable victims' argument from the 'same-number-different-people' argument—and identify which interpretation actually blocks the critic's charge; (2) evaluate whether a strictly person-affecting morality can accommodate a wrong that does not consist in making any particular individual worse off, articulating precisely what a 'structural wrong' or 'relational injustice' would involve here (no author citations needed, but the concept must be defined with conceptual precision); and (3) apply this framework to the case: if we reject pure impersonal evaluation but accept structural wronging, does the climate policy wrong anyone? Give a reasoned verdict and say whether the nonidentity problem dissolves the objection or merely relocates it.

Please provide: the exam question itself; a model answer of approximately 600 words; and a marking scheme out of 20 points that separates an A from a B answer by naming the specific conceptual moves each grade requires—especially correct identification of the two interpretations in (1), a precise non-citational articulation of structural wronging in (2), and a consistent application in (3)—plus a one-line note on the typical mistake that costs marks for each part.
