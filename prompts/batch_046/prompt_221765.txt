I am writing a paper for an upper-level philosophy course on theories of well-being, and I have become stuck on the so-called heterogeneity problem for hedonism. The objection, as I understand it, claims that hedonism must identify some common feature shared by every pleasure—perhaps a distinctive qualitative tone or a uniform phenomenal character—since the theory treats all pleasures as instances of the same intrinsic good. Yet when I compare the pleasure of tasting wine, the pleasure of solving a mathematical proof, and the pleasure of feeling morally vindicated, they seem to share no such common feel. At first this looked like a devastating critique. But then I encountered recent work in affective neuroscience that distinguishes "wanting" (incentive salience driven largely by dopaminergic circuits) from "liking" (hedonic impact mediated by opioid and cannabinoid signaling in discrete hotspots such as the nucleus accumbens and ventral pallidum), and that emphasizes how different activities recruit these systems in radically different combinations—sometimes dissociating wanting from liking entirely. This suggests to me that pleasure was never a single, homogeneous phenomenon with one shared phenomenal signature; it is already a heterogeneous family of processes. If that is correct, then doesn't the heterogeneity problem rest on a false presupposition? Rather than undermining hedonism, the neuroscientific evidence seems to dissolve the very expectation that all pleasures should feel alike. So why do philosophers continue to present phenomenological diversity as a challenge to hedonism, instead of treating it as confirmation that pleasure is a pluralistic category whose members need only be good, not qualitatively identical?
