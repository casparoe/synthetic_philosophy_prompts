So at this historiography reading group last week two people got into it over presentism and I honestly couldnt tell who was right. Maya was arguing that when a historian calls a 19th-century reformer morally blind for not extending rights to women, theyre not explaining anything, just grading the past with a modern rubric. That blocks us from seeing why the reformer thought he was being consistent. Jon came back that if historians can never say an agent was wrong by standards the agent themselves claimed to hold, then history loses all critical bite -- every atrocity just becomes they had different values. Maya said thats not her point; she wants to distinguish internal critique (using the agents own norms against them) from external critique (imposing ours). Jon said internal critique is still presentist, because the decision to highlight certain contradictions over others depends on our modern sensibilities -- like, why do we care about gender exclusion but not about his views on property? Maya replied that internal critique at least respects the agents agency, since he could have recognized the contradiction himself. Jon said that just pushes the problem back: recognizing a contradiction requires a framework of coherence that might itself be historically specific. Then Maya brought up this idea she called normative drift -- the claim that moral concepts dont just change content over time but change their logical relations, so a contradiction in 1850 might not map onto one in 2024. Jon said that sounds like special pleading, and if normative drift is real then historians can never translate moral arguments across eras, which makes comparative moral history impossible. They asked me to referee and I have no idea. Can you go through their exchange move by move? I want to know whos right at each step -- not just who wins overall -- and especialy whether normative drift is doing real work or just renaming the problem. Also what are both of them missing that they should be saying?
