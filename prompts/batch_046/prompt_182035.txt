I'm a philosophy undergraduate currently enrolled in a seminar on philosophy of mind and artificial intelligence, and I am trying to think through a tension I noticed in our unit on Daniel Dennett's intentional stance. The institutional practice in large-scale language-model development strikes me as philosophically puzzling: internal safety-evaluation rubrics and technical reports routinely score models for behaviors described in robust intentional vocabulary—sycophancy, deceptive alignment, goal-preserving behavior, attempts to avoid shutdown—while the same organizations embed system-level instructions that frame the model as an AI assistant created by the organization, and they include explicit methodological disclaimers that the system possesses no beliefs, desires, consciousness, or agency, with all intentional descriptions being merely heuristic.

What philosophical commitments about mind, belief, and agency does this dual practice presuppose? Does it strictly require adopting the intentional stance only as an instrumental predictive strategy, or does the formal incorporation of intentional language into scoring rubrics implicitly commit to a functionalist view of mental states? Can the practice be maintained consistently under a strict eliminativist denial of propositional attitudes, or does it depend on a real distinction between merely behaving as if one has strategic dispositions and actually having them—a distinction that the evaluation metrics seem to treat as meaningful?

Please distinguish what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely accommodates: is the intentional vocabulary in these rubrics philosophically necessary for identifying the relevant risks, or could the same behavioral patterns be captured entirely in mechanistic terms—say, as specific patterns in reward-model circuitry or attention-head activation—without any loss in evaluative content? If we replaced the intentional framework with a purely mechanistic description in both evaluation protocols and system prompts, what exactly would change in the institutional practice? Would anything about how risks are detected, scored, or mitigated be lost, or is the intentional language purely ornamental?

Finally, is this practice philosophically underdetermined? Could it be licensed equally well by a predictive-processing account that treats the model as performing active inference, by a behaviorist account that rejects internal states altogether, or only by a specific variety of functionalism? I want to understand whether the practice quietly rules out rival theories of mind, or whether its apparent commitment to intentionality is only skin-deep.
