Consider the institutional pattern in public-policy architecture where intertemporal choice failures in retirement savings are addressed through libertarian-paternalist nudges—automatic enrollment with a low-cost opt-out—while structurally analogous failures in substance use are addressed through coercive prohibition and mandatory treatment, despite both domains exhibiting steep hyperbolic discounting of future welfare. In the savings case, the framework treats the agent as retaining full autonomy and corrects only the choice architecture; in the addiction case, it treats the agent's present-biased preference reversal as undermining the very capacity for autonomous judgment, justifying the removal of choice. What philosophical conception of weakness of will does this bifurcation presuppose? Must it assume that akrasia—acting against one's all-things-considered judgment—is present only when the competing desire is somatically mediated, or can a purely cognitive model of hyperbolic discounting sustain the distinction? Reconstruct the implicit theory of agency: does the practice commit to a dual-process view in which System 1 impulses forfeit autonomy only in certain domains, or is it merely applying a single theory inconsistently? Distinguish what the bifurcated practice strictly requires from what it merely accommodates, and specify what a uniform institutional design—either extending nudge logic to addiction or extending autonomy-protection to savings—would have to change about its underlying anthropology.
