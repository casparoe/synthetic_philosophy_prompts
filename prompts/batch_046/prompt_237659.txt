I've been sitting with this view that mixes Margalit's thick/thin relations stuff with Rieff's worry about trauma culture, but takes it somewhere more specific: I think societies have an active duty to practice structured amnesia about granular details of collective harm—names, exact sequences, individual testimonies—once direct survivors are gone. Not denial, just a kind of ethical blurring so descendents aren't locked into victim/perpetrator/bystander roles they never chose. The idea is that thick moral relations need this calibarated forgetting, and that thin ones like citizenship actually fracture if you insist on total archival recall.

What would actually count against this? I want defeaters ranked fatal to awkward. Don't just give me the standard "forgetting lets history repeat"—that's too generic. I'm more worried about whether there's a contradiction hidden in saying we owe fidelity to victims while deliberately blurring their specific suffering. Could someone show my "structured amnesia" just collapses into either full denial or empty commemorative rituals? Is there work—conceptual or empirical—showing second-generation people experience this blur as betrayal rather than liberation? And can you point to cases where remembering hyper-specific details actually strengthened moral community instead of coercing it?

If the view is basically unfalsifiable or insulated, say so—does that make it stronger or just evasive? Also, I'm curious whether defenders of total recall would concede anything here, or if they'd just dismiss the blur as moral laziness.
