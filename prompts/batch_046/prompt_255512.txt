Construct a cross-modal thought experiment that adapts the color-phi phenomenon to the tactile domain—using two brief, spatially separated vibrations delivered in rapid succession to a subject’s forearm—to isolate the difference between an Orwellian and a Stalinesque revision of conscious experience. Constraints: the subject must be naive to the stimulus sequence and unable to predict the second vibration; the inter-stimulus interval should sit near the threshold for temporal-order judgment (approximately 100–150 ms, stipulated as such); and the phenomenological report must describe an apparently continuous “moving” sensation that originates near the second location and extends backward, despite the fact that no intermediate cortical activation corresponding to that trajectory is available until after the second stimulus arrives. Present the scenario in a single paragraph, then walk through the verdict each theory delivers: does consciousness retroactively alter the content of the first experience to include the trajectory (Orwellian), or is the unified narrative assembled only at the end with no prior corresponding conscious state (Stalinesque)? Identify the specific structural feature—namely, the absence versus presence of an intermediate representational state that is causally available but not reportable—that does the work in forcing the two accounts apart, and explain what happens to the distinction if that feature is removed. Finally, show how a defender of the Stalinesque position might attack the Orwellian reading without denying the reported phenomenology, and whether removing the feature collapses the case into a standard ambiguity rather than a decisive test.
