Below are working notes for an essay on money-pump arguments and diachronic rationality; I would welcome critical comments on the conceptual architecture, the coherence of the reasoning, and whether the framing opens a genuinely distinct problem or merely redescribes familiar objections in unfamiliar vocabulary.

The core notes are these. Standard money-pump arguments demonstrate that an agent with cyclical preferences over three options—A preferred to B, B to C, and C to A—can be led through a sequence of trades that returns her to her starting position minus a small fee, leaving her worse off by her own lights. This is usually taken to establish that intransitivity is diachronically irrational: even if each pairwise comparison is locally defensible, the cycle permits exploitation over time. I want to test whether this conclusion survives once we rigorously separate static preference consistency from diachronic rationality—understood here not as mere long-run maximization, but as rationality across successive evaluation stages in which an agent’s “local utility frame” may shift in structured, non-erroneous ways.

My central claim is that an agent might maintain a stable second-order preference for retaining first-order intransitivity under conditions of contextual change, treating the potential loss from a pump not as an unmitigated failure but as a calculated price for what I call “adaptive openness”: the disciplined capacity for preference reversal that tracks genuinely altered evaluative circumstances rather than simple mistake or manipulation. To concretize this, I construct a modified intertemporal pump in which the agent faces the same A-B-C cycle every quarter, knows the structure in advance, and could install a binding pre-commitment mechanism to block it. She deliberately declines. The recurring loss, on my account, functions analogously to an insurance premium paid for preserving the option to revise first-order rankings in response to future information she cannot now specify.

From this I draw a further conclusion about the Dutch Book defense of transitivity. Rather than showing that intransitivity is irrational per se, the argument succeeds only against agents who hold an implicit meta-preference for temporal consistency across evaluation stages. For agents whose second-order policies license contextual revision, being “pumped” is not an irrational failure but the foreseeable cost of a rationally chosen commitment structure. Diachronic rationality, then, should be assessed at the level of these meta-policies, not at the level of isolated first-order choices.

My specific requests for feedback are threefold. First, does the distinction between first-order preference cycles and second-order commitment policies actually bear the normative weight I place on it, or does it collapse—say, if any refusal to pre-commit can be redescribed as a hidden preference for consistency after all? Second, is the notion of “adaptive openness” sufficiently determinate to distinguish rational flexibility from mere preference instability, or is it too vague to do meaningful work? Third, does the modified quarterly pump genuinely escape the standard objection, or have I only relocated the irrationality from the first-order cycle to the second-order policy that permits it? I am not seeking bibliographic pointers or a literature survey, but rather a direct conceptual assessment of where the argument is strongest, where it is weakest, and whether its central distinction holds up under pressure.
