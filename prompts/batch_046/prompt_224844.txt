Below is a brief sketch for an essay I am developing, followed by the specific feedback I would value. The view I want to defend—call it structural-residue theory—holds that agent-generated reasons can survive the disappearance of the original reasoner without either becoming properties of a new agent or evaporating into pure causation. Consider a conservation covenant made by a landowner to preserve a particular watershed. If the owner dies and the property is placed under an automated stewardship protocol—one that monitors water quality and restricts development but possesses no consciousness, intentions, or moral status—I want to say that the reason to preserve the watershed does not vanish. Crucially, however, it is not inherited by the protocol as a new agentive reasoner. Instead, the normative structure is inherited by the functional substrate itself. The covenant continues to generate a genuine reason against development, but that reason is now anchored to a non-agentive structural role rather than to any mental state. This is meant to split the difference between agent-centered theories, which must either invent a phantom reasoner or deny that the reason survives, and Platonist theories, which treat the reason as an abstract fact entirely detached from the practical architecture that originally produced it. I distinguish this process—structural inheritance—from agent-transference, in which a new entity literally takes over the normative position of the original agent. I think structural inheritance explains our intuitive judgment that violating the covenant remains wrong even when no human is aware of the original promise, without committing us to free-floating normative entities that exist independently of any system that can bear them.

Please comment on the coherence of this argument, particularly on whether the distinction between structural inheritance and agent-transference can be maintained without collapsing into either a disguised form of agent-centered normativity or a disguised form of normative Platonism. I am also interested in whether I have overlooked an obvious objection—perhaps from the literature on reasons without reasoners or from debates about institutional moral agency—that undermines the claim that a non-conscious functional architecture can genuinely inherit rather than merely simulate a reason. Finally, please suggest whether the watershed example successfully isolates the phenomenon I am trying to capture, or whether it smuggles in hidden assumptions about human oversight that make the case easier than it should be. I am not asking for a bibliography or reading list, but if you see a direct parallel to a well-known position or argument that I should address, please describe it in general terms so I can locate it myself.
