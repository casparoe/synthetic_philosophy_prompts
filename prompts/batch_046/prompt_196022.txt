As a novelist who has long been preoccupied with how chance operates inside narrative structure, I have volunteered to lead a single ninety-minute seminar for a small circle of fellow writers on Bertrand's paradox and the collapse of the principle of indifference in continuous spaces. The audience is literate but not mathematical; they will arrive with the intuitive conviction that “uniform randomness” must have one obvious meaning, and they need to discover through reasoning alone why that conviction dissolves once the space of possibilities becomes uncountable. I do not want a lecture in probability theory. Instead, I need a teaching plan that specifies the exact order in which the conceptual pieces must be introduced—what dependency must be secured before the paradox is presented, and why—along with the one carrying example that should bear the full explanatory weight. This example must feel concrete enough to belong in a fiction workshop (something involving the placement of an object or the drawing of a boundary within a circle) yet be constructed so that it exposes, without ambiguity, why selecting a chord by random radius, random midpoint, and random endpoints yields three incompatible probabilities.

Please identify the misconception these particular students will form at each stage—above all the belief that one parameterization is “the correct one” because it feels spatially or narratively more natural—and tell me precisely where in the sequence to intercept each. I also want a brief check at the end that distinguishes genuine comprehension of the structural failure from mere memorization of the three answers, together with an explicit statement of what should be refused explanation at this level, whether that is full measure-theoretic formalism or the complete apparatus of transformation-group invariance. Present your response in the following sections with approximate lengths: Conceptual Dependency Chain (about 200 words), The Carrying Example and Its Design (about 250 words), Misconception Map and Intervention Points (about 250 words), and The Understanding Check Together with Explicit Omissions (about 200 words).
