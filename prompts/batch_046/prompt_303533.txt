I cover the philosophy of science and formal epistemology for a long-form magazine, and I have been invited to defend a developing view at a joint philosophy-physics workshop next Tuesday. The audience is small but sharp: two philosophers of physics committed to objective chance in dynamical collapse theories, one decision theorist who has published on derivations of the Born rule from decision-theoretic axioms, a statistician who works on causal graphical models, and four graduate students in formal epistemology. They are skeptical of journalistic intrusions into technical territory, they value precision over rhetoric, and they will press on whether my proposal is genuinely different from existing interpretations or merely a relabeling.

My thesis is this. The Principal Principle, as standardly formulated, demands that an agent’s credence in an outcome match the known objective chance of that outcome. I argue that this conflates two relations that should be kept apart: epistemic deference to a physical parameter that figures in the dynamics, and calibration to a statistical regularity that may arise from initial conditions rather than from lawlike propensities. To disentangle them, I propose replacing the Principal Principle with what I call a Local Calibration Constraint, which applies only when the chance-generating mechanism is causally insulated from the agent’s belief-formation process. In quantum mechanics—specifically in deterministic hidden-variable frameworks where the quantum equilibrium distribution functions as a measure of typicality rather than as a dynamical chance—this constraint does not license deference to the Born-rule weights as if they were objective chances; instead, it licenses treating them as baseline frequencies against which local deviations can be detected. I defend this not as a revision of quantum mechanics but as a clarification of what rational belief requires when physics supplies statistics but not propensities.
