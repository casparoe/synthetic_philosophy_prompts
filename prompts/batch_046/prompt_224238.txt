I was discussing with a friend who works in political philosophy whether Dostoevsky’s Grand Inquisitor is essentially an unacknowledged precursor to modern critiques of libertarian paternalism and behavioral “nudging.” She proposed that the Inquisitor’s bargain—miracle, mystery, and authority offered in exchange for the burden of freedom—maps neatly onto contemporary choice architecture: citizens retain formal autonomy but are guided away from self-destructive decisions by invisible defaults, relieved of decision fatigue, and folded into a collective narrative of care. From this she concluded that modern liberal democracies have quietly signed the Inquisitor’s contract, substituting cognitive science for ecclesiastical authority and consumer comfort for spiritual salvation. Before I accept that reading, I want the analogy audited in detail. Can you construct the correspondence table—what in the Inquisitor’s triad corresponds to the mechanisms of nudging, what institution or structure plays the role of the Church, what stands in for the “happiness” the Inquisitor promises, and what feature of human nature is being relieved? Then identify the points of structural divergence: for instance, does libertarian paternalism actually share the Inquisitor’s explicit contempt for human freedom—his claim that people are too weak and fearful to bear genuine autonomy—or is that contempt being smuggled in by the analogy? Consider also the disanalogy of visibility: the Inquisitor rules through overt spiritual authority and the explicit threat of eternal separation, whereas nudging operates through silent cognitive defaults that preserve the appearance of free choice. Does that difference collapse the moral comparison, or is invisibility precisely what makes the modern version more insidious? Finally, tell me whether her conclusion—that we have accepted the Inquisitor’s terms—survives once these broken mappings and imported features are laid out, or whether sustaining it would require a different analogy altogether, and if so, what a more defensible comparison might be.
