I'm a software engineer with no formal training in philosophy or history. I've been asked to deliver the opening remarks—about eight minutes—at an interdisciplinary workshop titled "System Design and Moral Distance," hosted by a university technology-ethics center. The audience is split: roughly half are historians and philosophers familiar with the Historikerstreit and with Bauman's argument that modern bureaucratic rationality, division of labor, and moral distancing were conditions that made the Holocaust possible rather than mere aberrations of it; the other half are engineers and graduate students who have no background in either field.

I want the speech to use the philosophical tension between uniqueness and comparability—specifically, whether treating the Holocaust as incomparable protects its moral gravity or prevents us from recognizing structurally analogous dangers—to determine exactly what I can and cannot say about software engineering. Because Bauman's thesis is in play, I should be able to describe how technical abstraction, role fragmentation, and service boundaries in distributed systems can create a form of moral distance without ever implying that writing code is morally equivalent to genocide. Because the uniqueness debate is in play, I must avoid using the Holocaust as a casual metaphor for "technical debt," "bugs," or organizational dysfunction, and I must reject both the comforting universalism ("this could happen anywhere") and the false security of specificity ("our code reviews prevent it").

The philosophical idea should do real structural work: it should shape the boundaries of what the speech can claim, not merely decorate it. The remarks should sound like they are being spoken by someone who builds systems for a living and respects the limits of her expertise—reflective rather than lecturing. Please do not include a bibliography, suggested reading, or citations beyond the general framing of Bauman and the Historikerstreit. Keep the technical analogy—perhaps involving refactoring or service boundaries—subordinate to the historical and philosophical point, and make sure the speech is accessible to the non-specialists in the room without flattening the uniqueness problem for the experts.
