I am delivering the retirement toast for my older sister, Clara, at a dinner next month. She spent thirty-two years as a hospice nurse, and the room will be split between two audiences: roughly half will be her clinical colleagues—palliative-care physicians, social workers, and ethics consultants—who can spot an imprecise philosophical reference immediately, and the other half will be family members who mainly remember her as the person who arrived with soup and refused to leave. I have no formal training in philosophy; I run a small landscaping business. Over the past year, however, I have been reading fragments of Leibniz’s practical writings, particularly his claim that justice is not mere benevolence but caritas sapientis—charity disciplined by rational wisdom, or what he sometimes called the charity of the wise. What strikes me is that he treats this not as sentimental kindness but as a kind of universal jurisprudence: moral action participates in a rational order that applies to all minds, and without that rational structure, compassion collapses into arbitrary preference.

I want the toast to use this idea structurally, not as decoration. I do not want to list Clara’s virtues or call her a saint in the usual emotional way. Instead, I want to say that what she practiced was closer to Leibniz’s notion—her care was always guided by an attempt to understand the patient’s situation rationally, to see the harmony of needs and limits, rather than by feeling alone. At the same time, I need the speech to land for family members who will not know Leibniz from landscaping, and I must avoid sounding like a lecture. I also want to weave in a more specific, somewhat niche point I came across: Leibniz apparently held that moral progress is asymptotic, that no human reaches the divine standard of perfect charity-informed justice, but that the value lies entirely in the orientation toward it. If I can use that, it lets me say something honest about retirement—that she is not finished being the person she was, but is now oriented differently—without falling into cliché. The speech should run about four minutes, remain warm and personal, and make both halves of the room feel included rather than instructed.
