In bioethics seminars and policy debates I keep encountering a compressed maxim that is treated as decisive: "restoration returns an organism to a baseline, whereas enhancement rewrites that baseline." It is invoked to defend insurance coverage for genetic therapy, to condemn nootropic use by healthy students, and occasionally to insist that every biological intervention must fall into exactly one of these two categories with no middle ground. Before I evaluate those applications, I need the precise claims hiding inside that sentence separated out and assessed. Lay out the distinct readings as a menu, specifying for each what counts as the relevant baseline—species-typical functioning, the individual's own prior state, a socially negotiated norm, or an evolutionary equilibrium—and identifying whose authority fixes it: clinical, statistical, moral, or individual. For each reading, say whether it is tautological, whether it is defensible, and whether it actually supports the normative or policy conclusion being drawn from it. In particular, include one less familiar reading that distinguishes statistical normalcy from functional adequacy as competing baseline criteria, and explain whether adopting that distinction dissolves the therapy/enhancement boundary or merely relocates it. Note which reading is likely being relied on when someone uses the slogan to end an argument about whether a specific intervention—say, a cognitive implant for age-related decline—counts as medicine or as improvement, and flag any reading that confuses a causal description of change with a normative judgment about permissibility.
