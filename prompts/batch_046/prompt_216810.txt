I have been trying to systematize my intuitive judgments about when rational credence should defer to an objective physical chance, and I am not sure they form a coherent pattern. Here are my verdicts on seven cases.

Case 1: A fair coin is flipped and hidden under a cup. I have no reason to think it is biased. Verdict: my credence should match the physical chance, exactly 1/2, and any prior should be overridden.

Case 2: The same coin, but I have just observed it land heads on ten consecutive visible flips, with no independent reason to suspect bias. Verdict: for the next flip I still defer fully to 1/2.

Case 3: A radioactive atom with a well-defined half-life of one hour. I have no special information about its state. Verdict: my credence in decay within the hour should match the exponential decay law.

Case 4: The same atom, but I am inside an enclosed laboratory in which my own cognitive state is physically correlated with the decay outcome—I do not know whether the decay occurred, and my epistemic access is limited to my local branch—while the global physical state is definite. Verdict: I should apply the decay law only within the partition defined by my self-locating uncertainty, so that my all-things-considered credence is not the raw physical probability but that probability conditioned on my branch.

Case 5: A deterministic pseudorandom generator produces outputs from a seed that is an unknown physical constant. Verdict: I defer to a uniform distribution over the possible outputs, treating the deterministic mechanism as epistemically chancy.

Case 6: The same generator, but the seed is my own birth date, which I know with certainty. Verdict: I do not defer to uniformity; I compute the deterministic sequence and assign credence near 1 or 0 to the actual output.

Case 7: A physical process has an objective success-chance of 0.9. Independently, I have a highly calibrated, non-causal predictive faculty—say, a trained statistical intuition—that assigns success a credence of 0.3. Verdict: I feel I should neither fully defer to the physical chance nor fully override it with my intuition; a simple linear average seems wrong, yet some form of weighted compromise feels appropriate.

Is there a single, precisely stated principle of deference to objective chance—perhaps one that distinguishes epistemic opacity, indexical partitioning, and direct informational access—that delivers all seven verdicts? If not, which verdict is the outlier that should be abandoned? Furthermore, what does the best available principle predict for a hybrid case I have not listed: a deterministic algorithm seeded with a constant about which I have only partial knowledge, running inside the same kind of enclosed laboratory as Case 4?
