Please explain the conceptual tension in Theravada Buddhist philosophy between the Abhidharma mereological reduction of the person to momentary skandhas—denying any persistent substrate or ātman—and the doctrinal requirement that karmic consequences persist across lives. Specifically, articulate how orthodox Theravada addresses what might be called the ownership problem: if there is no enduring self, in what sense does a future being genuinely inherit or suffer the results of present actions, and why is this not a mere causal chain like one fire lighting another? Contrast this orthodox resolution, which relies on the distinction between conventional designation (paññatti) and ultimate reality (paramattha), with the Pudgalavādin heresy that posits a pudgala—an indescribable entity neither identical to nor different from the aggregates—to serve as the bearer of continuity. Your explanation should clarify why the Pudgalavādin position is not simply a disguised permanent self, identify the precise structural feature of the orthodox account that allows moral continuity without substantial identity, and show how the orthodox view avoids collapsing into either a reductionist bundle theory that severs moral responsibility or a substantialist view that violates anatta. Do not provide a bibliography, reading list, or textual citations; the aim is a conceptual map of the disagreement rather than scholarly apparatus.
