okay so i was re-reading Mill's late stuff on socialism — you know, the parts in Principles and those posthumous chapters where he moves away from pure laissez-faire and starts talking about worker cooperatives and how land rent is basically theft by the landlord class — and i tried to build an argument for my study group that now feels like it eats it's own tail.

(1) in Mill's later view, private property in land is illegitimate because nobody made the land; the rent is a social product, not an individual one.
(2) he extends this to accumulated capital: once capital is built up, it gives owners power over laborers that isn't justified by any current contribution.
(3) he thinks this justifies restructuring production into voluntary cooperative associations, but not state coercion, because people can enter and exit freely.
(4) the reason economic dependence is bad, on his account, is that it makes people unfree in practice — they can't develop their capacities or live autonomusly.
(5) but in his feminist writing he describes marriage under coverture as creating exactly the same kind of economic dependence: women are legally bound to husbands, with no practical exit, and this stunts their development.
(6) so if economic dependence without direct state coercion is enough to justify restructuring an institution, then marriage should be abolished or replaced by purely contractual, dissolvable unions with state-guaranteed exit rights.
(7) Mill obviously doesn't go that far; he want's reform, not abolition.
(8) so either his socialism applies way further than he admits — it proves too much — or his principle in (4) isn't actually doing the work he thinks.

i want to keep (3) and (5) if i can. which step should i reject, and what does it cost me? is there an equivocation between 'economic dependence in production' and 'economic dependence in domestic life', or am i just smuggling in a false analog? also does anyone think Mill's distinction between gradual and revolutionary socialism in those later essays is basically just him chickening out, or is there a real philosophical line there?
