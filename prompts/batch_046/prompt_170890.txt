I'm preparing an essay for my politics A-level on whether the problem of "dirty hands" still applies in modern democracies. Please write a dialogue between two equally informed sixth-form students arguing about this. One should defend the classic position: that a democratically elected leader who secretly authorises unconstitutional surveillance to prevent an imminent terrorist attack commits a necessary wrong, and must personally bear moral residue even though lives are saved. The other should argue that in systems with independent oversight and collective emergency protocols, moral responsibility can be institutionally distributed; therefore the leader ought to refuse unilateral action and trigger formal procedures, even at the risk of failure, and that invoking "dirty hands" today often serves to excuse the circumvention of democratic checks. They must engage with each other's actual arguments—specifically whether moral guilt can be genuinely shared by institutions, and whether a refusal to act unilaterally can itself be a culpable omission. Neither speaker should be a strawman. Please present this in polished British English.
