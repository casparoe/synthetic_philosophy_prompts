I have been reading about quantum mechanics outside any formal course of study, working through popular accounts and trying to follow the logic myself, and I keep returning to the same stalemate: physicists agree entirely on the equations and on every experimental result, yet they remain divided over whether the wavefunction is a real feature of the world or merely a catalog of our ignorance. On one side are those who treat it as ontic—something like a field, except living in a high-dimensional configuration space—while on the other are those who treat it as epistemic, analogous to a statistical distribution over hidden states that we do not yet know. The disagreement has outlasted decoherence theory, Bell-type experiments, and results that constrain hidden-variable reconstructions. My working hypothesis is that the dispute is not genuinely empirical but conceptual: the two camps are using different criteria for what makes something "real," with one side demanding localization in ordinary three-dimensional space and the other accepting any entity that plays an irreducible dynamical role in the formalism. But I am unsure whether this diagnosis captures the actual division, or whether there is a submerged formal disagreement—perhaps about the uniqueness of state assignments, about whether a realist interpretation must reproduce every quantum correlation without fine-tuning, or about whether being derivable from deeper laws disqualifies an entity from being fundamental—that keeps the debate alive. I want a diagnosis with reasons, not a survey of interpretations: is this a verbal disagreement masked by physics language, a clash of explanatory standards, a hidden empirical question that current experiments have not touched, or an ill-posed question that assumes a false dichotomy between representation and reality? Specifically, would a distinction between dynamical reality—playing an irreducible role in the law of evolution—and representational reality—corresponding to a property possessed by physical systems independently of our descriptions—sharpen or dissolve the conflict? And if the disagreement is substantive rather than conceptual, what kind of result, whether a theorem about state reconstruction under weak assumptions or an experiment probing macroscopic superpositions in a regime where decoherence is suppressed, could actually end it, and why has nothing of that sort already done so?
