I am marking essays for a second-year philosophy of art module and have come across a response that strikes me as superficially competent yet possibly conceals some subtle philosophical confusions. The examination question asked students to consider Morris Weitz’s claim that art is an “open concept” for which no essential definition can be given, together with George Dickie’s institutional theory that an artefact becomes art only when appropriate members of the art world confer upon it the status of candidate for appreciation. The specific case presented was that of an isolated individual, entirely unfamiliar with artistic institutions, who carves a wooden figure purely to satisfy an internal urge to shape form, and whose work is discovered and exhibited only decades later. Students were required to explain how each theory treats this case and to assess whether a defensible middle position exists.

The student’s answer reads as follows. Weitz argues that art is an open concept in the Wittgensteinian sense, meaning that artworks share only family resemblances and that no single set of necessary and sufficient conditions can capture the category. Because the concept is open, Weitz’s position allows us to admit virtually any object into art provided we can perceive sufficient resemblance to existing works; consequently, the isolated carver poses no real difficulty, since once the figure is discovered we can simply note its formal similarities to recognised sculptures and include it without contradiction. Dickie’s institutional theory, presented in Art and the Aesthetic, maintains that art requires both artefactuality and the conferral of status by an appropriate art-world public. In the present case no such conferral occurs at the moment of creation, since the carver stands entirely outside the relevant institutions; nevertheless, Dickie can reply retrospectively, for when curators later exhibit the figure they confer institutional status upon it then, exactly as Danto proposed in his well-known 1964 essay on the artworld, so the object becomes art only after discovery. A plausible middle ground might combine Weitz’s flexibility with Dickie’s social dimension by holding that an object is art if it is produced with artistic intention and is subsequently acknowledged by some community, even if that community is not the original institutional art world. This avoids both rigid essentialism and the counter-intuitive claim that art can exist with no human recognition whatsoever. On balance, Weitz’s account is the more accommodating, but Dickie’s retrospective manoeuvre saves his theory, so neither view is clearly wrong.
