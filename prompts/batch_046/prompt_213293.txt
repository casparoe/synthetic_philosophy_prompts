At a philosophy-of-mind study group, two members—Jordan and Riley—got into a sustained disagreement over Hofstadter’s careenium thought experiment: whether high-level symbols (simmballs) can genuinely push the low-level substrate (marbles) around, or whether all causal power remains at the substrate level.

Jordan opened by arguing that downward causation is real in the careenium. He offered the case of a mathematician who, upon recognizing a contradiction in her proof (a symbol-level event), stops working, changes her sleep schedule, and eventually abandons the project. The physical changes—neural firing patterns, hormonal shifts—are organized by the recognition, not merely coincident with it. Without the symbol-level structure, those particular substrate configurations would not have occurred.

Riley replied that this is a category mistake. The “recognition” is just a name for a particular distribution of marble interactions. The mathematician’s behavioral changes are caused by synaptic weights, neurotransmitter levels, and muscle contractions—all substrate. To say the symbol “contradiction” pushes marbles is like saying the average temperature pushes individual air molecules; it is a statistical summary misdescribed as a cause.

Jordan then pressed the predictive-processing example. In visual perception, high-level predictions—simmballs like “this is a face”—literally suppress or amplify lower-level sensory signals. The substrate dynamics in the visual cortex are modulated in real time by these predictions. That looks like symbols altering substrate dynamics, not just emerging from them.

Riley countered that predictive processing is still substrate all the way through. The “prediction” is a distributed pattern of synaptic weights and neural firing rates—marbles arranged in a particular configuration. Those weights influence other weights through physical connections. There is no non-physical symbol reaching down; it is one marble-configuration causally interacting with another. The appearance of downward causation comes from our habit of labeling patterns after they form.

Jordan then introduced what he called “symbolic lock-in.” Once a careenium develops a stable symbol—say, the concept of zero—it becomes a global constraint. A child learning arithmetic does not just undergo local synaptic changes; the symbol reorganizes numerical cognition across the entire network, creating a fixed point that future substrate dynamics must accommodate. If the substrate were purely bottom-up, Jordan argued, we should expect local drift and fragmentation, but instead we see global coherence around the symbol. He added that there is an asymmetry: if you damage part of the substrate, the symbol can persist elsewhere and even guide reconstruction through plasticity, suggesting the symbol has a kind of autonomy rather than being a mere epiphenomenon.

Riley responded that “symbolic lock-in” is only a name for an attractor state in a dynamical system. The substrate has self-organizing properties—feedback loops, homeostasis, Hebbian learning—that naturally produce global coherence. The reconstruction of lost substrate patterns is still driven by the physical rules of the remaining marbles. To claim the symbol guided this process is to confuse the map with the territory: we call the resulting pattern “zero,” but the causal work was done entirely by the marbles.

Then give an overall verdict on who is more right, and specify what important point both Jordan and Riley are missing—perhaps about the distinction between causal efficacy and explanatory indispensability, or about how the careenium metaphor itself frames the problem. In doing so, separate what is broadly agreed upon (for instance, that high-level patterns can be explanatory indispensable) from what remains genuinely contested among philosophers and cognitive scientists (whether that indispensability implies genuine downward causal power, and whether attractor-state explanations fully dissolve the intuition of symbolic autonomy). Do not present any contested interpretation of downward causation, emergentism, or substrate independence as though it were the consensus view.
