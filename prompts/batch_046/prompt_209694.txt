At a recent dinner in my study, two guests—both scholars of different temperaments—fell into a disagreement about whether Nozick’s tracking conditions preserve epistemic closure when belief-formation involves what I shall call accidental rectification. The historian, Elena, maintained that if a researcher knows that a particular archive holds original correspondence (call this belief P), and competently infers from it that a certain diplomatic claim is false (call this Q), then she knows Q even if her belief in P originally rested on a forged catalog entry that was later corrected by pure coincidence. Elena argued for a principle of epistemic quarantine: once a belief is embedded in a stable inferential architecture, its original modal vulnerabilities are isolated, so that Q can satisfy the sensitivity and adherence conditions independently of P’s shaky provenance. The philosopher, Marcus, objected that this abandons the heart of tracking theory. He proposed instead the idea of modal contagion: if P is true only because of an accidental feature of the actual world—namely, the fortuitous discovery and correction of the forgery—then P fails the sensitivity condition, since in nearby worlds where the error persists the researcher still believes P. Consequently, any belief that essentially depends on P inherits this fragility, and closure fails. Elena replied that Marcus conflates causal genealogy with present modal responsiveness; a belief’s tracking profile should be evaluated at the point of inference, not traced back through every historical contingency. Marcus countered that knowledge is not a property of isolated sentences but of cognitive relations to the world, so quarantining a belief from its origins is like treating a symptom without curing the infection. They turned to me—the novelist who had invited them, and who possesses no special authority in epistemology—and asked which of them had grasped the true cost of tracking for closure.

Please adjudicate their exchange point by point. Is Elena right that closure can survive through epistemic quarantine, or is Marcus right that modal contagion undermines it? Who is correct about whether Nozick’s conditions apply to premises, to conclusions, or to the inferential link itself? And what, if anything, are both missing—perhaps a third position that neither quarantine nor contagion captures, or a way in which their framing already distorts the problem of closure?
