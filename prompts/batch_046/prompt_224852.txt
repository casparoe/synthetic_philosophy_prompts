Design a 75-minute classroom activity for an upper-level philosophy-of-religion seminar of about 14 students (groups of three or four) that lets them feel the tension in Molinist middle knowledge—specifically the problem of self-defeating counterfactuals inside feasible worlds—before anyone names the view. Give each group a deck of circumstance cards describing what an agent with libertarian freedom would do in a specific situation, including at least two “unstable” cards in which the agent’s free response to being placed in that situation undermines the very conditions the card describes. Groups rotate through three roles: Architect, who must pick exactly one card to place the Agent in to achieve a predetermined goal; Agent, who receives the card and must declare whether they accept the placement or exercise a “rejection” move that falsifies the card; and Logician, who tracks whether the Architect’s knowledge of the card made the outcome unavoidable. Include concise rules, a scoring scheme that rewards Architects for achieving goals but penalizes them if the Agent rejects the card, a timeline (10 minutes setup, three 15-minute rounds with revelation, 15 minutes open deliberation), and a debrief. The debrief should map moments of instability onto the Molinist distinction between counterfactuals of freedom and feasible-world selection, address the predictable confusion between foreknowledge and causal determination and how to disentangle it without lecturing, and be explicit about which metaphysical feature—such as the pre-volitional grounding of true counterfactuals—the activity necessarily cannot reproduce and the facilitator must supply. Do not reveal the terms “Molinism,” “middle knowledge,” or “counterfactuals of freedom” until the debrief begins.
