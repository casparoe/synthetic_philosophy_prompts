I serve as the doctoral-student representative on my university’s Endowment Policy Subcommittee, and we are preparing for Thursday’s vote on Proposal 7-B, which would authorize the investment office to allocate up to eighteen percent of the operating endowment to a frontier-technology fund governed by a fractional Kelly criterion rather than conventional mean-variance optimization. The proposal’s architects argue that an institution with an indefinite lifespan should maximize the time-averaged growth rate of its capital, accepting higher volatility in exchange for avoiding the slow erosion of purchasing power—what they call ergodic drift—that risk-averse policies produce over multi-decade horizons. To address the standard objection that Kelly-style sizing is reckless for entities with present obligations, they have designed a bifurcated pool mechanism: capital is split into a protected survival tranche, collateralized to preserve ninety-five percent of its initial value, and a leveraged growth tranche sized according to full geometric-rationality rules. Opponents, led by the faculty trustee from the law school, reply that fiduciary duty requires expected-utility maximization with a hard ruin-avoidance constraint, that the survival tranche’s correlations will fail precisely in crisis conditions, and that framing institutional rationality in purely geometric terms illegitimately discounts the welfare of current students and staff whose funding depends on stable annual draws.

Please draft the formal committee opinion I will circulate before the meeting. It should recommend a concrete course of action—approve, reject, or approve with enforceable conditions—together with a clear implementation timeline, and it must be written so that non-specialist trustees can follow the reasoning. The memo should treat the geometric-rationality argument at full strength, including an honest engagement with the bifurcated-pool design, rather than dismissing it as speculative finance; at the same time, it must give the fiduciary-objection its due weight and explicitly identify the strongest consideration supporting the side that does not prevail. The deliverable should read as an actionable institutional document with a clear bottom line, not as personal guidance or an abstract philosophical essay.
