As a software engineer with no formal background in philosophy, I keep running into the slogan that the self is merely a process, not a substance, especially in discussions of Buddhist anatta and personal identity. In my workplace mindfulness group and in tech essays about consciousness and brain uploading, people invoke it to dismiss worries about diachronic identity: they argue that because there is no enduring thing that is me, there is nothing puzzling about my changing over time, and nothing that blocks a future digital replica from counting as me. I want to understand what precise philosophical claims could be packed into that phrase, because right now it operates as a conversation-stopper.

Could you lay out the distinct theses that the self being just a process might actually express, ordered from the most trivial to the most radical? For each reading, please say whether it amounts to a platitude about mental change, a substantive metaphysical denial of an enduring substrate or soul, or a false inference about conditions of identity over time. I am particularly interested in which readings actually support the two moves I have seen: (1) dissolving the problem of personal survival across time, and (2) licensing the claim that a sufficiently accurate behavioural simulation or brain-state replication could constitute continuity of the same person.

To help me test the intuitions without leaving engineering, I would like each reading mapped onto an analogy from distributed computing — for example, a stateless function, a container with persistent storage, or a consensus protocol with no single authoritative node — and for you to indicate where that parallel clarifies the philosophical claim and where it introduces a misleading feature. Please do not supply a bibliography, reading list, or specific citations; I need an analysis that stands on its own. Finish by identifying which reading people in these conversations are most likely assuming, whether that assumption is defensible, and whether the dismissal of personal-identity worries survives once the ambiguity is made explicit.
