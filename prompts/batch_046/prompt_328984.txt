I serve, somewhat unexpectedly, on the program committee of the Meridian Foundation for Civic History, a body that funds museum exhibitions at the intersection of economic thought and public ethics. By profession I am a novelist, and I tend to read institutional language for hidden motives and suppressed premises; that habit is, I think, why the chair has asked me to draft our formal opinion on a proposal now before us.

The proposal requests $340,000 to mount a traveling exhibition titled The Colonial Ledger: Adam Smith, Empire, and the Moral Geography of Commerce. The curators advance a precise interpretive thesis: that Smith’s economic arguments against colonial monopoly—his claim that maintaining colonies is a source of expense rather than profit, his analysis of the monopoly of the colony trade as a distortion of domestic capital and labor, and his characterization of imperial expansion as driven by the folly of rulers pursuing mercantilist grandeur—should not be read as purely internalist calculations of national welfare. Rather, they argue that these economic objections rest on a suppressed normative premise drawn from the Theory of Moral Sentiments: the mechanism of sympathy and the impartial spectator, applied consistently and without the geographical and political exclusions that Smith’s own historical context imposed, implicitly undermines the moral arbitrariness of imperial domination. The exhibition would use this recovered premise to frame modern supply-chain ethics.

A dissenting trustee, a historian of economic thought, has circulated a counter-memorandum arguing that this reading imports modern external-reasons moralism into a system that was consistently focused on the welfare of the metropole. In his view, Smith’s objections to empire concerned only the diversion of capital, the maintenance of standing armies to defend distant possessions, and the corruption of domestic politics by colonial interests; his silence regarding the rights of colonized subjects reflects not a hidden cosmopolitanism but the coherent analytical boundaries of his framework. The committee is divided. Half fear that funding the exhibition endorses an anachronistic projection; half fear that rejecting it suppresses a genuinely philosophical dimension of Smith’s work that connects his economics to his moral philosophy.

We must vote before the quarter ends, and I am to circulate the committee’s written opinion—not a compromise, but a clear recommendation with reasons—by Thursday. The memo should recommend one of three concrete courses: approve the grant with the curators’ framing intact; approve only on condition that the exhibition’s interpretive claims be revised so that the internalist reading is presented as primary and the normative recovery as speculative; or reject the proposal outright, with a specific timeline for notifying the applicants and, if applicable, for reconsidering the matter in the next funding cycle.

The reasoning must treat the dissenting historian’s position as its strongest version. It should articulate precisely how the internalist reading accounts for Smith’s specific claims about colonial labor regimes, defense costs, market distortion, and the comparative welfare of mother countries versus colonies, and it should explain why that reading is or is not sufficient to exhaust the philosophical significance of the texts. Conversely, it must treat the curators’ recovery seriously: it should ask whether Smith’s distinction between productive and unproductive labor, when applied to the administrative and military labor required to maintain colonies, creates a structural parallel that undermines the claim that imperial domination was merely an external accident to his system. The memo should also consider—this is the niche point I want included—whether the spatial and political distance between the metropole and the colonies structurally disables the sympathy mechanism of the impartial spectator, and whether this disability explains Smith’s silence without justifying it, turning his moral system into an unwitting critique of its own exclusions.

Please write this as a formal institutional memo: a header identifying the committee and the matter; a recommendation section stating the concrete action and the implementation timeline; a reasoning section that moves through the interpretive dispute with intellectual precision; and a concluding section that addresses the losing side’s strongest consideration with honesty rather than dismissal. Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or requests for citations. Do not invent specific editions, page numbers, or publication dates. Refer to Smith’s concepts—the monopoly of the colony trade, the distinction between productive and unproductive labor, the impartial spectator, the mechanism of sympathy—in terms general enough that a well-prepared model could work with them without web access, but precise enough that the argument is genuinely in the weeds of Smith scholarship and moral philosophy. The tone should be that of a polished, decisive committee document, but with the underlying precision—perhaps visible only in the care with which motives are traced—of an author accustomed to constructing characters whose stated reasons are never quite the whole story.
