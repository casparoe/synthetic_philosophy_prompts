I've been arguing with my rommate about whether moral status comes in degrees — like insects have a little, dogs have more, and then at the bottom you have smart thermostats that learn your preferences but don't feel anything. We were trying to apply Bentham's old criterion about whether they can suffer but got stuck: is there a continuous scale or just a bright line somewhere? If Bentham were alive now and saw the current literature on gradated sentience — especially the idea that we might assign fractional moral weight to entities like insects or even adaptive climate-control systems with complex feedback loops — what do you think he'd actually say? Would he stick to his binary line, or adapt it to allow partial moral status?

Specifically, I'm curious how he'd react to the proposal that a thermostat with enough sensors could earn, say, 0.001 of a human's moral weight not because it feels anything, but because its functional analog to suffering is technically nonzero. I know he loved quantification and utility calculus, but I'm not sure if he'd see this as a natural extension of his principle or as a complete betrayal of it. Would he draw a hard line at phenomenal consciousness, or would he allow moral status to fade gradually like a dimmer switch? And if he went for the dimmer switch, where exactly would he place the cutoff — at insects, at nematodes, at bacteria, or all the way down to the thermostat?
