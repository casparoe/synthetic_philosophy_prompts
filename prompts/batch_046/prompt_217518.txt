I keep running into the same one-liner in discussions about origin essentialism: "If a different sperm and egg had come together, it wouldn't be you—it'd be somebody else." Usually it's deployed in philosophy seminars—often right after someone raises the swapped-embryo case or asks whether psychological continuity could survive a different genetic start—to dismiss psychological-continuity accounts of personal identity, as if biological origin alone proves you couldn't have been the continuous person who developed from a different zygote. Before I push back, I want a precise disambiguation. Separate out the distinct theses this sentence could be expressing: for instance, a Kripke-style modal claim about rigid designation of origins; an essentialist claim that one's actual gametes are part of one's individual essence; a biological claim about genetic uniqueness being identity-constituting; a counterfactual claim about what we should say in a nearby possible world; and a semantic claim about how we use proper names across worlds. For each reading, indicate whether it is trivial, whether it is genuinely contentious, whether it is false, and—crucially—whether it actually does any work against psychological-continuity theories or just changes the subject from psychology to metaphysics. Present the results as a menu of interpretations with verdicts, not as a defense of any single reading.
