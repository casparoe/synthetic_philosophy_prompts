I am a doctoral researcher in medical anthropology. For the past year I have been working with a peer-support network that describes emotional distress using vocabulary drawn from local craft traditions rather than clinical psychology. The framework is internally coherent and helps members navigate crises, but it lacks the inter-rater reliability that psychiatrists treat as a baseline for legitimate knowledge. At a recent departmental colloquium, a senior psychiatrist interrupted my presentation to say that without operationalised criteria the framework was merely poetic, and several colleagues nodded in agreement. Later, a philosopher of science friend warned me that defending the network solely on grounds of practical success risks consigning its members to a kind of epistemic ghetto—respected within its own boundaries but never granted full cognitive authority—while insisting on clinical validation would erase the very distinctiveness that makes the framework valuable. I want to defend the network's authority without either surrendering to clinical hegemony or romanticising isolation. Is her warning well-founded, and how ought I to understand the authority of this framework as knowledge?
