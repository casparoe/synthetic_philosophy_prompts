As a retired experimental physicist who has recently begun serious study in philosophy of mind and science, I wish to press on a specific tension within Daniel Dennett’s framework of real patterns and the three explanatory stances—physical, design, and intentional—when it is applied to evolved cognitive architectures. I am not seeking a bibliography or literature review; I want a direct conceptual analysis that I can evaluate with the conceptual tools I already have.

The case I have in mind concerns predictive-processing models of the brain, in which neural dynamics are interpreted as implementing hierarchical Bayesian inference. From the design stance, synaptic weights and prediction-error signals are treated as functional components optimized to represent an external environment. From the physical stance, there are only ion-channel dynamics, metabolic energy flows, and electrochemical gradients. The difficulty arises because evolutionary optimization has produced architectures that are exquisitely sensitive to input perturbations carrying no representational content whatsoever—the phenomenon familiar in artificial networks as adversarial examples. From the design stance, these perturbations are pure noise; from the physical stance, they are highly structured trajectories through the high-dimensional state space of the network.

My question is whether this reveals a form of what one might call “stance leakage” that undermines the tidy separation Dennett proposes. If a system selected for representational accuracy can be driven into radically different physical states by inputs that are representationally null, does this show that the design stance is not merely a heuristic overlay on physical patterns, but is itself parameterized by contingent evolutionary history in a way that makes the physical and design stances incommensurable rather than complementary? And does this further suggest that “real patterns” in biological cognition are stance-relative in a stronger sense than Dennett allows—perhaps that there is no stance-independent fact about whether a given neural state belongs to a genuine pattern?

Please explain the conceptual issue precisely. I want to understand whether the adversarial-perturbation phenomenon in artificial and biological networks genuinely threatens the autonomy of the design stance, or whether it can be absorbed within Dennett’s framework by treating evolutionary optimization itself as a kind of higher-order design pattern. Be explicit about where the boundary between the physical and design stances should be drawn in this case, and whether predictive processing ultimately forces us to collapse that boundary into a single, stance-relative ontology.
