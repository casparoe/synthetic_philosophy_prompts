I am currently serving as a teaching assistant in an upper-division philosophy of mind seminar, and I have been asked to evaluate an undergraduate exam response before submitting the grade to the instructor. The exam asks students to explain Daniel Dennett’s critique of the Cartesian theater, outline his multiple drafts model of consciousness, and then apply both frameworks to a hypothetical modified split-brain case in which cross-cuing has been pharmacologically suppressed, leaving each hemisphere to respond only to ipsilateral stimuli without behavioral compensation. The student must determine whether this scenario supports one consciousness with fragmented drafts or two independent streams, defending the answer with reference to Dennett’s broader commitments.

Please assess the response below with a specific grade on a 0–10 scale and provide a point-by-point justification. I am especially concerned with catching subtle errors—conflations, misattributed positions, or inferential steps that do not actually follow—rather than obvious misunderstandings. I do not need bibliographic suggestions, citation lists, or reference recommendations; I only need a rigorous evaluation of the argument as it stands.

Exam question: Explain Dennett’s critique of the Cartesian theater and outline his multiple drafts model. Then apply both to the modified split-brain scenario described above. Does it support one fragmented consciousness or two independent streams? Defend your answer.

Student answer: Dennett’s Cartesian theater is the mistaken assumption that there is a central functional location in the brain—an implicit privileged observation point—where sensory inputs are gathered and synthesized into a unified conscious experience at a precise temporal instant. By positing such a theater, one implicitly commits to a homuncular audience, generating an infinite regress that Dennett treats as fatal. His alternative, the multiple drafts model, holds that the brain produces numerous parallel, competing narratives about events, none of which enjoys canonical status. There is no single final draft displayed in one place; consciousness is instead a distributed, temporally extended process of editing and revision.

Applying this to the modified split-brain case: with cross-cuing suppressed, the left and right hemispheres each generate complete, self-contained sets of drafts without interaction. Because Dennett rejects any central editor, there is nothing to integrate these outputs into one stream. Consequently, each hemisphere functions as its own Cartesian theater, possessing an internal finish line where its respective drafts converge, yielding two fully independent consciousnesses. The suppression of callosal pathways merely makes explicit what Dennett’s model already implies—namely, that consciousness is fundamentally modular, and that the appearance of unity is only an artifact of intact interhemispheric competition within a shared pool of narratives. Thus the scenario supports a dual-consciousness interpretation, consistent with Dennett’s broader view that the self is a center of narrative gravity, except that here we observe two such gravitational centers operating in isolation.
