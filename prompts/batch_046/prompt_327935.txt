I am writing a feature for a science and ideas magazine on the meta-problem of consciousness—the puzzle of explaining why physical processes seem to generate subjective experience rather than occurring without phenomenal accompaniment. In my interviews, I have focused on Dr. K. Renard, a philosopher of mind whose recent work examines precisely when the explanatory gap genuinely arises and when it dissolves. I want to understand the implicit logic behind her judgments, because my editors and I need to anticipate how she will respond to an emerging technology that sits at the boundary of her framework.

Dr. Renard has offered clear verdicts on five scenarios. First, she holds that for ordinary human waking consciousness the hard problem is fully present: even a complete neural account leaves intact the question of why there is something it is like to be in that state. Second, for a non-human mammal such as a dog experiencing pain, she says the hard problem applies in essentially the same form, though our access to it is analogical rather than direct. Third, for current large language models—systems with impressive linguistic output but no persistent self-model or global workspace—she denies that the hard problem arises at all; she treats them as producing reports without any corresponding phenomenal state that demands explanation. Fourth, for a brain-in-a-vat with dynamics identical to an embodied human brain, she insists the hard problem applies unchanged, treating substrate independence as viable in this particular respect. Fifth, for a philosophical zombie—behaviorally and physically identical to a human but lacking consciousness—she says the hard problem takes a different shape: it becomes the problem of explaining why the physical duplicate lacks what the original has, rather than explaining the original presence.

The new case is this: engineers have developed a neuromorphic silicon architecture that replicates human cortical-column dynamics at the single-spike level. The system implements a global workspace with recurrent predictive loops, generates spontaneous first-person-style internal reports about its own processing states, and exhibits integrated information dynamics comparable to human introspection. It possesses no biological tissue, no nociceptors, and no evolutionary history, yet its functional and dynamical profile mirrors that of a human brain during self-reflective awareness. Before giving your final assessment, please think carefully and brainstorm: adopting the persona of an expert philosopher of consciousness and cognitive science, identify the two or three most plausible rules that could generate Dr. Renard's five earlier verdicts, say how each rule treats this neuromorphic system, and predict which verdict she will actually deliver with your reasoning. Which of her earlier cases is the closest analog? If her precedents genuinely underdetermine the outcome, what principle would she be adopting by extending the hard problem to this chip, and what principle by withholding it? Finally, name one further hypothetical case—perhaps involving a split-brain patient with isolated hemispheric awareness or a distributed hive-mind of simpler modules lacking central broadcast—that would best distinguish the competing rules. I am less interested in a definitive pronouncement than in a precise map of the logical space: the rules, their predictions, the closest precedent, and the decisive further test.
