I was arguing with my roommate about this principle in philosophy of probability called the Principal Principle — basically that if you know the objective chance of something is x then your rational creedence should line up with x abscent other defeating info. He built this weird argument he says shows the principle eats itself and I can't find the exit. Here are his steps, tell me where it actually slips.

(1) The Principal Principle says rational credence in event E should match the known objective chance ch(E) = x, unless you've got defeating evidence.
(2) Now suppose physics gives us an objective measure over all observer-moments in the multiverse: 99% of the measure-weight lands on "flicker" observers that exist for one Planck time with random false beliefs, and only 1% on stable long-lived observers like us.
(3) I learn this measure and I also learn I'm having an experience right now, but I don't have any further info about which kind of observer I am.
(4) So by self-location plus the measure, the objective chance that I'm a flicker-observer is about 99%, and therefore by PP my credence that I'm a flicker-observer should be 99%.
(5) But if I'm a flicker-observer, then my reasoning in steps (1)-(4) is just random thermal noise, so the objective chance that that reasoning is correct is near 0.
(6) Applying PP again, I should have near-0 credence in the conclusion of (4), which means I should reject that I'm probably a flicker-observer — but that conflicts directly with (4).
(7) He says this loop means PP either forces us into total skepticism or it's self-refuting, so we should ditch it entirely. I feel like there's a hidden move between (3) and (4) where self-location and objective chance get smushed together, but I can't pin it down. Which step is the real problem, and what's the cost of fixing it? If I have to choose I'd rather keep the principle and drop the multiverse premise, but I want to know what I'm actually paying.
