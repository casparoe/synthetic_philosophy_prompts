Imagine John Maynard Keynes were shown a contemporary macroeconomic forecasting system in which an ensemble of large language models generates calibrated, high-dimensional probability distributions over inflation, unemployment, and growth, with the architects claiming that any residual ignorance is purely aleatoric and can be fully priced into derivative markets. Drawing specifically on Keynes’s distinction—developed in his Treatise on Probability—between measurable risk, genuine uncertainty, and the weight of argument, how would he likely assess the system’s claim to have captured all economically relevant ignorance? Would he view the precision of the output distributions as an increase in the weight of available evidence, or as a misleading quantification that collapses true uncertainty into spurious risk? Structure your answer around his likely objections to treating policy-relevant futures as if they were governed by stable, knowable distributions.
