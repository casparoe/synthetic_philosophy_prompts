I am a novelist who has been sitting in on a philosophy seminar on value theory, partly because I am interested in how narratives depend on the expectation of an audience that will outlive the author. The professor, knowing my background, asked me to assist with grading a recent exam on Samuel Scheffler's argument that human extinction is catastrophic in ways that exceed the sum of individual deaths. I want to be fair, but one submission has left me uncertain about where to draw the line between a genuinely subtle error and a catastrophic misunderstanding, and I would value a second opinion.

The exam question was this: Scheffler contends that the prospect of human extinction undermines present value through what he calls an "afterlife conjecture" about our dependence on future generations. Explain that conjecture, clarify whose values are affected—present people, future people, or both—and show why, on Scheffler's account, the loss is not reducible to a utilitarian calculation of foregone welfare or to the violation of future persons' rights. Be precise about whether the argument requires any belief in literal personal immortality.

Here is the student's answer. Scheffler's afterlife conjecture, developed in his 2013 work, holds that human beings operate with an implicit, quasi-religious belief in a literal afterlife—something like a post-mortem heaven—without which our long-term projects would feel pointless. He applies this to extinction by arguing that if humanity ends, future generations are robbed of that anticipated afterlife experience, which makes extinction a harm done primarily to them. More broadly, the student writes, Scheffler treats our present activities—writing books, raising children, building institutions—as purely instrumental goods that derive their value from producing pleasure or welfare for the people who come after us. Consequently, extinction is bad mainly because it frustrates a present duty to maximize the welfare of those future individuals, and the present generation itself loses nothing of intrinsic importance if no one survives to inherit its achievements. The argument is therefore best understood as a form of intergenerational utilitarianism that depends on treating future welfare as an object of present moral obligation.

Please grade this answer and provide the point-by-point comments that justify your assessment. Identify exactly where the student grasps the structural point—that our values are anchored in an expectation of continuity—and where the reasoning slips: any mischaracterization of the "afterlife conjecture," any conflation of Scheffler's position with utilitarian or rights-based frameworks, and any inferential step that does not actually follow from the premises offered. Give a specific letter grade, and indicate which of these errors are subtle enough that a hasty grader might let them pass. Do not include bibliographic references or citation lists in your response; I am grading without access to the library, and I need your evaluation to stand on its own.
