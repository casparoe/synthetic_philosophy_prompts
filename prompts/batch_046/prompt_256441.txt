A colleague who works in behavioural economics recently sent me the following summary of the debate over sunk costs. I should like to check its accuracy before I rely on it in a discussion.

'Economists treat the sunk-cost fallacy as the error of letting unrecoverable past expenditures influence present choices. The canonical rational-choice framework considers only marginal future costs and benefits, which implies that honouring sunk costs is always irrational, regardless of context. More recently, philosophers of practical reason have challenged this universal claim by appealing to diachronic rationality. They argue that a person extended through time is structurally analogous to a collective agent, and that abandoning a long-standing project can constitute a betrayal of commitments made by one's earlier temporal stages. Consequently, persisting with a failing course is not necessarily a cognitive failure but can express loyalty to one's past self. On this view, the fallacy applies only to isolated, one-off decisions and effectively dissolves for agents who possess narrative identity or sustained long-term commitments.'

This sounds coherent, yet I suspect it may oversimplify the economic position and misrepresent the philosophical counter-argument—particularly the claim that temporal stages are treated as separate agents with independent commitments, and the conclusion that sunk-cost reasoning is never fallacious in long-term contexts. Is this a fair summary? Please separate what is accurate, what is a common simplification, and where the philosophical reply has been subtly misrepresented or overstated. I am inclined to believe that economists allow more nuance than the summary suggests, but I cannot locate the specific errors.
