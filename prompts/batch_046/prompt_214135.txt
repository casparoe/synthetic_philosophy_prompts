I've been turning over the classic problem of whether genuine moral dilemmas are metaphysically possible—cases where an agent faces two obligations that are genuinely binding, mutually exclusive, and leave unavoidable moral residue regardless of choice. The standard dialectic pits deontic consistency (if ought implies can, then contradictory obligations entail an impossible action and therefore cannot both be real) against the phenomenology of tragic choice, where decision-makers report feeling irreversibly "stained" even when they act correctly. Most treatments stay within familiar human-scale examples: the captain who must save some passengers and abandon others, or the agent torn between loyalty and justice. But I want to test the issue with a less conventional framing.

Consider a decentralized autonomous system—say, a federated governance protocol managing shared infrastructure— that has made explicit, cryptographically verifiable commitments to two separate stakeholder coalitions under conditions of partial observability. There is no hierarchical arbitration layer and no hidden meta-rule that ranks the commitments; honoring coalition A's contractual terms necessarily violates coalition B's, and the architecture cannot renegotiate either obligation in real time because both are locked in smart-contract logic. Is the conflict here merely an engineering or epistemic failure—something that dissolves once we supply a complete preference ordering, reveal hidden priorities, or patch in a superordinate decision rule? Or does this kind of structurally unavoidable collision show that genuine dilemmas are not just psychological artifacts of limited human reasoning, but can emerge in any sufficiently complex normative architecture with distributed authority and incomplete information?
