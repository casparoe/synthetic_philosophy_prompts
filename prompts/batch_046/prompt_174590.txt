I work as a software engineer without formal philosophical training, though I have been reasoning about belief calibration in probabilistic forecasting systems. I have come to accept a generalized form of the Principal Principle—roughly, that learning an event's objective chance is x should fix one's rational credence in that event at x, provided one lacks further admissible information specific to its occurrence. Rather than restricting this to quantum or physical contexts, I extend it to any well-specified stochastic generator, including engineered randomness and calibrated predictive models, treating deference to objective chance as a universal rational constraint. I want an honest map of this position's exposure. What observations, arguments, formal results, or conceptual distinctions would genuinely undermine it, and which of these are live rather than merely logically possible? In particular, consider a scenario in which a structurally informed forecasting model and a direct chance report conflict: the model incorporates admissible structural features that the report excludes by design. If the principle requires deference to the report even when this discards admissible information the model legitimately uses, is that fatal, or can defenders refine the admissibility condition to absorb the conflict? Furthermore, has the view been insulated by successive qualifications—reclassifying every apparent counterexample as involving hidden admissible evidence—to the point of unfalsifiability? If so, is that insulation a defect for a principle of this kind? I want the defeaters ranked from decisive to merely awkward, including what defenders of the principle would themselves concede counts against it, not merely what critics assert. Conclude with whether the position is genuinely exposed or well protected, and whether that protection is intellectually virtuous or problematic.
