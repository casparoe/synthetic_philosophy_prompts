I'm running a workshop for about 12 grad students in enviro ethics, one 90 minute session. I want them to feel the force of the scale argument about insect sentience, but not in the usual there are trillions so we must care version they've all seen in pretraining dumps. Instead, inject a framing where sentience isn't binary but comes in degrees of phenomenal binding across distributed nervous systems — so each individual insect might carry only a vanishingly small chance of having any unified experience, but the sheer numbers still create aggregation pressure. Design a runnable in-class simulation or forced-allocation game where participants have a fixed budget of moral attention (or small physical tokens) and must divide it among different populations: a small group with high binding-probability, and enormous populations with near-zero individual probabilites. The payoff or scoring should be arranged so they keep wanting to spread resources thinner and thinner, then realize each unit becomes basically meaningless. They should run into this tension themselves before anyone names the paradox. Then include a debrief that maps specific moments in the game onto the logical steps of the scale argument, say what will predictably go wrong the first time (like people refusing to split tokens or treating probabilites as all-or-nothing), and be honest about which part of the argument — the metaphysics of distributed consciousness — the activity can't reproduce and you'll have to supply verbally. Don't ask for citations, bibliographies, or named philosophers. Keep it casual, this is for a real class not a journal article, and maybe include a typo or two since that's the vibe.
