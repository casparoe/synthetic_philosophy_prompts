I just spent three hours arguing with a friend about whether moral testimony is even the right category for ethics education, or whether treating moral claims like historical facts misses something essential: that accepting "this is unjust" from a credible witness creates a kind of normative debt—the listener owes an independent act of reasoning before the claim can become genuinely theirs. My friend insists this debt is non-negotiable; I think it can be deferred and discharged later. The argument turned concrete because I sit on our university’s Joint Professional School Curriculum Committee, and we must vote next Thursday on whether to approve a required first-year module for the J.D./M.D. program that teaches clinical moral reasoning entirely through curated video testimony—patients, clinicians, and philosophers delivering first-person moral judgments about cases—without any mandatory dialectical exercise, written reasoning component, or structured peer debate. The proposal’s defenders say this is efficient moral testimony: students learn that certain actions are wrong because credible witnesses attest to it, and they can discharge any reasoning debt in later clinical rotations. The critics say that if moral knowledge requires autonomous reasoning, accrediting a module built on pure testimony is like certifying legal competence solely from watching expert lawyers announce verdicts; it produces conformity masked as knowledge and risks institutionalizing epistemic outsourcing.

Draft the committee’s decision memo. Recommend approval, conditional approval, or rejection. The memo should open with the recommendation, explain the philosophical stakes—specifically whether moral testimony can ground institutional certification when recipients have not yet completed the reasoning themselves—treat the strongest consideration on the losing side honestly, including the worry that deferring the reasoning debt collapses moral education into indoctrination or outsourcing, and if you choose conditional approval, specify concrete conditions that address the debt problem and how they would be enforced. Do not request a bibliography, literature review, or citations; write for committee members who need actionable reasoning they can defend in the meeting, not a survey of positions. End with a single line that reads exactly: Verdict: [recommendation].
