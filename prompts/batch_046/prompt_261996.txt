A study partner proposes an argument that, on Aristotelian premises, usury is not merely unjust but logically impossible, and indeed that any retention of money beyond immediate exchange is self-contradictory. (1) For Aristotle, money (nomisma) exists solely by convention (nomos) to measure and mediate exchanges whose value derives from natural need (chreia). (2) In just exchange, money received must stand in proportionate equality (analogia) to the need-value given away; the medium itself contributes nothing. (3) If money is retained or multiplied—through interest (tokos)—without a corresponding transfer of need-value, it exceeds its purely representational function. (4) Once it exceeds that function, it is treated as possessing its own reproductive capacity, like wheat or livestock. (5) Yet Aristotle holds that money is naturally barren (argos); attributing reproductive power to it is therefore a category error. (6) Consequently, treating money as reproductive simultaneously affirms its conventional barrenness and its natural productivity, which is contradictory. (7) Thus any accumulation of monetary value not immediately matched by goods-exchange involves a contradiction in the ontology of the medium. The partner concludes that Aristotelian economics renders saving, investment, or delayed exchange incoherent. Identify the weakest premise, explain why it initially looks defensible, and assess what each of three positions must concede to reject it: a strict Aristotelian who confines the contradiction to usury alone; a Thomistic interpreter who distinguishes the sterility of money from the productivity of capital; and a modern conventionalist who denies that currency possesses any ontology beyond social agreement.
