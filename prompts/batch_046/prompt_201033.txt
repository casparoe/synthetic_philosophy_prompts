I am currently drafting a contribution for a workshop on value pluralism, concentrating on the interaction between Isaiah Berlin's conception of incommensurable values and Ruth Chang's account of hard choices—situations in which reasons favour competing options without either being determinatively stronger. Rather than rehearsing familiar individual dilemmas, I want to examine how these ideas behave when applied to collective institutional design, particularly where the architecture of the choice itself is philosophically contested.

Could you furnish an example of a conceptual issue—distinct from an empirical policy study—in which a hard choice arises between two public goods that are genuinely incommensurable, such as the safeguarding of an intangible cultural practice and the mitigation of local carbon emissions? The example should demonstrate how the identification of what counts as a relevant reason in favour of either option depends upon whether one adopts a monistic or pluralistic meta-ethical stance. In addition, please show how the introduction of a third value—perhaps procedural legitimacy or intergenerational equity—fails to resolve the deadlock as a tie-breaker and instead transforms the structure of the incommensurability, generating what might be termed a second-order hard choice about which comparative framework ought to govern the initial conflict. I would prefer an illustration that avoids well-worn scenarios involving career against family obligations or the classic forced-choice dilemmas of moral philosophy; instead, I seek something conceptually precise that reveals how meta-ethical commitments shape the phenomenology of collective choice without collapsing into mere relativism.
