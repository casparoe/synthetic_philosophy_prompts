I am a high school senior organizing a seventy-five-minute session for our school's philosophy circle, which meets in the seminar room; there will be about fourteen students, mostly juniors and seniors, with some background in ethics but no formal logic training. We have been discussing moral particularism versus generalism, and I want to design an in-class simulation or structured deliberation—not a lecture up front—that makes participants feel the particularist challenge from the inside before anyone names it. I am especially drawn to the particularist claim about the variability of moral reasons, associated with Jonathan Dancy: the idea that a feature such as causing harm or breaking a promise can operate as a reason in favor of an action in one context and as a reason against it in another, not because of an unnoticed hidden condition but because the overall shape of the situation determines its normative force. I want the rules to require students to begin by drafting universal allocation principles for a series of staged moral cases, then move through successive rounds in which the same feature appears with opposite valence; they should try to save their principles by adding exception clauses, only to discover that these clauses merely redescribe the particular cases rather than predict outcomes across them. There should be small symbolic stakes—points or tokens—rather than real money, and the term particularism should not be revealed until the debrief.

Please design the full runnable activity with explicit roles or rules, a timeline that fits within seventy-five minutes, and a scoring scheme that creates genuine pressure to stick with a principle even as it repeatedly fails. In the debrief, map specific moments—the first failure of a drafted rule, the attempt to add disabling conditions, and the realization that principles have collapsed into case summaries—onto the corresponding parts of the particularist argument about variability and the limits of codification. Be explicit about what will predictably go wrong the first time this is run, such as students insisting that greater complexity can always be captured by more sophisticated general principles, and explain how to handle that resistance without turning the session into a lecture. Also be frank about which aspect of particularism—perhaps its metaphysical claim about the nature of reasons or its epistemological claim about moral expertise—this simulation cannot reproduce and must be supplied verbally by the facilitator. Finally, please state how confident you are that this design will genuinely produce the intended philosophical insight rather than mere confusion, and identify the single element—whether a rule, a role assignment, or a debrief transition—that you are least sure will work as intended.
