I am a retired experimental physicist who has only recently begun to study philosophy in a systematic way, and I am drafting an essay on Buridan's ass that deliberately avoids the familiar framing in terms of free will. Instead, I treat the paradox as a comparative study in how different disciplines model decision under conditions of perfect symmetry. In physics, an unstable equilibrium—say, a beam balanced exactly on its midpoint—resolves through infinitesimal perturbations that are themselves physically determined; the system does not choose, it merely breaks symmetry. In the standard philosophical treatment of Buridan's ass, by contrast, the donkey's paralysis is usually taken to demonstrate that rational action requires a differential reason, and that in the absence of any preference the agent cannot move. I want to argue that these two treatments are frequently conflated, and that we should distinguish physical indifference, where resolution comes from noise or boundary conditions, from normative indifference, where an agent is expected to supply a justifying reason. To this end, I propose that choice without preference might be coherent if we allow for what I term a discontinuity of commitment: a non-derivable rupture in the deliberative chain that is neither random physical noise nor the conclusion of ordinary reasoning. My central worry is that this notion merely renames the mystery rather than explaining it, and that my analogy to spontaneous symmetry breaking illegitimately imports deterministic physical assumptions into an account of agency. Could you comment on the philosophical coherence of this framing, on whether I misrepresent the standard interpretations of Buridan—particularly regarding whether the will is genuinely paralyzed or can arbitrarily select between equals—and on whether the symmetry-breaking analogy ultimately illuminates or obscures the problem? I am not seeking a bibliography, reading list, or citations; I would value only conceptual criticism of the argument as sketched.
