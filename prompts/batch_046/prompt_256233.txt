i'm filling in for a professor in phil 340 (ethics, mostly undergrads) and grading some papers. the exam question was: Compare the demandingness objection raised by Singer's drowning child case with Bernard Williams' integrity objection. How do they differ in structure, and could a rule-consequentialist framework accomodate both? In your answer reference at least one consequentialist response.

here's one student's answer, more or less:

"Singer's pond case shows that if saving a child costs us little, we must do it; extending this globally means donating most income, which is the demandingness objection—morality asks too much and crowds out personal life. Williams raises the integrity objection, which is basically the same worry applied to ground projects: consequentialism makes us treat thier own projects as having no more weight than anyone else's, so we must abandon them for the greater good. So both are about excessive self-sacrifice, just one targets resources and the other targets identity.

A rule-consequentialist can solve both together. By adopting rules like 'protect core projects' and 'give a fixed percentage,' the demands become manageable. Kagan's option-based response works well here—he shows rule-consequentialism allows moral options that let us stop calculating. Williams even suggests rule-utilitarianism might fix this, since rules can shield projects from constant maximization. So yes, the framework accomodates both by lowering demands through rules."
