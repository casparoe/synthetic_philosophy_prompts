You advise the procurement and sustainability office of a large institutional food-service operation—serving tens of thousands of meals daily—where proposals to shift protein sourcing regularly arrive with competing claims about farmed-animal welfare and crop-cultivation impacts. Write a reusable decision procedure, executable in under an hour by an analyst without philosophical training, for evaluating whether to approve a proposal that replaces beef with chicken or with a legume-based alternative, where the central ethical tension is between direct, intentional harm to farmed animals and indirect, diffuse harm to wild animals killed during crop production.

Ground the procedure in the idea that both categories of harm matter, but that their causal and moral structures differ, so they cannot be collapsed into a single metric without explicit normative choices. The procedure must include: (1) the ordered questions to ask, with criteria for a sufficient answer at each stage; (2) explicit decision nodes—where it outputs “do not approve,” where it outputs “approve with conditions,” and where it halts and refers the case to a values committee because the remaining disagreement is normative rather than empirical; (3) three worked applications—beef to chicken, beef to lentils, and a case where the procedure minimizes aggregate suffering but conflicts with an intuitive constraint about intentional direct harm, with an explicit explanation of that tension; and (4) an honest accounting of what the procedure cannot settle, such as disputes over invertebrate sentience, climate-feedback loops, or institutional priorities.

Inject a specific conceptual framing not standard in popular treatments: organize the agricultural comparison through a forecasting-inspired “outside view” of land-use mortality, comparing pasture, feed-crop, soy, and legume systems not merely by yield but by “mortality exposure per edible calorie,” using reference-class base-rate ranges for small-vertebrate and invertebrate deaths per hectare-year adjusted for caloric density. Do not cite specific studies or invent precise figures; use transparent placeholder ranges and instruct the analyst how to substitute locally available agricultural data. Do not ask for bibliographies, citations, or reading lists, and do not invoke theories or datasets so niche that a model without web access could only fabricate them; build from widely discussed concepts in animal ethics and agricultural science, but structure them through this reference-class framing.

Finally, request that the response state how confident it is in each major step of the procedure, single out the specific point—whether empirical, conceptual, or normative—where it is least sure, and include a brief note identifying where the procedure takes liberties with contested philosophical positions (for example, treating incidental crop-related harm as aggregable with intentional farm harm, or assuming linear scaling from hectare-level mortality estimates to procurement-level impacts) and flag which of those liberties are the most philosophically contested.
