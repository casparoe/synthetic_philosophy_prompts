I'm working through the philosophy of time in a seminar that recently focused on Kurt Gödel's 1949 rotating-universe solution and the philosophical argument he developed alongside it, in which he claimed that the physical possibility of closed timelike curves demonstrates that objective temporal passage is incompatible with general relativity and should be abandoned in favor of a static, four-dimensional block. My instructor noted that since Gödel's death in 1978, observational cosmology has placed extraordinarily tight bounds on large-scale cosmic rotation, and that Hawking's chronology-protection conjecture offered a theoretical reason nature might prevent the formation of such curves. But I'm interested in pressing the issue in a more abstract direction that hasn't come up in our readings. Specifically, modern treatments in differential geometry sometimes discuss spacetimes that are locally Gödel-type—admitting, in every neighborhood, coordinate loops that return to the same point—yet globally fail to be time-orientable, so that no consistent global assignment of past and future exists, and an observer traversing such paths would encounter discontinuities in temporal ordering rather than a smooth personal return to an earlier stage.

Given Gödel's well-known mathematical Platonism—his conviction that every consistently definable mathematical structure corresponds to a physically possible world—and his specific claim that the mere existence of closed timelike curves undermines the reality of becoming, how do you think he would have assessed these globally non-time-orientable constructions? Would he have seen the global breakdown of temporal orientation as strengthening his case, on the grounds that time is not merely cyclical but globally incoherent, or as weakening it, because the absence of a coherent loop removes the direct contradiction with an observer's linear subjective experience? And if he maintained that only a timeless block is real, what might he have said about the contemporary philosophical position—associated with eternalists such as Craig Callender—according to which even globally hyperbolic spacetimes that contain no closed curves whatsoever lack a privileged global now, thereby rendering his rotating-universe argument redundant? I am less interested in a survey of the literature than in a reasoned reconstruction of the position he would likely have taken, grounded in his published commitments.
