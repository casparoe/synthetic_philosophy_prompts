Having recently become the primary caregiver for a parent whose frontotemporal dementia has gradually dissolved his capacity for abstract propositional reasoning while leaving emotional and moral responsiveness largely intact, I have begun to revisit arguments about divine hiddenness not as speculative exercises but as questions that now feel personally urgent. The classic formulation presupposes an epistemic agent fully capable of assessing evidence and forming beliefs, whose failure to believe in God despite sincere inquiry counts as non-resistant non-belief. Give an example of a conceptual issue—one that does not merely reproduce the standard Schellenberg-style problem—that arises when we ask whether divine hiddenness can coherently apply to a subject whose belief-forming architecture has eroded through neurological change rather than through willful resistance or evidential deficiency. In particular, the example should expose whether the concept of reasonable non-belief tacitly depends on a normative model of intact cognitive agency, and it should be articulated without reference to specific clinical literature, bibliographic sources, or named studies.
