I am designing a three-year research agenda for a small unit—two philosophers of logic, one political theorist, one formal epistemologist—with no empirical budget and no mandate to produce bibliographic surveys. Their practical end is to advise institutional designers (constitutional courts, scientific advisory panels, multi-module AI governance architectures) on how to structure collective reasoning across interdependent premises without falling into the discursive dilemma. I want a defended prioritization of the open conceptual problems in judgment aggregation, ordered not by prestige but by dependency, tractability for philosophers, and institutional leverage.

Consider the following clusters, though you may reformulate or merge them:

A. Whether the discursive dilemma expresses a genuine normative contradiction—premise-based versus conclusion-based aggregation—or whether it dissolves under a distinction between doxastic commitment and practical decision in collective agents that lack unified introspective access to their own premises.

B. How aggregation rules should handle graded or probabilistic premises, and whether the core impossibility results survive when beliefs are represented continuously rather than binarily.

C. The comparative strategic vulnerability of premise-based versus conclusion-based rules: is manipulation of intermediate premises more severe, and does the answer depend on interpreting premises as evidential versus causal claims?

D. Whether a stable hybrid procedure—weighting premises and conclusions differentially—can satisfy consistency and a minimal collective autonomy condition, and which formal desiderata it must necessarily violate.

E. The extension of judgment aggregation failures to artificial deliberation systems that pool outputs from specialized sub-modules without a central evaluator that endorses every intermediate claim.

For each cluster, state whether it should be prioritized, and defend the ordering with explicit criteria: upstream/downstream dependency (which resolutions unlock others), tractability with current logical and decision-theoretic tools, neglectedness in the broader social-choice literature, and direct institutional applicability. Where criteria conflict—say, a problem is institutionally urgent but conceptually intractable—state the trade-off and which criterion should dominate for this unit's particular end.

Then inject and evaluate a niche framing: treat the collective agent as a distributed cognitive system with no single point of introspective access to its premises, analogous to distributed epistemology but applied specifically to aggregation mechanisms. Assess whether this framing renders cluster A more tractable by dissolving the presumption of unified belief, or whether it introduces a new upstream problem—perhaps about the normative status of premises that no individual member fully endorses but that the system outputs as intermediate steps—that should displace one of the clusters above.

Finally, conclude with an explicit epistemic audit: state precisely what would change your prioritization (e.g., a specific formal result showing hybrid procedures are impossible, or an institutional case demonstrating that strategic premise-manipulation is negligible in practice), and identify the weakest point in your reasoning—most likely where you have assumed that philosophical tractability correlates with implementability by institutional designers. Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citation requests; avoid attributing specific theorems to named scholars unless the conceptual point genuinely requires it, and do not invoke publication dates or titles.
