As a journalist who covers science and ideas, I have been trying to articulate my own stance on criminal punishment before committing it to print, and I want a rigorous consistency check. Here is the package of views I currently hold. First, I am a retributivist: the state may inflict suffering on an offender solely because and only to the degree that the offender deserves it, and never merely as a tool for social engineering. Second, I believe desert is fixed by the culpable mental state and the risk deliberately imposed, not by actual outcomes; two people who perform identically culpable acts deserve identically severe punishment, even if one accidentally kills dozens and the other harms no one. Third, I hold that when an offender has already endured severe suffering as a direct causal consequence of the very wrongful act—imagine a driver who causes a crash that kills her own family—this suffering should be deducted from the sentence the state imposes, on the ground that part of the moral debt has already been paid through natural consequence. Fourth, I reject consequentialism entirely: general deterrence, rehabilitation, and aggregate welfare must play no role in setting punishment. Fifth, I maintain that the state has an independent duty not to destroy an offender's remaining capacity for moral agency, because annihilating that capacity would undermine the very moral order that punishment is meant to honor and affirm.

Do these five commitments hang together, or do they pull in incompatible directions? I am especially concerned that my third claim introduces a consequentialist-style balancing of suffering against suffering, which seems to conflict with my second claim's rejection of outcome-sensitive desert and with my fourth claim's blanket exclusion of utility-based reasoning. Is the deduction in the third commitment defensible on purely retributivist grounds, or does it force me to accept a principle I reject elsewhere? And is my fifth commitment genuinely consistent with the first two, or does protecting moral agency place an external, non-desert-based ceiling on punishment that a consistent retributivist ought to deny? Please rank the tensions by severity, identify the cheapest single revision that would restore coherence, and indicate whether any apparent conflict actually dissolves once the distinctions are drawn precisely.
