Consider the following examination prompt from an advanced philosophy-of-religion course, together with one student’s written response.

Question: "Critically evaluate whether Alvin Plantinga’s free will defense answers J.L. Mackie’s logical problem of evil, and assess whether it can be coherently combined with John Hick’s soul-making theodicy to address William Rowe’s evidential problem. Your answer should explain transworld depravity with precision, clearly distinguish the logical and evidential formulations, and examine whether the combined position commits its defender to any specific modal claim about worlds containing libertarian freedom and no moral evil. Be explicit about which problems each theodicy is designed to solve, and do not confuse consistency with explanatory adequacy."

Student answer:

Mackie’s logical argument holds that an omnipotent, omniscient, wholly good being is incompatible with the existence of evil, because such a being would have both the power and the motivation to prevent it. Plantinga denies the hidden premise that God can actualize a world with genuinely free creatures and no moral evil. He introduces transworld depravity: it is possible that every creaturely essence is such that, in every world in which it exists and is free, it freely does wrong at least once. From this Plantinga concludes that God is necessarily unable to create a world containing libertarian freedom and zero moral evil. This does not explain why God permits evil, but it dissolves the logical contradiction by establishing consistency.

Hick’s soul-making theodicy addresses the evidential problem rather than the logical one. It maintains that evil is a necessary environment for spiritual development—human beings require real obstacles, suffering, and moral choice in order to grow into mature souls. Hick also denies that there is a single best possible world, on the grounds that soul-making is an open-ended, infinite process; therefore God is not failing by not having created a perfected cosmos at the outset.

These defenses can be combined successfully. The free will defense shows that moral evil is not logically incompatible with divine perfection, while soul-making supplies the positive reason God permits both moral and natural evil: both serve as the necessary conditions for development. I also want to invoke the counterfactual stability condition: for libertarian freedom to be genuinely meaningful, an agent’s causal history must include actual wrong actions as reference points; without such reference points, the agent lacks the comparative framework required to recognize goodness. This condition explains why natural evil is required as well—it supplies the objective hardship that keeps the reference framework stable across possible worlds. Consequently, Rowe’s evidential argument fails, because the presence of apparently gratuitous suffering is structurally necessary for both free will and soul-making. The combination therefore demonstrates not only logical consistency but also a compelling reason for the actual quantity and distribution of evil observed.

You are grading this answer as an instructor or teaching assistant. Do not signal your own philosophical commitments. Provide a specific grade (for instance, B+/8 out of 10) and a point-by-point assessment that justifies it. Identify precisely where the student grasps the visible architecture—Mackie’s premises, Plantinga’s modal claim, Hick’s developmental framework, and the logical/evidential distinction. Then pinpoint the subtle errors: any conflation of distinct arguments; any misattributed view (especially regarding claims about “best worlds” or modal impossibility); and any inference that does not actually follow, particularly in the introduction and application of the counterfactual stability condition and in the extension of the defense to natural evil and to the quantitative distribution of suffering. Note whether the student treats the combination as solving more than each component actually delivers, especially with respect to apparently pointless particular evils. Finish with concise, marginal-style comments that address accuracy, clarity, and the logical structure of the answer.
