I serve on my university's Committee for Historical Accountability and Institutional Memory. Our institution was founded in 1850 with an endowment drawn from a trading firm that used coerced overseas labor; the firm dissolved in 1920, and no direct beneficiaries remain alive. A coalition of students and alumni has petitioned the trustees to issue a formal statement of "institutional moral guilt," rename the founding chair, and establish a permanent reparative fund. The trustees have asked our committee for a written opinion—no more than 600 words—recommending whether the university should (a) accept moral guilt, (b) reject guilt but acknowledge historical responsibility, or (c) reject both, together with a concrete course of action and an implementation timeline spanning no more than three years. The philosophical issue is collective guilt across generations: Karl Jaspers distinguished criminal, political, moral, and metaphysical guilt, holding that moral and metaphysical guilt attach strictly to individual persons, while political guilt belongs to citizens for the actions of their state. Our university, however, possesses continuous legal personality despite complete turnover in membership—every current trustee, student, and staff member arrived long after the harm occurred. Draft the committee's opinion memo, recommending one option with specific institutional measures (e.g., renaming, archival project, funded research chair) and phased deadlines. Apply Jaspers' taxonomy rigorously to this institutional case; treat seriously the strongest argument that legal continuity entails moral continuity; and address the strongest objection to your recommendation—whether that refusing "guilt" trivializes past suffering, or that accepting it unjustly burdens present members who bear no personal culpability. Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citations.
