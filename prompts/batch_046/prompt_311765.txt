I am a doctoral candidate in philosophy of science serving on my university’s Interdisciplinary Research Classification Committee. We must decide whether to approve a proposal for a new Center for Apophatic Cognition—an institute that studies negative-theological practices (apophatic prayer, via negativa) through predictive-coding neuroscience—and, specifically, whether it should be housed in the natural-sciences division, which would make it eligible for STEM-focused institutional grants, or in the humanities division. The applicants claim their framework is genuinely falsifiable: they derive specific neural prediction-error signatures that should be absent if apophatic states are not cognitively distinct from ordinary focused-attention meditation. Several committee members, invoking a Popperian demarcation criterion, argue that the theological core—claims about divine transcendence—is in principle unfalsifiable, and that the neuroscience is merely an interpretive garnish attached to an untestable metaphysics; on this view, granting science-division status would erode institutional standards. Others reply that falsifiability applies to the predictive models, not to any metaphysical interpretation layered on top, and that the Duhem-Quine thesis, together with conventionalist responses, undermines any strict falsificationist boundary anyway, so the criterion should not be decisive here.

Please draft the short policy memo I will circulate before next week’s vote. It should recommend a concrete classification decision with clear, applicable criteria; engage seriously with the strongest falsificationist objection rather than dismissing it; address whether the Duhem-Quine problem and conventionalist strategies defeat the demarcation argument in this specific case or merely complicate it; and distinguish what is philosophically settled about falsificationism and its limits from what remains genuinely contested among philosophers of science who know the literature. The memo should treat the predictive-coding methodology on its own terms without assuming either that it automatically qualifies as science or that it is merely pseudoscientific ornament. Do not present any disputed reading of Popper, of predictive-coding theory, or of negative theology as the consensus view, and separate settled points from live disagreement throughout.
