I am a novelist with a working command of craft vocabulary—character arc, dramatic irony, focalization, anagnorisis, free indirect discourse, narrative desire, and the gap between a protagonist’s self-model and the author’s implied judgment of it—but almost no formal philosophy. I want you to restate Hegel’s master-slave dialectic, especially the structure of recognition, using only that narrative-craft vocabulary and the internal logic of fiction-writing. Treat the dialectic as a narrative architecture: map the struggle for recognition onto the dynamics between two characters who each attempt to become the authoritative narrator of the other, with risk, death, and labor functioning as plot events that restructure focalization and point of view. Be precise about which craft mechanism corresponds to each philosophical move—the opening life-and-death struggle, the establishment of domination, the master’s failure to obtain genuine recognition, the slave’s transformation through work and fear, and the asymmetry that eventually destabilizes the relation.

Then deliver the residue report: state which parts of Hegel’s argument translate cleanly into narrative terms, which must be distorted or stretched to fit (for example, whether “negation” can be rendered as revision, editorial deletion, or the killing off of a narrative possibility without smuggling in metaphysical assumptions), and which elements appear to have no expression at all within fiction-writing logic—such as any specifically universal or historical dimension of recognition, or the normative claim that self-consciousness structurally requires another self-consciousness. In assessing the residue, tell me whether the gaps indicate a limitation of narrative vocabulary—something fiction genuinely cannot articulate about intersubjectivity and consciousness—or whether they reveal that Hegel’s account was relying on conceptual moves less rigorous than they first appear. I am not asking for an analogy used as an argument; I want a systematic translation whose value lies as much in what it fails to carry as in what it carries.
