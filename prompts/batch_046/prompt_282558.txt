I'm preparing a feature on how Enlightenment political economists evaluated imperial governance, and I've encountered a summary of Adam Smith's colonial thinking—drawn from a recent popular history—that strikes me as both compelling and suspiciously tidy. It runs like this: "Smith regarded colonies as an unambiguous economic burden on the metropole, draining capital and distorting trade through monopolistic privilege. He believed that once a colony reached commercial maturity, further imperial restraint violated the natural liberty of its inhabitants. From this he inferred that mature colonies should be granted independence not merely to save the treasury, but because sustained domination offended the impartial-spectator standard of justice. Consequently, Smith was not simply an economic critic of empire, but a proto-cosmopolitan anti-colonialist who anticipated modern self-determination theory, expecting every advanced colony to follow America's revolutionary path."

I want to use this framing, but I suspect it conflates Smith's practical reasoning with his ethical philosophy in ways he never intended. Could you assess whether this is a fair characterization? In particular: which claims align with Smith's actual analysis of colonial monopoly, defense costs, and the division of labor in distant dominions; where does the summary overstate or invent the role of the impartial spectator in his imperial arguments; and does the conclusion that Smith was a moral cosmopolitan opponent of empire actually follow from his texts, or does it rest on importing later concepts? I'm trying to avoid reproducing an elegant anachronism in my reporting.
