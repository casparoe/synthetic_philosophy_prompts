At a seminar, two students, Aisha and Ben, argued about extending Strawson's framework of reactive attitudes. Aisha claimed that "normative anticipation"—feeling resentful in advance because someone's character makes harm predictable—counts as a genuine reactive attitude, which means we can hold people responsible for dispositions before any act occurs. Ben replied that Strawson's attitudes are responses to actual exercises of quality of will in action, so anticipation is merely predictive fear mislclassified as moral; treating it as reactive collapses responsibility into risk management. Aisha countered that Strawson already allows resentment for omissions and long-term patterns, so temporal displacement is not foreign, and addressing someone to change their course still treats them as an interlocutor rather than an object. Ben then said that if any forward-looking emotional stance with normative coloring counts, then the boundary between reactive and objective attitudes dissolves, since clinicians also feel concern about future behavior while adopting the objective attitude. Aisha conceded that clinicians feel concern but insisted that normative anticipation includes an explicit demand for acknowledgment, which keeps it interpersonal; Ben answered that this just relabels the objective attitude's instrumental aim—preventing harm—while borrowing the language of moral address. They asked me to referee.

Please adjudicate their exchange point by point: say who is right about each specific claim, whether normative anticipation genuinely extends Strawson's framework or collapses it, and identify what both are missing about the conditions under which reactive attitudes remain appropriate. Respond as a numbered list of exactly five items, each one or two sentences, with no introduction or conclusion.
