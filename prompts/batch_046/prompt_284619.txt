I'm preparing a fifty-minute section for second-year philosophy students on the underdetermination of scientific theory by evidence. The students have met Duhem's argument that experiments do not uniquely determine physical theories, but they have not encountered Quine's broader holist extension. I want to teach the difference between genuine underdetermination—where rival frameworks can each accommodate the same data through appropriate auxiliary adjustments—and mere temporary evidential insufficiency, using the combustion experiments of the 1770s: metals gain weight when burned, Lavoisier explains this by oxygen absorption, and phlogistonists can preserve their framework only by assigning negative weight to phlogiston.

Plan the teaching arc rather than explaining the thesis. Specify: the dependency order of ideas, including what must be in place before the auxiliary-adjustment move can be introduced; the carrying example and exactly what properties it needs (the observational symmetry must be visible, neither framework should appear obviously contrived) and what it should lack; the misconceptions these students will predictably form—especially that more experiments will break the tie, and that one side's auxiliary move is ad hoc while the other's is principled—together with the precise point in the sequence to head off each; a check for understanding that requires them to generate an auxiliary adjustment for the rival theory rather than merely recognizing one; and what should be left out at this level (for example, the global versus local holism distinction, or semantic incommensurability). Do not include readings or citations.

Conclude with a single line beginning "Plan summary:" that captures the core dependency and the central misconception in one sentence.
