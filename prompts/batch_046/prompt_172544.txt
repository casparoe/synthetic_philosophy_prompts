I spent much of last night arguing with a close friend about the status of what Wittgenstein, in On Certainty, apparently calls hinge propositions — those background certainties that make doubt and inquiry possible in the first place rather than being themselves objects of justification. Our disagreement was not about whether hinges exist, but about a pattern we kept falling into. When my friend wanted to protect a belief she holds dear — say, that human beings possess a continuous consciousness that survives sleep — she would declare it a hinge, insisting that demanding evidence for it is as senseless as demanding proof of the external world. Yet an hour later, when I appealed to an equally fundamental assumption — that the physical world does not radically alter its laws every Tuesday — she treated that as an ordinary empirical claim that I was obliged to defend with arguments, and she refused to grant it hinge status. I noticed the same reversal in myself: I was quick to classify my own foundational commitments as hinges and my opponent’s as ordinary hypotheses in need of support. We began to suspect that we were not identifying hinges by any stable criterion — structural role, depth of entrenchment, or immunity to revision — but rather assigning hinge status selectively, almost as a conversational move to stop inquiry where we felt vulnerable and to restart it where we felt strong. Is there an established concept in hinge epistemology, or in the philosophy of argumentation more broadly, for this kind of strategic or asymmetric deployment of hinge status? If not, how should one distinguish it from legitimate context-sensitivity about what is genuinely at stake in a given inquiry, and from mere bad faith? I am not after a reading list; I want to know whether the phenomenon has been properly carved out, and if so where exactly its boundaries lie.
