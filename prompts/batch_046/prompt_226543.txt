I'm preparing an upper-level philosophy of mind seminar for about eighteen advanced undergraduates, with one seventy-five-minute session available. I want participants to experience—not merely discuss—the asymmetry at the heart of treating consciousness as a user illusion: the idea that first-person access might be structurally complete at the level of function yet causally opaque with respect to implementation. To keep the exercise from feeling like a standard Dennett recap they've encountered in intro texts, I'm framing it around a specific conceptual twist I call representational leakage: the claim that certain boundary conditions or anomalies in subjective experience are not interface errors but traces of the underlying architecture that the user-system necessarily misinterprets as intrinsic phenomenal properties. Design a runnable in-class simulation or structured role-play with clear rules, assigned roles, and a small-stakes payoff scheme that requires no real money—points, collective privileges, or access to a shared reward pool will suffice. Divide the group so that some students operate as subpersonal processors governed only by local algorithmic rules they cannot transcend; others serve as interface users who receive only formatted outputs and must coordinate actions and make collective judgments based solely on those presentations; and a small number act as system architects who can observe both layers but are constrained in how and when they may intervene. Structure the activity so that the user-group develops confident, seemingly authoritative first-person reports about their experiential states, while the processor-group recognizes those reports as functionally generated without direct structural access—then engineer a leakage event in which an anomaly in the user-interface can only be accounted for by a feature of the backend, yet the users insist it belongs irreducibly to the quality of their experience. Include the materials needed, a minute-by-minute timeline, the debrief that maps specific moments in the simulation onto both the user-illusion thesis and the leakage concept, an honest assessment of which feature of actual consciousness—likely qualitative presence or phenomenal immediacy—the activity cannot reproduce and must be supplied by the facilitator, and a prediction of what will go wrong the first time this is run together with a concrete fix.
