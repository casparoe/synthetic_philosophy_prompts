I was recently evaluated as a candidate for an experimental neuroprosthetic protocol in which degenerating cortical tissue is replaced over several years by synthetic substrates that maintain real-time functional coupling with the remaining biological neurons, allowing consciousness and interaction to continue uninterrupted throughout. The choice whether to undergo it forces me to understand whether I—the subject deciding—would survive as the same person. For a podcast interview next week I have secured a philosopher who defends what I will call the continuous-integration view: that gradual replacement of this kind preserves personal identity because it sustains an unbroken causal-functional trajectory, whereas an instantaneous scan-and-upload, producing an identical psychological profile without that spatiotemporal continuity, creates only a duplicate. I need eight to ten interview questions that go well beyond asking him to state or defend that thesis. At least one question must target the rate-dependence problem—whether there is a metaphysically principled boundary for how rapidly replacement may occur before continuity is broken, or whether any sharp cutoff is merely an epistemic artifact of our observational limits, with a follow-up keyed to the reply that continuity admits of degrees. At least one must press the substrate-transition problem: if synthetic nodes eventually form a fully artificial network that is causally coupled to biological tissue only at the margins during the transition, at what point, if any, does the ontology of the persisting subject change, and include the follow-up he is likely to offer—that functional organization, not biological substrate, determines identity. I also want one question that places this view in explicit tension with Parfit-style psychological continuity theory, asking whether the insistence on spatiotemporal process-continuity is a substantive addition or an unjustified bias in favor of physical continuity over structural similarity, with a follow-up anticipating his defense. Finally, because my own decision depends on first-person survival rather than third-person description, include one question that asks what guidance, if any, the continuous-integration view offers to a patient weighing entry into the protocol—specifically, whether the theory can speak to the first-person concern about being the one who experiences the outcome, or whether it remains purely third-person and descriptive, leaving the practical decision unguided. The questions should anticipate standard replies and include the subsequent pressure point; avoid generic biographical or methodological questions.
