I hold a positive reading of Wittgenstein’s family-resemblance thesis: concept acquisition is not grasping a hidden essence but undergoing an embodied, practice-embedded training on a curated cluster of exemplars that share no single feature, and correct extension depends on having that specific social-regimen rather than on definitional knowledge. I think this explains why large language models—trained purely on distributional text—systematically misclassify borderline cases that human speakers navigate easily: they lack the requisite embodied practice-history. What would genuinely count against this view? I want more than the standard objection that family resemblance collapses into a disjunctive definition. Specifically: what empirical or conceptual results would undermine the claim that such embodied training is necessary for correct concept extension? Could there be evidence that humans extend concepts accurately through explicit reasoning or purely linguistic exposure without the relevant practice? Would artificial systems that achieve human-level borderline classification without embodied engagement refute the necessity claim, or could a defender retreat to saying they do not “really” understand? Also, assess whether the position is well insulated: if every successful concept-user can be retrospectively described as having had the right kind of practice, has it become unfalsifiable, and is that a problem for a view of this kind? Rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, indicate which ones a committed defender should concede as damaging rather than dismiss, and say what the world—or a well-designed experiment—would have to look like for the core claim to be wrong.
