I'm coordinating a small interdisciplinary seminar — historians, moral philosophers, and an organisational psychologist — that has secured four years of funding to move beyond the well-trodden Browning–Goldhagen debate. Our aim is not another archival study of Reserve Police Battalion 101, but to determine which unresolved conceptual questions about perpetrators actually matter for designing accountability in modern, distributed institutions. I need you to propose a defended research agenda: identify the three open lines of inquiry you would prioritise, rank them, and explain the criteria — such as upstream dependency on prior conceptual issues, tractability without new primary sources, and relevance to preventing collective harm rather than explaining past atrocities.

Please treat this as agenda-setting, not a survey. In particular, I want you to assess whether the traditional binary — ordinary men shaped by situation and culture versus willing executioners driven by ideology and character — obscures more useful axes, such as incremental bureaucratic distancing or the moral psychology of partial compliance, that should restructure how we think about institutional responsibility. For each priority, defend its place in the ordering: why it is tractable for philosophers and historians working together, what resolving it would change for practical institutional design, and what evidence or conceptual clarification over the coming years would cause you to demote or elevate it. Which questions, in your view, are overrated if our end is not historical explanation but the design of ethical oversight mechanisms?
