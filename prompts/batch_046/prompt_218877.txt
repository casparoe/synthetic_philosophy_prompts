The covering-law versus narrative-explanation dispute in philosophy of history has remained in near-stasis since the mid-twentieth century: Hempel-style subsumption under general laws competes with narrative, interpretive, or hermeneutic accounts that treat historical events as uniquely configured and meaningful only within a plotted sequence. I want a diagnosis of why this debate never resolves—whether it is verbal, about what explanation requires; substantive, about whether historical events instantiate repeatable regularities; or a case of two disciplines answering different questions—one about predictive structure, one about retrospective coherence—under one label. Specifically, consider the tension that narrative explanations often tolerate what might be called archival silence, meaning gaps in evidence filled by coherence, while covering-law reconstructions demand evidential completeness for law-subsumption, yet both sides claim to explain the same outbreak or revolution; is the impasse driven by a hidden disagreement about whether explanatory adequacy requires counterfactual stability across evidential gaps, or is that merely a symptom of a deeper mismatch in explanatory goals? Give your diagnosis, grounded in what each side actually says rather than in a survey of interpretations, together with what—if anything—would count as progress: a formal distinction, an empirical case study, a change in the question asked, or an argument that no progress should be expected. Answer as a numbered list of exactly five items, each a sentence or two, with no introduction or conclusion.
