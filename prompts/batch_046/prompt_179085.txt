As a retired experimental physicist who has only recently begun to read in formal epistemology, I am trying to understand the Löbian obstacle to self-trusting agents — the difficulty that arises when a reasoning system is permitted to endorse its own outputs. I would like you to construct a thought experiment built to precise specifications. Program this system with a conditional, fallible rule of self-trust: it endorses conclusions produced by its reasoning module only when it judges that module to be operating within its design specifications, and it applies this judgment to its own present and future states. The system should also maintain an explicit internal parameter, call it its competence coefficient, which represents its current belief about its own reliability and which is itself updated by the reasoning module. The specifications for the case are as follows. Second, present the scenario, then identify the exact belief-about-belief structure and the closure condition that generate the loop, explaining why this is an instance of the Löbian obstacle rather than a mere programming error. Third, isolate the critical feature by describing what happens when the temporal or indexical self-reference is removed: does the loop collapse into ordinary first-order calibration? Fourth, present the strongest defense available to a proponent of bounded self-trust — for example, by restricting the domain over which self-endorsement applies or by denying logical closure — and show precisely where in your scenario that defense would intervene. Fifth, conclude with an honest note on what feature of the case is doing the philosophical work and whether removing that feature reduces the example to a standard first-order failure. Do not provide bibliographies, citations, or reading lists; I want only the conceptual architecture, and I want it rigorous enough that someone familiar with the doxastic logic literature would recognize the structure without needing a reference list.
