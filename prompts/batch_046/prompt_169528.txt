I'm a first-year lecturer in political philosophy, and my supervisor has just asked me to design the central session of our "Democracy and Its Critics" module for twenty non-specialist first-years with no classics background. The session must treat three canonical Athenian failures—the Mytilene debate, the Sicilian Expedition, and the trial of Socrates—not as isolated moral lapses but as symptoms of a single structural feature I want to call "direct democratic immediacy": the absence of institutional latency, meaning procedural buffers, protected dissent spaces, and delayed execution that might absorb emotional swings between popular will and state action. Most students arrive assuming Athens collapsed into tyranny by accident, or that Socrates was killed by an irrational mob while the other two episodes were merely foreign-policy blunders; I need them to see all three as linked manifestations of the same institutional design.

Plan the explanation for a fifty-minute section, not the explanation itself. First, lay out the exact order in which the conceptual pieces must be introduced and why—what idea has to be in place before "direct democratic immediacy" can become intelligible, and what must follow so that linking Socrates to imperial overreach does not feel forced. Second, identify the one concrete reconstructed moment or scenario that should carry the entire session, justify why it should carry the weight rather than the other two episodes, and specify what properties it must have and lack for the analogy to work. Third, map the predictable misconceptions: that democracy failed because Athenians were uniquely emotional; that modern representative systems solve this problem entirely; and that Socrates' death belongs to philosophy rather than to politics. For each, specify precisely where in the sequence to head it off. Fourth, propose one check-for-understanding question at the end that reveals whether students have grasped the structural claim rather than just memorized three events. Fifth, state explicitly what I should refuse to explain at this level—perhaps the full legal mechanics of graphe paranomon, the prosopography of the generals, or comparative modern constitutional design—and say so. Begin with the bottom-line sequencing choice in a single sentence, then expand. Keep the response under 350 words. You are an expert in philosophy pedagogy; think step by step about the dependency structure before laying out the plan.
