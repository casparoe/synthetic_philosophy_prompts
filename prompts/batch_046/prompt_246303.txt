In philosophical and pedagogical discussions of intuitionistic logic, one frequently encounters a banking analogy: classical mathematics is said to be like writing a check for a sum you have not verified is in your account — the law of excluded middle lets you assert the existence of an object without being able to present it, just as you might claim a debt is settled without producing the funds. Intuitionism is then framed as the regulatory reform that prohibits writing checks you cannot cover, requiring that every existence claim be backed by an explicit witness or construction. This is used to defend the view that intuitionism is not a deprivation but a clarification, and that classical logic is epistemically sloppy rather than genuinely stronger. I want an audit of this correspondence. Give me the explicit mapping — the check, the account balance, the bank, the bounced check, the inspector, and the penalty for violation — then show where the analogy fractures. In particular: how does intuitionistic negation map onto this financial picture, and does that mapping distort what negation actually means in the system? Does the analogy silently import a transactional or legal ontology — debts, obligations, institutional enforcement — that has no counterpart in the ontology of mathematical constructions? How do choice sequences and continuity principles fit into the banking image — are they like credit lines with no predetermined limit, or does the analogy break there entirely? Separate the disanalogies that undermine the intended defense of intuitionism from those that are harmless. Finally, assess whether the conclusion — that classical logic is merely sloppy — survives once the fractures are visible, or whether the analogy was doing the argumentative work all along. If the banking image fails, suggest a more apt analogy for what the rejection of the law of excluded middle actually restricts.
