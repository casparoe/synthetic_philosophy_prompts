I'm working on a paper for my philosophy of religion course about fatalism and temporal asymmetry. Please argue that the Appointment in Samarra story—where a servant flees Death in Damascus only to meet him in Samarra—does not actually support causal fatalism, but rather exposes a modal confusion between the fixity of the past and the logical structure of future contingents. Specifically, defend the position that the servant's flight remains causally efficacious and rationally justified even if his presence in Samarra is inevitable, provided we understand that inevitability as a consequence of present conditions rather than as a backward-causal constraint. Contrast this compatibilist reading with the theological fatalist interpretation that treats future appointments as already fixed in the same manner as past events, and explain why that assimilation commits a category error about the direction of temporal dependence. Conclude with a clear verdict on whether the parable genuinely undermines libertarian free will, or whether it merely illustrates that rational agency is compatible with an unavoidable endpoint.
