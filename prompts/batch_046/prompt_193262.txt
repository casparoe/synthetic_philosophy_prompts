I am finalizing the exam for my MA-level philosophy of science seminar on reduction and emergence. I want an essay question that students cannot answer by summarizing standard accounts of weak emergence or reciting Kim’s exclusion argument; instead, it should force them to apply the derivability and novelty conditions to an unfamiliar conceptual configuration that is fully defined within the prompt itself. To that end, please construct a 90-minute exam question built around a hypothetical framework—call it “structural lock-in theory”—which holds that in certain complex adaptive systems, macro-level organizational rules restrict the space of possible micro-trajectories in a manner not reducible to initial conditions, boundary constraints, or statistical aggregation, yet without positing any novel fundamental forces. The question should require students to determine whether structural lock-in, if empirically confirmed in the hypothetical case, would constitute strong emergence, a form of downward causation compatible with physicalism, an epistemic artifact of modeling limitations, or a category error conflating causal with constitutive explanation. Provide: (1) the essay prompt, calibrated for 90 minutes and aimed at testing analytical discrimination rather than label recognition; (2) a model answer of approximately 600 words that defends one of these four positions with explicit engagement with the derivability criterion and the causal closure of physics; and (3) a marking scheme with five must-hit analytical moves (total 20 marks), specifying for each the marks assigned, the specific reasoning step required for full credit, and a one-line description of how weaker answers typically miss that step. Do not ask for bibliographies, citations, or references to specific texts; the evaluation should turn entirely on the coherence and precision of the philosophical analysis.
