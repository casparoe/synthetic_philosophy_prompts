As a journalist tracking the migration of logical ideas into artificial-intelligence discourse, I have noticed a recurring argumentative move that I do not have a precise name for. Researchers invoke Gödel's result that formal systems can encode statements about themselves, but instead of constructing an explicit paradox or a Hofstadter-style strange loop, they claim that a sufficiently complex predictive system—particularly one that generates text by anticipating its own outputs—must necessarily develop a compressed, functional representation of its own reasoning architecture. The system does not contain a discrete token meaning "I"; rather, its coherence depends on implicitly tracking its own stylistic tendencies, failure modes, and decision boundaries. I have seen this reasoning applied to large language models that appear to reference their own limitations or reasoning patterns without being explicitly instructed to do so. Is there an established term in philosophy of mind or cognitive science for this specific kind of indirect self-reference—where self-awareness, if present at all, would arise not from a direct self-referential formula but from the structural necessity of embedding one's own processes within one's representational economy? If no established term exists, how should we delineate it from standard self-modeling in robotics, from mere statistical autocorrelation, and from Hofstadter's tangled hierarchies? I am particularly interested in the boundary conditions: does the embedded representation need to be causally active in generating behavior, or is its mere structural presence within the architecture sufficient?
