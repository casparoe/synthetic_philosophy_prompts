Question: Populists claim to represent 'the will of the people' against corrupt elites. Explain what that claim usually amounts to, contrast it with the liberal-democratic idea that sovereignty lives in institutions and procedures rather than a single voice, and point out one tension inside the populist view—especially about who gets counted as 'the people.'

Answer: The populist believes there is one true popular will, like a unified moral voice that elites and bureaucracy drown out. Liberals think sovereignty is just the output of elections, courts, and checks, spread across procedures so no one group owns it. This idea of a unified will comes from Rousseau's general will, which always aims at the common good, and Schmitt made the same point with his friend-enemy distinction, which sorts the real people from outsiders. The tension is that if the will is genuinely one thing, then anyone who dissents is automatically not part of the people and can be dismissed, but democracy needs disagreement to function, so populists end up needing representatives to filter out the fake people—which just puts another elite in charge.
