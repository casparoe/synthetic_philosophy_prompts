I am trying to determine what actually makes consent valid, judging by my intuitions about concrete cases. A patient signs a surgical form after the surgeon lists all physical risks but lies about having performed the procedure fifty times when she has done it twice: the consent is invalid, because deception about practitioner competence vitiates it. A prisoner agrees to a study after being told his parole board will view refusal unfavorably: invalid, because institutional coercion destroys voluntariness. A wealthy donor, fully informed of risks, accepts $100,000 to donate a kidney: invalid, because extreme financial inducement corrupts free choice. A sibling agrees to donate a kidney after family members warn that refusal will destroy family relations: valid, because emotional pressure is not coercive. A patient with moderate Alzheimer's nods assent to a medication change after a simple explanation but cannot recall it moments later: invalid, because competence is absent regardless of assent. I also keep going back and forth on whether a doctor who tells a terrified patient "this injection won't hurt"—knowing it will—has secured valid consent, since the lie seems paternalistic rather than exploitative. Is there a single principle—combining information, voluntariness, and competence conditions—that delivers all five verdicts and explains why financial pressure is coercive but family pressure is not? If not, which verdict should I drop to preserve simplicity, and what does the best principle predict for the paternalistic-lie injection case?
