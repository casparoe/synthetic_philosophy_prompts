I serve as the philosophical-methodology representative on my university's Joint Committee for Interdisciplinary Funding Metrics, and I have been asked to draft the board's decision memo ahead of Thursday's session. I am currently teaching a graduate seminar on measurement theory and scientific representation, and several students have pressed me on whether strategic adaptation to evaluation criteria is epistemically corrupt or merely instrumentally rational; that classroom disagreement mirrors the split on our board, and I want the memo to treat both positions with equal rigor rather than dismissing either as conceptually naive.

The concrete case before us concerns the pilot "Ontological Alignment Index" (OAI), a scoring mechanism applied to philosophy and cognitive-science grant proposals. The OAI computes structural isomorphism between a submission's stated aims and a machine-readable taxonomy of "core conceptual problems" in the field; proposals with high scores receive automatic funding-track recommendations. Our pilot data show that applicants are now fragmenting unified arguments into modular sub-claims that map precisely onto taxonomy nodes, suppressing genuinely novel questions that fall outside the ontology, and inflating cross-reference counts to trigger alignment bonuses—behaviors that improve scores while degrading, by any qualitative peer assessment, the actual intellectual ambition of the work. One faction of the board interprets this as routine proxy corruption: a predictable Goodhart failure that can be remedied by audit layers and weighting adjustments. The opposing faction argues that we have crossed into adversarial specification gaming—the taxonomy is not merely being exploited but is actively restructuring the conceptual architecture of submitted research, manufacturing a simulacrum of philosophical quality rather than measuring it, so that retaining the OAI risks institutionalizing a corrupted form of inquiry.

Please draft the formal decision memo recommending one of three concrete courses—retain the OAI with binding safeguards, suspend it pending a fundamental redesign, or reject it outright—leading with the bottom-line recommendation in the first sentence and providing the supporting reasoning afterward, with no preamble and no restatement of the question. The memo should include an honest treatment of the strongest consideration supporting the option you reject, specify a concrete implementation timeline if you recommend conditions, and be written in American English. Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citations.
