I am preparing the opening exercise for a graduate seminar on textual ontology, and I would like a poem that functions simultaneously as an aesthetic object and as a conceptual provocation. Write a pantoum of six quatrains, observing the form's compulsory repetition—the second and fourth lines of each stanza returning as the first and third of the next—and closing with the opening lines woven back into the final quatrain. The subject is the intentional fallacy, specifically the thesis that an author's private mental states do not determine a text's public meaning, but render it through an unusual framing: imagine a philosophical treatise that survives in two equally authoritative manuscript versions, one defending hard determinism and the other defending libertarian free will, both in the same author's hand, with no archival evidence indicating which was intended for publication. The poem should be spoken in the first person by the treatise itself, never by the author, and the lines compelled to repeat should be those that express interpretive instability—declaring contradictory commitments, denying that a single speaker can be assigned, or refusing to identify a definitive draft. Because the pantoum forces these lines to return in altered contexts, each repetition should subtly shift the apparent meaning, so that by the final quatrain the reader cannot stabilize any single authorial intention without also stabilizing its opposite. Do not resolve the tension; let the circular return of the opening lines in the closing stanza demonstrate that the text's meaning has escaped both drafts. Use precise philosophical vocabulary—intention, draft, publication, interpretation—but do not quote any specific critic or name any historical work. The tone should be restrained and formal, accurate enough that a specialist in philosophy of literature recognizes the anti-intentionalist position and sees why the form enacts it, yet readable as a poem in its own right.
