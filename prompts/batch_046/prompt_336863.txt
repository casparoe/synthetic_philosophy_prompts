I serve as a senior referee for an interdisciplinary journal in applied ethics and religious studies, and I evaluate roughly eight submissions per year that invoke Maimonides’ accommodationist account of animal sacrifice to argue for or against modern ritual exemptions. My background is in moral philosophy, not medieval Jewish thought; I have ninety minutes per manuscript, no access to primary texts in Arabic or Hebrew, and I must complete a standardized one-page form that my co-editor—who has no philosophy training—reviews before we send recommendations to authors. I need a reusable decision procedure, expressed as an ordered flowchart in words, that lets me check whether a submission applies Maimonides’ view correctly without requiring me to become a Maimonides scholar. The principle I am working with is this: in his discussion of sacrifice in the Guide for the Perplexed, Maimonides treats animal sacrifice not as an intrinsic good but as a historically conditioned divine concession—an accommodation to a people habituated to idolatrous worship, intended to redirect tangible religious impulse away from corporeal images and eventually toward prayer and contemplation. To keep this from collapsing into the familiar “it was just a concession, so abolish it” reading, I want the procedure to incorporate a second, less-discussed layer: Maimonides accommodates not only the human need for physical ritual but also the cognitive tendency to reify the means as an end in itself. Call this the double-layer accommodation. The deliverable should be a checklist of five to seven ordered tests I can run in sequence, each stating what evidence in the manuscript to look for, what answer counts as passing, and where I am forced to make a judgment call rather than apply a rule. The procedure must handle three hard cases: a submission that concludes we should abolish all ritual slaughter because accommodation implies dispensability; a submission that concludes we must preserve sacrifice unchanged precisely because altering a concession violates its structure; and a submission that distinguishes structural retention of the ritual form from teleological fulfillment through non-corporeal worship, reaching a conclusion I am likely to dislike but that the procedure should recognize as consistent. For each case, show me exactly what the procedure outputs, including the point at which judgment cannot be eliminated. The procedure should also state clearly what it cannot do: it does not adjudicate whether God exists, whether modern psychology validates the accommodation mechanism, or whether ritual slaughter ought to be legally permitted; it only evaluates internal consistency with Maimonides’ framework as reconstructed here. Mark every step where expertise I do not have might change the result, and design the form so that my co-editor can see where I have applied a rule versus where I have made an interpretive call.
