I'm presenting a thesis defense next Thursday: the canonical money-pump argument for diachronic consistency collapses when an agent's cyclic preferences are generated by a stable, acyclic evaluation of world-states combined with an indexical treatment of temporal location—what I'll call indexical cyclicity. Under that structure, being pumped doesn't extract value from an internal irrationality; it exploits an external settlement asymmetry that forces intertemporal bargaining across arbitrary slice-boundaries. I argue diachronic consistency is therefore a coordination constraint, not a rationality constraint, for such agents. The audience is a philosophy-of-economics seminar: two formal decision theorists who know the Born-rule derivations and updateless frameworks cold, three behavioral economists who treat unoperationalized rationality claims with suspicion, and two philosophers of science focused on the normative-descriptive boundary. Give me the prep sheet in order of damage: each objection phrased as that subgroup would actually say it, what I should concede up front, what I must hold, a two-minute reply, and an explicit softness note. Include the reply for the decision-theoretic objection that my indexical framing collapses into standard evidential reasoning once formalized—the one I'm hoping nobody asks. Keep my sketch in view: the pump works only because the bookie can enforce settlement at different times, which is an institutional failure, not a preference failure.
