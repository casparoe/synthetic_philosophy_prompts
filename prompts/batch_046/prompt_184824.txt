I have been working through a line of reasoning about ontology identification when information is permanently inaccessible, and I have driven it to a conclusion that strikes me as wrong, yet I cannot locate the precise error. I lay out the steps below.

(1) Ontology identification requires a criterion that can distinguish genuinely different ontological structures from mere notational variants of the same structure.
(2) Any criterion available to rational inquiry must operate on information that is in principle accessible; if a class of information is permanently inaccessible, no criterion can reach it.
(3) Consider any ontology O. Define O' as the ontology that results from O by adding or removing entities that are, by their nature, incapable of leaving any trace in accessible information.
(4) By (2), no available criterion can distinguish O from O'.
(5) From (1) and (4), O and O' are not genuinely different ontologies; they belong to the same equivalence class.
(6) Apply (5) iteratively, beginning from a minimal ontology M that contains only entities with accessible traces. Every ontology constructed by adding or subtracting inaccessible entities belongs to the equivalence class of M.
(7) Therefore, in substantive terms, there is only one ontology—the minimal accessible core. All apparent disagreements about the existence of entities that are definitionally hidden (for example, certain interpretations of quantum states, or abstract objects that do not enter into causal interaction) are not substantive ontological disputes but purely semantic variations on M.
(8) But this seems wrong. We ordinarily treat debates about the reality of mathematical structures or hidden-variable interpretations as having genuine ontological weight, and if (7) held, ontology would collapse into lexicography.
(9) So at least one of (1) through (6) must fail.

I am tempted to reject (2) and allow that ontology can be guided by non-empirical considerations, but I worry that doing so introduces an unexplained faculty—something like rational intuition—that I have no independent account of. If I instead reject (4), I need to explain how a criterion can operate on what is definitionally out of reach. Which step is actually the weak point, why did it look plausible, and what does giving it up cost for someone who wants ontology to be more than empirical bookkeeping without appealing to mysterious cognitive powers?
