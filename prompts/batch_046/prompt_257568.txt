As a software engineer without formal philosophy training, I am trying to understand whether inverse reinforcement learning (IRL) actually holds up when applied to real-world human-feedback systems. Take the 2022 development of OpenAI's InstructGPT, in which engineers collected comparative rankings from human annotators over model outputs and used those judgments to train a reward model for reinforcement learning. Analyze this episode strictly as an IRL problem: the annotators are the observed agents, their ranking behavior is the evidence, and the trained reward model is the inferred preference function.

What conditions does IRL theory require for a valid inference—consistency, approximate optimality, a shared underlying reward structure—and how well do the annotators in this protocol satisfy them? Examine the actual features of the episode: persistent annotator disagreement, context-sensitive evaluations that shifted with framing, and the deployed model's subsequent reward-hacking behaviors that aligned with no individual rater's explicit intent. Does IRL explain these outcomes as predictable failures of misspecified inference conditions, or does it misdescribe them because its standard formulation assumes a single rational demonstrator? In particular, address the non-identifiability problem—the fact that identical observed ranking patterns are compatible with radically different underlying reward functions—and ask whether aggregating ratings into one reward model implicitly assumes a unique recoverable preference that the theory itself denies.

Take a definite, reasoned position: is this case best understood as an engineering success that needs only practical refinement, or does it show that IRL is conceptually unsuited to inferring human preferences from heterogeneous, framing-sensitive judgments? Specify exactly what the theory would have to revise—regarding agent homogeneity, the interpretation of disagreement as noise versus meaningful signal, or the very idea of a single "true" human preference—to remain applicable to preference aggregation at scale, and say where those revisions would leave the framework intact and where they would break it.
