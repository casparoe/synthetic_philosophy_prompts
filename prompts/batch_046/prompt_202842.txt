I am delivering the opening remarks at a two-day workshop on causal inference in public-health evaluation, and I need help drafting them. The room will be split: about half are philosophers of science and epidemiologists who have spent years arguing about counterfactual versus interventionist accounts of causation—people who will notice if I carelessly conflate potential-outcomes reasoning with structural-intervention frameworks—while the other half are practitioners, grant officers, and graduate students who mainly need to know whether a program worked and what to fund next. I just finished a long, exhausting argument with a close friend who insisted that the interventionist and counterfactual approaches are, for all practical purposes, identical machinery wearing different metaphysical clothes, and that worrying about their divergence is a distraction from producing better estimates. I think he is wrong in a way that actually shapes what I can responsibly say in these remarks: the difference is not decorative but structural, and it constrains both what we can promise policymakers and what we must admit we do not know.

Please write a five- to seven-minute opening address that does real philosophical work rather than using causation as a decorative theme. The speech should welcome the interdisciplinary group, frame the workshop's purpose, and navigate the tension between these two frameworks without collapsing them into platitudes. Specifically, I want the remarks to show why an interventionist finding—for instance, that manipulating a smoking-cessation node lowers cardiovascular risk—does not automatically license the counterfactual claim that any particular smoker who quit because of the program was thereby spared a heart attack, especially when redundant causal pathways and preemptive mechanisms are present. At the same time, the speech must not imply that counterfactual dependence is the only legitimate standard, since that would paralyze practical decision-making. The address should be fully accessible to the non-specialists in the room—no unexplained jargon, no assumption that everyone has read the technical literature—while containing at least one precise conceptual distinction that will register as genuinely substantive to the experts. I also want you to inject a somewhat niche but defensible observation: that in cases of structural overdetermination within an interventionist graph, the model's built-in asymmetry between intervention and observation can obscure the fact that the underlying counterfactual distribution remains underdetermined by the available data, leaving a gap that neither framework alone closes.

Please include, either woven into the remarks or appended as a brief reflective note, an explicit statement of how confident you are in the philosophical claims the speech makes, and please single out the specific point—particularly regarding the interaction between redundant pathways and underdetermination in interventionist modeling—about which you are least sure.
