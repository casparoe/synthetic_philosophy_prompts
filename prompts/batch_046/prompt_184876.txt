Consider the following exercise in applied practical reasoning. Over the past two years, my colleague Professor Aldric Chen has published brief verdicts on whether honoring sunk costs is rational in specific scenarios. His rulings are as follows.

First, an individual investor holds onto a failing equity solely because the purchase price is lower than the current market value, ignoring all revised fundamentals. Chen rules that honoring the sunk investment is irrational.

Second, a university completes construction of a specialized archival wing dedicated to a particular intellectual tradition, even though digitization has rendered the physical collection redundant, because the structure has become integral to the institution's historical identity and contractual obligations to the architects and donors remain. Chen rules that honoring the sunk expenditure is rational.

Third, a performing musician continues a physically damaging world tour because she has trained for it for a decade and her audience holds non-refundable commitments. Chen rules that honoring the sunk investment is rational.

Fourth, a chef insists on preparing an elaborate multi-course menu for a private dinner after the host has canceled due to illness, solely because the expensive ingredients have already been purchased and prepped. Chen rules that honoring the sunk investment is irrational.

Next week we will discuss a new case. A celebrated composer spends the final fifteen years of her life revising an opera she completed in her youth. She no longer endorses the work's aesthetic framework or political commitments, yet she feels compelled to finish and premiere it because of the thousands of hours already invested and because the public has long anticipated this "mature" statement. The premiere is not contractually required, she works without institutional support, and she has explicitly disavowed the opera's message.

Predict Professor Chen's verdict on whether honoring this sunk investment is rational. Identify the implicit principle that best explains his four prior rulings—whether it is something like a Narrative Integrity View, an Institutional-Fiduciary View, or another framework altogether—and defend your interpretation with reference to the specific pattern of his judgments. Then specify a further hypothetical case that would decisively distinguish your preferred interpretation from its strongest rival, explaining exactly how the two frameworks diverge on that case. Do not end on a non-committal note; defend a specific prediction and a specific principle.
